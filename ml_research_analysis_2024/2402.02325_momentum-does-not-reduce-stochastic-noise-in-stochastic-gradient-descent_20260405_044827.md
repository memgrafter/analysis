# Momentum Does Not Reduce Stochastic Noise in Stochastic Gradient Descent

## Quick Facts
*   **ArXiv ID**: 2402.02325
*   **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02325
*   **Reference Count**: 40
*   **Primary Result**: Momentum in SGD does not reduce the variance of stochastic gradient noise but instead increases the "degree of smoothing" of the objective function, which explains its superior generalizability even at large batch sizes.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the common belief that momentum in stochastic gradient descent (SGD) reduces stochastic gradient noise. Instead, through theoretical analysis and empirical validation on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 and WideResNet, the authors show that momentum methods (specifically SHB and QHM) increase a "degree of smoothing" parameter, δ, which convolves the objective function with a uniform ball distribution. This smoothing effect, not noise reduction, explains their better generalization. The paper introduces a method to estimate the critical batch size, revealing an effective lower gradient variance for momentum due to smoothing. Based on this, the authors propose an implicit graduated optimization algorithm that decays the learning rate and momentum factor to gradually reduce δ over training, which empirically outperforms constant-parameter SGD with momentum.

## Method Summary
The authors' method involves three main steps: (1) estimating the critical batch size \(b^\star\) for SGD and momentum methods by sweeping batch sizes and locating the batch that minimizes the stochastic first-order oracle (SFO) complexity (steps × batch size). Using a derived lower bound, this \(b^\star\) gives an upper bound on the gradient variance \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\). (2) Computing the degree of smoothing \(\delta\) for each optimizer using formulas that depend on learning rate \(\eta\), batch size \(b\), momentum factor \(\beta\), and the estimated \(C^2\) and a gradient norm bound \(K\). (3) Designing an implicit graduated optimization schedule (Algorithm 3) where the smoothing degree \(\delta_m\) is decreased in a polynomial schedule across training phases. Hyperparameters (\(\eta\), \(b\), \(\beta\)) are adjusted per phase to achieve the target \(\delta_{m+1} = \gamma_m \delta_m\), where \(\gamma_m = ((M-m)/(M-(m-1)))^p\) and \(M\) is total phases. The algorithm is tested against constant-parameter baselines on CIFAR-100.

## Key Results
*   Momentum (SHB/QHM) does not reduce stochastic gradient noise variance but increases the degree of smoothing δ compared to plain SGD for the same learning rate and batch size.
*   Critical batch size \(b^\star\) is larger for momentum methods (e.g., ~\(2^{10}\) for SHB on ResNet-18) than for SGD (~\(2^{5}\)), leading to a significantly lower inferred effective gradient variance \(C^2\) (e.g., 25 vs 1280).
*   The implicit graduated optimization algorithm with decaying learning rate and momentum factor achieves higher test accuracy and lower training loss than constant-parameter SGD with momentum on CIFAR-100.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Momentum increases smoothing via search direction error, not noise reduction.
The search direction error ω (e.g., \(ω_{\text{SHB}} = m_t - ∇f(x_t)\)) is an additive noise term with zero mean and bounded variance. When averaged over iterations, this error acts as an additive convolution with a uniform ball distribution, smoothing the objective function. The magnitude of δ depends on hyperparameters (η, b, β) and the true gradient variance \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\), not on any reduction in \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\) itself. Evidence: Proposition 4.1 derives δ formulas for SHB and QHM showing δ > δ_SGD for same η,b; abstract states smoothing explains superior generalizability.

### Critical batch size estimation reveals lower effective noise due to smoothing.
By measuring \(b^\star\) where SFO complexity is minimized and using Proposition 3.1's lower bound \(b^\star > η C^2_{\text{opt}} / ϵ^2\), an upper bound on \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\) is back-calculated. Momentum methods yield a much smaller estimated \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\) than SGD. This lower effective noise is not from direct variance reduction but because smoothing makes optimization behave as if gradient estimates had lower variance. Evidence: Fig. 2,7 show SHB \(b^\star ≈ 2^{10}\) vs SGD \(≈ 2^5\); derived \(C^2_{\text{SHB}} < C^2_{\text{SGD}}\).

### Graduated optimization exploits smoothing by decaying δ over time.
If the objective is a "new σ-nice" function, starting with a large δ (high smoothing) early helps escape sharp minima. Decaying δ (via decaying η and/or β) allows convergence to a sharper minimum of the original function. Theorem 4.1 guarantees convergence in \(O(1/ϵ^{1/p})\) iterations if δ decays as \(\gamma_m\). Evidence: Algorithm 3 and Theorem 4.1; Figs. 5,16,17,22-25 show empirical superiority of decaying parameters over constant.

## Foundational Learning

**Stochastic Gradient Noise & Variance**
*Why needed*: The paper distinguishes between true gradient variance \(C^2\) and the effective smoothing noise δ. Understanding that mini-batch gradients are unbiased with variance ~\(C^2/b\) is fundamental.
*Quick check*: If batch size b doubles, what happens to \(\mathbb{E}[‖∇f_S(x_t) - ∇f(x_t)‖^2]\)? (Answer: halves, per Lemma A.1).

**Momentum as an AR(1) process**
*Why needed*: SHB and QHM create correlated updates. This correlation structure is key to deriving the ω error terms and their bounded norm (Proposition 4.1).
*Quick check*: For SHB with β=0.9, what is \(m_t\) as an infinite sum of past gradients? (Answer: an exponentially weighted sum, a low-pass filter; paper argues this does not reduce variance).

**Implicit Graduated Optimization & σ‑nice functions**
*Why needed*: The proposed algorithm treats each phase as optimizing a progressively less smoothed σ‑strongly‑convex version of the original function. Understanding this geometry is essential.
*Quick check*: What property of a new σ‑nice function guarantees graduated optimization works? (Answer: the optimal point of a less smoothed function is close to that of a more smoothed one by a factor \(||δ_m| - |δ_{m+1}||\), and each smoothed function is σ‑strongly convex in a neighborhood).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**
Input Data -> Optimizer (SGD/SHB/NSHB) -> Training Loop -> Metrics (T, gradient norms) -> SmoothingCalc (δ) -> GraduatedScheduler (update η,b,β) -> Next Phase

**Critical Path**
1.  Estimate \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\) and \(K\): sweep batch size \(b\) for a fixed η, record steps \(T(b)\) until gradient norm < 0.5, find \(b^\star = \arg\min T(b)·b\). Use Proposition 3.1 to compute upper bound on \(C^2\). Measure maximum full‑gradient norm during training for \(K\).
2.  Compute initial δ: plug \(C^2\), \(K\), chosen η, \(b\), β into the δ formula (Eq. 2-4).
3.  Set decay schedule: choose total phases \(M\), polynomial decay exponent \(p∈(0,1]\) (e.g., 0.9), initial parameters \((\eta_1, b_1, β_1)\).
4.  For each phase \(m=1..M\): train for \(T_m\) steps (from Theorem D.1 with \(\epsilon_m = \sigma^2 \delta_m^2/2\)), then update hyperparameters to achieve \(\delta_{m+1} = \gamma_m \delta_m\). Typically, decay η (\(\eta_{m+1} = \gamma_m \eta_m\)) and adjust β and/or b accordingly.

**Design Tradeoffs**
*   **b vs computational cost**: Large b reduces variance but may hit a δ plateau if \(K^2\) term dominates. Use b up to \(b_{\text{sat}}\) where δ stops decreasing significantly (from δ vs. b plot).
*   **η vs. β decay**: Decaying only η is simple (\(\kappa_m = \gamma_m\)). Decaying only β requires solving a cubic for \(\rho_m\) to satisfy δ decay. Joint decay offers more control but increases complexity.
*   **p in γ_m**: Smaller p (e.g., 0.1) → slower δ decay → more smoothing epochs → better escape from sharp minima but slower final convergence. Larger p (e.g., 1.0) → faster final convergence but risks poorer generalization.

**Failure Signatures**
1.  **δ too high throughout**: Loss decreases very slowly or diverges; test accuracy poor. Cause: initial η or β too large. Fix: reduce η₁ or β₁.
2.  **δ drops too fast**: Early loss decreases rapidly, but final test accuracy plateaus at a high value. Cause: p too large or aggressive batch increase (λ_m). Fix: increase p or reduce λ_m.
3.  **δ saturates at high b**: No δ benefit beyond a certain batch size, but compute cost rises. Cause: \(K^2\) term dominates, making \(C^2/b\) negligible. Fix: reduce β to lower its factor, or accept b plateau and focus on η/β decay.

**First 3 Experiments**
1.  **Measure critical batch size**: For ResNet-18/CIFAR-100, run SGD and SHB with fixed η=0.1, sweep b ∈ {2³, ..., 2¹³}. Record steps T(b) until moving average of mini‑batch gradient norms < 0.5. Compute SFO = T(b)·b, find empirical b⋆ (minimum). Repeat with 3 seeds.
2.  **Compute and plot δ vs. b**: Using estimated \(C^2\) from step 1 and measured \(K\), compute δ(b) for SGD and SHB. Plot on log scale. Identify b_sat (δ plateau). Choose initial b₁ ≤ b_sat for graduated optimization.
3.  **Test graduated optimization vs. constant**: Train with the graduated schedule (M=200, p=0.9, initial η₁=0.1, b₁=256, β₁=0.9) and with constant η=0.1, b=256, β=0.9 for 200 epochs. Plot training loss and test accuracy per epoch. Expected: graduated schedule yields lower final loss and higher test accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**
*Question*: Does Nesterov accelerated gradient (NAG) exhibit the same smoothing properties as SHB and QHM, or does its lookahead mechanism fundamentally change the relationship between hyperparameters and the smoothing degree δ?
*Basis in paper*: This paper focuses on SHB and QHM, explicitly excluding NAG.
*Why unresolved*: NAG computes gradients at a lookahead point, which may alter the variance \(C^2_{\text{opt}}\) and norm bound \(K_{\text{opt}}\) in Proposition 4.1, leading to a different δ formula.
*What evidence would resolve it*: Convergence analysis for NAG analogous to Theorems 3.1–3.2 to derive δ_NAG, and experimental validation by measuring critical batch size and test accuracy vs. batch size for NAG.

**Open Question 2**
*Question*: Do the derived relationships between batch size, learning rate, momentum and smoothing degree hold for Transformer architectures, or are they specific to CNNs like ResNet?
*Basis in paper*: Experiments are limited to ResNet-18 and WideResNet-28-10 on CIFAR-100; theory is general but empirical validation is not extended.
*Why unresolved*: Transformers may have different gradient statistics (e.g., heavier tails, different scaling with batch size) that violate the assumptions or constants in the smoothing formulas.
*What evidence would resolve it*: Repeat critical batch size estimation and degree-of-smoothing calculation for Vision Transformers or language models; if discrepancies appear, identify which assumptions (e.g., Lipschitz constants, variance bounds) need refinement.

**Open Question 3**
*Question*: Is the polynomial decay schedule for the smoothing degree provably optimal for implicit graduated optimization across all problem classes, or could adaptive schedules perform better for practical DNNs that only approximately satisfy the new σ-nice assumptions?
*Basis in paper*: Algorithm 3 adopts polynomial decay based on prior work but does not compare systematically to other strategies; Theorem 4.1 guarantees convergence but not optimality.
*Why unresolved*: The theoretical derivation of p∈(0,1] relies on properties of the new σ-nice function; real DNNs may benefit from schedules responding to actual progress (e.g., based on gradient norm or validation performance).
*What evidence would resolve it*: Theoretical analysis showing polynomial decay minimizes total queries under the σ-nice model; extensive empirical study comparing polynomial to exponential, cosine, and adaptive schedules on SHB/NSHB across tasks.

**Open Question 4**
*Question*: Is the increased smoothing from momentum the primary explanation for its superior generalization, or do other factors (e.g., implicit bias, noise covariance structure) contribute independently?
*Basis in paper*: The paper correlates higher δ with better test accuracy but does not isolate smoothing from other momentum effects; the conclusion that smoothing "resolves inconsistencies" is not definitively proven causal.
*Why unresolved*: Momentum simultaneously affects many aspects (effective step size, directional bias). Without disentangling these, smoothing may not be the dominant mechanism.
*What evidence would resolve it*: Controlled experiments matching smoothing between SGD and momentum (e.g., via additive noise) while controlling for other factors; if generalization gaps persist, additional mechanisms are at play.

## Limitations
*   Theoretical assumptions (bounded variance, "new σ‑nice" functions) may not hold for real deep networks; experiments are confined to ResNet‑18/WideResNet‑28‑10 on CIFAR‑100.
*   Critical batch size estimation is noisy; back‑calculated \(C^2\) and \(K\) depend on the stopping criterion (gradient norm < 0.5) and approximations of full‑gradient evaluation.
*   The implicit graduated optimization schedule is underspecified in the paper; the heuristic used for reproduction may differ from the authors', affecting reproducibility.

## Confidence
*   Momentum increases smoothing, not noise reduction: **Medium‑High** (derivation solid under stated assumptions).
*   \(b^\star\) analysis yields lower effective \(C^2\): **Medium** (empirical pattern is clear but inference is approximate and noisy).
*   Graduated optimization improves generalization: **Medium** (benefit observed in experiments, but theory requires strong "new σ‑nice" conditions rarely verified in practice).
