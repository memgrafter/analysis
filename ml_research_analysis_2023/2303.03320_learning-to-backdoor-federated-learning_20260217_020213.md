# Learning to Backdoor Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.03320
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03320
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: RL-based backdoor attack outperforms static heuristics under defenses and achieves high durability

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a reinforcement learning-based framework for backdoor attacks in federated learning, modeling the attack as a Markov decision process. The method uses a simulator built from local data and system knowledge to train a policy that optimizes hyperparameters for backdoor injection over multiple rounds. Experiments show this approach outperforms state-of-the-art attacks under various defenses while maintaining both high backdoor accuracy and main-task performance, with superior durability even after the attack stops.

## Method Summary
The framework formulates federated learning backdoor attacks as a Markov decision process and uses reinforcement learning to train an attack policy. It employs a two-step action compression process (local search and model crafting) with RL optimizing hyperparameters for both steps. The attacker builds a simulator using local data and known system parameters to train the policy, which is then deployed in real federated learning rounds. The method uses TD3 (Twin Delayed DDPG) for continuous action spaces and optimizes for cumulative discounted reward over multiple rounds.

## Key Results
- RL-based attack achieves near 100% backdoor accuracy under defenses like Krum, Median, and norm-bounding while maintaining main-task performance
- Outperforms state-of-the-art attacks (BFL, DBA, PGD) in both attack performance and durability
- Maintains high backdoor accuracy even after attack stops, showing superior durability compared to baseline methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: RL-based policy outperforms static heuristics by simulating future system behavior
- Mechanism: The attacker models backdoor injection as a Markov decision process and trains a policy in a simulated environment using local data and known system parameters, allowing optimization over multiple rounds
- Core assumption: The simulator accurately approximates the real FL environment including defenses and benign client behavior
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The main idea is to formulate the backdoor attack problem as a Markov decision process (MDP) and utilize the local data of malicious devices and common knowledge to build a simulator..."
  - [section 2.1] "With the simulator built, the set of malicious devices can adopt a deep reinforcement learning algorithm such as TD3... to solve the MDP to train an attack policy..."
- Break condition: Simulator mismatch with real FL dynamics causes policy failure

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Two-step action compression preserves attack effectiveness while reducing complexity
- Mechanism: First update model using local and poisoned data, then adjust top-α% parameters to align with clean updates; RL optimizes hyperparameters for both steps
- Core assumption: Parameter masking effectively hides malicious updates from aggregation defenses
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.2] "For each layer of ˜gt, the attacker modifies a fractionαt of parameters that differ the most from the corresponding parameters of gt..."
  - [section 2.2] "Rather than searching for all the parameters together, the two sub-actions are optimized alternatively."
- Break condition: Defense detects masked parameter differences or aggregation rule neutralizes the crafted update

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Long-term reward optimization yields higher backdoor durability
- Mechanism: Reward function includes both main and backdoor tasks, and RL optimizes for cumulative discounted reward over T rounds, leading to persistent backdoors even after attack stops
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our attack framework is both adaptive and flexible and achieves strong attack performance and durability even under state-of-the-art defenses."
  - [section 3] "After the first 100 FL, our RL attack shows the slowest accuracy decay among all attacks... still maintains80% backdoor accuracy, while other attacks' backdoor accuracy is all below40%."
- Break condition: Defense explicitly resets or sanitizes model parameters post-training

  - Why needed here: Understanding Krum, Median, and norm-bounding is essential to design attacks that evade them and to simulate their behavior

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Simulator -> RL Agent (TD3) -> Attack Execution -> Reward Function
- Critical path:
  1. Build simulator from known system parameters
  2. Train RL policy in simulator (alternating local search and model crafting steps)
  3. Deploy policy in real FL rounds
  4. Observe reward and adjust if needed
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Action space compression vs. attack flexibility: Two-step approach reduces complexity but may limit optimal attack strategies
  - Simulator fidelity vs. training efficiency: More accurate simulators improve policy quality but require more computation
  - Black-box vs. white-box: White-box assumes known defenses; black-box requires adaptive exploration, increasing risk of failure
- Failure signatures:
  - Simulator mismatch: Policy performs well in simulation but poorly in real FL
  - Aggregation detection: Crafted updates are filtered out by Krum/Median/norm-bounding
  - Reward misalignment: Policy achieves backdoor success but fails main-task maintenance
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train RL policy in simulator with FedAvg aggregation, evaluate backdoor accuracy after 100 rounds
  2. Test policy under Krum defense in simulator; compare backdoor/main-task accuracy vs. BFL baseline
  3. Measure backdoor durability: Stop attack after 100 rounds, observe decay under FedAvg aggregation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we design an effective reinforcement learning-based backdoor attack in a black-box setting where the attacker lacks knowledge of the federated learning system parameters?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the failure of a black-box RL attack due to the attacker's incorrect assumptions about the FL environment, suggesting the need for a more conservative approach and continuous adaptation
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the challenge but does not provide a concrete solution for designing an effective attack in the black-box setting
- What evidence would resolve it: A successful demonstration of an RL-based backdoor attack in a black-box setting, showing comparable performance to white-box attacks

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can we develop a constrained reinforcement learning approach to optimize the backdoor attack performance while maintaining a desired level of main task accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions the importance of choosing a proper λ (or poison ratio) to maximize backdoor attack performance while maintaining good main task accuracy, and suggests considering constrained RL with the desired main task accuracy as a constraint
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a concrete implementation or evaluation of a constrained RL approach for this purpose
- What evidence would resolve it: A successful implementation and evaluation of a constrained RL approach that achieves high backdoor accuracy while maintaining the desired main task accuracy

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of trigger patterns and sub-triggers affect the performance of backdoor attacks in federated learning, and can we develop more effective trigger patterns?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the use of global triggers and sub-triggers in backdoor attacks and mentions that DBA attackers using random sub-triggers have lower performance compared to attackers using the same global trigger
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the impact of different trigger patterns on attack performance or propose methods for developing more effective triggers
- What evidence would resolve it: An empirical study comparing the performance of different trigger patterns and sub-triggers in backdoor attacks, along with the development of new, more effective trigger patterns

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can we improve the durability of backdoor attacks in federated learning, especially after the attack stops?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that the proposed RL-based attack achieves better durability compared to other attacks, but it does not provide insights into how to further improve the durability of backdoor attacks
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore strategies for enhancing the longevity of backdoors in the model after the attack has ceased
- What evidence would resolve it: A successful demonstration of a backdoor attack that maintains high backdoor accuracy even after the attack has stopped, along with an analysis of the factors contributing to this durability

## Limitations

- Simulator fidelity is critical but not validated against real FL implementations
- Defense generalization claims untested against broader range of defenses beyond Krum, Median, and norm-bounding
- Two-step action compression effectiveness under defenses asserted but not independently validated

## Confidence

- High: The RL framework formulation (MDP, TD3 training) is clearly described and technically sound
- Medium: Experimental results showing attack superiority over baselines are well-documented, but rely on a single simulator implementation
- Low: Claims about simulator accuracy, defense robustness, and durability are not externally validated

## Next Checks

1. **Simulator Validation**: Compare the simulator's aggregation and defense behavior against real FL implementations (e.g., FedML, Flower) to confirm fidelity
2. **Cross-Defense Robustness**: Test the trained RL policy against a broader set of defenses (e.g., FoolsGold, DP-based defenses) to assess generalization
3. **Alternative RL Algorithms**: Re-implement the attack using alternative RL methods (e.g., PPO, SAC) to determine if the observed performance is specific to TD3 or robust across algorithms
