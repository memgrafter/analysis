# Model Based Explanations of Concept Drift

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.09331
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09331

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of explaining concept drift in data streams, which occurs when the underlying data distribution changes over time. The authors propose a method to explain drift by first training a model to capture the relevant characteristics of the drift and then using various explanation techniques to extract information from the model. The approach is model and explanation independent and can be applied to both semantic and non-semantic data. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in identifying drifting features and providing intuitive explanations for the drift.

## Method Summary
The proposed method involves two main steps: drift localization or segmentation using probabilistic classification or regression models, and applying explanation techniques such as feature importance, counterfactual explanations, or dimensionality reduction to extract information about the drift. The method is model and explanation independent, allowing for the use of various models and techniques depending on the specific problem and data characteristics.

## Key Results
- The method can correctly identify drifting features in synthetic data with known drift characteristics.
- The approach provides intuitive explanations for drift in real-world datasets, such as the Electricity market prices dataset.
- The method is effective in explaining drift in high-dimensional non-semantic data, such as images, using local explanation techniques like counterfactuals.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Drift explanation can be reduced to model explanation by training a model to capture the conditional distribution of time given data.
- Mechanism: By training a probabilistic classifier h(t|x) to estimate P[T=t|X=x], the model implicitly learns the drifting behavior. Analyzing this model with XAI techniques reveals which features and regions of the data space are most affected by drift.
- Core assumption: The conditional distribution P[T|X] contains all relevant information about the drift.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose a methodology to reduce the explanation of concept drift to an explanation of models that are trained in a suitable way extracting relevant information regarding the drift."
  - [section 3.2]: "Thus, by training a model h(t|x) to estimate P[T=t|X=x] we essentially extract information about the drift."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. No corpus paper directly discusses this model-reduction mechanism.
- Break condition: If the trained model fails to capture the true conditional distribution, the explanations will be misleading or incorrect.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Drift localization can be performed by analyzing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the model's output and the prior distribution.
- Mechanism: For each data point, a probabilistic model is trained on all other points. The KL divergence between the model's prediction for the left-out point and the prior probability of the time class indicates whether that point lies in a drifting region.
- Core assumption: High KL divergence between h(x) and PT indicates the presence of drift at x.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.4]: "If the probability coincides with the prior probability up to statistical insufficiencies, i.e., h(x) ≈ PT, then there is no drift and vice versa."
  - [algorithm 1]: Line 9 shows the use of DKL(h(xk)||h0) ≥ θ as the decision rule.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence in corpus. Weak support from related drift detection literature.
- Break condition: If the model overfits or underfits, the KL divergence may not accurately reflect the true drift.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Drift segmentation can be achieved by training a segmentation-based multi-regression model to predict (T, T², ..., Tᵈ) from X.
- Mechanism: The variance of the predicted time moments given a segmentation provides an upper bound on the conditional independence of T and X given the segmentation. Minimizing this variance yields segments with homogeneous drift behavior.
- Core assumption: Conditional independence T⊥⊥X|L(X) is equivalent to homogeneous drift behavior within segments.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.4]: "Using [39, Theorem 2] this can be upper bounded by the variance of (T,T²,...,Tᵈ) given L(X), i.e., the MSE of the obtained decision tree, and a constant term that depends on d and goes to zero for d → ∞."
  - [algorithm 2]: Line 4 shows training a segmentation-based multi-regression model.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence in corpus. Weak support from related segmentation literature.
- Break condition: If the regression model is misspecified or the variance bound is loose, the segmentation may not capture the true drift segments.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data stream preprocessing -> Model training -> XAI method application -> Explanation generation
- Critical path:
  1. Preprocess data stream to obtain labeled segments (before/after drift or multiple segments).
  2. Train a model to capture the relevant information about the drift (classifier for localization, regression for segmentation).
  3. Apply a suitable XAI method to the trained model to extract explanations.
  4. Present the explanations in an interpretable format to the user.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Global vs. local explanations: Global explanations provide an overview of the entire drift but may miss local nuances, while local explanations are more detailed but require selecting characteristic samples.
  - Model complexity vs. interpretability: More complex models may capture the drift better but be harder to explain, while simpler models may be easier to interpret but miss important aspects of the drift.
  - Computational cost vs. real-time applicability: Some XAI methods, like counterfactual explanations, are computationally expensive and may not be suitable for real-time applications.
- Failure signatures:
  - Inaccurate drift detection: If the drift is not detected correctly, the subsequent explanations will be based on incorrect labels.
  - Model underfitting or overfitting: If the model fails to capture the true conditional distribution, the explanations will be misleading.
  - XAI method misalignment: If the chosen XAI method is not well-suited for the specific drift characteristics, the explanations may not be informative.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Synthetic data with known drift characteristics: Generate data with a controlled drift (e.g., feature perturbations) and evaluate the method's ability to correctly identify the drifting features.
  2. Real-world benchmark data with induced drift: Apply the method to real-world datasets (e.g., sensor readings) with artificially induced drift and assess the quality of the explanations.
  3. High-dimensional image data with complex drift: Test the method on image streams with complex, non-semantic drift (e.g., changing object appearances) and evaluate the effectiveness of local XAI methods in providing intuitive explanations.
