# Transfer of knowledge among instruments in automatic music transcription

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.00426
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00426
- Reference count: 22
- One-line primary result: Transfer learning from synthesized instruments to real-world AMT data achieves comparable performance with significantly reduced training time

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates transfer learning in automatic music transcription (AMT) across different instruments using synthesized audio data. The authors train a U-net model with BiLinear LSTM on synthesized samples from various instruments and then fine-tune it on real-world recordings from MAPS and GuitarSet datasets. Their results show that a model trained on synthesized data performs well on real-world recordings even without seeing any real-world samples, achieving comparable or better F1 scores than models trained directly on target datasets. Fine-tuning on a model trained on synthesized data significantly reduces the time to achieve satisfactory results compared to training from scratch or fine-tuning from a model trained on a different real instrument. The study demonstrates that synthesized data can serve as a valuable base for creating generalizable AMT models that can be quickly adapted to new instruments through transfer learning.

## Method Summary
The method involves generating synthesized audio from instrument annotations using FluidSynth and a general MIDI soundfont, creating CQT spectrograms as input features. A U-net encoder-decoder architecture with BiLinear LSTM layers is trained on this synthesized data. The trained model is then fine-tuned on real-world AMT datasets (MAPS for piano and GuitarSet for guitar). The approach leverages the hypothesis that synthesized instruments capture fundamental pitch and rhythm patterns that transfer well to real recordings, allowing the model to focus on frequency analysis tasks rather than instrument-specific acoustic artifacts.

## Key Results
- A model trained on synthesized instruments achieves comparable or better F1 scores than models trained directly on real-world datasets (MAPS and GuitarSet) when evaluated on the same datasets
- Fine-tuning from a model trained on synthesized data significantly reduces training time compared to training from scratch or fine-tuning from a model trained on a different real instrument
- Zero-shot transfer from synthesized instruments to real-world recordings produces reasonable performance without any fine-tuning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Synthesized data provides a generalizable base for transcription models by capturing fundamental pitch and rhythm patterns independent of instrument-specific timbre details.
- Mechanism: Synthesized instruments produce clean, noise-free spectrograms that focus the model on frequency and rhythm recognition rather than learning instrument-specific acoustic artifacts.
- Core assumption: The human ability to universally recognize pitch and rhythm across different instruments applies to machine learning models when trained on clean synthesized data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The intuition behind this idea is related to the possibility of universally recognizing pitch and rhythm by the human ear - it performs similarly no matter the kind of audio source."
  - [section 2]: "Synthesized instruments may contain fewer noises than the audio recorded via microphone. However, a model trained on such data may focus more on frequency analysis tasks, which is a good base for fine-tuning it to real-world data."
- Break condition: If the model overfits to the idealized characteristics of synthesized data and fails to generalize to the noise patterns present in real recordings.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Transfer learning from synthesized data significantly reduces training time on real-world recordings compared to training from scratch.
- Mechanism: The model trained on synthesized data already captures general transcription patterns, allowing fine-tuning to adapt to instrument-specific timbre variations with fewer epochs.
- Core assumption: The knowledge learned from clean synthesized data transfers effectively to real-world data with different acoustic characteristics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Fine-tuning on a model trained on synthesized data significantly reduces the time to achieve satisfactory results compared to training from scratch or fine-tuning from a model trained on a different real instrument."
  - [section 4.3]: "Learning from randomly initialized weights needed about 600 epochs to achieve good results in measured metrics. When we tried to fine-tune model trained on GuitarSet it needed a similar time to achieve the same results for MAPS metrics as the model trained on Synthesized Instruments."
- Break condition: If the transfer gap between synthesized and real data is too large, requiring extensive fine-tuning that negates the time savings.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Synthesized data enables zero-shot transfer performance on real-world recordings without requiring any real-world training samples.
- Mechanism: The model learns fundamental frequency and rhythm recognition patterns from synthesized data that are applicable across instruments, allowing it to perform reasonably well on real recordings without adaptation.
- Core assumption: The core transcription task of identifying pitch and timing is sufficiently similar across instruments that a model trained on one instrument can generalize to others.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Their results show that a model trained on synthesized data performs well on real-world recordings even without seeing any real-world samples, achieving comparable or better F1 scores than models trained directly on target datasets."
  - [section 4.2]: "We may claim from the results that the model trained on Synthesized Instruments performed well for MAPS and GuitarSet even after zero-shot transfer."
- Break condition: If the instrument-specific timbre differences are too significant for the model to recognize without explicit exposure to that instrument's characteristics.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: CQT spectrogram generation -> U-net feature extraction -> BiLinear LSTM temporal modeling -> Linear classification -> Note prediction
- Critical path: CQT spectrogram generation → U-net feature extraction → BiLinear LSTM temporal modeling → Linear classification → Note prediction
- Design tradeoffs: U-net provides good feature extraction but may be computationally intensive; BiLinear LSTM captures temporal dependencies but adds complexity; synthesized data reduces noise but may not capture real-world acoustic variations
- Failure signatures: Poor generalization to real recordings (overfitting to synthesized data), slow convergence during fine-tuning (insufficient transfer), or degraded performance on specific instruments (insufficient diversity in training data)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a baseline model directly on real-world recordings (MAPS or GuitarSet) to establish performance benchmarks
  2. Train a model on synthesized data only and evaluate zero-shot transfer performance on real recordings
  3. Fine-tune the synthesized-data model on real recordings and compare convergence speed and final performance to training from scratch

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of transfer learning from synthesized instruments vary when using different types of synthesized audio (e.g., with added noise, different soundfonts, or more instruments)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that synthesized instruments contain fewer noises than real-world recordings and that the model may focus more on frequency analysis tasks. It also mentions that further research may focus on the impact of adding further synthesized instruments to experiments.
- Why unresolved: The current study only uses a single soundfont and two instrument types. The effects of varying the quality and diversity of synthesized audio on transfer learning performance remain unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying soundfont quality, number of instruments, and presence of synthetic noise in the training data, measuring transfer learning effectiveness across different target domains.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different spectrogram representations (e.g., Mel spectrogram vs. CQT) affect the transfer learning capability from synthesized instruments to real-world recordings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that different spectrograms may pass different information and that CQT was chosen for this study, but acknowledges that Mel spectrogram is mainly used to maximize transcription accuracy as frequencies are essential for training.
- Why unresolved: The study only used CQT spectrograms. The relationship between input representation choice and cross-domain transfer learning performance has not been investigated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments training models with different spectrogram types (CQT, Mel, linear-scale) and measuring their zero-shot transfer performance and fine-tuning efficiency on real-world datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between the size and diversity of the synthesized training dataset and the quality of transfer learning to real-world recordings?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses annotations from existing datasets to generate synthesized data and mentions that data synthesis is present in almost every currently used dataset for AMT, but does not systematically explore how dataset size or diversity affects transfer learning.
- Why unresolved: The study uses a fixed amount of synthesized data without exploring how scaling or diversifying this dataset would impact transfer learning performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the amount and diversity of synthesized training data (number of instruments, musical styles, complexity) and measuring corresponding changes in transfer learning effectiveness and convergence speed.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the transfer learning performance from synthesized instruments compare to semi-supervised approaches that use unlabeled real-world audio?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that current research often focuses on semi-supervised training methods that give outstanding results but are computationally expensive, suggesting this as an alternative approach to the synthesized data method.
- Why unresolved: The study only compares transfer learning from synthesized data to direct training and transfer from other real-world datasets, but does not compare against semi-supervised approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison experiments between models trained using synthesized data transfer learning and models trained using semi-supervised methods on the same real-world datasets, measuring both performance and computational cost.

## Limitations
- Limited evaluation to only piano and guitar instruments, which may not generalize to other instrument families
- Use of idealized synthesized audio that may not capture the full complexity of real-world acoustic variations
- Lack of statistical significance testing and multiple random seed experiments to validate result stability

## Confidence
- Synthesized data as effective pre-training: Medium confidence - results are promising but limited to specific instrument combinations and datasets
- Transfer learning efficiency gains: Medium confidence - time savings are demonstrated but absolute training times vary across datasets
- Zero-shot transfer capability: Medium confidence - performance is reasonable but not state-of-the-art compared to domain-specific training
