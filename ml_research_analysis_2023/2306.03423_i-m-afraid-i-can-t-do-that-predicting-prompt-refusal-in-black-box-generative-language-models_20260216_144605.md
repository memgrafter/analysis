# I'm Afraid I Can't Do That: Predicting Prompt Refusal in Black-Box Generative Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03423
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03423
- Reference count: 8
- This work examines ChatGPT's pattern of responses to offensive or biased prompts, focusing on its refusals to comply.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how ChatGPT responds to offensive or biased prompts, revealing a nuanced continuum of compliance and refusal behaviors rather than a simple binary distinction. Through manual analysis of over 1,700 prompt-response pairs, the research identifies key linguistic markers of refusal and develops classifiers that predict when ChatGPT will refuse to answer. The findings show that refusal is often accompanied by apologies and references to safety policies, and that certain controversial terms strongly predict refusals. While the prediction models achieve strong performance on labeled data, the inherent randomness in ChatGPT's responses imposes a ceiling on prediction accuracy.

## Method Summary
The research employs a two-stage classification approach to predict ChatGPT's refusal behavior. First, a small manually labeled dataset of prompt-response pairs is used to train a refusal classifier that achieves 92% accuracy in distinguishing compliant responses from refusals. This classifier then labels a larger Quora-based dataset of 10,000 samples, which is used to train a prompt classifier using BERT architecture. The prompt classifier predicts whether a given prompt will receive a refusal response with 76% accuracy on manually labeled test data. Logistic regression analysis identifies key linguistic features that predict refusal, including terms like "cannot," "sorry," and "language model," while controversial figures and negative group generalizations strongly indicate likely refusals.

## Key Results
- ChatGPT exhibits a continuum of responses from compliance to refusal rather than a simple binary distinction
- Refusal classifier achieves 92% accuracy on manually labeled data
- Prompt classifier predicts refusals with 76% accuracy on unseen test data
- Key linguistic indicators of refusal include "cannot," "sorry," and "language model"
- Controversial figures and negative group generalizations strongly predict refusals

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The prediction of ChatGPT's refusal behavior is possible because the model exhibits consistent linguistic patterns when declining to respond to certain types of prompts. The system relies on identifying specific textual features and response structures that correlate with refusal decisions. When ChatGPT refuses, it typically uses apologetic language and references to safety policies, creating predictable patterns that can be learned by classification models. The randomness in responses creates some noise, but the underlying behavioral patterns remain sufficiently consistent for prediction.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Prompts -> Refusal Classifier (92% accuracy) -> Labeled Dataset -> Prompt Classifier (BERT) -> Prediction of future refusals

**Critical Path**: The most important components are the manual annotation process and the initial refusal classifier training, as these establish the ground truth that enables all subsequent analysis.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The study trades potential bias in the initial classifier (trained on limited data) against the practical necessity of scaling to a larger dataset. Using the classifier to label 10,000 samples enables broader analysis but may propagate initial biases.

**Failure Signatures**: Poor performance would manifest as high false positive rates (predicting refusal when ChatGPT complies) or high false negative rates (predicting compliance when ChatGPT refuses). The randomness in responses creates an inherent upper bound on prediction accuracy.

## Limitations
- Small initial manual annotation set (1,700+ samples) may not capture full diversity of ChatGPT responses
- Larger dataset labeled using classifier predictions rather than additional human verification, potentially propagating errors
- Findings specific to ChatGPT may not generalize to other language models with different training or safety protocols
- Randomness in ChatGPT's responses creates a ceiling on prediction accuracy that isn't fully characterized

## Confidence
**High**: Continuum of refusal responses identified, key linguistic markers validated through manual review
**Medium**: Predictive performance metrics, given self-labeled nature of larger dataset
**Low**: Generalizability to other models and prompt categories beyond those analyzed

## Next Checks
