# Achieving Better Regret against Strategic Adversaries

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.06652
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06652
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: AFTRL achieves O(1) external regret against no-external regret adversaries compared to O(√T) dynamic regret of Prod-BR

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses online learning against strategic adversaries who follow no-external regret algorithms, proposing methods that exploit this extra structure while maintaining worst-case regret guarantees. The authors introduce AFTRL (Accurate Follow the Regularized Leader) and Prod-BR (Prod-Best Response) algorithms. AFTRL achieves O(1) external or forward regret against no-external regret adversaries compared to O(√T) dynamic regret of Prod-BR, while both maintain O(√T) regret against general adversaries. The AMWU (Accurate Multiplicative Weights Update) variant of AFTRL achieves last-round convergence to Nash Equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games, outperforming state-of-the-art methods like MWU and OMWU.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes three main algorithms: AFTRL, Prod-BR, and AMWU. AFTRL uses an "exploiting rate" α to weight future predictions more heavily than historical data, achieving O(1) regret when the adversary follows a no-external regret algorithm. Prod-BR combines FTRL and Best Response strategies to achieve O(√T) dynamic regret against no-external regret adversaries while maintaining O(√T log(T)) external regret in the worst case. AMWU is a special case of AFTRL that achieves last-round convergence to Nash Equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games with unique NE. The methods are tested against various adversaries including oblivious MWU, non-oblivious MWU, and self-play scenarios.

## Key Results
- AFTRL achieves O(1) external regret or O(1) forward regret against no-external regret adversaries
- AMWU achieves last-round convergence to Nash Equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games with unique NE
- Prod-BR achieves O(√T) dynamic regret against no-external regret adversaries while maintaining O(√T log(T)) external regret in the worst case
- AMWU outperforms MWU and OMWU in both convergence speed and regret bounds across random matrix games and meta games

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: AFTRL achieves O(1) external or forward regret against no-external regret adversaries by exploiting the small distance between consecutive strategies.
- Mechanism: The algorithm uses an "exploiting rate" α to weight future predictions more heavily than historical data. When the adversary follows a no-external regret algorithm, the distance between consecutive strategies is O(1/√T), which enables the AFTRL algorithm to achieve constant regret bounds.
- Core assumption: The adversary follows a no-external regret algorithm, and the exploiting rate α is appropriately tuned.
- Evidence anchors:

- Break condition: If the adversary doesn't follow a no-external regret algorithm, the distance between consecutive strategies may not be O(1/√T), breaking the constant regret guarantee.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: AMWU achieves last-round convergence to Nash Equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games with unique NE.
- Mechanism: The algorithm decreases the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the current strategy and the Nash Equilibrium by a factor of η²⁺ᵇ unless the strategy is O(ηᵇ/³)-close to the NE. This process continues until convergence.
- Core assumption: The game has a unique Nash Equilibrium, and the learning rate η is sufficiently small.
- Evidence anchors:

  - [section]: "Theorem 6.1... the dynamic of AMWU converges last round to the NE of the game"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mention of AMWU or last-round convergence mechanisms
- Break condition: If the game doesn't have a unique Nash Equilibrium, or if the learning rate is too large, the convergence guarantee may not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Prod-BR achieves O(√T) dynamic regret against no-external regret adversaries while maintaining O(√T log(T)) external regret in the worst case.
- Mechanism: The algorithm runs two separate algorithms (FTRL and Best Response) and combines their strategies. The FTRL maintains a performance guarantee against worst-case scenarios, while the Best Response exploits the extra structure against no-external regret adversaries.
- Core assumption: The adversary follows a no-external regret algorithm, and the best response strategy is effective against it.
- Evidence anchors:

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can AFTRL achieve O(1) forward regret against no-external regret adversaries in settings beyond two-player zero-sum games?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates O(1) forward regret for AFTRL against no-external regret adversaries in the context of online linear optimization and specifically mentions zero-sum games as a special case with AMWU achieving last round convergence.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses primarily on zero-sum games and online linear optimization. The authors suggest extending AFTRL to other online learning classes but don't provide specific examples or theorems for other game-theoretic settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Formal proof that AFTRL maintains O(1) forward regret in other strategic settings like general-sum games or repeated games with more than two players, or empirical evidence showing the same behavior in these settings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does Prod-BR maintain its O(√T) dynamic regret guarantee against no-external regret adversaries when the adversary uses algorithms like AdaHedge where the distance between consecutive strategies is O(1/√t)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly notes that while Lemma 4.1 holds for many no-external regret algorithms, there are exceptions like AdaHedge where the distance between consecutive strategies is O(1/√t).
- Why unresolved: The paper only mentions this as a theoretical consideration but doesn't provide a formal analysis or experimental validation of Prod-BR's performance against such algorithms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Formal regret analysis of Prod-BR against AdaHedge or similar algorithms, or experimental results comparing Prod-BR's performance against these algorithms.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can AMWU be extended to achieve last round convergence in games with non-unique Nash equilibria?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper assumes uniqueness of Nash equilibrium for the last round convergence result of AMWU and explicitly states that relaxing this assumption is left for future work.
- Why unresolved: The proof of last round convergence relies heavily on the uniqueness assumption, and the authors acknowledge that games with non-unique equilibria have measure zero but don't provide a method to handle them.
- What evidence would resolve it: A modified version of AMWU or a different algorithm that provably achieves last round convergence in games with multiple equilibria, or theoretical proof that such an extension is impossible.

## Limitations

- The theoretical guarantees assume adversaries perfectly follow no-external regret algorithms, which may not hold in realistic settings where adversaries are strategic but imperfect.
- The last-round convergence guarantee for AMWU requires the uniqueness of Nash Equilibrium, which may not hold in many practical games.
- The paper focuses on two-player zero-sum games and online linear optimization, leaving open the question of whether these results extend to more general game-theoretic settings.

## Confidence

High confidence in the O(1) regret guarantees of AFTRL against no-external regret adversaries.
Medium confidence in the last-round convergence guarantees of AMWU due to the uniqueness assumption requirement.
Medium confidence in the dynamic regret improvements of Prod-BR against strategic adversaries due to limited experimental validation.
