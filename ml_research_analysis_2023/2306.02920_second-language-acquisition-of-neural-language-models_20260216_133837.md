# Second Language Acquisition of Neural Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02920
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02920
- Reference count: 34
- Key outcome: L1 pretraining generally promotes better linguistic generalization in L2 neural language models, with varying effects across different grammar types and language pairs.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates second language acquisition in neural language models by training bilingual LMs under scenarios similar to human L2 acquisition. The authors compare LMs with and without first language (L1) pretraining to examine how L1 affects the efficiency of L2 grammar acquisition. Using French, German, Russian, and Japanese as L1s and English as L2, they train models on monolingual and bilingual corpora, then evaluate grammatical knowledge using BLiMP and CLAMS datasets. The study reveals that L1 pretraining generally improves L2 performance, with different L1s showing varying transfer effects, and that morphological and syntactic knowledge transfers more effectively than semantic knowledge.

## Method Summary
The study employs an XLM-based bilingual language model architecture with masked language modeling objectives. First, monolingual language models are pretrained on 100M-word corpora for each L1 (French, German, Russian, Japanese) for 100 epochs. These models are then further trained on bilingual (L1-L2) data from the Tatoeba parallel corpus for another 100 epochs using three different training configurations: TLM-nopara (no translation pairs), TLM-para (with translation pairs), and TLM-drop (with translation pairs but dropped during training). Models are evaluated on BLiMP and CLAMS grammatical judgment tasks using pseudo-perplexity to select correct sentences in minimal pairs.

## Key Results
- L1 pretraining generally promotes better linguistic generalization in L2 compared to training without L1 pretraining
- Different L1s incur different generalization effects in L2, with French and German being more effective than Japanese and Russian
- L1 pretraining has varying effects on different types of grammar items, with morphological and syntactic items benefiting more than semantic and syntax&semantics items
- L2 knowledge acquisition does not progress significantly until models see the entire dataset multiple times (50-100 epochs)
- L1 knowledge degrades during L2 acquisition while L2 effects on L1 gradually fade

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: L1 pretraining accelerates L2 grammar acquisition in neural language models.
- Mechanism: Pretraining on L1 exposes the model to language-specific patterns and structures, which serve as transferable knowledge when learning L2. The model leverages shared linguistic universals and structural similarities between L1 and L2 to bootstrap L2 learning.
- Core assumption: There exist cross-linguistic universals and structural similarities that can be transferred between languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "L1 pretraining generally promotes better linguistic generalization in L2"
  - [section 4.1]: "Most of the ∆ values are positive; i.e., the models pretrained with L1s achieved better results than those without pretraining."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - limited cross-linguistic studies on neural LM transfer in this specific setup.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Different L1s incur different generalization effects in L2 acquisition.
- Mechanism: The degree of linguistic similarity between L1 and L2 determines transfer effectiveness. Languages closer to English (e.g., French, German) share more grammatical features and transfer more effectively than more distant languages (e.g., Japanese, Russian).
- Core assumption: Linguistic distance correlates with transfer effectiveness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2]: "French is the highest, followed closely by German, and Japanese and Russian are far behind these two languages"
  - [section 2.1]: "We expect that the transfer to English becomes more difficult in order of French, German, Russian, and Japanese"
  - [corpus]: Moderate evidence - human language acquisition studies support this ordering.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: L1 pretraining has varying effects on different types of grammar items.
- Mechanism: Morphological and syntactic knowledge transfers more effectively than semantic knowledge between languages. The model can leverage surface-level structural patterns more readily than abstract semantic mappings.
- Core assumption: Different grammatical categories have different transferabilities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3]: "morphological and syntactic items get larger gains than semantic and syntax&semantics items"
  - [section 4.2]: "different L1s incur different generalizations in L2"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - limited research on cross-linguistic semantic transfer in neural LMs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different neural network architectures (e.g., unidirectional vs bidirectional LMs) affect second language acquisition patterns compared to the XLM model used in this study?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that testing unidirectional LMs would be a reasonable direction considering humans' incremental language processing.
- Why unresolved: The study only used the XLM bidirectional architecture, limiting generalizability to other model types.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments training and evaluating multiple LM architectures (including unidirectional models) on the same L2 acquisition task.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between the number of L2 exposures (epochs) required for effective grammatical knowledge acquisition in LMs versus humans, and what factors influence this difference?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that L2 knowledge acquisition in LMs did not progress significantly until seeing the entire dataset 50-100 times, while humans are argued to acquire vocabulary after encountering the same word about 12 times.
- Why unresolved: The study only compared these rough numbers without deeper investigation into the factors causing the difference or potential methods to improve LM efficiency.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed comparative studies of L2 acquisition trajectories across different data sizes, frequencies, and model architectures, alongside human learning data.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the asymmetric effect between L1 and L2 knowledge degradation during bilingual training manifest in terms of specific linguistic phenomena and what mechanisms cause this asymmetry?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observed that L1 knowledge degraded during L2 acquisition while L2 effects on L1 gradually faded, showing an asymmetry effect between L1 and L2.
- Why unresolved: The study did not investigate which specific grammatical phenomena were most affected by this asymmetry or the underlying mechanisms causing the differential effects.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of performance changes across specific linguistic phenomena in both L1 and L2 during training, combined with probing studies of internal representations to identify causes of asymmetric degradation.

## Limitations
- Dataset sizes used (100M words for monolingual pretraining and Tatoeba parallel corpus for bilingual training) may be insufficient to draw definitive conclusions about L2 acquisition dynamics
- Evaluation focuses primarily on grammatical acceptability judgments rather than broader measures of language proficiency or communicative competence
- Study examines only four typologically diverse languages as L1s, limiting generalizability to other language families or linguistic structures

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The finding that L1 pretraining generally improves L2 grammatical generalization is well-supported by consistent positive delta values across multiple language pairs and grammar categories. The observation that L1 knowledge degrades during L2 acquisition is also robustly demonstrated through comparative evaluations.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that different L1s incur different generalization effects has moderate support, though the specific ordering (French > German > Russian > Japanese) could benefit from testing with additional languages and larger datasets. The differential effects across grammar categories (morphological/syntactic vs semantic) are observed but require more granular analysis to establish causality.

**Low Confidence**: The specific claim about the non-significance of translation pairs in bilingual training (TLM-para vs TLM-drop comparison) is based on relatively small performance differences that may not be practically meaningful. The characterization of the "entire dataset" threshold for L2 acquisition progress is somewhat arbitrary and could vary with different dataset sizes or model architectures.

## Next Checks

1. **Dataset Size Sensitivity Analysis**: Replicate the experiments with varying dataset sizes (e.g., 50M, 200M, 500M words) to determine whether the observed L1 transfer benefits and L2 acquisition trajectories are robust to data scale. This would clarify whether the current findings are artifacts of the chosen dataset sizes.

2. **Cross-linguistic Generalization**: Extend the L1 language set to include additional language pairs that vary in typological distance from English (e.g., Spanish, Mandarin, Arabic) to test whether the observed patterns generalize beyond the current language sample and to refine the linguistic distance hypothesis.

3. **Longitudinal Knowledge Tracing**: Implement fine-grained tracking of both L1 and L2 knowledge acquisition throughout training (e.g., every 10 epochs) to determine whether the "multiple passes through dataset" observation represents a true threshold phenomenon or a gradual progression, and to identify potential early indicators of successful L2 acquisition.
