# A proof of convergence of inverse reinforcement learning for multi-objective optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06137
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06137
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: Proves convergence of Wasserstein inverse reinforcement learning (WIRL) for multi-objective optimization using the projective subgradient method

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes convergence guarantees for Wasserstein inverse reinforcement learning (WIRL) in multi-objective optimization settings. The authors reformulate WIRL as a convex optimization problem and prove that the projective subgradient method converges to within any desired error tolerance. The theoretical framework applies not only to WIRL but also to maximum entropy IRL and guided cost learning. Through examples in linear and quadratic programming, the paper demonstrates how the algorithm successfully learns reward functions that capture expert behavior, providing a rigorous foundation for applying IRL to complex optimization tasks.

## Method Summary
The paper formulates an inverse problem equivalent to WIRL, where the objective function F(φ) is convex and Lipschitz continuous. By applying the projected subgradient method with appropriate learning rates, F(φ) can be minimized to within any desired error ε in a finite number of iterations. The algorithm iteratively updates the reward function parameters φ by computing subgradients based on the difference between optimal actions under the current reward function and expert actions, then projecting the update back onto the feasible set Φ.

## Key Results
- Convergence of WIRL is guaranteed by reducing it to a convex optimization problem solvable via the projective subgradient method
- The objective function F(φ) is proven to be both convex and Lipschitz continuous under reasonable assumptions
- The subgradient of F at φ can be computed as the difference between optimal and expert actions
- Algorithm successfully learns reward functions in linear and quadratic programming examples

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The objective function F(φ) = (1/N)∑φ⊺a(φ,s(n)) - (1/N)∑φ⊺a(n) is convex and Lipschitz continuous

- Evidence anchors:

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The subgradient of F at φ can be computed as (1/N)∑a(φ,s(n)) - (1/N)∑a(n)
- Mechanism: By showing that for any φ₁, φ₂ ∈ Φ, the difference F(φ₂) - F(φ₁) can be bounded below by the inner product of (φ₂ - φ₁) with this subgradient, proving it satisfies the subgradient inequality
- Core assumption: The action a(φ,s) is uniquely defined as the arg max solution to the optimization problem
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "For any φ₁, φ₂ ∈ Φ, we have φ₂⊺(a(φ₁,s(n)) - a(n)) = φ₁⊺(a(φ₁,s(n)) - a(n)) + (φ₂ - φ₁)⊺(a(φ₁,s(n)) - a(n))"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Feature map f -> Generator gφ -> IMOOP solver -> Projection operator
- Critical path:
  1. Initialize φ₁ ∈ Φ
  2. Compute subgradient: (1/N)∑a(φₖ,s(n)) - (1/N)∑a(n)
  3. Update: φₖ₊₁ = ProjΦ(φₖ - αₖ × subgradient)
  4. Check convergence: Is F(φₖ₊₁) - min F < ε?

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Learning rate schedule: Nonsummable diminishing rates guarantee convergence but may be slower than constant rates
  - Projection frequency: Projecting after every update ensures feasibility but adds computational overhead
  - Subgradient approximation: Exact subgradients require solving optimization problems, while approximations may speed up computation but affect convergence

- Failure signatures:
  - Non-convergence: Check if Φ is truly closed and convex, and if h is continuous with compact X(s)
  - Oscillations: May indicate inappropriate learning rate schedule
  - Numerical instability: Could arise from ill-conditioned optimization problems in the generator

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Linear programming case: Test with simple 2D linear constraints to verify the algorithm finds the correct reward weights
  2. Quadratic programming case: Verify convergence with positive semidefinite A matrices and bounded b vectors
  3. Sensitivity analysis: Perturb expert trajectories and measure how quickly the algorithm adapts to new optimal weights

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Under what specific conditions does Wasserstein Inverse Reinforcement Learning (WIRL) guarantee that learner trajectories will mimic expert trajectories?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note this as a future work, stating "We have proved that WIRL converges, whereas it has not been proven whether learner's trajectories mimic expert's trajectories when WIRL is convergent."
- Why unresolved: While the paper proves convergence of the algorithm, it does not establish the relationship between the convergence of the algorithm and the convergence of the learned policy to mimic expert behavior.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal proof demonstrating that when WIRL converges, the generated trajectories will converge to those of the expert, potentially through analyzing the relationship between the Wasserstein distance and trajectory similarity.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the convergence of WIRL compare to other inverse reinforcement learning methods (like Maximum Entropy IRL and Guided Cost Learning) in terms of speed and quality of learned reward functions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proves convergence for WIRL, MEIRL, and GCL using the projective subgradient method, but does not compare their performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on theoretical convergence guarantees but does not provide empirical comparisons between different IRL methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing the convergence speed, final reward function quality, and computational efficiency of WIRL, MEIRL, and GCL on benchmark multi-objective optimization problems.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of the learning rate schedule on the convergence rate and final solution quality of WIRL?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions the use of a nonsummable diminishing learning rate for convergence guarantees, but does not explore different learning rate schedules.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides a theoretical convergence guarantee for a specific learning rate schedule, it does not investigate how different schedules might affect practical performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing different learning rate schedules (e.g., constant, diminishing, adaptive) on the convergence speed and final solution quality of WIRL in various multi-objective optimization scenarios.

## Limitations

- The proof assumes unique arg max solutions for the action mapping, which may not hold in practice
- Theoretical guarantees do not address scalability challenges for large-scale multi-objective optimization problems
- The paper does not investigate the relationship between algorithmic convergence and the quality of learned policies in terms of mimicking expert behavior

## Confidence

- High confidence in the theoretical convergence results for convex and Lipschitz continuous objective functions
- Medium confidence in the practical applicability of the algorithm, as real-world multi-objective optimization problems may violate some assumptions
- Low confidence in the scalability of the approach without further empirical validation on complex problems
