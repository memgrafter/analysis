# Training Differentially Private Graph Neural Networks with Random Walk Sampling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.00738
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00738
- Reference count: 27
- Key outcome: Novel approach using disjoint subgraph sampling via random walks significantly outperforms state-of-the-art FDP baseline, achieving up to 95% of non-DP GCN performance on large graphs while providing strong privacy guarantees

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of training differentially private graph neural networks (GNNs) by tackling the fundamental issue of exponential gradient sensitivity with network depth. The authors propose a novel approach that trains GNNs on disjoint subgraphs generated through random walk sampling, effectively reducing gradient sensitivity from exponential to constant with respect to network depth. Through careful theoretical analysis and extensive experiments on seven datasets, their method achieves significantly better privacy-utility trade-offs compared to existing approaches, with up to 95% of non-DP performance on large graphs.

## Method Summary
The method adapts DP-SGD to the graph domain by partitioning graphs into disjoint subgraphs using random walk sampling, then training on these subgraphs with gradient clipping and Gaussian noise addition. Three sampling methods (DRW, DRW-R, DRW-D) create independent subgraphs where each node appears in exactly one subgraph, allowing application of amplification-by-subsampling theorems. Privacy is tracked using Rényi differential privacy (RDP), and training stops when the privacy budget is exhausted. The approach leverages random walks to create connected subgraphs that preserve GNN aggregation while limiting receptive field growth.

## Key Results
- Achieves 81.4% F1-micro score on Reddit dataset compared to FDP's 56.7% at epsilon=8
- Maintains up to 95% of non-DP GCN performance on large graphs
- Significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baseline (FDP) across all seven datasets tested
- Successfully applies amplification-by-subsampling theorems through disjoint subgraph sampling

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Disjoint subgraph sampling reduces gradient sensitivity from exponential to constant with respect to network depth
- Mechanism: By partitioning the graph into disjoint subgraphs where each node appears in exactly one subgraph, changing one node's features can only affect gradients within that single subgraph rather than affecting gradients across exponentially many nodes in its receptive field
- Core assumption: Subgraphs remain disjoint throughout training and the random walk sampling ensures each node appears in exactly one subgraph
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose to solve this issue by training graph neural networks on disjoint subgraphs of a given training graph"
  - [section 4.1]: "Due to the disjointness of subgraphs, changing one node's features – whether it is a training node or a neighbor – will affect at most one subgraph (i.e. sample) in the batch, which reduces the upper bound on the sensitivity of the total gradient to 2C"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers discuss differential privacy and random walks but don't provide specific evidence about disjoint subgraph gradient sensitivity reduction
- Break condition: If subgraphs overlap or nodes appear in multiple subgraphs, the exponential sensitivity problem would re-emerge

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Amplification-by-subsampling theorems can be effectively applied when using disjoint subgraph sampling
- Mechanism: Since each subgraph is sampled independently and nodes appear in exactly one subgraph, the probability of sampling a modified node in a batch can be tightly bounded as m/M_min, where M_min is the minimum number of subgraphs that can be generated
- Core assumption: The sampling mechanism creates subgraphs in a way that provides a tight upper bound on sampling probability that holds for all possible graphs
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2]: "We use the upper bound P[˜v] = 1/M ≤ 1/M_min where M_min is the minimum number of subgraphs that can be generated in any graph of N nodes"
  - [section 4.2]: "the probability of sampling node ˜v in a batch of m subgraphs using sampling mechanism S is at most P_S[˜v] ≤ m/M_min"
  - [corpus]: Missing - corpus doesn't provide specific evidence about amplification-by-subsampling theorems applied to disjoint subgraph sampling
- Break condition: If the bound on sampling probability is too loose or the sampling mechanism creates subgraphs with varying probabilities, the privacy amplification benefit would be reduced

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Random walk sampling creates connected subgraphs that preserve GNN aggregation while limiting receptive field growth
- Mechanism: Random walks starting from training nodes create connected subgraphs that include both the training node and a limited number of neighbors for aggregation, preventing the exponential growth of the receptive field that causes gradient sensitivity issues
- Core assumption: Random walks effectively capture the local neighborhood structure needed for GNN aggregation while limiting the number of nodes included
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2]: "We choose random walk sampling, since it ensures that nodes form a connected subgraph of a training node's neighborhood, while limiting the number of nodes being sampled from that neighborhood (i.e. from the receptive field)"
  - [section 4.2]: "We propose three different random-walk-based sampling methods"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers mention random walks and graph neural networks but don't provide specific evidence about this particular mechanism
- Break condition: If random walks sample too many or too few neighbors, the GNN may not have sufficient information for accurate predictions

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Graph preprocessing → Disjoint subgraph generation (via random walks) → Batch sampling of subgraphs → DP-SGD training with clipped gradients and Gaussian noise → Privacy budget tracking
- Critical path: The subgraph generation and sampling process is critical - any overlap or incorrect bounds will break the privacy guarantees
- Design tradeoffs: Smaller subgraphs provide better privacy but may reduce model utility; larger subgraphs may improve utility but increase gradient sensitivity
- Failure signatures: If privacy budget is exceeded, utility drops significantly; if subgraphs are not truly disjoint, exponential sensitivity returns; if sampling bounds are too loose, privacy guarantees weaken
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify disjointness: Check that generated subgraphs have no overlapping nodes across different runs
  2. Sensitivity bound validation: Compute actual gradient sensitivity on small graphs and compare to theoretical bound of 2C
  3. Privacy amplification test: Compare privacy budget consumption when training on full graph vs. disjoint subgraphs with same number of training iterations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal frequency (i parameter) for dynamically re-sampling subgraphs in DRW-D?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that i is a hyper-parameter chosen based on the cost of sampling procedure on each dataset, but doesn't provide systematic analysis of optimal values
- Why unresolved: The paper only states that i is chosen based on sampling cost but doesn't explore how different frequencies affect model performance or privacy-utility trade-offs
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing DRW-D performance across different i values (e.g., every 1, 5, 10, 50 iterations) on multiple datasets, showing the impact on final F1 scores and privacy budgets

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the random walk length L affect the quality of subgraphs and model performance across different graph types?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper derives bounds based on L but doesn't systematically analyze how different L values impact performance on different graph structures
- Why unresolved: While the paper mentions random walk length as a parameter, it doesn't provide comprehensive analysis of how L should be tuned for different graph characteristics (e.g., sparse vs dense, small vs large diameter graphs)
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing F1 scores and privacy budgets across multiple L values (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) on various graph types, demonstrating optimal L selection criteria

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between the number of restarts R in DRW-R and the trade-off between computational efficiency and model performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces DRW-R with restarts but only mentions it as a method to create better subgraphs without analyzing the impact of different R values
- Why unresolved: The paper presents DRW-R as a method but doesn't explore how increasing R affects both the quality of subgraphs and the computational overhead, nor does it provide guidance on selecting R
- What evidence would resolve it: Results showing model performance (F1 scores) and computational time across different R values (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) on multiple datasets, establishing the optimal balance point

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the proposed method perform on directed graphs and heterophilic graphs where the homophily assumption may not hold?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only evaluates on undirected graphs and doesn't test the method's effectiveness on graph types with different structural properties
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses exclusively on undirected graphs and doesn't investigate whether the random walk sampling approach remains effective when the graph structure violates assumptions about neighborhood relationships
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing performance on directed graphs (e.g., citation networks with temporal direction) and heterophilic graphs (e.g., low homophily graphs like Texas or Cornell datasets) versus the current undirected graph results

## Limitations

- Theoretical bounds on sampling probabilities may be loose in practice, potentially weakening privacy guarantees
- Implementation details for random walk sampling algorithms are not fully specified, creating reproducibility challenges
- The method's effectiveness on directed and heterophilic graphs remains unexplored
