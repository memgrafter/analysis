# How do humans perceive adversarial text? A reality check on the validity and naturalness of word-based adversarial attacks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.15587
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15587
- Reference count: 13
- Key outcome: Humans detect 60.33% of adversarial examples as computer-altered, revealing significant limitations in existing text attack methods

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how humans perceive the validity and naturalness of text adversarial examples generated by nine state-of-the-art word-based attacks. Through a large-scale human study with 378 participants evaluating 3000 original and adversarial examples, the authors find that humans can detect 45.28% of perturbed words and suspect 60.33% of adversarial examples as being computer-altered. The research reveals that existing text attacks fail to achieve both validity and naturalness, with 38.9% of adversarial examples containing perceived grammar errors. These findings suggest that current text attack methods are impractical in real-world scenarios where human interaction with NLP systems is involved.

## Method Summary
The study evaluated nine state-of-the-art word-based adversarial attacks on three sentiment analysis datasets (IMDB, Rotten Tomatoes, Yelp polarity) using 378 human participants recruited through social media and the Prolific platform. Participants completed a three-part online questionnaire assessing validity, suspiciousness, detectability, grammaticality, and meaningfulness of original and adversarial texts. The questionnaire included individual text evaluations, detectability tests with known perturbation sizes, and direct comparisons between original and adversarial pairs. Statistical analyses included proportion tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Pearson correlation tests to examine relationships between perturbation size and human perception metrics.

## Key Results
- 60.33% of adversarial examples were detected as computer-altered by humans
- 45.28% of perturbed words in adversarial text were correctly identified by participants
- Perturbation size negatively affects suspiciousness and meaning clarity but has no impact on validity or detectability

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Humans perceive adversarial text attacks as unnatural, with 60.33% suspecting computer alteration.
- Mechanism: Participants were shown original and adversarial examples without being told which were altered. Their suspicion was measured based on whether they believed the text had been automatically modified.
- Core assumption: Participants can reliably detect when text has been computer-altered based on subtle cues in language.

- Claim: Perturbation size negatively affects suspiciousness and meaning clarity.
- Mechanism: As more words are altered in adversarial examples, humans are more likely to suspect the text and find it less meaningful.
- Core assumption: There is a linear relationship between the number of altered words and human perception of naturalness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The perturbation size negatively affects suspiciousness and meaning"
  - [section 5.3] "Pearson correlation tests have revealed that perturbation size does not affect validity and detectability, but correlates with suspiciousness, grammaticality and meaning clarity"

- Break condition: If the relationship between perturbation size and human perception becomes non-linear or non-existent.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Grammar errors do not correlate with naturalness in adversarial text.
- Mechanism: Participants were asked to identify grammar errors in both original and adversarial texts. The presence of errors did not significantly impact their perception of naturalness.
- Core assumption: Human perception of grammar errors differs from automated grammar checking tools.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Humans can detect 45.28% of the perturbed words in adversarial text, and perceive grammar errors in 38.9% of adversarial examples"

## Limitations

- The study relies on self-reported language proficiency rather than standardized testing, which may introduce bias in how different participant groups perceive adversarial text
- The questionnaire design required participants to evaluate 9 adversarial examples in Part 1, potentially causing fatigue that could affect responses
- The analysis focuses exclusively on word-based attacks, limiting generalizability to other attack types like character-level or synonym substitution methods

## Confidence

- High confidence: The finding that 60.33% of adversarial examples are detected as computer-altered, supported by large sample size (378 participants) and clear statistical significance
- Medium confidence: The negative correlation between perturbation size and suspiciousness/meaningfulness, as the relationship appears consistent but may have non-linear components not captured in the analysis
- Low confidence: The claim about grammar errors not correlating with naturalness, given limited discussion of how grammar errors were identified and potential subjectivity in human perception

## Next Checks

1. Replicate the study with standardized language proficiency testing to control for potential bias in how different proficiency levels perceive adversarial text
2. Conduct a follow-up study with fewer examples per participant to test whether fatigue affected the suspiciousness and detectability metrics
3. Test the same attacks on character-level or phonetic-based adversarial examples to assess whether the human perception patterns generalize beyond word-based attacks
