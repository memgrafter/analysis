# Probing optimisation in physics-informed neural networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15196
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15196
- Reference count: 40
- This paper introduces a low-cost curvature tracking method for PINNs and demonstrates that second-order optimizers outperform first-order methods by navigating highly curved regions where good solutions lie.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how optimizer choice affects physics-informed neural network (PINN) accuracy by introducing a novel curvature tracking method. The authors study the linear advection equation with varying wave speeds and find that good PINN solutions are located in highly curved regions of the parameter space. Using their curvature measure, they demonstrate a negative correlation between convergence error and curvature, explaining why second-order optimizers like LBFGS outperform first-order methods. The curvature tracking method provides a computationally efficient alternative to Hessian-based calculations while capturing essential information about training trajectory behavior.

## Method Summary
The authors compare four optimizers (GD, LBFGS, ADAM, BBI) on PINNs solving the linear advection equation with varying wave speeds. They implement a curvature tracking method that monitors the training trajectory's bending in parameter space without requiring Hessian computation. The method parameterizes the trajectory as ω(t) and computes curvature κω from the tangent vector's rate of change. Experiments use two network architectures ([2,25,25,1] and [2,50,50,50,50,1]) with 100 initial condition points, 2000 bulk points, and 80 boundary points. The curvature measure is computed throughout training and correlated with final MSE values on analytical solutions.

## Key Results
- Second-order optimizers (LBFGS) achieve lower convergence errors and higher curvature values than first-order methods for PINN problems
- A negative correlation exists between convergence error and curvature, indicating good solutions lie in highly curved regions
- The curvature tracking method provides a computationally efficient alternative to Hessian-based calculations
- Optimizer performance varies significantly with wave speed β, with LBFGS maintaining superior performance across all tested values

- What evidence would resolve it: Mathematical derivation or empirical study establishing a quantitative relationship between generalization error and trajectory curvature across different PINN architectures and optimization problems.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why do some optimizers like BBI and GD perform poorly on PINN problems despite their theoretical advantages (energy conservation for BBI, simplicity for GD)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "BBI's low performance requires further investigation" and attributes GD's poor performance to "no momentum to be able to overcome saddle points and no mini-batches as a source of stochasticity."
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides preliminary explanations based on observed behavior without deeper theoretical analysis of why these optimizers fail in PINN contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of loss landscape topology for PINN problems versus traditional ML tasks, identifying specific landscape features that make these optimizers ineffective.

## Limitations
- The curvature-error correlation has only been demonstrated for the linear advection equation and may not generalize to more complex PDEs
- The curvature tracking method uses first-order approximations that may lose accuracy in highly complex or noisy loss landscapes
- The study focuses on specific MLP architectures with tanh activations, limiting conclusions about other neural network architectures

## Confidence
- High confidence: The observation that second-order optimizers outperform first-order methods for PINNs in terms of convergence error and curvature values, supported by multiple experiments across different wave speeds and architectures
- Medium confidence: The claim that good PINN solutions lie in highly curved regions of the parameter space, as this is based on correlation analysis rather than direct evidence of solution optimality in those regions
- Medium confidence: The effectiveness of the curvature tracking method as a low-cost alternative to Hessian-based calculations, though the approximation's accuracy in complex landscapes needs further validation
