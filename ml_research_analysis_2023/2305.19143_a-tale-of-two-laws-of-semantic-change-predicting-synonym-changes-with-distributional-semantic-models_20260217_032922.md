# A Tale of Two Laws of Semantic Change: Predicting Synonym Changes with Distributional Semantic Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.19143
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19143
- **Reference count**: 12
- **Primary result**: 85% of synonym pairs differentiate over a century; supervised model achieves 62% balanced accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates two competing linguistic laws about synonym evolution: the Law of Differentiation (LD) and the Law of Parallel Change (LPC). The authors propose a computational method to predict whether synonym pairs will remain synonymous or differentiate over time using distributional semantic models. Their approach tracks semantic distances between word pairs across time periods, combining historical and modern lexical resources to construct an evaluation dataset. The study finds strong evidence supporting the Law of Differentiation, with most synonym pairs showing semantic drift across a century.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a supervised classification approach to predict synonym pair evolution. They represent word meanings using distributional semantic models and track changes in semantic distances over time. The methodology involves extracting synonym pairs from WordNet, computing synchronic distances and frequency features, and training classifiers to distinguish between pairs that remain synonymous versus those that differentiate. The dataset combines historical lexical resources with modern WordNet to provide ground truth labels for synonym differentiation.

## Key Results
- 85% of synonym pairs show differentiation over a century
- Best supervised model achieves 62% balanced accuracy
- Distributional distance changes and word frequencies are key predictive features
- Strong empirical support for the Law of Differentiation over the Law of Parallel Change

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by leveraging distributional semantic models to capture word meaning representations and track their evolution over time. As words are used in different contexts across historical periods, their distributional representations shift, causing semantic distances between originally synonymous pairs to increase. The computational approach formalizes this linguistic intuition by quantifying semantic drift through measurable changes in vector space distances, allowing systematic prediction of differentiation patterns.

## Foundational Learning

**Distributional Semantic Models** - Represent word meanings based on contextual co-occurrences in text corpora
- *Why needed*: Provide mathematical framework to quantify semantic similarity and track meaning changes over time
- *Quick check*: Can capture basic semantic relationships like synonymy and hypernymy through vector distances

**Synchronic vs Diachronic Analysis** - Synchronic examines language at a single time point; diachronic tracks changes across time
- *Why needed*: Essential for studying semantic change and understanding how word relationships evolve
- *Quick check*: Diachronic analysis reveals patterns that synchronic snapshots miss

**WordNet Lexical Relations** - Hierarchical lexical database containing synonym sets and semantic relationships
- *Why needed*: Provides reliable ground truth for initial synonym pairs and semantic relations
- *Quick check*: Standardized resource for computational semantic research

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Lexical Resource Extraction -> Distributional Model Training -> Distance Computation -> Feature Extraction -> Supervised Classification

**Critical path**: The core pipeline follows: (1) extract historical/modern synonym pairs, (2) train distributional models on corresponding time periods, (3) compute semantic distances between pairs, (4) extract frequency and distance features, (5) train classifier to predict differentiation

**Design tradeoffs**: The approach trades off computational complexity for interpretability - using static embeddings is simpler than contextual models but may miss nuanced meaning shifts. The supervised approach requires labeled data but provides more accurate predictions than unsupervised distance thresholds.

**Failure signatures**: High classification error when pairs involve polysemous words, when distributional shifts reflect domain changes rather than semantic differentiation, or when historical/modern lexical resources have inconsistent labeling standards.

## Limitations
- Method conflates polysemy shifts, domain-specific changes, and true synonym differentiation
- Supervised classification achieves only 62% balanced accuracy, leaving substantial uncertainty
- Dataset construction may introduce inconsistencies between historical and modern synonym labeling
- Focus on noun pairs limits generalizability to other parts of speech
