# Learning to Operate in Open Worlds by Adapting Planning Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.14272
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14272
- Reference count: 5
- The approach shows planning agents can quickly recover optimal performance (around 20 episodes) when environmental changes occur, compared to DQN agents

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a method for planning agents to operate in open worlds where environmental characteristics change unexpectedly. The core innovation involves detecting novelties by comparing expected and observed state trajectories during plan execution, then repairing the domain model through heuristics-guided search over model manipulation operators. The approach was evaluated on the CartPole benchmark with changes to pole length, gravity, and cart mass, demonstrating that planning agents with model repair quickly recovered optimal performance while maintaining interpretability of their adaptations.

## Method Summary
The method works by first executing plans using the agent's current domain model, then detecting novelties through trajectory mismatch analysis. When a discrepancy between expected and observed states is detected, the system initiates a repair process that searches over predefined model manipulation operators to update the domain model. This repair is guided by heuristics that help prioritize which model changes are most likely to resolve the detected novelties. The system operates in an online fashion, continuously updating its model as new novelties are encountered during operation.

## Key Results
- Planning agents with model repair recovered optimal performance within approximately 20 episodes when facing environmental changes
- The approach demonstrated resilience to novelties compared to DQN agents which struggled more significantly
- Adaptations were expressed in the same language as the original model, maintaining interpretability
- Two types of novelties were successfully handled: changes to pole length and gravity, and changes to pole length and cart mass

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism works by leveraging the agent's ability to generate expected trajectories based on its current model, then comparing these predictions against actual observations during execution. When discrepancies are detected, it indicates that the environment has changed in ways not captured by the current model. The heuristics-guided search over model manipulation operators allows the system to efficiently explore possible model updates that could explain the observed discrepancies, effectively learning new environmental characteristics without requiring complete retraining.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component Map:
Domain Model -> Trajectory Generator -> Observer -> Novelty Detector -> Model Repair Engine -> Model Update -> Domain Model (loop)

Critical Path:
During execution: Domain Model → Trajectory Generator → Observer → Novelty Detector
During repair: Novelty Detector → Model Repair Engine (with heuristics) → Model Update → Domain Model

Design Tradeoffs:
The approach trades computational overhead during execution (for trajectory comparison and potential repairs) against the benefit of maintaining a valid model without complete retraining. The use of heuristics-guided search reduces the search space but requires careful selection of operators and weighting functions. This design prioritizes interpretability and sample efficiency over raw computational speed.

Failure Signatures:
- False positives in novelty detection leading to unnecessary model repairs
- Inability to detect novelties if the mismatch is subtle or gradual
- Model repair getting stuck in local optima if heuristics are poorly designed
- Computational overhead becoming prohibitive with complex domain models or numerous operators
- Failure to recover when novelties require combinations of model changes not covered by available operators

First 3 Experiments:
1. Implement a simple domain (e.g., grid navigation) and introduce a single, easily detectable novelty to verify the basic repair loop works
2. Test the system with multiple simultaneous novelties to evaluate its ability to handle complex changes
3. Measure the computational overhead of the repair process and compare against baseline planning performance

## Limitations
- The method's effectiveness depends heavily on the predefined set of model manipulation operators and their associated heuristics
- Results are demonstrated only on the CartPole benchmark with limited novelty types, raising questions about generalization
- The comparison with DQN agents does not include other state-of-the-art model-based or adaptive planning approaches

## Confidence
- High confidence: The core mechanism of novelty detection through trajectory comparison and subsequent model repair is technically sound
- Medium confidence: The claim that the method enables planning agents to "learn to operate" in open worlds, given the limited experimental scope
- Medium confidence: The interpretability claim, as the paper shows adaptations in the same language but doesn't evaluate human understanding

## Next Checks
1. Test the approach on more complex control domains (e.g., robotic manipulation tasks) with a wider variety of novelty types, including non-physical changes to action costs or goal specifications
2. Evaluate the system's performance when multiple novelties occur simultaneously or in quick succession, and measure the cumulative impact on planning efficiency
3. Conduct a systematic ablation study to determine the relative importance of different model manipulation operators and heuristics in the repair process, identifying which components are essential versus supplementary
