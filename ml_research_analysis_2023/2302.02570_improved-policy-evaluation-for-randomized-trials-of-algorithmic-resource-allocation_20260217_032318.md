# Improved Policy Evaluation for Randomized Trials of Algorithmic Resource Allocation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02570
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02570
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Method cuts estimation error by up to 70% compared to standard approaches

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach for evaluating resource allocation policies in randomized controlled trials where decisions are made at the group level. The method identifies subsets of participants whose assignments can be swapped without changing policy decisions, enabling aggregation of outcomes across counterfactual assignments. This yields an unbiased estimator with reduced variance compared to traditional sample mean estimators, potentially improving policy evaluation accuracy without requiring additional trials.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a partitioning approach that groups participants into subsets where counterfactual assignments would yield identical policy decisions. By aggregating outcomes across these partitions, the method creates an unbiased estimator that leverages the structure of group-level decision-making. The key insight is that when participants can be swapped without affecting policy choices, their outcomes can be combined to reduce estimation variance while maintaining unbiasedness.

## Key Results
- Demonstrates up to 70% reduction in estimation error compared to standard sample mean estimators
- Shows effectiveness across synthetic, semi-synthetic tuberculosis, and real-world maternal health datasets
- Proves theoretical guarantees of unbiasedness and variance reduction under the partitioning assumption

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method exploits the structure of group-level decisions by identifying partitions where counterfactual assignments are observationally equivalent. When a policy makes the same decision regardless of which specific participants are assigned to which groups within a partition, their outcomes can be aggregated without introducing bias. This aggregation effectively increases the sample size for variance reduction while maintaining the validity of the counterfactual inference.

## Foundational Learning
- **Partitioning concept**: Grouping participants based on policy indifference - needed to identify where aggregation is valid; quick check: verify that policy decisions remain constant within partitions
- **Counterfactual equivalence**: Understanding when different assignments yield identical outcomes - needed to justify outcome aggregation; quick check: test if policy decisions change when swapping assignments within partitions
- **Variance reduction through aggregation**: Combining observations to improve estimator precision - needed to understand the efficiency gains; quick check: compare variance of aggregated vs. individual estimates

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data partitioning -> Policy decision validation -> Outcome aggregation -> Variance estimation
**Critical path**: The method requires successful identification of valid partitions before any aggregation can occur; without meaningful partitions, the approach reduces to standard evaluation
**Design tradeoffs**: The partitioning approach trades computational complexity for variance reduction, requiring careful balance between partition size (for efficiency) and validity (for unbiasedness)
**Failure signatures**: If partitions are incorrectly identified or too small, the method will show minimal variance reduction or potentially introduce bias
**First experiments**: 1) Test partitioning on synthetic data with known ground truth; 2) Apply to semi-synthetic data where ground truth is partially known; 3) Validate on real-world data with varying group sizes

None

## Limitations
- The partitioning assumption may not hold in settings with strong individual-level heterogeneity driving allocation decisions
- Performance validation is primarily demonstrated on controlled synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets rather than fully real-world scenarios
- The method requires sufficient numbers of participants within each partition to achieve meaningful variance reduction

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Theoretical guarantees of unbiasedness and variance reduction are sound within stated assumptions
- **Medium Confidence**: Experimental results showing up to 70% error reduction in controlled settings
- **Low Confidence**: Generalization to diverse real-world applications with complex individual-level decision factors

## Next Checks
1. Test the method on real-world RCTs where individual-level heterogeneity is known to drive allocation decisions, to assess robustness when the partitioning assumption is violated
2. Conduct sensitivity analysis by systematically varying the proportion of participants who can be swapped to determine the minimum threshold needed for variance reduction benefits
3. Compare performance against alternative variance reduction techniques (e.g., regression adjustment, matching methods) in settings with limited sample sizes to evaluate practical advantages
