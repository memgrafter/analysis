# Preference Transformer: Modeling Human Preferences using Transformers for RL

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.00957
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00957
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: Preference Transformer outperforms existing preference-based RL methods on complex navigation, locomotion, and robotic manipulation tasks, achieving near-optimal performance with real human feedback.

## Executive Summary
Preference Transformer introduces a novel framework for modeling human preferences in reinforcement learning using transformers. Unlike prior methods that assume Markovian rewards contribute equally to preference decisions, this approach models preferences as a weighted sum of non-Markovian rewards, capturing temporal dependencies and critical events in trajectories. The method uses a transformer architecture with causal and bidirectional self-attention layers to generate rewards and importance weights. Experiments on complex navigation, locomotion, and robotic manipulation tasks from D4RL and Robomimic benchmarks demonstrate that Preference Transformer outperforms existing methods, achieving near-optimal performance with real human feedback.

## Method Summary
Preference Transformer is a preference-based reinforcement learning method that uses transformer architecture to model human preferences over trajectory segments. The model takes as input trajectory segments of state-action pairs and learns to predict human preferences between them. It consists of a causal transformer that encodes context information using masked self-attention, followed by a preference attention layer that computes non-Markovian rewards and importance weights using bidirectional self-attention. The final preference prediction is based on a weighted sum of these non-Markovian rewards. The model is trained using cross-entropy loss on preference labels and can be used to train policies with algorithms like IQL.

## Key Results
- Preference Transformer achieves near-optimal performance on D4RL and Robomimic benchmarks with real human feedback
- The method learns to identify critical events in trajectories and aligns well with human intent
- Preference Transformer succeeds in learning novel behaviors like double backflips in Hopper, where traditional reward functions fail
- The approach outperforms baselines including Markovian reward models and LSTM-based non-Markovian models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Preference Transformer captures temporal dependencies in human decision-making by modeling non-Markovian rewards weighted by importance scores.
- Mechanism: The architecture uses a bidirectional self-attention layer to compute importance weights wt that capture which parts of a trajectory segment are critical for human preference decisions. These weights are combined with non-Markovian rewards (ˆrt) that depend on the full preceding sub-trajectory.
- Core assumption: Human preferences depend on both the sequence of events and specific critical moments within trajectories, not just the sum of equally-weighted rewards.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Unlike prior approaches assuming human judgment is based on the Markovian rewards which contribute to the decision equally, we introduce a new preference model based on the weighted sum of non-Markovian rewards."
  - [section 4.2]: "We then utilize them to define the preference model" where importance weights are computed through bidirectional self-attention.
  - [corpus]: Weak - related papers focus on multimodal transformers and trajectory regularization but don't directly address temporal importance weighting.
- Break condition: If human preferences are truly Markovian (depend only on current state) or if all events are equally important, the weighting mechanism becomes unnecessary.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Preference Transformer learns well-aligned reward functions that capture task-relevant context by leveraging historical information.
- Mechanism: The causal transformer encodes context from initial timestep to timestep t, allowing the model to learn rewards that depend on the full trajectory history rather than just current state-action pairs.
- Core assumption: Task-relevant information often requires knowledge of past states and actions, making non-Markovian rewards more expressive than Markovian ones.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Preference Transformer can induce a well-specified reward and attend to critical events in the trajectory by automatically capturing the temporal dependencies in human decision-making."
  - [section 5.3]: "We find that the reward function is well-aligned with human intent. In the successful trajectory... reward value increases as the agent gets close to the goal."
  - [corpus]: Weak - related papers mention dynamics-aware rewards but don't specifically address context capture through causal transformers.
- Break condition: If the task can be fully specified with Markovian rewards or if historical information doesn't provide additional signal.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Preference Transformer generalizes better to real human preferences than synthetic scripted teachers by learning to model actual human behavior patterns.
- Mechanism: The transformer architecture can learn complex patterns in human preferences that scripted teachers (which use task reward directly) cannot capture, particularly for multi-objective tasks where humans balance competing priorities.
- Core assumption: Scripted teachers based on task rewards don't accurately model real human decision-making, especially when tasks have multiple objectives or when humans consider context beyond simple reward maximization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.4]: "We observe that disagreement rates are quite high" between human and scripted teachers, and "evaluation on scripted teacher can generate misleading information."
  - [section 5.2]: Human evaluation shows "human evaluators prefer agents from PT compared to agents from MR or NMR, showing PT is more aligned with human preferences."
  - [corpus]: Weak - related papers don't directly compare human vs scripted teacher preferences in detail.
- Break condition: If scripted teachers perfectly model human preferences or if the task reward fully captures all human objectives.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: The paper uses transformer layers to model sequential dependencies in trajectories and compute importance weights through self-attention.
  - Quick check question: How does bidirectional self-attention differ from causal self-attention, and why are both needed in this architecture?

- Concept: Reinforcement learning from preferences vs rewards
  - Why needed here: The work builds on preference-based RL, which learns rewards from pairwise trajectory comparisons rather than predefined reward functions.
  - Quick check question: What are the key differences between preference-based RL and standard reward-based RL in terms of feedback efficiency and alignment with human intent?

- Concept: Non-Markovian vs Markovian rewards
  - Why needed here: The paper introduces non-Markovian rewards that depend on the full trajectory history, contrasting with standard Markovian rewards that depend only on current state-action pairs.
  - Quick check question: In what types of tasks would non-Markovian rewards provide significant advantages over Markovian rewards?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input trajectory segments → Causal Transformer → Preference Attention Layer → Weighted reward aggregation → Preference prediction

- Critical path: Input → Causal Transformer → Preference Attention Layer → Weighted reward aggregation → Preference prediction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Bidirectional vs causal attention: Bidirectional captures full context for importance weights, causal maintains temporal order for reward computation
  - Single vs multi-layer transformers: Current design uses single layers for efficiency, but deeper architectures might capture more complex patterns
  - Reward vs importance weight separation: Current design jointly learns both, but separate training might improve stability

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on tasks where Markovian rewards suffice (wasted complexity)
  - Instability in training due to complex joint optimization of rewards and weights
  - Overfitting to training preferences if dataset is small

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare Preference Transformer vs Markovian baseline on a simple grid-world task with clear temporal dependencies
  2. Test importance weight visualization on a task where critical events are known (e.g., reaching goal vs falling)
  3. Evaluate transfer performance from scripted teacher to human preferences on a multi-objective task

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper acknowledges several open questions:
1. How can the importance weights learned by the preference attention layer be utilized for improving feedback efficiency in preference-based reinforcement learning?
2. How does the performance of Preference Transformer compare to other non-Markovian reward learning methods like return decomposition approaches?
3. Can Preference Transformer effectively learn reward functions from preferences collected from crowd-sourcing platforms like Amazon Mechanical Turk, or is expert feedback essential?

## Limitations
- The work relies on specific benchmark tasks that may not fully capture real-world complexity
- Human preference collection methodology lacks detail about participant selection and aggregation procedures
- The assumption that non-Markovian rewards are superior to Markovian ones may not hold for all task types
- Computational overhead compared to simpler preference models is not thoroughly analyzed

## Confidence
- **High Confidence:** The core architecture design and its empirical performance on benchmark tasks
- **Medium Confidence:** The claim that human preferences require non-Markovian modeling
- **Low Confidence:** The generalization of results to truly open-ended real-world scenarios

## Next Checks
1. Conduct ablation studies varying the attention layer configurations (number of layers, attention heads) to quantify the impact on both performance and computational efficiency.
2. Test the method on tasks requiring longer temporal reasoning (e.g., multi-stage puzzles) to validate the claimed advantage of non-Markovian reward modeling.
3. Perform cross-dataset validation by training on preferences from one demographic group and testing on another to assess the robustness of learned reward functions to human preference variation.
