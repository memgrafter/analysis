# CHiLS: Zero-Shot Image Classification with Hierarchical Label Sets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02551
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02551
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Improves zero-shot image classification accuracy by leveraging hierarchical label sets, achieving up to 30% gain with ground-truth hierarchies and consistent smaller gains with GPT-3-generated hierarchies.

## Executive Summary
CHiLS enhances zero-shot image classification with CLIP by decomposing each superclass into subclasses and performing prediction over the combined subclass set. The method maps predictions back to superclasses and optionally reweights subclass probabilities by superclass confidence. Tested across 15 diverse datasets, CHiLS consistently outperforms standard zero-shot CLIP, with larger gains when true hierarchies are available. The approach is simple to integrate and works across different CLIP model sizes.

## Method Summary
CHiLS maps each superclass to a set of subclasses using either ground-truth hierarchies or GPT-3-generated labels. It performs zero-shot prediction on the union of all subclasses using standard CLIP prompting, then maps the predicted subclass back to its superclass. A reweighting step multiplies subclass logits by superclass logits to balance confidence between the two levels. The method is evaluated on 15 datasets with varying hierarchy quality, showing consistent accuracy improvements over baseline zero-shot CLIP.

## Key Results
- Up to 30% accuracy improvement over standard zero-shot CLIP when ground-truth hierarchies are available
- Consistent smaller improvements (typically 1-5%) when using GPT-3-generated hierarchies
- Performance gains maintained across six different CLIP model architectures (RN50, RN101, RN50x4, ViT-B/16, ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14@336)
- Reweighting step provides additional robustness, especially when using GPT-3-generated subclasses

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Subclasses provide more discriminative visual cues than superclass names alone.
- Mechanism: By decomposing each superclass into finer-grained subclasses, the model can match images to more specific textual descriptors that better capture the visual differences within a coarse class.
- Core assumption: The superclass name is too general to be discriminative in the embedding space, while subclass names are visually informative.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] CHiLS leads to improved accuracy in situations both with and without ground-truth hierarchical information.
  - [section 3.1] "Given a label set Sci for each class, we proceed with the standard process for zero-shot prediction, but now using the union of all label sets as the set of classes."
- Break condition: If subclasses are not more discriminative than superclasses, or if the superclass name is already sufficiently specific, CHiLS gains may vanish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reweighting by superclass probability improves robustness to noisy or incomplete subclass sets.
- Mechanism: Multiplying subclass logits by superclass logits downweights subclass predictions that disagree with a confident superclass prediction, effectively fusing two complementary sources of evidence.
- Core assumption: CLIP's superclass probability is well-calibrated and assigns higher probability to correct classes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We additionally include a reweighting step wherein we leverage the raw superclass probabilities in order to make our method robust to less-confident predictions at the superclass and subclass level."
  - [section 3.2] "We include an additional reweighting step within our main algorithm... Heuristically, as the prediction is now taken as the argmax over products of probabilities, large disagreements between subclass and superclass probabilities will be down-weighted."
- Break condition: If superclass probabilities are poorly calibrated or if subclass labels are perfect matches, reweighting could dilute good subclass signals.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: GPT-3 can approximate subclass labels when ground truth hierarchies are unavailable.
- Mechanism: GPT-3 generates candidate subclass names given a superclass and optional context; these synthetic subclasses are used in place of ground-truth subclasses.
- Core assumption: GPT-3's commonsense knowledge about category hierarchies is sufficiently accurate to generate useful subclass candidates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "In the latter, we show that rather than enumerating the hierarchy by hand, using GPT-3 to query a list of possible subclasses for each class (whether or not they are actually present in the dataset) still leads to consistent improved accuracy over raw superclass prediction."
  - [section 5.2] Describes the prompt format and inclusion of superclass name to mitigate sense errors.
- Break condition: If GPT-3's subclass generation is consistently off (wrong sense, irrelevant modifiers), synthetic hierarchies may hurt performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input image + superclass list -> G mapping (hierarchy lookup or GPT-3 query) -> Subclass set generation -> Prompt encoding -> CLIP inference (union of subclasses) -> Reweighting (multiply by superclass logits) -> Argmax -> Inverse mapping to superclass
- Critical path: G mapping → prompt encoding → CLIP inference → reweighting → argmax → inverse mapping
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Subclass granularity vs. label set size (memory and speed)
  - GPT-3 generation cost vs. ground-truth hierarchy availability
  - Reweighting strength vs. subclass purity
- Failure signatures:
  - No improvement over baseline → subclass labels too noisy or superclass names already fine-grained
  - Degraded performance → reweighting over-corrects, GPT-3 subclasses off-topic
  - High memory usage → too many subclasses per class
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run baseline zero-shot CLIP on a dataset with known hierarchy, record accuracy.
  2. Apply CHiLS using the ground-truth hierarchy, compare accuracy and inspect subclass predictions.
  3. Replace ground-truth hierarchy with GPT-3-generated subclasses, compare results and label quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of CHiLS vary with different label set sizes across diverse datasets, and what determines the optimal size?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions experiments with varying label set sizes (m = 1, 5, 10, 15, 50) but notes no consistent trend across datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper states the optimal size is context-specific, depending on total classes and semantic granularity, but does not provide a clear rule or predictive model.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study across many datasets showing the relationship between label set size, class granularity, and accuracy, with a formula or heuristic for choosing m.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Is there a more principled method for combining superclass and subclass predictions than the current multiplicative reweighting approach?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses multiplicative reweighting based on CLIP's calibration, but notes it's heuristic and experiments with alternatives.
- Why unresolved: While alternatives like using aggregate subclass probabilities were tried, the paper does not explore other potential methods or theoretical justification for why multiplicative reweighting works.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparison of multiple principled combination methods (e.g., Bayesian fusion, entropy-based weighting) on accuracy and robustness across datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does CHiLS perform in zero-shot classification tasks where the class hierarchy is unknown and cannot be approximated by GPT-3?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on GPT-3-generated hierarchies but does not address scenarios where GPT-3 fails to generate useful subclasses.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not test cases where GPT-3 outputs are irrelevant or incorrect, nor does it propose fallback strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on datasets where GPT-3-generated labels are systematically poor, and evaluation of alternative hierarchy generation methods (e.g., using knowledge graphs or human annotation).

### Open Question 4
- Question: Does the benefit of CHiLS depend on the size or architecture of the CLIP model, or is it consistent across different models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tests CHiLS on six CLIP backbones (RN50, RN101, RN50x4, ViT-B/16, ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14@336) and finds relatively consistent gains.
- Why unresolved: While gains are consistent, the paper does not explore why or whether there are diminishing returns for larger models, nor does it test models outside the CLIP family.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of performance vs. model size, including smaller or non-CLIP models, to determine if CHiLS has universal applicability or model-specific benefits.

## Limitations
- Effectiveness heavily depends on quality of subclass hierarchies; GPT-3-generated hierarchies provide smaller improvements than ground truth
- Reweighting assumes CLIP's superclass probabilities are well-calibrated, which may not hold across all datasets
- Memory and computational overhead scale with number of subclasses per class, with no discussion of practical limits for very large subclass sets

## Confidence

**High confidence**: Empirical results showing CHiLS outperforming standard zero-shot CLIP across 15 datasets with both ground-truth and GPT-3 hierarchies. The mechanism of mapping superclass → subclass → superclass is clearly described and reproducible.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that GPT-3 can reliably generate useful subclass hierarchies. While results show consistent improvement, the paper doesn't analyze the semantic quality of generated subclasses or provide error analysis for common failure modes.

**Medium confidence**: The robustness of the reweighting step across diverse datasets. The paper shows CHiLS with reweighting outperforms without in GPT-3 cases, but doesn't explore calibration issues or alternative weighting schemes.
