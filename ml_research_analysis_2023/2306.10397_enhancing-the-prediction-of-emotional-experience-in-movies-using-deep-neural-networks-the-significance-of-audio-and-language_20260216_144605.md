# Enhancing the Prediction of Emotional Experience in Movies using Deep Neural Networks: The Significance of Audio and Language

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10397
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10397
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Multimodal deep learning approach using ResNet152, SoundNet, and BERT outperforms state-of-the-art on COGNIMUSE dataset, with language most influential for arousal and sound most influential for valence prediction.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a multimodal deep learning approach to predict emotional responses during movie viewing by integrating visual, auditory, and linguistic features. The authors employ pre-trained neural networks (ResNet152 for visual features, SoundNet for audio features, and BERT for linguistic features) to extract modality-specific representations, which are then combined through fully connected layers to predict valence and arousal dimensions of emotions. Experiments on the COGNIMUSE dataset demonstrate that their multimodal model outperforms state-of-the-art approaches, with language significantly influencing arousal prediction and sound being the primary determinant for valence prediction.

## Method Summary
The approach uses pre-trained ResNet152, SoundNet, and BERT models to extract visual, audio, and text features respectively from movie segments. These features are concatenated and passed through fully connected layers to predict valence and arousal dimensions using 7-class classification. The model is trained using SGD with learning rate 0.005, weight decay 0.005, softmax temperature T=2, batch size 128, and early stopping after 25 epochs of no improvement. Evaluation uses leave-one-out cross-validation on the COGNIMUSE dataset.

## Key Results
- Multimodal model achieves 58.4% accuracy for arousal and 63.6% accuracy for valence prediction
- Language significantly influences arousal prediction, while sound is primary determinant for valence prediction
- Visual input has the least impact among the three modalities
- Model outperforms state-of-the-art approaches on the COGNIMUSE dataset

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Integration of pre-trained neural networks captures complementary emotional cues
- Mechanism: Pre-trained ResNet152, SoundNet, and BERT extract high-level semantic features representing distinct aspects of emotional content, combined through fully connected layers
- Core assumption: Pre-trained features generalize well to emotion prediction tasks
- Evidence anchors: Pre-trained model usage stated in abstract and methods section
- Break condition: If pre-trained features don't generalize or cross-modal relationships aren't learnable

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Language significantly influences arousal prediction due to explicit emotional markers in dialogues
- Mechanism: BERT's bidirectional contextual embeddings capture semantic and emotional content in dialogues mapping strongly to arousal intensity
- Core assumption: Dialogue contains sufficient emotional intensity markers
- Evidence anchors: Language's influence on arousal stated in abstract
- Break condition: If subtitles are sparse, ambiguous, or contain minimal emotional intensity information

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Sound is primary determinant for valence prediction due to acoustic features capturing pleasantness/unpleasantness
- Mechanism: SoundNet extracts acoustic features correlating with valence dimension through learned audio-visual synchronization
- Core assumption: Acoustic features have strong correlation with valence perception in movie contexts
- Evidence anchors: Sound's influence on valence stated in abstract
- Break condition: If movie audio lacks sufficient emotional variation or valence is primarily conveyed through other modalities

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input (video frames, audio, subtitles) → ResNet152 (visual features) + SoundNet (audio features) + BERT (text features) → Fully connected layers (feature fusion) → Output (valence and arousal predictions)
- Critical path: Feature extraction from each modality followed by fully connected fusion layers
- Design tradeoffs: Pre-trained networks reduce training time but limit customization; simple fully connected fusion may not capture complex cross-modal interactions as well as attention mechanisms
- Failure signatures: Low accuracy on one emotion dimension may indicate that modality is not contributing effectively; high variance in predictions may suggest overfitting in fusion layers
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test each modality independently on both valence and arousal to establish baseline performance
  2. Test pairwise modality combinations to determine complementary information
  3. Test full multimodal model with different fusion architectures (concatenation vs. weighted sum vs. attention)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different combinations of modalities affect prediction of specific emotions like fear or happiness?
- Basis in paper: Authors mention investigating how diverse modalities contribute to classification of viewers' emotional states but don't provide specific results for individual emotions
- Why unresolved: Paper focuses on overall accuracy for arousal and valence dimensions without analyzing modality contributions for specific emotions
- What evidence would resolve it: Results showing accuracy for each emotion category when using different modality combinations

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does temporal alignment between modalities affect emotion prediction accuracy?
- Basis in paper: Authors mention extracting features from five-second segments but don't explore how temporal alignment or segment length impacts performance
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't investigate whether shorter or longer temporal windows or different alignment strategies would improve emotion prediction
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying segment lengths, temporal alignment methods, and testing different fusion strategies for temporally misaligned modalities

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do relative contributions of modalities differ between intended emotions (filmmaker's intent) and experienced emotions (viewer's actual response)?
- Basis in paper: Authors note text features perform better for intended arousal while sound features perform better for intended valence but don't deeply analyze this difference
- Why unresolved: Paper observes discrepancy but doesn't explain why different modalities are more effective for filmmaker intent versus actual viewer experience
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis comparing modality importance weights and feature contributions between intended and experienced emotion prediction

## Limitations

- Small dataset size (7 movie clips) may limit generalization to other movie genres or cultural contexts
- Lack of statistical significance testing for performance differences between modalities
- Does not explore alternative fusion architectures that might better capture cross-modal interactions

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Technical implementation of pre-trained networks and use of leave-one-out cross-validation are well-established

**Medium Confidence**: Relative importance findings based on ablation studies but lack statistical validation; visual input's least impact needs further testing across genres

**Low Confidence**: Paper lacks error analysis to understand failure cases or explain why certain modalities underperform
