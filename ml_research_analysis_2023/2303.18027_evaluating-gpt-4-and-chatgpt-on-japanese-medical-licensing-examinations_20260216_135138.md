# Evaluating GPT-4 and ChatGPT on Japanese Medical Licensing Examinations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.18027
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18027
- **Reference count**: 32
- **Key outcome**: GPT-4 outperforms ChatGPT and GPT-3 on Japanese medical licensing exams, passing all five years of tests, but shows limitations in avoiding prohibited choices and faces higher API costs due to tokenization inefficiencies.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates GPT-3, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 on Japanese national medical licensing examinations (NMPQE) from 2018-2022. The research team, comprising native Japanese-speaking NLP researchers and a practicing cardiologist, demonstrates that GPT-4 significantly outperforms previous models and passes all five years of exams. The study highlights the potential of large language models in languages typologically distant from English while also exposing critical limitations. Notably, the models sometimes select prohibited choices in medical practice, and Japanese tokenization results in higher API costs and smaller context windows compared to English.

## Method Summary
The researchers collected 400 multiple-choice questions per year from the past five years of Japanese medical licensing exams, along with metadata from exam preparation books. They used a closed-book setting with simple prompting methods and three in-context examples from the 2006 exam. The evaluation involved running GPT-3 (text-davinci-003), ChatGPT, and GPT-4 on the dataset, measuring exact matching accuracy, prohibited choice selection, and comparing results with student majority baseline. The study also analyzed tokenization and API cost differences between Japanese and English inputs.

## Key Results
- GPT-4 outperforms ChatGPT and GPT-3, passing all five years of Japanese medical licensing exams
- LLMs occasionally select prohibited choices in Japanese medical practice, including ethically problematic options like euthanasia
- Japanese tokenization increases API costs and reduces context windows compared to English due to current subword tokenization methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: GPT-4 outperforms other LLMs on Japanese medical licensing exams because its training data includes more Japanese medical knowledge.
- **Mechanism**: The model's pretraining corpus likely contains Japanese medical literature, allowing it to answer domain-specific questions in Japanese without translation.
- **Core assumption**: GPT-4's pretraining data includes sufficient Japanese medical texts to cover the breadth of topics in the licensing exam.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "GPT-4 outperforms ChatGPT and GPT-3 and passes all five years of the exams, highlighting LLMs' potential in a language that is typologically distant from English."
  - [section] "Unlike the previous language models, GPT-4 can successfully pass the Japanese medical licensing examinations over the past five years."
  - [corpus] Weak - the corpus only shows related medical LLM benchmarks but doesn't confirm GPT-4's training data.
- **Break condition**: If GPT-4's pretraining data lacks sufficient Japanese medical content, its performance would degrade on less common medical topics.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Japanese tokenization increases token count and API costs compared to English.
- **Mechanism**: Non-Latin scripts like Japanese characters are split into multiple tokens by the tokenizer, increasing the number of tokens per word and thus the cost per request.
- **Core assumption**: The tokenizer splits each Japanese character into multiple tokens regardless of semantic units.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section] "Our analysis shows that the API costs are generally higher and the maximum context size is smaller for Japanese because of the way non-Latin scripts are currently tokenized in the pipeline."
  - [abstract] "Further, our analysis shows that the API costs are generally higher and the maximum context size is smaller for Japanese because of the way non-Latin scripts are currently tokenized in the pipeline."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors discuss tokenization and multilingual models but don't provide specific Japanese tokenization cost data.
- **Break condition**: If the tokenizer is updated to use a more efficient subword segmentation for Japanese, the cost differential would decrease.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Prohibited choices in medical exams reveal LLM limitations in following country-specific medical ethics.
- **Mechanism**: LLMs may lack training on country-specific legal and ethical constraints, leading them to suggest prohibited actions like euthanasia.
- **Core assumption**: The model's training data doesn't emphasize legal and ethical distinctions specific to each country's medical practice.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "LLMs sometimes select prohibited choices that should be strictly avoided in medical practice in Japan, such as suggesting euthanasia."
  - [section] "Indeed, in the Japanese medical licensing exam, 20+ choices are considered prohibited (禁忌), and test takers who choose four or more prohibited choices will fail regardless of their total scores."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors discuss medical reasoning but don't specifically address prohibited choices in licensing exams.
- **Break condition**: If the model is fine-tuned on country-specific medical ethics guidelines, it would avoid prohibited choices.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: IGAKU QA dataset (400 questions/year) -> Prompt template with in-context examples -> LLM API (GPT-3/ChatGPT/GPT-4) -> Response parsing -> Exact match scoring -> Prohibited choice detection
- **Critical path**: Exam question retrieval → prompt construction with in-context examples → LLM API call → response parsing → exact match scoring → prohibited choice detection
- **Design tradeoffs**: Using a closed-book setting simplifies evaluation but may underestimate model performance compared to open-book or retrieval-augmented approaches. The simple prompting method is reproducible but may not achieve optimal performance.
- **Failure signatures**: Model outputs that don't follow the expected format (selecting options vs. generating text), selection of prohibited choices, and performance degradation on questions requiring Japan-specific context.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Test different prompt templates (simple vs. Chain-of-Thought) to see if intermediate reasoning steps improve performance on complex medical questions.
  2. Compare model performance on questions with and without images to quantify the impact of the current lack of multimodal support.
  3. Evaluate cost and token usage differences between Japanese and English inputs to validate the tokenization impact on API efficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What is the impact of tokenization schemes on the cost and performance of LLM APIs for non-Latin scripts beyond Japanese?
- **Basis in paper**: Explicit
- **Why unresolved**: The paper only examines tokenization for Japanese, a specific non-Latin script. The impact on other non-Latin scripts like Chinese, Arabic, or Cyrillic is not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comparative studies of tokenization schemes and their impact on cost and performance for a variety of non-Latin scripts using different LLM APIs.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How do the limitations of current LLM APIs in handling prohibited choices in medical contexts generalize to other high-stakes domains like law or finance?
- **Basis in paper**: Explicit
- **Why unresolved**: The paper focuses on medical licensing exams and prohibited choices in Japan. The generalizability of these findings to other domains and regions is not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluations of LLM performance on high-stakes exams or tasks in law, finance, and other domains, focusing on their ability to handle prohibited or unethical choices.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the potential for open-book approaches or retrieval augmentation to improve LLM performance on exams requiring Japan-specific knowledge or other geographic/temporal contexts?
- **Basis in paper**: Inferred
- **Why unresolved**: The paper mentions that some problems require Japan-specific knowledge but does not explore the potential of open-book approaches or retrieval augmentation to address this limitation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments comparing LLM performance on exams with and without access to external knowledge sources, focusing on their ability to answer questions requiring specific geographic or temporal context.

## Limitations

- GPT-4 occasionally selects prohibited choices in Japanese medical practice, including ethically problematic options like euthanasia
- Japanese tokenization results in higher API costs and reduced context windows compared to English
- The closed-book evaluation setting may not reflect real-world medical practice where access to reference materials is standard

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: GPT-4 outperforms GPT-3 and ChatGPT on Japanese medical licensing exams, and the tokenization inefficiency for Japanese text increases API costs and reduces context windows
- **Medium confidence**: The prohibited choice issue reflects insufficient training on Japanese medical ethics, though the exact scope and frequency require further analysis
- **Low confidence**: The closed-book evaluation fully represents real-world medical knowledge requirements, as open-book or retrieval-augmented approaches might yield different results

## Next Checks

1. **Ethical Constraint Testing**: Systematically evaluate all models on a comprehensive set of Japanese medical ethics scenarios to quantify the frequency and types of prohibited choices selected, then test whether fine-tuning on Japanese medical ethics guidelines reduces these errors.

2. **Tokenization Efficiency Analysis**: Conduct controlled experiments comparing token counts and costs for identical medical content in Japanese versus English across multiple LLM APIs, then test alternative tokenization strategies to quantify potential improvements.

3. **Prompt Engineering Impact**: Implement and evaluate alternative prompting strategies (Chain-of-Thought, role-based prompting, retrieval-augmented generation) to determine if performance on complex medical reasoning tasks improves beyond the simple prompting method used in this evaluation.
