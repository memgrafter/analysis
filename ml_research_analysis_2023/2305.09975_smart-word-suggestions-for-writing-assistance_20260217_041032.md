# Smart Word Suggestions for Writing Assistance

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09975
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09975
- Reference count: 22
- Key outcome: Smart Word Suggestions (SWS) task and benchmark for writing assistance; end-to-end evaluation shows existing methods struggle with detection and providing multiple useful suggestions.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Smart Word Suggestions (SWS) task and benchmark to address the challenge of providing word-level writing assistance in realistic scenarios. SWS requires models to both identify improvable words or phrases in sentences and suggest appropriate substitutions, distinguishing it from isolated lexical substitution tasks. The benchmark includes a human-annotated test set of 1,000 sentences and a large distantly supervised training set of 3.7 million sentences. Experiments with seven baselines reveal that detection of improvable targets and provision of multiple useful suggestions remain difficult, highlighting the need for better training data and methods.

## Method Summary
SWS combines detection of improvable targets with substitution suggestion in a single end-to-end framework. The human-annotated test set contains 1,000 sentences with over 16,000 substitution suggestions from 10 native speakers, validated by voting indices. The training set is generated by randomly replacing words with synonyms from a thesaurus in Wikipedia sentences. Seven baselines are evaluated: rule-based synonym replacement, ChatGPT, BERT sp,sv, LexSubCon, CMLM, BART, and BERT. Evaluation uses end-to-end metrics (Precision, Recall, F0.5) and sub-task metrics for detection and suggestion quality.

## Key Results
- Existing lexical substitution methods underperform on SWS, particularly in detecting improvable targets (low Rdet).
- Models struggle to provide multiple useful suggestions, as NDCG drops significantly with increasing number of suggestions.
- Detection performance improves with higher voting indices, indicating the importance of annotator agreement for target quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: End-to-end evaluation in SWS forces models to detect improvable targets before making substitutions, making the task more realistic than isolated lexical substitution.
- Mechanism: By combining detection and suggestion in a single pass, models must learn both to identify words needing improvement and to propose contextually appropriate replacements, rather than assuming the target word is given.
- Core assumption: The detection sub-task is necessary and non-trivial; simply providing suggestions for every word would not reflect actual writing assistance needs.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "SWS emphasizes end-to-end evaluation and presents a more realistic writing assistance scenario."
  - [section 2.1]: "In LS, the target word is already provided, while in SWS, the system needs to detect the improvable targets first."
  - [corpus]: Evidence from Table 4 shows models struggle to detect improvable targets (low Rdet) even when substitutions are accurate, indicating the detection step is genuinely challenging.
- Break condition: If detection becomes trivial (e.g., via a perfect oracle or trivial keyword list), the task would reduce to standard lexical substitution and lose its distinguishing value.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Human-annotated data with multiple annotators and voting indices provides a robust signal for both the necessity of a substitution and its quality.
- Mechanism: Voting indices weight the importance of each target and suggestion; models that align with higher-voting items (detected more often by annotators) perform better in weighted accuracy (WAdet) than in plain recall.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.4]: "We calculate pairwise inter-annotator agreement (PA) to assess the quality of the dataset."
  - [section 6.1]: "Figure 3 shows Rdet for the improvable targets with different voting indexes. As depicted in Figure 3, improvable targets identified by a greater number of annotators are more easily detected by the models."
  - [corpus]: Table 4 compares Rdet and WAdet, showing WAdet is consistently higher, indicating the weighting improves detection evaluation.
- Break condition: If annotator agreement is low or biased, the voting index would not reliably indicate true improvement needs, and weighted accuracy would mislead model selection.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Distantly supervised data from synonym replacement in Wikipedia provides broad lexical coverage but lacks contextual appropriateness, making it useful for training substitution candidates but insufficient for teaching contextual word usage.
- Mechanism: Randomly substituting synonyms in clean Wikipedia text generates many word pairs that appear in context, but these pairs may be semantically inappropriate in the original sentence; models trained only on this data learn word usage patterns that do not generalize to the writing assistance scenario.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2]: "The sentences are processed in 3 steps: (1) Selecting all the words or phrases in the synonym thesaurus... (3) Randomly substituting the improvable targets with one synonyms of the same part of speech."
  - [section 5.2]: "Both the rule-based method and end-to-end baselines... have high Pdet and low Rdet values."
  - [section 6.3]: Case study shows BERT substituting "knowing" with "understanding" without considering context, a failure traced to training data lacking contextual signals.
- Break condition: If training data includes strong context-aware signals (e.g., via contrastive examples or context embeddings), models could learn appropriate usage even from distantly supervised data, reducing the gap.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Inter-annotator agreement (IAA)
  - Why needed here: IAA validates the quality and consistency of human annotations, ensuring the dataset is reliable for training and evaluation.
  - Quick check question: What is the pairwise inter-annotator agreement (PAsug) reported for SWS, and how does it compare to prior lexical substitution datasets?

- Concept: Weighted accuracy in detection tasks
  - Why needed here: Weighted accuracy (WAdet) accounts for varying confidence levels among targets, giving more credit for detecting high-confidence improvable words.
  - Quick check question: How is WAdet calculated, and why does it differ from plain recall?

- Concept: Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) for ranked suggestions
  - Why needed here: NDCG evaluates the quality of multiple substitution suggestions by rewarding higher-ranked correct suggestions and penalizing lower-ranked ones, reflecting practical writing assistance needs.
  - Quick check question: How is NDCG computed for m suggestions in SWS, and what does a drop in NDCGm as m increases indicate?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: SWS benchmark comprises three main modules—data collection (human-labeled test set and distantly supervised training set), evaluation framework (end-to-end and sub-task metrics), and baseline models (knowledge-driven, lexical substitution, end-to-end sequence models). Data flows from collection to training baselines, then evaluation uses the same metrics for both detection and suggestion quality.

- Critical path: For end-to-end evaluation, the critical path is: input sentence → detect improvable targets → generate substitution suggestions → score each suggestion for correctness → aggregate precision/recall/F0.5. A failure in detection nullifies substitution accuracy for that target.

- Design tradeoffs: Using distantly supervised data trades annotation cost for lower signal quality—synonym replacements are abundant but contextually inappropriate. Human-annotated data is high quality but limited in size. End-to-end models can learn joint patterns but may underperform on detection if training data lacks true improvable targets.

- Failure signatures: Low Rdet with high Accsug suggests the model detects few targets but gives good suggestions when it does; low Accsug with high Rdet suggests the model detects many targets but provides poor substitutions. A drop in NDCGm with increasing m indicates difficulty generating multiple useful suggestions.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a BERT-base MLM on the distantly supervised dataset and evaluate end-to-end F0.5; observe if detection recall is low despite substitution accuracy.
  2. Run the rule-based synonym replacement baseline to establish an upper bound on detection (since it uses the same data generation process).
  3. Compare end-to-end performance of BERT vs. BART vs. CMLM on the test set to see if pre-training helps substitution quality.

  - What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing how different training data or model architectures affect ImpR, along with maintaining or improving overall performance.

- How can the model be improved to provide multiple useful substitution suggestions for each detected improvable target?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes, "BERT may perform better than other methods, but as the number of suggestions m increases, the NDCGm of BERT drops significantly. This suggests that BERT struggles when providing multiple suggestions."
  - Why unresolved: The paper identifies the challenge of providing multiple suggestions but does not offer a solution or method to address this issue.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results demonstrating improved performance in providing multiple suggestions, possibly through different model architectures, training data, or loss functions.

- How can the model be trained to better understand word usage in different contexts, rather than relying on one-substitute-one data?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states, "We think it’s hard to train a model aware of word usage in different contexts with the current distantly-supervised training data. Because we think the one-substitute-one data doesn’t provide enough information for model training on word usage."
  - Why unresolved: The paper identifies the limitation of the current training data but does not provide a solution or alternative approach to improve context-aware word usage understanding.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing improved performance in context-aware word usage, possibly through different training data, pre-training objectives, or fine-tuning techniques.

## Limitations

- The human-annotated test set is limited to 1,000 sentences, raising questions about generalization to broader writing assistance contexts.
- Distantly supervised training data, while large, is generated through random synonym replacement without context awareness, potentially limiting the models' ability to learn appropriate usage.
- The reliance on voting indices for target importance assumes annotator agreement reflects true improvement needs, but the dataset's inter-annotator agreement and its stability across domains are not fully characterized.

## Confidence

- High confidence in the mechanism that end-to-end evaluation is more realistic than isolated lexical substitution, supported by clear evidence that detection is a non-trivial sub-task.
- Medium confidence in the robustness of the human-annotated dataset, as IAA is reported but specific agreement values and cross-domain validation are not provided.
- Low confidence in the sufficiency of the distantly supervised data for training contextually appropriate substitutions, given the evidence of contextually inappropriate substitutions in case studies.

## Next Checks

1. Analyze the pairwise inter-annotator agreement (PAsug) values across different sentence types and annotator groups to assess the consistency and reliability of the human-annotated data.
2. Conduct a domain transfer experiment by evaluating SWS models on a held-out subset of the test data from a different domain (e.g., formal essays vs. informal messages) to test generalization.
3. Augment the distantly supervised training data with context-aware signals (e.g., contrastive examples or context embeddings) and retrain end-to-end models to see if detection recall improves without sacrificing substitution accuracy.
