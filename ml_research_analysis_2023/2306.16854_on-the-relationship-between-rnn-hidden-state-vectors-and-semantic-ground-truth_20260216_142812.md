# On the Relationship Between RNN Hidden State Vectors and Semantic Ground Truth

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.16854
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16854
- Reference count: 40
- The paper examines the clustering hypothesis that RNN hidden states form semantically meaningful clusters.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether hidden-state vectors of recurrent neural networks trained on regular languages exhibit semantically meaningful clustering. Through extensive experiments with 59 different regular languages, the authors demonstrate that well-trained RNNs (Elman, LSTM, GRU) develop piecewise linear decision boundaries in their hidden state space that correspond to automaton states. The study shows that unsupervised clustering algorithms can successfully identify semantically meaningful clusters, supporting the clustering hypothesis for the majority of cases examined. The research provides both empirical evidence and theoretical insights into the relationship between RNN internal representations and ground-truth automata.

## Method Summary
The authors train RNNs (Elman, LSTM, GRU) to recognize 59 regular languages defined by DFAs and Moore machines. Each RNN is trained to perfect accuracy on validation data (50,000 training samples, 2,000 validation samples). Hidden-state vectors are extracted from validation data and clustered using k-means, DBSCAN, OPTICS, and mean shift. Clustering quality is evaluated using LDA and logistic regression classification accuracy, as well as an ambiguity metric measuring information entropy between cluster mappings and optimal state mappings. The analysis compares clustering results against ground-truth automata states to assess the clustering hypothesis.

## Key Results
- Hidden-state vectors of well-trained RNNs are linearly separable in 74-91% of cases
- Unsupervised clustering techniques successfully find semantically meaningful clusters
- Initial conditions and training parameters significantly affect clustering quality
- The clustering hypothesis holds for the majority of examined regular languages and RNN architectures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Hidden-state vectors of well-trained RNNs are linearly separable into regions corresponding to automaton states.
- Mechanism: When an RNN is trained to recognize a regular language, its hidden-state space develops piecewise linear decision boundaries that correspond to transitions between states in the ground-truth DFA. This allows linear models like LDA and logistic regression to achieve high classification accuracy.
- Core assumption: The RNN learns a representation where semantically similar states cluster together in the hidden space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We observed that the hidden-state vectors of well-trained RNNs are separable, and that the unsupervised clustering techniques succeed in finding clusters of similar state vectors."
  - [section]: "The LDA and LR are able to achieve perfect classification in 74% and 91% of the cases, respectively... this high level of accuracy indicates that in the majority of considered cases, even with networks that did not achieve perfect accuracy, piecewise linear separability of the hidden state space is possible."
  - [corpus]: Weak. Related papers focus on RNN efficiency and architecture but do not directly support linear separability claims.
- Break condition: If the RNN is undertrained, overtrained, or the target language is too complex, the linear separability may break down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Unsupervised clustering techniques can find meaningful clusters in the hidden-state space that correlate with automaton states.
- Mechanism: Clustering algorithms like k-means, DBSCAN, and mean shift group hidden-state vectors based on their proximity in the high-dimensional space. When the RNN learns a good representation, these clusters correspond to semantically similar states in the ground-truth DFA.
- Core assumption: The hidden-state space has a structure that allows density-based or centroid-based clustering to find semantically meaningful groups.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our evaluation supports the validity of the clustering hypothesis in the majority of examined cases."
  - [section]: "The median weighted ambiguity of DBSCAN with ϵ ≥ 1 is almost equal to 0, which means that in at least half of the cases, we can identify semantically meaningful discrete states from clusters."
  - [corpus]: Weak. Related papers do not provide evidence for clustering effectiveness in RNN hidden spaces.
- Break condition: If the hidden-state space is too high-dimensional or the clusters are too overlapping, clustering may fail to find meaningful groups.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training initialization affects the clustering of hidden-state vectors.
- Mechanism: Initializing RNN weights close to a correct-by-construction automaton encoding leads to dense clusters in the hidden space. Adding noise and then training allows the RNN to adjust weights to maintain meaningful clustering.
- Core assumption: The initial clustering structure influences the final learned representation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We observed that initial conditions do affect clustering. For example, our constructed weights seem to lead to smaller distances in the state space, such that DBSCAN performed better with smaller ϵ."
  - [abstract]: "We observed that the hidden-state vectors of well-trained RNNs are separable, and that the unsupervised clustering techniques succeed in finding clusters of similar state vectors."
  - [corpus]: Weak. No direct evidence from related papers on initialization effects.
- Break condition: If the noise level is too high or the training process diverges, the initial clustering structure may be lost.

- Component map:
  - Data generation: Regular languages defined by DFAs/Moore machines
  - RNN training: Elman RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs with various sizes and activations
  - Hidden-state extraction: Processing validation data through trained RNNs
  - Clustering: k-means, DBSCAN, OPTICS, mean shift with various parameterizations
  - Evaluation: LDA, logistic regression for separability, ambiguity for clustering quality

- Critical path:
  1. Generate regular language dataset
  2. Train RNN on dataset
  3. Extract hidden-state vectors from validation data
  4. Apply clustering algorithms
  5. Evaluate clustering quality using ambiguity metric

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Network size vs. clustering quality: Larger networks may learn better representations but are harder to cluster
  - Clustering algorithm choice: Different algorithms have different strengths and weaknesses
  - Parameterization: Clustering and RNN training parameters significantly affect results

- Failure signatures:
  - High ambiguity values indicate poor clustering
  - Low RNN accuracy suggests poor representation learning
  - Inconsistent results across runs may indicate sensitivity to initialization

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a simple Elman RNN on a small DFA and examine hidden-state separability using LDA
  2. Apply k-means clustering with k equal to the number of DFA states and compute ambiguity
  3. Repeat experiment with different RNN types (LSTM, GRU) and compare clustering results

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of activation function (tanh vs ReLU) affect the clustering hypothesis in RNNs trained on regular languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that ReLU activations are unbounded, while tanh is bounded, and suggests that tanh Elman RNNs may create clusters in the saturated area of tanh, similar to their RNN encoding of automata.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how different activation functions impact clustering quality and the validity of the clustering hypothesis.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the clustering results of RNNs with different activation functions (e.g., tanh, ReLU, sigmoid) trained on the same regular languages, along with a thorough analysis of the impact on clustering accuracy and the number of clusters found.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of different optimizers on the clustering hypothesis in RNNs trained on regular languages?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the ADAM optimizer was used for all experiments, but does not explore the effect of different optimizers on clustering results.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate how the choice of optimizer (e.g., SGD, RMSprop) might affect the clustering hypothesis and the quality of clusters found in the hidden state space.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training RNNs with different optimizers on the same regular languages and comparing the clustering results in terms of accuracy, number of clusters, and the validity of the clustering hypothesis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the size of the training dataset affect the clustering hypothesis in RNNs trained on regular languages?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses a fixed training dataset size (50,000 words) for all experiments, but does not explore the impact of dataset size on clustering results.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate how varying the size of the training dataset might influence the clustering hypothesis, the quality of clusters found, and the overall performance of RNNs in recognizing regular languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training RNNs on regular languages with varying dataset sizes and analyzing the impact on clustering accuracy, the number of clusters found, and the validity of the clustering hypothesis across different dataset sizes.

## Limitations
- Analysis limited to deterministic finite automata and regular languages only
- Requires ground-truth automata for evaluation, which may not be available in practical applications
- Does not examine more expressive language classes beyond regular languages

## Confidence
- High: Hidden-state vectors are linearly separable in 74-91% of cases
- Medium: Clustering quality varies significantly with training conditions and language complexity
- Low: Generalizability to non-regular languages and real-world sequential data remains untested
