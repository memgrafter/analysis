# VR.net: A Real-world Dataset for Virtual Reality Motion Sickness Research

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03381
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03381
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: VR.net is a large-scale, accurately-labeled dataset for VR motion sickness research, containing 12 hours of gameplay videos from 10 real-world games across 10 diverse genres, with automatically extracted ground truth labels for camera/object movement, depth field, and motion flow.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces VR.net, a dataset designed to advance research on virtual reality (VR) motion sickness. VR.net addresses the critical need for accurately-labeled, real-world, and diverse data to improve the accuracy and generalizability of machine learning approaches for identifying VR motion sickness. The dataset is built by automatically extracting ground truth data from 3D game engines' rendering pipelines, avoiding the noise and labor of manual annotation or vision-based methods. VR.net contains approximately 12 hours of gameplay videos from ten real-world games across ten diverse genres, each frame labeled with 13 motion sickness-related features such as camera/object movement, depth field, and motion flow. The paper demonstrates the utility of VR.net through applications including risk factor detection and sickness level prediction, and plans to expand the dataset to 10X more data in its next version.

## Method Summary
The method centers on a data collection tool called VRhook, which intercepts and queries the rendering pipelines of Unity and Unreal engines to automatically extract accurate ground truth labels for each video frame, including camera/object movement, depth, and motion flow. This approach bypasses the need for manual annotation or vision-based noise, enabling high-quality labels at scale. The dataset covers 10 real-world VR games across 10 diverse genres. Machine learning models—specifically self-supervised SlowFast for risk factor detection, VideoMAE for object density estimation, and 3D CNNs for comfort rating prediction—are trained and evaluated on VR.net to demonstrate its utility for both research and practical applications in VR motion sickness.

## Key Results
- VR.net contains approximately 12 hours of gameplay videos from 10 real-world games across 10 diverse genres.
- Ground truth labels for camera/object movement, depth field, and motion flow are automatically extracted from 3D engines' rendering pipelines.
- Risk factor detection and comfort rating prediction models achieve promising performance using VR.net.
- The dataset is designed to scale to 10X more data in its next version, enabling broader machine learning research.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Automatic extraction of ground truth data from 3D engines' rendering pipelines enables high-quality labeled data without manual annotation or vision-based noise.
- Mechanism: By intercepting engine-level rendering functions and querying C++ object metadata at runtime, the system retrieves accurate camera matrices, object transformations, depth textures, and motion vectors directly from the rendering pipeline.
- Core assumption: The rendering pipeline provides consistent, noise-free ground truth for scene parameters (e.g., camera movement, object motion, depth) that are otherwise difficult to estimate accurately via computer vision.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "For each video frame, a rich set of motion sickness-related labels, such as camera/object movement, depth field, and motion flow, are accurately assigned."
  - [section] "We enhance our previous data collection tool called VRhook to automatically extract accurate ground truth data from 3D game engines' graphics pipelines without accessing VR games' source code."
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus neighbors focus on motion sickness effects, not dataset construction methods.
- Break condition: If engine APIs change or games use non-standard rendering pipelines, the data extraction method may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Self-supervised learning models like SlowFast can effectively detect motion sickness risk factors using the diverse and large-scale video data in VR.net.
- Mechanism: The SlowFast model learns spatiotemporal video representations from raw gameplay frames, then fine-tunes fully connected layers to predict risk factors such as excessive speed, acceleration, and multi-axis rotation.
- Core assumption: The diversity and scale of VR.net enable the model to generalize across game genres and user behaviors, overcoming the limitations of smaller, synthetic datasets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Self-supervised learning generally outperforms conventional supervised learning, particularly when the training dataset is small."
  - [section] "Our model is built upon SlowFast, a commonly-used self-supervised learning model for video recognition."
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus neighbors discuss motion sickness but not machine learning methods for detection.
- Break condition: If the training set is not representative of all VR experiences, the model may not generalize to unseen games or users.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The combination of motion flow and depth texture labels enables accurate prediction of comfort ratings using 3D CNNs.
- Mechanism: 3D CNNs convolve over spatial and temporal dimensions of motion flow and depth texture sequences to map video content to subjective comfort scores on a 1–5 scale.
- Core assumption: Motion flow and depth information are sufficient predictors of motion sickness severity, as validated by prior studies and replicated using VR.net.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We replicate a previous study that exploits the motion flow and depth texture labels to predict motion sickness levels."
  - [section] "Our results indicate that the model provides a decent prediction accuracy of 0.70."
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus neighbors do not discuss prediction methods using motion flow or depth.
- Break condition: If additional factors (e.g., audio, narrative) significantly influence sickness, models relying only on visual cues may underperform.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data extraction tool (Unity/Unreal hooks) -> Video/label storage (cloud) -> ML pipeline (SlowFast, 3D CNN) -> Risk factor/comfort prediction -> User interface for developers
- Critical path: Render pipeline hooks -> Accurate label extraction -> Large-scale dataset assembly -> Model training and validation -> Integration with VR development tools
- Design tradeoffs: Automatic extraction (high accuracy, low noise) vs. manual annotation (flexibility, but time-consuming); self-supervised learning (scalable) vs. supervised (requires more labels)
- Failure signatures: Incomplete or noisy labels; model overfitting to specific game genres; inability to detect new risk factors; poor generalization across user groups
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate accuracy of extracted labels by comparing to manually annotated ground truth for a small set of frames.
  2. Train SlowFast on VR.net subset to detect excessive camera speed; measure accuracy and F1 score.
  3. Use 3D CNN to predict comfort ratings on a hold-out set; compute Pearson correlation and prediction accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does VR.net's ground truth data collection from rendering pipelines compare to vision-based labeling methods in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency?
- Can VR.net's risk factor detection system be extended to identify and predict motion sickness risk factors specific to different VR game genres?
- How does the inclusion of physiological and gaze-tracking signals in future versions of VR.net impact the prediction accuracy of motion sickness levels?

## Limitations
- Accuracy and robustness of the automatic data extraction tool may be compromised for games with custom or non-standard rendering pipelines.
- Model generalization across diverse user populations and game genres is not fully demonstrated.
- Claims regarding self-supervised learning's superiority are supported only by indirect evidence, and comfort rating prediction accuracy (0.70) suggests room for improvement.

## Confidence

**High**: Automatic extraction of ground truth data from rendering pipelines; diversity and scale of the VR.net dataset.
**Medium**: Effectiveness of self-supervised learning models (SlowFast) for risk factor detection; accuracy of 3D CNNs for comfort rating prediction.
**Low**: Generalizability of the dataset and models to all VR experiences and user groups.

## Next Checks

1. Compare automatically extracted labels to manually annotated ground truth for a representative sample of video frames to assess accuracy.
2. Evaluate model performance on a held-out test set from games not seen during training to measure generalization.
3. Conduct a user study with diverse participants to validate model predictions of motion sickness risk and comfort ratings against subjective reports.
