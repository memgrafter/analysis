# Computational modeling of semantic change

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.06337
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06337
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: Comprehensive overview of computational modeling techniques for detecting and analyzing semantic change in language using large textual corpora

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a comprehensive overview of computational approaches to semantic change detection using large textual corpora. It presents a clear taxonomy of semantic change research formulations and compares count-based, static embedding, and contextualized embedding approaches. The authors discuss theoretical foundations including the distributional hypothesis, and provide practical guidance for data selection, preprocessing, and evaluation. The work serves as both an introduction to computational semantic change detection and a reference for established methods in the field.

## Method Summary
The paper describes computational methods for detecting semantic change through distributional semantic models. Count-based models build co-occurrence matrices tracking word-context relationships, static embeddings learn vector representations through predictive training that are compared across time periods after alignment, and contextualized models generate token-level vectors capturing sense distinctions. The methods require dividing corpora into temporal bins, training models on each period, and comparing representations to detect shifts in meaning.

## Key Results
- Taxonomy of semantic change research formulations with clear distinctions between research questions
- Detailed comparison of count-based, static embedding, and contextualized embedding approaches
- Discussion of data selection and preprocessing requirements for semantic change detection
- Analysis of evaluation strategies including a priori testsets and control settings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Count-based models detect semantic change by tracking shifts in co-occurrence patterns between words.
- Mechanism: The model builds a matrix where each row is a target word and each column is a context word. Changes in meaning are detected by comparing these matrices across time bins.
- Core assumption: Semantic meaning is encoded in the statistical co-occurrence patterns of words in context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The basis for our work is thus textual data and a computational method to extract information about words and their meaning from the text and so create meaning representations, on the basis of which we can detect semantic change."
  - [section] "For each target word, the models count how many times other words appeared (co-occurred) in the context of the target word in a large textual corpus."
- Break condition: If the co-occurrence patterns are dominated by function words or if the window size is too small to capture meaningful semantic context.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Predictive models detect semantic change by tracking shifts in learned vector representations of words.
- Mechanism: The model trains to predict masked words in context, adjusting parameters iteratively. Changes in meaning are detected by comparing vectors from different time periods after alignment.
- Core assumption: Words used in similar contexts will have similar vector representations, and changes in usage contexts will be reflected in vector shifts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "These models learn the meaning of words directly from text, by training to predict a MASKED word given its context."
  - [section] "If a word has changed its meaning, so will its vector representation, and as a result the word will have moved to a different region of the space."
- Break condition: If the training data is insufficient for the model to converge, or if the temporal bins are too short for meaningful semantic change to occur.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Contextualized models detect semantic change by tracking shifts in token-level representations across contexts.
- Mechanism: The model generates different vectors for each occurrence of a word based on its context. Changes are detected by clustering or comparing these token vectors over time.
- Core assumption: Different senses of a word will have distinct vector representations based on their usage contexts, allowing sense-level change detection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Contextualized models create token vectors, that is, each individual occurrence of a word is represented by its own vector."
  - [section] "The comparison of vectors from different time periods is trivial: all vectors share the same semantic space as they are derived from the same model."
- Break condition: If the pretraining corpus dominates the learned representations, masking genuine historical changes, or if the contextual model lacks sufficient data to capture sense distinctions.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Distributional hypothesis
  - Why needed here: This is the theoretical foundation underlying all computational approaches to semantic change detection described in the paper.
  - Quick check question: Can you explain how the distributional hypothesis relates to the idea that "you shall know a word by the company it keeps"?

- Concept: Vector space alignment
  - Why needed here: Static embedding models train independently on different time periods, requiring alignment before comparison to detect change.
  - Quick check question: Why can't we directly compare word vectors from static models trained on different time periods without alignment?

- Concept: Token vs. type representations
  - Why needed here: The paper distinguishes between modeling words as whole units (type) versus individual occurrences (token), which affects change detection capabilities.
  - Quick check question: How does using token-level representations enable sense-level semantic change detection that type-level representations cannot achieve?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing → Model training → Temporal binning → Vector comparison → Change detection → Evaluation
- Critical path: Data preprocessing → Model training → Temporal binning → Vector comparison → Change detection → Evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Count-based models are interpretable but may miss nuanced changes; static models require alignment but capture rich semantic information; contextualized models capture sense distinctions but may inherit pretraining biases.
- Failure signatures: Poor model convergence (detected through random variation exceeding change signals), alignment failures (causing spurious changes), or pretraining contamination (masking genuine historical changes).
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a count-based model on a small corpus with known semantic changes and verify co-occurrence patterns shift as expected.
  2. Train static embeddings on two time periods and test alignment methods to ensure vectors from different periods can be compared.
  3. Use a contextualized model on a corpus with clear polysemy and verify token clustering separates different word senses.

## Limitations

- Data quality requirements: Effectiveness heavily depends on corpus size, temporal resolution, and linguistic preprocessing
- Limited evaluation benchmarks: Gold standard datasets for semantic change are scarce, making comprehensive validation challenging
- Pretraining contamination: Contextualized models may inherit biases from pretraining corpora that mask genuine historical semantic shifts

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The theoretical foundations (distributional hypothesis) and general taxonomy of computational approaches to semantic change detection are well-established and clearly presented.

**Medium Confidence**: The comparative effectiveness of different model types for specific semantic change scenarios is discussed but not empirically validated across multiple benchmarks.

**Low Confidence**: Claims about the relative importance of various hyperparameters for different types of semantic change lack systematic empirical validation.

## Next Checks
