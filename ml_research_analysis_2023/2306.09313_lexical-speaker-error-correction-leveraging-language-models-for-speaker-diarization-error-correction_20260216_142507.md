# Lexical Speaker Error Correction: Leveraging Language Models for Speaker Diarization Error Correction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.09313
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09313
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Relative WDER reductions of 15-30% on three telephony datasets (Fisher, RT03-CTS, Callhome American English) using a two-pass approach that combines acoustic speaker embeddings with lexical information from ASR transcriptions.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses speaker diarization errors in multi-speaker conversations, particularly around speaker turns and overlapping speech. The proposed Lexical Speaker Error Correction (SEC) method uses a two-pass approach where a language model corrects speaker labels from an initial acoustic-only diarization system using lexical information from ASR transcriptions. The core innovation is a Transformer encoder that fuses word embeddings from a pre-trained language model with speaker embeddings from the first-pass diarization system to predict corrected speaker labels. Experiments on three telephony datasets demonstrate significant improvements in diarization accuracy, with relative WDER reductions of 15-30% and absolute WDERs as low as 1.53 on the Fisher dataset.

## Method Summary
The SEC method employs a two-pass approach for speaker diarization error correction. First, an acoustic-only speaker diarization system generates initial speaker labels. Then, a Transformer encoder takes both the ASR transcriptions and the initial speaker labels as input, fusing word embeddings from a pre-trained language model with speaker embeddings to predict corrected labels. The model is trained using simulated speaker and ASR errors with curriculum learning, gradually increasing the difficulty from high ASR error rates to higher speaker error rates. During inference, corrections are applied on sliding windows of 30 words, allowing the model to handle conversations with more than two speakers globally while being trained on two-speaker cases locally.

## Key Results
- Relative WDER reductions of 15-30% compared to baseline acoustic-only diarization systems
- Best model achieves WDERs of 1.53 (Fisher), 3.28 (RT03-CTS), and 2.97 (Callhome American English)
- Effective with limited paired data, requiring only small amounts of text data for training and fine-tuning
- Sliding window approach of 30 words provides optimal balance between context and computational efficiency

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The two-pass approach improves diarization accuracy by combining acoustic-only speaker embeddings with lexical information from ASR transcriptions.
- Mechanism: First pass uses acoustic features to generate initial speaker labels, while second pass uses a language model to correct these labels based on textual context.
- Core assumption: Acoustic-only systems are prone to errors around speaker turns and overlapping speech, which can be corrected using lexical information.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "This approach can lead to speaker errors especially around speaker turns and regions of speaker overlap." [section] "Lexical information can contain complementary information which can be very useful in accurately predicting speaker turns."
- Break condition: If ASR transcriptions are highly inaccurate or conversations lack sufficient lexical context for speaker turn prediction, the correction mechanism may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Curriculum learning effectively balances information flow from SD labels and ASR word lexical information during training.
- Mechanism: Training starts with higher ASR error rates and lower speaker error rates, gradually shifting to lower ASR error rates and higher speaker error rates as training progresses.
- Core assumption: Starting with high ASR errors forces model to rely on speaker labels initially, then gradually introduces lexical information to improve corrections.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We simulate ASR, Speaker errors using a curriculum learning paradigm to make sure that we don't under or over correct the speakers and balance the information flow from the SD labels and ASR word lexical information."
- Break condition: If curriculum schedule is not properly tuned, model may either overfit to initial conditions or fail to learn correction patterns effectively.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Sliding window approach enables handling conversations with more than two speakers globally while being trained on two-speaker cases.
- Mechanism: Model processes overlapping windows of fixed size (30 words) and corrects only windows containing two speakers, bypassing windows with more speakers.
- Core assumption: Most speaker errors occur within short segments where only two speakers are active.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Though the lexical corrector is trained to only correct two speakers locally, we can still handle use-cases where more than two speakers are detected globally in the audio."
- Break condition: If speaker overlaps occur frequently or more than two speakers are active in most windows, correction effectiveness may be limited.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  ASR system -> First-pass SD system -> SEC module -> Reconciliation stage

- Critical path:
  1. Audio input → ASR system → Transcribed words
  2. Audio input → First-pass SD system → Initial speaker labels
  3. SEC module processes both outputs to produce corrected speaker labels
  4. Final output: Words with corrected speaker labels

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Modularity vs. integration: Two-pass approach allows independent optimization of ASR and SD systems but requires reconciliation
  - Window size: Larger windows provide more context but may include more speaker changes, complicating correction
  - Training data: Simulated errors reduce need for paired audio-text data but may not capture all real-world error patterns

- Failure signatures:
  - Persistent errors around speaker turns despite correction
  - Degradation in accuracy when ASR transcriptions are noisy
  - Limited improvement when conversations have many overlapping speakers

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate impact of different sliding window sizes on correction accuracy using validation set
  2. Test model's performance with varying levels of simulated ASR errors to determine optimal training conditions
  3. Assess effectiveness of fine-tuning backbone LM on conversational datasets versus using pre-trained model directly

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does the SEC model's performance scale with increasing number of speakers in a conversation beyond two speakers?
- How robust is the SEC model to significant ASR errors, particularly in cases where word recognition accuracy drops below a certain threshold?
- How does the SEC model perform across different languages and dialects, and what modifications are needed for effective cross-lingual application?

## Limitations
- Methodology relies heavily on simulated errors during training, which may not fully capture real-world error patterns
- Evaluation focuses primarily on WDER metrics without deeper analysis of error types or qualitative assessment
- Approach requires accurate ASR transcriptions as input, creating dependency chain where ASR errors could propagate
- Model's effectiveness with more than two speakers is limited by its two-speaker training paradigm

## Confidence
**High Confidence** in the core finding that lexical information can improve speaker diarization accuracy when combined with acoustic features. Experimental results show consistent WDER reductions across multiple datasets, and methodology of fusing LM embeddings with speaker embeddings is technically sound.

**Medium Confidence** in the curriculum learning approach's effectiveness. While paper demonstrates improved results with this training strategy, specific parameters and schedules are not fully explored, and benefits could be dataset-specific.

**Medium Confidence** in generalizability to non-telephony domains. Evaluation is limited to telephony datasets, and approach's performance on broadcast media, meetings, or other conversational contexts remains untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Error Pattern Analysis**: Conduct detailed error analysis comparing pre-correction and post-correction speaker labels to identify which types of errors (e.g., speaker turns, overlaps, confusion between similar speakers) are most effectively corrected by the SEC model.

2. **Cross-Domain Evaluation**: Test the SEC model on non-telephony datasets (e.g., broadcast news, meetings, or podcasts) to assess its generalization capabilities beyond the telephony domain where it was developed and evaluated.

3. **End-to-End Integration Study**: Evaluate model's performance when integrated into complete pipeline with varying ASR quality levels, measuring how ASR errors propagate through correction system and identifying minimum ASR accuracy threshold for effective SEC performance.
