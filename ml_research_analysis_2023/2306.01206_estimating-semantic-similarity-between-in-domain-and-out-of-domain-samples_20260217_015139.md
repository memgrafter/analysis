# Estimating Semantic Similarity between In-Domain and Out-of-Domain Samples

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01206
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01206
- Reference count: 14
- Key outcome: Unsupervised similarity metrics, particularly Wasserstein distance, show promise for estimating semantic similarity between in-domain and out-of-domain samples to detect OOD data.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the problem of estimating semantic similarity between in-domain (ID) and out-of-domain (OOD) samples to detect OOD data without using a trained model. The authors analyze different OOD definitions, assess performance-based metrics, and explore unsupervised similarity metrics including Cosine similarity, Mauve score, Wasserstein distance, and Jensen Shannon distance. Using 12 datasets across 4 tasks, they find that Wasserstein distance shows the most consistent correlation with model performance in identifying OOD samples.

## Method Summary
The study uses 12 datasets from 4 tasks (Sentiment Analysis, MCQ, Extractive QA, NLI), each balanced and downsampled to match the smallest dataset size. A BERT base uncased model is fine-tuned for 2 epochs on each training set, then evaluated on both ID and OOD test sets. For similarity computation, 20 random instances are sampled from each train and test set, and pairwise similarity scores are calculated using Cosine, Mauve, Wasserstein, and JSD metrics. Kendall Tau and Pearson correlations are computed between model performance and similarity metrics to identify the most effective similarity metric for OOD detection.

## Key Results
- Wasserstein distance shows the most consistent correlation (10 out of 12 cases) with model performance according to Kendall Tau analysis
- Unsupervised similarity metrics demonstrate promising potential for OOD detection without requiring a trained model
- The study evaluates correlation between performance metrics and similarity metrics across 12 datasets from 4 different tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Performance-based metrics can reliably detect OOD samples when a supervised model is available
- Mechanism: By training a model on the in-domain dataset and testing on both in-domain and out-of-domain test sets, the performance difference serves as an indicator of OODness
- Core assumption: The supervised model's performance on OOD data will consistently be lower than on ID data
- Break condition: If ID performance is occasionally better than OOD performance, the reliability of this mechanism decreases

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Unsupervised similarity metrics can estimate semantic similarity between ID and OOD datasets
- Mechanism: By computing pairwise similarity scores between samples from the training set and test set using metrics like Cosine similarity, Mauve score, Wasserstein distance, and Jensen Shannon distance, one can assess how close the distributions are
- Core assumption: Higher similarity scores indicate that the test samples are from a similar distribution as the training samples
- Break condition: If the correlation between similarity metrics and performance is weak or inconsistent across different tasks

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Correlation between performance metrics and similarity metrics can validate the effectiveness of unsupervised methods
- Mechanism: By analyzing the Kendall Tau and Pearson correlation between model performance under ID/OOD settings and the similarity scores, one can determine which similarity metric is most predictive of OODness
- Break condition: If the correlation is low or varies significantly across different datasets and tasks

## Limitations

- The use of only 20 randomly sampled instances for similarity computation introduces significant variance and may not capture the full distribution characteristics
- The correlation analysis is based on a relatively small sample size across tasks, and the corpus evidence supporting this specific methodology is weak
- The study does not address potential class imbalance effects in the similarity metrics, nor does it explore the impact of different embedding models on the results
