# Doubly Robust Counterfactual Classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.06199
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06199
- Reference count: 40
- The paper proposes a doubly-robust estimator for counterfactual classification that can incorporate flexible constraints

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a doubly robust estimator for counterfactual classification under hypothetical scenarios. The method uses influence-function-based estimation of counterfactual classification risk and solves a constrained optimization problem to obtain model parameters. The estimator achieves √n convergence rates and has a closed-form asymptotic distribution, allowing for valid inference. Simulation results demonstrate its performance and double-robustness properties, and the method is applied to recidivism risk prediction using the COMPAS dataset, showing improved performance compared to baseline methods.

## Method Summary
The proposed method estimates counterfactual classification risk using an influence-function-based approach with sample splitting for nuisance estimation. It casts the classification problem as a nonlinear mathematical program involving counterfactuals, where the objective is to minimize the counterfactual classification risk subject to deterministic inequality constraints. The nuisance components (propensity score and outcome regression) are estimated using flexible machine learning methods, and the optimization problem is solved using a combination of stochastic global optimization followed by local refinement. The approach achieves √n convergence rates even when nuisance components are estimated at slower rates, and provides a closed-form expression for the asymptotic distribution of the estimator.

## Key Results
- The proposed estimator achieves √n convergence rates even when nuisance components are estimated at slower rates (e.g., n⁻¹/⁴)
- The estimator has a closed-form asymptotic distribution allowing valid inference under constraint qualification conditions
- Simulation results demonstrate the double-robustness properties and improved performance compared to baseline methods
- Application to COMPAS dataset shows improved performance compared to plug-in and oracle methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The estimator achieves √n convergence rates even when nuisance components are estimated at slower rates (e.g., n⁻¹/⁴).
- Mechanism: Double robustness ensures that the estimation error is driven by the product of nuisance estimation errors, not their individual rates. If either π̂a or μ̂a is estimated at √n rate, the overall error remains √n.
- Core assumption: (A2) states that either ∥π̂a − πa∥₂,P = oP(1) or ∥μ̂a − μa∥₂,P = oP(1), and (A3) ensures their product is oP(n⁻¹/²).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our analysis shows that the proposed estimator is robust against nuisance model misspecification, and can attain fast √n rates..."
  - [section 4]: Theorem 4.1 states "dist(β̂, s*(P)) = OP(∥π̂a − πa∥₂,P∥μ̂a − μa∥₂,P + n⁻¹/²)" and Corollary 4.1 extends this to the optimal value.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The estimator has a closed-form asymptotic distribution allowing valid inference.
- Mechanism: Under LICQ and SC, the solution mapping is directionally differentiable, enabling application of the delta method for stochastic programming to derive the asymptotic normality of n⁻¹/²(β̂ − β*).
- Core assumption: (B2) ensures a unique optimal solution, and (B1) ensures local convexity around β*, which together with LICQ and SC guarantee the required differentiability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "provide a closed-form expression for its asymptotic distribution"
  - [section 4]: Theorem 4.2 gives the explicit asymptotic distribution using Lagrange multipliers and the influence function variance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The estimator can incorporate flexible constraints into counterfactual classification.
- Mechanism: The problem is formulated as a nonlinear mathematical program where constraints gj(β) ≤ 0 are directly embedded in the optimization, allowing arbitrary smooth deterministic constraints beyond simple box constraints.
- Core assumption: The constraints gj are C² functions and the constraint qualification (B1) holds at the optimum.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "incorporate flexible constraints by casting the classification problem as a nonlinear mathematical program involving counterfactuals"
  - [section 2]: Explicitly defines the constrained optimization program (P) with general gj functions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Data (Y, A, X) tuples → Nuisance estimators (π̂a, μ̂a) using ML methods → Sample splitting for ˆη−b → Influence function construction → Optimization (StoGo + BOBYQA) → Solution β̂ → Inference using Theorem 4.2 formula

- Critical path:
  Data → Nuisance estimation (with sample splitting) → Influence function construction → Optimization → Solution β̂ → Inference

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Sample splitting vs. Donsker conditions: Sample splitting allows flexible ML but increases variance; Donsker conditions avoid splitting but limit estimator flexibility
  - Solver choice: Global optimization (StoGo) for accuracy vs. local methods for speed; constrained vs. unconstrained reformulation
  - Constraint specification: Deterministic constraints are analytically tractable vs. estimated constraints requiring different theory

- Failure signatures:
  - Optimization fails to converge: Check constraint qualification, scaling, or try different solver
  - Inference gives NaN: Check if LICQ/SC conditions are violated (active constraints nearly linearly dependent)
  - Poor performance vs. plug-in: Verify double robustness conditions (A1)-(A3) are satisfied in simulation

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify √n rates: Generate data where one nuisance estimator is n⁻¹/⁴ rate, check if β̂ achieves n⁻¹/² error
  2. Test constraint incorporation: Add box constraints and verify they are satisfied in solution
  3. Validate inference: Simulate under known distribution, check coverage of confidence intervals using Theorem 4.2 variance formula

## Limitations

- The √n convergence rate guarantee critically depends on the product of nuisance estimation errors being oP(n⁻¹/²), but the analysis does not fully characterize the regime where both nuisance estimators have errors between n⁻¹/⁴ and n⁻¹/² rates.
- The closed-form asymptotic distribution relies on constraint qualification conditions (LICQ and SC) that are difficult to verify in practice, particularly for nonlinear constraints where the active set may change with data.
- The simulation evidence demonstrates double robustness and improved performance vs baselines, but uses a single data-generating process, limiting generalizability.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The mechanism of double robustness ensuring √n rates when at least one nuisance estimator is √n-rate, supported by explicit error bounds in Theorem 4.1 and simulation evidence.
- **Medium confidence**: The closed-form asymptotic distribution under LICQ and SC, as these conditions are stated but their practical verification is challenging.
- **Medium confidence**: The improvement over baseline methods in the COMPAS application, as the comparison is against reasonable baselines but the absolute magnitude of improvement and sensitivity to modeling choices is not fully explored.

## Next Checks

1. **Boundary testing of double robustness**: Construct simulation scenarios where nuisance estimation errors are deliberately set between n⁻¹/⁴ and n⁻¹/² rates (e.g., n⁻¹/³) to identify the exact regime where the √n guarantee holds or breaks down.

2. **Constraint qualification verification**: For each constraint in the COMPAS application, compute the rank of the Jacobian of active constraints at the solution and test whether small perturbations in the data change the active set, directly verifying LICQ and SC conditions.

3. **Robustness to constraint specification**: Modify the simulation setup to test different constraint types (e.g., nonlinear, non-convex) and magnitudes to assess how constraint complexity affects estimation accuracy and whether the optimization solver consistently finds feasible solutions.
