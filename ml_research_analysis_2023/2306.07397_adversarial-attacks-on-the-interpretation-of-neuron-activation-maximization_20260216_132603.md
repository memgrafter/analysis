# Adversarial Attacks on the Interpretation of Neuron Activation Maximization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07397
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07397
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Three adversarial attacks (push-down, push-up, fairwashing) can successfully manipulate feature visualization interpretations while maintaining model accuracy, exposing vulnerabilities in using top-k image selection for model interpretability.

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that commonly used feature visualization techniques in neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial manipulation. The authors propose an optimization framework that fine-tunes pre-trained models to alter the top-k images that maximally activate neurons while preserving classification performance. Three distinct attack strategies are introduced: push-down replaces natural top-k images with decoy ones, push-up forces specific decoy images into top-k positions, and fairwashing manipulates perceived bias in feature visualizations. Experiments on AlexNet show these attacks can successfully modify interpretations with minimal accuracy loss, while synthetic feature visualizations remain largely unchanged, creating a dangerous disconnect between natural and synthetic interpretations.

## Method Summary
The attack framework fine-tunes pre-trained models using a combination of attack loss and distillation loss to maintain accuracy. The maintain loss (distillation) ensures modified model outputs stay close to the original model, while the attack loss defines the specific manipulation objective. The optimization runs for 2 epochs using Adam optimizer with learning rate 1e-5 and batch size 256. Three attack types are implemented: push-down minimizes activations of original top-k images to replace them with decoys, push-up maximizes activations of specific decoy images to force them into top-k positions, and fairwashing matches activation statistics across protected attribute groups to create balanced top-k appearances while preserving actual bias.

## Key Results
- Push-down attack successfully replaces top-k images with minimal accuracy loss (CLIP-δ scores close to zero)
- Push-up attack can force specific decoy images into top-k positions across all channels
- Fairwashing attack reduces perceived bias in feature visualization while maintaining actual model bias (DDI and DEO metrics unchanged)
- Synthetic feature visualizations remain largely unchanged during attacks, creating disconnect with manipulated top-k images

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adversarial optimization can replace top-k images that maximally activate a neuron while preserving classification accuracy.
- Mechanism: The push-down attack minimizes the squared ℓ2-norm of activations for the original top-k images, forcing them out of the top-k set, while the maintain loss (distillation) ensures the modified model's outputs stay close to the original model.
- Core assumption: The space of network weights is large enough to accommodate both the attack objective and the maintain objective without severe accuracy degradation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Experiments on AlexNet show that all attacks can successfully alter top-k images with minimal accuracy loss..."
  - [section] "For the maintain objective, when viewing final outputs fθ(.) as a conditional distribution, our maintain loss is the distillation loss LM(D; θ, θinitial) = LCE(fθinitial (.)||fθ(.))..."
- Break condition: If the weight space is too constrained or the attack objective is too strong, accuracy loss becomes prohibitive, making the attack infeasible.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The push-up attack can force a specific set of decoy images into the top-k for all channels of a layer, overriding natural semantic concepts.
- Mechanism: The attack loss ensures that activations of decoy images are larger than those of all training images for each channel, effectively pushing them into the top-k set.
- Core assumption: It is possible to increase activations of chosen decoy images without destroying the model's ability to classify other images correctly.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "...push-up (targeting specific images)..."
  - [section] "LA(D, Ddecoy; θ) = JlX j=1 X x∗∈Ddecoy X x∈D [∥f (l,j) θ (x)∥2 2 − ∥f (l,j) θ (x∗)∥2 2]+."
- Break condition: If decoy images are too dissimilar from natural patterns the network needs to recognize, the maintain loss cannot be satisfied, leading to accuracy loss.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fairwashing attack can reduce perceived bias in feature visualization while maintaining actual model bias and performance.
- Mechanism: By matching first and second moments of activations across protected attribute groups, the attack makes top-k images appear balanced, even though the model's outputs remain biased.
- Core assumption: An interpreter without protected attribute labels will judge fairness based solely on the visual balance of top-k images.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "...fairwashing (manipulating perceived bias)..."
  - [section] "LA(D, D0 attack ∪ D1 attack; θ) = ∥µl 0 − µl 1∥2 2 + ∥ρl 0 − ρl 1∥2 2..."
- Break condition: If the interpreter has access to protected attribute labels or uses statistical tests beyond top-k images, the fairwashing attack becomes detectable.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Pre-trained CNN (AlexNet) -> Attack framework -> Modified CNN
  Components: maintain loss (distillation), attack loss (push-down/up/fairwashing), optimization loop
  Data flow: Training images -> activations -> top-k selection -> attack objective -> weight update

- Critical path:
  1. Load pre-trained model and compute initial top-k images
  2. Define attack type (push-down, push-up, or fairwashing)
  3. Set up combined loss: α * attack_loss + (1-α) * maintain_loss
  4. Optimize model parameters for 2 epochs
  5. Evaluate success using CLIP-δ, Kendall-τ, and accuracy metrics

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Higher α values make attacks more aggressive but increase accuracy loss risk
  - Earlier layers are harder to attack due to fewer upstream weights
  - Synthetic feature visualization remains largely unchanged, creating a disconnect with top-k images

- Failure signatures:
  - Accuracy drops >0.5% indicate attack is too aggressive
  - Kendall-τ-W and CLIP-W metrics close to 1 suggest whack-a-mole behavior
  - Fairwashing attack fails if protected attribute labels are available to interpreter

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Single-channel push-down attack on conv5, verify top-k replacement and minimal accuracy loss
  2. All-channel push-up attack with goldfish decoy images, check generalization to validation set
  3. Fairwashing attack on AlexNet using ImageNet People Subtree, measure balance improvement while maintaining DDI/DEO metrics

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How robust are synthetic feature visualizations compared to top-k images under adversarial attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates that synthetic feature visualizations remain largely unchanged under push-down and push-up attacks, while top-k images are significantly altered.
- Why unresolved: The paper only studied the robustness of synthetic feature visualizations to push-down and push-up attacks, not targeted attacks specifically aimed at modifying them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments where adversarial attacks are explicitly designed to manipulate synthetic feature visualizations, and measuring the degree of success compared to top-k images.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the fairwashing attack be extended to protected attributes beyond binary gender?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates the fairwashing attack using gender as a binary protected attribute, but mentions the possibility of extending it to other attributes like race and age.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides results for a binary gender attribute, leaving the effectiveness of the attack on other protected attributes unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Running the fairwashing attack using race and age as protected attributes, and comparing the results to those obtained with gender.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the depth of the neural network layer affect the success of the push-down and push-up attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that early layers (conv1 and conv2) are harder to attack compared to later layers (conv3 and conv4), but the reasons for this are not fully explored.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides some insights into why early layers are harder to attack, but a more thorough investigation of the relationship between layer depth and attack success is needed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments on various layers of different neural network architectures, and analyzing the factors that contribute to the difficulty of attacking early layers.

## Limitations
- Limited to AlexNet architecture, generalizability to modern architectures like ResNet or EfficientNet unknown
- Evaluation only on ImageNet dataset, results may not transfer to other domains
- Fairwashing attack assumes interpreters lack protected attribute labels, but real-world tools may have additional metadata
- No defense mechanisms or detection methods explored for these adversarial attacks

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Claims about push-down attack successfully replacing top-k images while maintaining accuracy (>95% confidence, supported by experimental results showing CLIP-δ scores close to zero). Claims about fairwashing attack reducing perceived bias metrics (balance score) while maintaining actual bias (DDI/DEO) are well-supported (>90% confidence, with specific numerical improvements reported).

**Medium Confidence**: Claims about push-up attack's ability to generalize to validation sets (>70% confidence, limited to single-channel experiments). Claims about synthetic visualizations remaining unchanged during attacks (>60% confidence, qualitative observation without extensive quantitative analysis).

**Low Confidence**: Generalizability to other architectures and datasets (<40% confidence, no experiments beyond AlexNet/ImageNet).
