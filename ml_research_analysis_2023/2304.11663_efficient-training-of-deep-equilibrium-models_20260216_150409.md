# Efficient Training of Deep Equilibrium Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.11663
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11663
- Reference count: 4
- Key outcome: 2.16x speedup in training deep equilibrium models while maintaining classification accuracy

## Executive Summary
Deep equilibrium models (DEQs) are effective for learning data representations but are computationally expensive to train due to the need to solve Jacobian-based equations during backpropagation. This paper proposes GDEQ, a method that uses the Jacobian approximation from Broyden's method during the forward pass to compute gradients in the backward pass, avoiding expensive iterative solves. Experiments on CIFAR-10 image classification demonstrate that GDEQ achieves competitive accuracy while being significantly faster than existing methods.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes GDEQ (Gradient-based Deep Equilibrium training), which reuses the Broyden approximation B⁻¹_T computed during the forward pass to compute gradients during the backward pass. Instead of solving an expensive Jacobian-based equation, GDEQ uses a single matrix multiplication with the stored inverse Jacobian approximation. The method is evaluated on CIFAR-10 using MDEQ (Multiscale Deep Equilibrium) as the backbone model, comparing against implicit differentiation, JFB, and NPG methods across 220 training epochs.

## Key Results
- Achieves 2.16x speedup in training time compared to implicit differentiation
- Maintains competitive classification accuracy on CIFAR-10
- Shows cosine similarity between gradients computed by GDEQ and implicit differentiation remains high across training epochs
- Reduces computational complexity of backward pass from O(K) to O(1)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The Broyden approximation B⁻¹_T computed during the forward pass is sufficiently close to the true inverse Jacobian to provide valid ascent directions for training.
- Mechanism: Broyden's method iteratively updates B⁻¹ to approximate the inverse Jacobian while solving for the equilibrium. By reusing this final approximation during the backward pass, we avoid solving a new linear system while still obtaining a reasonable gradient estimate.
- Core assumption: The Broyden approximation at convergence is close enough to the true inverse Jacobian to maintain training progress.
- Evidence anchors: Experiments show cosine similarity between gradients computed by the two methods remains high across training epochs.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The computational complexity reduction from O(K) to O(1) for the backward pass enables significant training speedup.
- Mechanism: Instead of solving a system of linear equations iteratively during backpropagation, GDEQ uses a single matrix multiplication with the precomputed B⁻¹_T approximation.
- Core assumption: The time saved by avoiding iterative solves outweighs any potential accuracy loss from using the approximation.
- Evidence anchors: GDEQ achieves 2.16x faster training than implicit differentiation while maintaining accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The Broyden approximation captures the essential Jacobian structure needed for effective gradient descent.
- Mechanism: The Sherman-Morrison formula updates B⁻¹ to maintain proximity to the true Jacobian through low-rank updates, preserving the essential curvature information.
- Core assumption: The accumulated low-rank updates in B⁻¹_T capture sufficient Jacobian structure for meaningful gradient computation.
- Evidence anchors: The Broyden approximation maintains high cosine similarity with true gradients across training epochs.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Forward pass with Broyden iteration -> Store B⁻¹_T approximation -> Backward pass with single matrix multiplication -> Parameter update

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass with Broyden iteration (limited iterations)
  2. Store B⁻¹_T approximation
  3. Backward pass with single matrix multiplication
  4. Parameter update using computed gradients

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Memory vs. accuracy: Store more low-rank updates for better approximation but increased memory usage
  - Iteration count vs. speed: Fewer forward iterations for speed but potentially worse equilibrium approximation
  - Approximation quality vs. training stability: Better approximations may require more careful hyperparameter tuning

- Failure signatures:
  - Training divergence: Indicates poor gradient approximation quality
  - Slow convergence: May suggest Broyden's method isn't finding good equilibrium
  - Memory errors: Too many low-rank updates stored
  - NaN or Inf values: Numerical instability in matrix operations

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare training curves with different maximum iteration counts for Broyden's method
  2. Test impact of storing different numbers of low-rank updates on both speed and accuracy
  3. Validate gradient cosine similarity across training epochs to ensure approximation quality remains stable

## Limitations

- Broyden's method may fail to converge within iteration limits, leading to poor gradient approximations
- Theoretical convergence guarantees for stochastic gradient descent with GDEQ are not established
- Performance on task domains beyond image classification remains unexplored

## Confidence

- High confidence in speedup claims (2.16x faster training) and cosine similarity measurements
- Medium confidence in generalization claims, as experiments are limited to CIFAR-10 classification with single MDEQ architecture
