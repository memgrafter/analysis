# AnoRand: A Semi Supervised Deep Learning Anomaly Detection Method by Random Labeling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18389
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18389
- Reference count: 40
- This paper presents AnoRand, achieving state-of-the-art performance on 57 real-world datasets, outperforming 17 unsupervised algorithms and even some supervised methods.

## Executive Summary
AnoRand is a novel semi-supervised deep learning method for anomaly detection that addresses the challenge of limited labeled data. The method combines a noise detection block (feed-forward perceptron) with an autoencoder block to jointly optimize anomaly detection. The key innovation is creating synthetic anomalies by adding noise to normal samples, then training the model in a supervised manner on both normal and synthetic data. AnoRand achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming 17 unsupervised algorithms on 57 real-world datasets with AUC ROC and AUC PR metrics.

## Method Summary
AnoRand uses a two-block architecture consisting of a feed-forward perceptron (FFP) noise detection block and an autoencoder (AE) block that are jointly optimized. The method creates synthetic anomalies by randomly selecting 2% of normal samples, adding Gaussian noise, and oversampling with SMOTE to reach 5% of the dataset. The final anomaly score is a weighted combination of the FFP output and the AE reconstruction-based anomaly score, with weights determined by their third quantiles. The model is trained end-to-end with a weighted loss combining FFP and autoencoder components.

## Key Results
- Outperforms 17 unsupervised anomaly detection algorithms on 57 real-world datasets from the AD Bench benchmark
- Achieves best performance (AUC ROC and AUC PR) on the majority of reference datasets
- Even outperforms supervised methods in some cases, demonstrating the effectiveness of synthetic data generation
- Particularly effective when only a small percentage (2%) of labeled anomalies is available

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The joint optimization of FFP and AE blocks improves anomaly detection by balancing the influence of the majority class. The FFP block acts as a noise detector, reinforcing the AE block's ability to embed normal samples. The AE block learns reconstruction patterns, while the FFP block identifies synthetic anomalies, creating a feedback loop that strengthens the overall model.

### Mechanism 2
Synthetic anomaly generation via noise addition creates realistic outlier examples that bridge the gap between labeled and unlabeled data. By adding controlled Gaussian noise to a small subset of normal samples, the method creates synthetic anomalies that are close to the normal distribution but sufficiently different to be detected, enabling semi-supervised learning without requiring true anomaly labels.

### Mechanism 3
The weighted combination of FFP and AE predictions captures anomalies more effectively than either component alone. The final prediction combines the FFP noise detection output and AE reconstruction error, weighted by their respective third quantiles, ensuring that anomalies detected by either component are captured in the final score.

## Foundational Learning

- **Imbalanced class learning in neural networks**: Understanding why standard supervised methods fail on anomaly detection data where the anomaly class is severely underrepresented. Quick check: What happens to gradient updates when one class vastly outnumbers another in a neural network?

- **Autoencoder reconstruction error as anomaly score**: The AE block's ability to reconstruct normal samples well but fail on anomalies is central to the detection mechanism. Quick check: Why do autoencoders typically produce higher reconstruction errors for anomalous inputs?

- **Synthetic data generation for semi-supervised learning**: The method relies on creating realistic synthetic anomalies to enable supervised training without true anomaly labels. Quick check: What are the key considerations when generating synthetic anomalies that should approximate real anomalies?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: X → FFP block (2 hidden layers: 32, 16 neurons) → ˆy1; X → AE block (2 hidden layers: 32, 16 neurons, 16-dim latent space) → ˆx → ˆy0; combine ˆy1 and ˆy0 → final anomaly score

- **Critical path**: Input features flow through both FFP and AE blocks in parallel, with their outputs combined using quantile-based weighting to produce the final anomaly score

- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - FFP vs AE balance: Weight w controls the relative influence; too much FFP focus may ignore reconstruction patterns, too much AE focus may miss noise-based anomalies
  - Noise level selection: Must balance between creating distinguishable synthetic anomalies and maintaining realistic distribution
  - Architecture depth: Deeper networks may capture more complex patterns but risk overfitting on limited labeled data

- **Failure signatures**:
  - FFP overfitting: High AUC on synthetic data but poor generalization to real anomalies
  - AE mode collapse: AE learns to reconstruct both normal and synthetic samples well, indicating insufficient noise level
  - Class imbalance sensitivity: Performance degrades significantly when synthetic anomaly proportion changes

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Vary weight w (0 to 1) and measure AUC PR to find optimal FFP/AE balance
  2. Test different noise levels (standard deviation) to determine impact on synthetic anomaly quality
  3. Compare performance on balanced vs imbalanced synthetic datasets to verify class imbalance handling

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but several key uncertainties remain:

1. How does the optimal noise level for synthetic label generation (w=0.2, noise SD>0.52) vary across different data distributions and anomaly types?
2. Does the AnoRand architecture provide benefits over simpler ensemble methods combining autoencoders and FFPs?
3. How does AnoRand's performance degrade as the percentage of labeled anomalies in training data increases?
4. What is the theoretical justification for using third quantiles (Q3) to compute the weighting coefficient α in the final prediction?

## Limitations

- The paper's claims about state-of-the-art performance lack direct comparisons with other semi-supervised approaches using the same synthetic data generation strategy
- Specific noise level (standard deviation) and SMOTE parameters are not fully specified, which could affect reproducibility
- The method's performance on truly small datasets (<1000 samples) is not demonstrated

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The core mechanism of combining FFP and autoencoder blocks for anomaly detection is well-specified and theoretically sound
- **Medium confidence**: The synthetic anomaly generation approach is reasonable but lacks empirical validation of how well synthetic anomalies match real anomaly distributions
- **Medium confidence**: The claimed state-of-the-art performance is supported by extensive benchmarking but the specific conditions and hyperparameter sensitivity are not fully explored

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study**: Systematically vary the weight w (0.0 to 1.0) and noise level (0.1 to 1.0 standard deviation) to identify sensitivity and optimal configurations
