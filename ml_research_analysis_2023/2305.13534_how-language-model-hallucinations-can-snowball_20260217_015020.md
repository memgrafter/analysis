# How Language Model Hallucinations Can Snowball

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.13534
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13534
- Reference count: 13
- Key outcome: Language models can recognize 67-87% of their own hallucinated claims when presented in isolation

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals a novel failure mode in language models called "hallucination snowballing," where models commit to incorrect answers and then generate additional incorrect claims to justify these initial mistakes. The authors demonstrate that models like ChatGPT and GPT-4 can recognize 67% and 87% of their own incorrect claims respectively when these claims are presented in isolation, suggesting the phenomenon stems from reasoning pressure rather than knowledge gaps. Through three carefully constructed QA datasets (primality testing, senator search, and graph connectivity), the study shows that this behavior persists across different prompting strategies and represents a distinct failure mode from traditional knowledge-based hallucinations.

## Method Summary
The researchers constructed three QA datasets with 500 yes/no questions each, focusing on problems requiring sequential reasoning (primality testing, graph connectivity) and knowledge recall (senator search). They used zero-shot prompting with ChatGPT and GPT-4 to generate answers and explanations, then extracted incorrect claims from wrong answers using task-specific heuristics. In separate sessions, they asked the same models to verify these extracted claims individually to measure self-recognition rates. The study employed a two-stage evaluation: first measuring initial answer accuracy, then quantifying how many incorrect explanations the models could identify when claims were presented in isolation.

## Key Results
- ChatGPT and GPT-4 recognize 67% and 87% respectively of their own incorrect claims when presented in isolation
- Hallucination snowballing occurs across different prompting strategies, including "let's think step by step" approaches
- The phenomenon appears in both computational reasoning tasks and knowledge-based questions
- Models generate incorrect justifications that they can separately identify as wrong, demonstrating context-induced rather than knowledge-based errors

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Language models commit to an initial Yes/No answer before generating an explanation, creating consistency pressure that forces incorrect justifications.
- Mechanism: The model outputs the final answer token (Yes/No) as the first token of its response, then must generate a coherent explanation that supports this answer. Since the answer was generated without sufficient reasoning, the subsequent explanation often contains incorrect claims to maintain consistency with the initial choice.
- Core assumption: Instruction-tuned LMs reflect natural language patterns where speakers give final answers before explanations, and transformers cannot revise their initial answer once generated.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We hypothesize that in some cases, when justifying previously generated hallucinations, LMs output false claims that they can separately recognize as incorrect." [section 2] "In English and many other languages, speakers often say the final Yes/No answers to questions before explaining their answer. We therefore hypothesize that LMs and especially instruction-tuned LMs will reflect this answer format where the answer comes before the explanation."
- Break condition: If the model is prompted to generate reasoning before stating the final answer, or if it uses a decoding strategy that allows reconsideration of the initial answer.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Transformers cannot solve inherently sequential reasoning problems like primality testing or graph connectivity within a single timestep, forcing them to commit to incorrect answers.
- Mechanism: Problems requiring multiple reasoning steps (like checking all factors of a number or exploring all paths in a graph) cannot be solved by transformers in one generation step due to their computational limitations. When forced to answer immediately, the model commits to an incorrect answer and then generates supporting incorrect claims to justify this premature commitment.
- Core assumption: Primality testing and graph connectivity are inherently sequential problems outside the complexity class TC0, making them impossible for transformers to solve in one step.
- Evidence anchors: [section 2] "transformers cannot solve inherently sequential reasoning problems like primality testing or graph connectivity within a single timestep, as documented in recent theoretical results" [section 3] "Our graph connectivity and primality datasets are concrete instantiations of these problems"
- Break condition: If the model is allowed multiple generation steps to reason through the problem, or if the problem can be solved with single-step heuristics.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: When LMs recognize their own snowballed hallucinations in isolation, they acknowledge the incorrect claims, demonstrating the inconsistency between their contextualized and isolated reasoning.
- Mechanism: The model generates incorrect supporting claims during explanation generation, but when these same claims are presented in isolation without the context of the initial wrong answer, the model can recognize them as incorrect. This shows that the hallucinations are context-induced rather than due to knowledge gaps.
- Core assumption: The model has the capability to recognize incorrect facts when evaluated independently, but loses this ability when generating within a context that requires consistency with an initial mistake.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "we find that ChatGPT and GPT-4 can identify 67% and 87% of their own mistakes, respectively" [section 3.3] "We probe whether LMs recognize their snowballed hallucinations by verifying the model's incorrect claims in the output against the model itself" [section 3.4] "ChatGPT detects 67.37% of incorrect claims in explanations (i.e., snowballed hallucinations), and GPT-4 detects 87.03%"
- Break condition: If the model's recognition ability degrades significantly or if the verification setup becomes more complex and ambiguous.

  - Why needed here: The phenomenon relies on how LMs maintain coherence with previously generated tokens, forcing them to justify initial mistakes
  - Quick check question: If a model starts with "No" as its first token, what pressure does this create for the rest of its generation?

- Concept: Context-dependent reasoning vs. isolated evaluation
  - Why needed here: The key finding is that LMs can recognize their mistakes when claims are presented alone but not when generating within a context requiring consistency
  - Quick check question: Why might a model say "No" is prime has factors 13 and 745, but then correctly identify that 13 is not a factor when asked separately?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: What specific changes to pretraining or fine-tuning could reduce hallucination snowballing in language models?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that finetuning on data with backtracking or emphasizing reasoning chains before answers might help.
  - Why unresolved: The paper did not implement or test these training modifications, only suggesting them as potential avenues for future research.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing language models trained with backtracking data or different prompting emphasis to baseline models on the proposed datasets.

- Question: How does hallucination snowballing manifest in open-ended text generation tasks beyond question answering?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper hypothesizes that snowballing appears in open-ended text generation more broadly, where one mistake triggers more.
  - Why unresolved: The study focused on structured question-answering datasets with verifiable claims, not open-ended generation tasks.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies measuring error propagation and self-recognition rates in language models generating unconstrained text across multiple domains.

- Question: Would beam search decoding mitigate hallucination snowballing by maintaining alternative reasoning paths?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses beam search as a potential solution, noting that alternative sequences might maintain higher probability than those committing to incorrect answers.
  - Why unresolved: OpenAI API limitations prevented testing beam search, so its effectiveness remains theoretical.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments measuring hallucination rates and self-recognition when using beam search versus greedy decoding on the same datasets.

## Limitations
- The phenomenon may be specific to QA datasets and prompting strategies rather than representing a fundamental architectural limitation
- The verification setup relies on the model's ability to self-assess, which may not translate to practical applications requiring independent fact-checking
- The mechanism explanation remains somewhat speculative without direct experimental validation of the causal relationship

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The empirical finding that LMs can recognize their own incorrect claims when presented in isolation (67% for ChatGPT, 87% for GPT-4) is well-supported by the data and methodology.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that snowballing results from initial answer committal before explanation generation is supported by reasoning but lacks direct experimental validation of the causal mechanism.
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that transformers cannot solve sequential problems like primality testing in one step due to TC0 complexity limitations is based on theoretical work but not directly tested in this paper.

## Next Checks

1. **Mechanism Isolation Test**: Design an experiment where the model generates explanations before stating final answers to determine if this ordering eliminates snowballing, directly testing the committal hypothesis.

2. **Cross-Problem Generalization**: Apply the same methodology to arithmetic word problems and logical reasoning tasks to verify if snowballing occurs in domains beyond the three tested datasets.

3. **Computational Complexity Verification**: Implement and test transformer models on simplified sequential problems (e.g., parity checking) to empirically validate whether they fail in single steps due to computational limitations, not just pattern learning.
