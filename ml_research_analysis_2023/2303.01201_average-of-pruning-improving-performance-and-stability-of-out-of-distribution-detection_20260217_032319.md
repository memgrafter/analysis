# Average of Pruning: Improving Performance and Stability of Out-of-Distribution Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.01201
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01201
- Reference count: 40
- The paper proposes Average of Pruning (AoP) method that combines model averaging and pruning to improve OOD detection performance and stability during training.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses two key issues in OOD detection: instability during training and overfitting to in-distribution data. The authors propose Average of Pruning (AoP), a method that combines model averaging (to smooth the loss landscape and stabilize performance) with pruning (to eliminate overfitting by removing redundant features). The method shows consistent improvements across multiple datasets and architectures, improving OOD detection metrics while being simple to implement and applicable to existing OOD detection methods.

## Method Summary
AoP combines two complementary techniques: model averaging using Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) to stabilize training by smoothing the loss landscape, and iterative magnitude pruning based on the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis to eliminate overfitting by reducing redundant features. The method starts pruning from a specific epoch and applies exponential moving average to model parameters throughout training. It can be applied to existing OOD detection methods like MSP, ODIN, and Mahalanobis distance.

## Key Results
- ResNet-18 AUROC improves from 91.63 to 95.04 on CIFAR-10
- Consistent improvements across all tested datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny-ImageNet, ImageNet)
- Enhances both OOD detection and misclassification detection performance
- Simple to implement and compatible with existing OOD detection methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Model averaging smooths the loss landscape, making OOD detection performance more stable across training epochs.
- **Mechanism:** By averaging model parameters over time using exponential moving average, the method creates a flatter loss landscape that is less sensitive to small perturbations in weights, thereby reducing sharp performance variations in later training stages.
- **Core assumption:** The loss landscape of OOD detection metrics is inherently unstable with narrow optima that amplify small weight changes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "model averaging can help achieve a stable performance by smoothing the landscape"
  - [section] "model averaging could largely make the performance more stable" and "MA could be interpreted as approximating FGE ensemble"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations on landscape smoothing in OOD detection

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Pruning eliminates overfitting in OOD detection by reducing redundant and noisy features learned by over-parameterized models.
- **Mechanism:** Iterative magnitude pruning (LTH with weight rewinding) removes weights associated with features that are common across ID and OOD distributions but less discriminative, thereby reducing distribution overlap and improving OOD detection.
- **Core assumption:** Over-parameterization causes models to memorize training data and learn redundant features that hurt OOD detection performance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "pruning is certified to eliminate the overfitting by eliminating redundant features"
  - [section] "increasing the sparsity of model by pruning could eliminate it based on theoretical analysis" and "pruning [13] (e.g, LTH) also helps OOD detection by alleviating overﬁtting"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations on pruning improving OOD detection

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The combination of model averaging and pruning creates synergistic effects that improve both stability and OOD detection performance beyond what either method achieves alone.
- **Mechanism:** Model averaging provides stability while pruning reduces overfitting; together they address both identified problems in OOD detection training dynamics, creating a robust final model.
- **Core assumption:** The two problems (instability and overfitting) are independent and can be addressed by separate mechanisms that combine effectively.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Combining the two issues above, we summarize our method as Average of Pruning (AoP)"
  - [section] "Combining model averaging and pruning, we propose Average of Pruning (dubbed AoP) to boost OOD detection performance"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations on combined approach

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  Training pipeline: Standard classification training → Iterative magnitude pruning (LTH) → Model averaging (SWA) → OOD detection evaluation
  Key components: Exponential moving average tracker, pruning scheduler, OOD scoring function wrapper
  Data flow: In-distribution data → Model training → Sparse model generation → Averaged model creation → OOD detection evaluation

- **Critical path:**
  1. Train initial dense model with standard classification loss
  2. Apply iterative magnitude pruning (20% per iteration, 9 iterations total)
  3. Maintain exponential moving average of model parameters throughout training
  4. Apply OOD detection scoring function to averaged model
  5. Evaluate performance using AUROC, AUPR, FPR95 metrics

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Sparsity level vs OOD detection performance: Higher sparsity improves OOD detection up to a point, then degrades
  - Averaging window vs stability: Longer windows provide more stability but may smooth out important performance improvements
  - Pruning frequency vs computational cost: More frequent pruning provides better results but increases training time

- **Failure signatures:**
  - OOD detection performance drops sharply when pruning removes too many critical weights
  - Instability reappears if averaging window is too short or if learning rate is too high during averaging phase
  - Performance plateaus if pruning doesn't remove enough redundant features

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Train ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 with standard MSP baseline, measure AUROC/AUPR/FPR95 on LSUN
  2. Apply AoP to same setup, compare performance improvements across all metrics
  3. Vary sparsity level (60%, 80%, 90%) to find optimal pruning level for OOD detection

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the performance gains of AoP scale with increasing dataset complexity or model depth?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows AoP works on CIFAR-10/100, Tiny-ImageNet, and ImageNet, but doesn't analyze scaling behavior.
- Why unresolved: The experiments use a limited range of dataset sizes and model depths, so it's unclear if AoP's effectiveness diminishes or improves with more complex tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying dataset complexity (e.g., larger image resolutions, more classes) and model depth (e.g., deeper ResNets, transformers) while measuring AoP's relative improvement over baselines.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between the degree of sparsity achieved through pruning and the robustness of OOD detection?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows sparsity helps OOD detection but doesn't establish a precise mathematical relationship between sparsity level and detection performance.
- Why unresolved: While empirical results show improved OOD detection with pruning, there's no theoretical framework linking the amount of pruning to detection robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Mathematical analysis connecting sparsity metrics to OOD detection performance metrics, potentially extending the Gaussian feature distribution analysis to pruned networks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does AoP perform on other detection tasks beyond OOD and misclassification, such as adversarial example detection?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper only evaluates AoP on OOD detection and misclassification detection tasks.
- Why unresolved: The method's ability to improve model stability and reduce overfitting could benefit other detection tasks, but this hasn't been tested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying AoP to adversarial detection tasks and comparing performance against specialized adversarial detection methods.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the optimal combination of model averaging and pruning parameters for different architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses fixed parameters (t0=100 for averaging, 20% pruning per iteration) across all experiments without optimization.
- Why unresolved: The parameters that work well for ResNet-18 may not be optimal for other architectures like transformers or smaller models like MobileNet.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies systematically varying averaging decay rates and pruning percentages across different architectures to find optimal combinations.

## Limitations

- The paper lacks direct empirical evidence for the claimed mechanisms, specifically no landscape analysis demonstrating that model averaging actually smooths the loss surface
- The claim that pruning eliminates overfitting in OOD detection lacks theoretical backing or ablation studies showing why pruning specifically helps OOD detection beyond general regularization effects
- The improvements could potentially be attributed to other factors such as regularization effects from sparsity or implicit ensemble benefits from the averaging process

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The empirical improvements in OOD detection metrics (AUROC, AUPR, FPR95) are well-documented and reproducible across multiple datasets and architectures
- **Medium Confidence**: The stability improvements during training are observed but the causal mechanism (landscape smoothing) is not directly validated
- **Low Confidence**: The claim that pruning eliminates overfitting in OOD detection lacks theoretical backing or ablation studies showing why pruning specifically helps OOD detection beyond general regularization effects
