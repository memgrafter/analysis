# Can discrete information extraction prompts generalize across language models?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.09865
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09865
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: AutoPrompt outperforms manual prompts but prompts learned on one model show performance drop when used with another model; mixed-model training produces more generalizable prompts

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether prompts generated by the AutoPrompt algorithm for extracting information from one language model can be effectively used with other models. The authors confirm that AutoPrompt outperforms manual and semi-manual prompts on slot-filling tasks but find that prompts optimized for one model show degraded performance when transferred to different models. To address this limitation, they propose a mixed-training approach that uses two different language models during prompt induction, which results in prompts that generalize better across model architectures. The study provides insights into the properties of more generalizable prompts, including higher proportions of real English words and more uniform information distribution across tokens.

## Method Summary
The paper uses the AutoPrompt algorithm with gradient-guided search to generate discrete prompts for slot-filling tasks on the T-ReX dataset (a filtered subset of LAMA). The standard AutoPrompt approach trains prompts using a single model for both generation and evaluation. The proposed mixed-training method instead uses two different language models: a generator that proposes candidate prompts and an evaluator that selects the best ones. The study evaluates prompt performance using micro-averaged accuracy (precision@1) and analyzes prompt properties including semantic overlap with manual prompts, real-word ratio, robustness to token shuffling, and information distribution across tokens.

## Key Results
- AutoPrompt significantly outperforms manual and semi-manual prompts in the slot-filling task
- Prompts trained on one language model show performance degradation when used with different models
- Mixed-training with two different LMs produces prompts that generalize better across model architectures
- More generalizable prompts contain a higher proportion of existing English words and have more uniform information distribution across tokens

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Mixing multiple language models during prompt induction leads to more generalizable prompts.
- Mechanism: Using different LMs for generation and evaluation phases prevents overfitting to a single model's quirks and captures more general properties shared across models.
- Core assumption: Different LMs have different tokenization schemes and statistical quirks but share common English language properties.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We introduce a way to induce prompts by mixing language models at training time that results in prompts that generalize well across models."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Better-generalizing prompts contain more real English words rather than subword concatenations.
- Mechanism: Mixed-training prompts produce token sequences that are more likely to be existing English words, helping them transfer better across models with different tokenization schemes.
- Core assumption: Prompts composed of non-word sequences may tokenize differently across models, leading to brittleness.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "the more general prompts include a larger proportion of existing English words"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Better-generalizing prompts distribute information more evenly across tokens and are less sensitive to token order.
- Mechanism: Prompts that pack information at specific positions or rely heavily on token order may overfit to one model's statistical quirks, while more evenly distributed prompts generalize better.
- Core assumption: LMs may not capture genuine syntactic rules and instead exploit statistical co-occurrence quirks of specific models.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "have a less order-dependent and more uniform distribution of information across their component tokens"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: AutoPrompt generation (generator model) -> prompt evaluation (evaluator model) -> T-ReX slot-filling task evaluation -> prompt analysis
- Critical path: Generate candidate prompts → evaluate candidates → select best prompt per relation → test on held-out data → analyze generalization properties
- Design tradeoffs: Discrete prompts vs continuous prompts (discrete are more interpretable and can be used out-of-the-box, continuous may perform better but require model access)
- Failure signatures: Poor generalization when prompts are used on different LMs than those they were trained on; prompts containing non-word sequences that tokenize differently across models
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify AutoPrompt outperforms manual and semi-manual prompts on same-source-and-target setup
  2. Test AutoPrompt prompt generalization by using prompts trained on one LM and testing on another LM
  3. Implement mixed-training AutoPrompt using two different LMs for generation and evaluation phases

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific properties of mixed-training prompts lead to better generalization across language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies some differences but explicitly states it's unclear if these differences are causally connected to generalization
- Why unresolved: While differences are observed, causation hasn't been established through controlled experiments
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments that manipulate specific properties and measure their impact on generalization performance

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the order of tokens in prompts matter for all language models, or is this property model-dependent?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds mixed-training prompts are more robust to shuffling but doesn't explore model-specific sensitivities
- Why unresolved: Limited exploration of whether different models have varying sensitivities to token order
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic testing of token shuffling effects across a wider range of language models

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the generalization of discrete prompts be improved by optimizing for properties identified in the analysis during the prompt induction process?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests this as a future direction but doesn't implement or test the idea
- Why unresolved: This approach hasn't been implemented or tested yet
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments that incorporate these properties as objectives during prompt induction

## Limitations
- Generalization scope limited to different model architectures, not explored for different data distributions or language domains
- Analysis lacks statistical testing to determine significance of observed differences
- No quantification of trade-off between source-model performance and cross-model generalization

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: 
- AutoPrompt outperforms manual and semi-manual prompts on slot-filling task
- Prompts trained on one model show performance degradation when used on different models

**Medium Confidence**:
- Mixed-training with two different LMs produces more generalizable prompts
- Better-generalizing prompts contain more real English words

**Low Confidence**:
- Better-generalizing prompts have more uniform information distribution across tokens
- Token order dependence affects generalization

## Next Checks
1. Apply appropriate statistical tests to verify whether differences in real-word ratio, semantic overlap, and robustness metrics between single-model and mixed-training prompts are statistically significant across all tested relations.

2. Systematically measure the accuracy drop when using mixed-training prompts on their original source models compared to single-model prompts, and analyze the Pareto frontier between source performance and cross-model generalization.

3. Test the mixed-training prompts on language models from different training regimes or domains (e.g., multilingual models, domain-specific models) that were not included in the original training set to evaluate true generalization capability beyond the tested architecture families.
