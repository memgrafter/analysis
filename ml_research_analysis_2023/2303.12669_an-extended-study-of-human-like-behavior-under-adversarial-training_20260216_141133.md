# An Extended Study of Human-like Behavior under Adversarial Training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.12669
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12669
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Adversarial training consistently increases shape bias and human-like behavior across architectures and norms

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how adversarial training affects neural networks' human-like behavior, specifically their tendency to classify objects by shape rather than texture. Through extensive experiments across multiple architectures (ResNets, WideResNets, Transformers) and training regimes (ℓ2 and ℓ∞ adversarial training), the study reveals that adversarial training consistently increases shape bias and improves generalization to out-of-domain data with similar spectral distributions to clean training data. Transformers show superior human-like behavior compared to CNNs, often surpassing human performance on certain tasks. The research also provides a frequency-based explanation for these phenomena, showing that adversarial training shifts models toward low-frequency information processing.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates pre-trained ImageNet models (ResNet-18/50, WideResNet-50-2, XCiT-S/M/L12, ConvMixer-768-32) with and without adversarial training using ℓ2 and ℓ∞ norms. Models are tested on 12 out-of-distribution datasets including color, contrast, eidolon transformations, false-color, frequency-based corruptions, rotation, uniform noise, edge, silhouette, sketch, stylized, and cue-conflict tasks. Performance metrics include accuracy on clean and OOD data, cue-conflict scores measuring shape versus texture bias, and consistency with human predictions. Frequency spectrum analysis using FFT power spectra compares spectral distributions between clean and corrupted data.

## Key Results
- Adversarial training consistently increases shape bias across all tested architectures and norms
- Transformers exhibit more human-like behavior than CNNs, often surpassing human performance on certain tasks
- Adversarial training improves OOD performance on datasets with similar spectral distributions to clean data while harming performance on datasets with divergent spectra

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adversarial training shifts neural networks from texture-based to shape-based decision making by desensitizing high-frequency information
- Mechanism: During adversarial training, models are exposed to perturbations that primarily affect high-frequency components. To maintain robustness, the network learns to rely more on low-frequency features, which encode global shape information rather than local texture details
- Core assumption: High-frequency information corresponds to texture details while low-frequency information corresponds to shape information
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Humans identify objects by shape, while neural nets mainly employ texture cues"
  - [section] "adversarial examples can attack such models by slightly altering the image in these frequency bands"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for this specific frequency-texture/shape relationship
- Break condition: If texture information at low frequencies is sufficient for classification, the shape bias shift may not occur

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adversarial training improves generalization to out-of-distribution data when the spectral distribution of the OOD data is similar to the clean training data
- Mechanism: Adversarial training adds samples with similar spectral distributions to the original training data, improving robustness to perturbations within this spectral space. When OOD data shares this spectral distribution, the model can generalize better
- Core assumption: The spectral distribution of adversarial examples closely matches the clean training data
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Adding more training samples from the original spectral distribution can harm the generalization to other diverging spectral distributions"
  - [section] "some OOD categories have power spectra that are quite similar to the spectra of the original data"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for this specific spectral generalization mechanism
- Break condition: If the OOD data has a significantly different spectral distribution from both clean and adversarial training data

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Transformers exhibit more human-like behavior than CNNs because they inherently process information in a way that emphasizes global features over local texture details
- Mechanism: Transformers process patches of the image as tokens, which may naturally emphasize global structure and shape information over local texture details compared to CNNs' convolutional operations
- Core assumption: Patch-based processing in Transformers leads to more global feature extraction than local convolution operations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Transformers exhibit more human-like behavior than CNNs, often surpassing human performance on certain tasks"
  - [section] "the clean ConvMixer which contains no self-attention but patch-embeddings, shows also an increased cue-conflict"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for this specific architectural difference mechanism
- Break condition: If CNNs with patch-based architectures show similar human-like behavior as Transformers

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why does adversarial training degrade performance on high-frequency corrupted datasets (e.g., high-pass, phase scrambling) but improve shape bias?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that adversarial training consistently improves shape bias but causes a significant drop in accuracy on high-frequency corrupted datasets like high-pass, phase scrambling, and power equalization
- Why unresolved: The paper provides a frequency-based explanation for why adversarial training shifts models towards shape bias, but doesn't fully explain the mechanism behind the performance degradation on high-frequency corrupted datasets
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring the impact of adversarial training on models with varying frequency sensitivities, or ablation studies isolating the effects of different frequency bands during training

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific architectural differences between CNNs and Transformers contribute to Transformers' superior performance on out-of-distribution data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that Transformers generally outperform CNNs on out-of-distribution benchmarks and are more human-like in their reasoning, but doesn't identify the exact architectural features responsible for this advantage
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates the performance gap, it doesn't isolate which architectural elements (e.g., self-attention, patch embeddings, depth) are most critical for OOD generalization
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic architectural ablation studies comparing CNNs and Transformers with varying design choices, or controlled experiments isolating specific architectural components

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the frequency spectrum of adversarial perturbations during training affect the resulting model's frequency sensitivity and generalization capabilities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses how adversarial training shifts models towards low-frequency information and away from high-frequency texture cues, but doesn't explore how the frequency characteristics of training perturbations influence this shift
- Why unresolved: The paper establishes a correlation between adversarial training and frequency sensitivity, but doesn't investigate how different perturbation frequency profiles during training affect the final model behavior
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the frequency characteristics of adversarial perturbations during training and measuring their impact on model performance across different frequency domains

## Limitations

- The study relies on pre-trained checkpoints without full control over training hyperparameters
- Human baseline performance across all tested tasks is not fully characterized, making direct comparisons challenging
- The exact mechanism by which frequency domain properties translate to shape versus texture recognition remains incompletely understood

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Adversarial training consistently increases shape bias across all architectures tested. The spectral analysis methodology is sound and the comparison between OOD performance and spectral similarity shows clear patterns
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that Transformers exhibit more human-like behavior than CNNs is supported by the data, but the underlying mechanism (patch-based processing) lacks direct experimental validation. The comparison to human performance, while showing Transformers surpassing humans on some tasks, needs additional controls for human baseline variability
- **Low confidence**: The assertion that adversarial training alone is insufficient for fully human-like reasoning is based on incomplete coverage of potential architectural and training modifications that could enhance human-like behavior

## Next Checks

1. Conduct ablation studies on patch size and token processing in Transformers to isolate architectural contributions to human-like behavior
2. Systematically vary adversarial training budgets and measure their impact on both spectral distribution and OOD performance across multiple spectral distance thresholds
3. Establish more rigorous human performance baselines across all OOD datasets, particularly for cue-conflict tasks where human shape bias is assumed
