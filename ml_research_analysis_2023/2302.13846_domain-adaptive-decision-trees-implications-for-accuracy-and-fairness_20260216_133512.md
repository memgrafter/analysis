# Domain Adaptive Decision Trees: Implications for Accuracy and Fairness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.13846
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13846
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: DADT improves accuracy and fairness when covariate shift assumptions are met or approximately met

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of deploying machine learning models trained on source data to target populations with different attribute distributions, a scenario known as domain adaptation. The authors propose domain-adaptive decision trees (DADT), an in-processing method that adjusts the information gain split criterion using outside knowledge of the target population's attribute distribution. This allows the model to adapt to covariate shifts without requiring labeled target data. Experiments on the ACSPublicCoverage dataset, using US state pairs to simulate source-target domain shifts, demonstrate that DADT improves both accuracy and fairness metrics (demographic parity and equal opportunity) compared to standard decision trees when covariate shift assumptions are met or approximately met.

## Method Summary
The authors propose DADT, which modifies the standard decision tree algorithm by adjusting the information gain split criterion using target domain attribute distribution knowledge. The method embeds target domain knowledge into probability estimates used for calculating entropy and information gain during tree training. By adjusting probabilities to reflect the target distribution, the tree can better allocate splits to regions of the feature space that are more representative of the target population. The approach handles both full and partial target domain knowledge through linear combinations of source and target probability estimates, with a dynamic weighting parameter that adjusts based on the availability of target information.

## Key Results
- DADT improves both accuracy and fairness metrics compared to standard decision trees
- The method is particularly effective when target domain knowledge is available for pairs or triples of variables
- Results show improvement in demographic parity and equal opportunity metrics across various source-target state pairs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: DADT improves accuracy and fairness by adjusting the information gain split criterion using target domain attribute distribution knowledge.
- **Mechanism**: The method embeds target domain knowledge into probability estimates used for calculating entropy and information gain during tree training. By adjusting probabilities to reflect the target distribution, the tree can better allocate splits to regions of the feature space that are more representative of the target population.
- **Core assumption**: Covariate shift holds, meaning the conditional distribution of the class given the attributes remains constant between source and target domains, while only the marginal distribution of attributes changes.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "proposes an in-processing step that adjusts the information gain split criterion with outside information corresponding to the distribution of the target population."
  - [section 2.3] "When training any classifier, the key assumption is that the 𝑃 probability distribution is the same for the data used for growing the decision tree... Under covariate shift (1) this assumption does not hold."
- **Break condition**: If covariate shift does not hold, the method's effectiveness diminishes as the conditional distribution changes between domains.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: DADT's partial knowledge approach allows effective adaptation even with limited target domain information.
- **Mechanism**: By linearly combining source and target probability estimates using a tuning parameter, the method can leverage partial target domain knowledge. The weight is set proportional to the contribution of source domain information when target knowledge is incomplete.
- **Core assumption**: Partial knowledge of the target attribute distribution is available and can be estimated from external sources like cross-tabulation data.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.1.1] "We extend ˆ𝑃 (𝑋 = 𝑥 |𝜑) to the case that ˆ𝑃𝑇 (𝑋 = 𝑥 |𝜑) is not directly available in the target domain knowledge... by affine combinations for discrete and continuous attributes using the source domain knowledge."
  - [section 4.3.1] "The weight 𝛼 in (9)–(10) is set dynamically as the proportion of attributes in 𝜑 which are not in 𝜑 ′."
- **Break condition**: If the available partial knowledge is too sparse or unrepresentative, the linear combination may not provide sufficient adaptation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: DADT's fairness improvements stem from better representation of demographic groups in the target domain during training.
- **Mechanism**: By adjusting the tree structure to better reflect the target population's attribute distribution, the model can achieve more equitable treatment of different demographic groups. This is measured using demographic parity and equal opportunity metrics.
- **Core assumption**: Fairness metrics are sensitive to the representation of different groups in the training data and can be improved by better aligning the model with the target population.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Results show an improvement in fairness with the use of DADT."
  - [section 4.3.3] "Similarly, in Fig. 3 (right) we observe likewise results for DADT concerning the fairness metrics of demographic parity (DP) and equal opportunity (EOP)."
- **Break condition**: If fairness disparities are due to factors other than data distribution shifts, such as inherent bias in the labeling process, DADT may not fully address them.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Decision tree classifier with modified information gain calculation -> Target domain knowledge integration module -> Fairness evaluation module (for demographic parity and equal opportunity) -> Partial knowledge handling mechanism

- **Critical path**:
  1. Receive source training data and target domain attribute distribution knowledge
  2. Initialize decision tree with standard parameters
  3. During tree growth, adjust probability estimates using target domain knowledge
  4. Calculate modified information gain for split decisions
  5. Evaluate model performance on accuracy and fairness metrics
  6. If partial knowledge is used, dynamically adjust weights based on available information

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Full target domain knowledge provides best performance but may be unrealistic; partial knowledge is more practical but less effective
  - Linear combination of source and target probabilities balances between adaptation and stability
  - The choice of attribute for probability estimation in the absence of full target knowledge can impact results

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Accuracy degradation when covariate shift assumption does not hold
  - Minimal improvement in fairness metrics if disparities are not due to distribution shifts
  - Instability in model performance with very sparse or noisy partial target domain knowledge

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train a standard decision tree on source data and test on target data without any adaptation to establish baseline performance
  2. Implement DADT with full target domain knowledge to measure maximum potential improvement
  3. Test DADT with partial target domain knowledge (e.g., only pairs or triples of variables) to assess the impact of limited information

## Limitations
- The method's effectiveness relies heavily on the covariate shift assumption, which may not hold in all real-world scenarios
- The paper only tests DADT on one dataset (ACSPublicCoverage), limiting generalizability to other domains and data distributions
- The performance with very sparse partial target domain knowledge is not extensively explored

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The mechanism of adjusting information gain with target domain knowledge is theoretically sound and well-explained
- **Medium confidence**: The empirical results show improvements, but the sample size (50 state pairs) may limit generalizability
- **Low confidence**: The long-term stability of fairness improvements and the method's performance on different types of domain shifts (e.g., conditional shift) are not evaluated
