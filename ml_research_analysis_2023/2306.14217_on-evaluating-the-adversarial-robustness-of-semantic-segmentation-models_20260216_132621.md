# On Evaluating the Adversarial Robustness of Semantic Segmentation Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.14217
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14217
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Models trained with 50% adversarial examples exhibit gradient obfuscation; using only adversarial examples during training produces significantly more robust segmentation models.

## Executive Summary
This work systematically evaluates adversarial robustness in semantic segmentation, revealing that many prior robustness claims are overstated due to gradient obfuscation. The authors propose novel internal representation attacks (CIRA, CIRA+) and demonstrate that using only adversarial examples during training (100% batch) produces more genuine robustness than mixed batches (50%). Their evaluation methodology, which combines diverse high-iteration attacks, shows that robust models like CIRA+AT-3 and PGD-AT-100 require only 0.015 ℓ∞ perturbation norm to achieve 99% pixel error, compared to near-zero for standard models.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates semantic segmentation robustness using PSPNet on Cityscapes with images normalized to [0,1]. Key methodological innovations include internal representation attacks (CIRA, CIRA+) that target the backbone's final feature layer via cosine similarity maximization with random vectors, and adversarial training using only adversarial examples (no clean images). The attack suite includes PGD, CIRA, CIRA+, PAdam, ALMAProx, DAG, and PDPGD with up to 120 iterations. Models are trained with varying adversarial batch compositions (50% vs 100%) and evaluated using both bounded attacks (mIoU reduction) and minimum perturbation attacks (ℓ∞ norm to achieve target pixel error).

## Key Results
- PGD-AT-100 requires only 0.015 ℓ∞ perturbation norm for 99% pixel error vs near-zero for standard models
- CIRA+AT-3 achieves highest clean mIoU while maintaining strong robustness
- 50% adversarial batch training produces gradient obfuscation, failing against diverse attacks
- Internal representation attacks (CIRA, CIRA+) expose vulnerabilities missed by output-space attacks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Combining diverse, high-iteration gradient-based attacks reveals gradient obfuscation in supposedly robust models. When multiple uncorrelated attacks (PGD, CIRA, CIRA+) are applied with aggressive parameters, they expose regions of the input space where the model's gradients fail to provide meaningful adversarial directions, making the model appear robust to single attacks but vulnerable to others.

### Mechanism 2
Attacking internal representations (CIRA) bypasses surface-level gradient defenses and finds deeper vulnerabilities. By targeting the backbone's final feature representation with a random vector in cosine similarity space, the attack forces the model to produce features that maximally diverge from normal ones, exploiting the fact that internal representations may not be as robustly defended as output logits.

### Mechanism 3
Adversarial training with only adversarial examples (100% batch) yields more genuine robustness than mixed batches (50%). Pure adversarial training forces the model to learn decision boundaries that are stable under worst-case perturbations, whereas mixed batches allow gradient obfuscation to develop because the model can rely on clean examples during updates.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Adversarial attacks in image classification (FGSM, PGD, C&W)
  - Why needed here: Semantic segmentation inherits attack frameworks from classification but extends them to per-pixel outputs. Understanding classification attacks is prerequisite to adapting them.
  - Quick check question: How does the PGD attack update rule differ from FGSM, and why is this important for segmentation?

- Concept: Semantic segmentation output space (H×W×C softmax maps)
  - Why needed here: The loss function and attack objectives must be computed over this multi-dimensional output. Misunderstanding this space leads to incorrect attack implementations.
  - Quick check question: In a segmentation network with 20 classes, what is the shape of the output tensor and how is the final mask extracted?

- Concept: Cosine similarity as an optimization objective
  - Why needed here: CIRA attacks rely on maximizing cosine similarity between perturbed features and random vectors. This requires understanding of vector geometry in high dimensions.
  - Quick check question: Why is cosine similarity preferred over L2 distance when attacking internal representations?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Backbone (e.g., ResNet, HRNet) -> Feature extractor -> Segmentation head (ASPP, PPM) -> Per-pixel classification -> Loss function -> Cross-entropy over softmax outputs -> Adversarial attack module -> Perturbation generator -> Training loop -> Mixed/clean batch selection, optimizer

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass: Input → Backbone → Head → Output
  2. Loss computation: Cross-entropy between output and ground truth
  3. Backward pass: Gradient computation for attack or training
  4. Attack step: Perturb input using gradient and projection
  5. Update weights: Apply adversarial examples in training

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Attack strength vs. computational cost (more iterations = stronger but slower)
  - Batch composition (50% adversarial may reduce overfitting but increase obfuscation)
  - Internal vs. output attacks (CIRA cheaper but may miss surface-level defenses)

- Failure signatures:
  - High mIoU on clean data but near-zero on any attack → gradient obfuscation
  - CIRA succeeds where PGD fails → model overfits to PGD-style gradients
  - Large perturbations needed for 99% pixel error → lack of robustness

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Apply PGD(6) and PGD(120) to a normally trained model; compare mIoU drop.
  2. Replace PGD with CIRA(120) on the same model; observe attack success.
  3. Train a model with 100% adversarial examples using CIRA+; evaluate against all attacks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does using 100% adversarial examples during training consistently produce more robust models than 50% adversarial batches across different architectures and datasets? The study only tested PSPNet on Cityscapes with a limited set of models; the observed gradient obfuscation might be architecture- or dataset-specific.

### Open Question 2
How does the choice of internal representation layer affect the effectiveness of CIRA-style attacks? The authors attack only the final backbone feature representation, but note that previous work attacked multiple layers, raising the question of whether targeting intermediate layers yields stronger attacks.

### Open Question 3
Is there an optimal trade-off between the number of iterations and learning rate in CIRA+ adversarial training that maximizes robustness without overfitting? The authors used 2- and 3-iteration CIRA+ with fixed learning rate 0.01, but did not explore the hyperparameter space systematically.

## Limitations

- Gradient Obfuscation Detection: The exact mechanisms by which 50% adversarial batches create gradient obfuscation remain unclear, making it difficult to predict when and why obfuscation occurs.

- Internal Representation Attack Generalization: CIRA and CIRA+ attacks may be specific to PSPNet architecture or Cityscapes domain, as the random vector initialization could produce varying results across different backbones.

- Computational Constraints: The evaluation uses 120-iteration attacks and multiple diverse methods, which are computationally expensive and may not scale to larger models or datasets.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The core finding that 100% adversarial training produces more genuine robustness than 50% adversarial batches is well-supported by empirical results.
- Medium Confidence: The claim that CIRA and CIRA+ attacks reveal deeper vulnerabilities is supported but could benefit from ablation studies explaining why internal attacks succeed where output attacks fail.
- Low Confidence: The assertion that "trying harder pays off" with more attack iterations lacks theoretical justification and is only empirically observed.

## Next Checks

2. Evaluate the robust models (PGD-AT-100, CIRA+AT-3) on out-of-domain datasets to test whether adversarial training provides genuine robustness or overfits to Cityscapes-specific patterns.
