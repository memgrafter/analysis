# Oversampling Higher-Performing Minorities During Machine Learning Model Training Reduces Adverse Impact Slightly but Also Reduces Model Accuracy

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.13933
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13933
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Oversampling high-performing minority applicants slightly reduces ML model adverse impact but also slightly reduces accuracy

## Executive Summary
This study investigated whether oversampling high-performing minority applicants in machine learning training data reduces adverse impact in automated screening models. Using real-world job application data (N = 2,501), researchers manipulated training data selection ratios for Black and Hispanic applicants across 9,702 models. The results showed that adjusting training data adverse impact ratios only slightly improved model fairness, reducing adverse impact by 0.04-0.16 across different predictor types, while also slightly reducing model accuracy (by 0.006-0.027). The effects were similar whether oversampling real or synthetic observations, and whether equalizing sample sizes.

## Method Summary
The study used 2,501 job applicants from a service industry with self-report survey scores, interview transcripts, historical screening decisions, and demographics. Researchers conducted stratified 3-fold cross-validation across 11 ML algorithms and 14 predictor sets (6 text mining techniques plus self-reports). They manipulated training data adverse impact ratios through undersampling/oversampling at levels of 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, and 1.4, testing both real (bootstrap) and synthetic (SMOTE) oversampling techniques. The study examined effects on adverse impact ratios and model accuracy across different predictor combinations.

## Key Results
- Oversampling high-performing minorities reduced adverse impact by only 0.04-0.16 across predictor types
- Model accuracy decreased by 0.006-0.027 when reducing adverse impact through oversampling
- Real and synthetic oversampling provided similar effects; equalizing sample sizes had minimal additional benefit

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Oversampling high-performing minority applicants slightly reduces ML model adverse impact by adjusting training data subgroup selection ratios.
- Mechanism: By increasing the number of minority applicants who passed screening in the training data, the model learns that minority status is less predictive of failure, thereby reducing the model's tendency to discriminate.
- Core assumption: The training data subgroup differences reflect historical discrimination rather than true performance differences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "removing adverse impact from training data only slightly reduced ML model adverse impact"
  - [section]: "oversampling high-performing minorities... reduces ML model adverse impact slightly"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence in corpus about minority performance patterns.
- Break condition: If minority underperformance reflects actual performance differences rather than discrimination, oversampling would reduce model accuracy without meaningfully improving fairness.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Equalizing subgroup representation in training data has minimal additional effect beyond adjusting adverse impact ratios.
- Mechanism: When minority success rates are already adjusted via oversampling, further equalizing sample sizes provides little additional benefit because the model has already learned to de-emphasize subgroup differences.
- Core assumption: The primary driver of model bias is subgroup selection ratio differences, not absolute sample size differences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "equal representation had very minor, positive effects on ML model AI ratios"
  - [section]: "equalizing sample sizes tended to exert minimal, positive effects beyond manipulating training data AI ratios"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers focus on oversampling for class imbalance, not subgroup balance.
- Break condition: If differential representation creates systematic prediction errors independent of selection ratios, equalizing Ns could provide additional benefit.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Synthetic oversampling provides similar effects to real observation oversampling for reducing adverse impact.
- Mechanism: Both techniques increase minority success examples in training data, allowing the model to learn that minority status doesn't predict screening failure, regardless of whether examples are real or synthetic.
- Core assumption: Synthetic observations generated by SMOTE are sufficiently similar to real observations to provide equivalent learning signals.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "oversampling real or synthetic observations provided similar effects"
  - [section]: "the effects were similar regardless of whether real or synthetically generated observations were oversampled"
  - [corpus]: Moderate - corpus includes papers on synthetic oversampling for class imbalance, but not specifically for subgroup fairness.
- Break condition: If synthetic observations create unrealistic patterns that the model overfits to, they may perform worse than real observation oversampling.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Raw data -> Oversampling transformation -> ML algorithm training -> Prediction generation -> Adverse impact ratio calculation -> Accuracy measurement
- Critical path: Raw data -> Oversampling adjustment -> ML algorithm training -> Prediction generation -> Adverse impact ratio calculation -> Accuracy measurement
- Design tradeoffs: More aggressive oversampling reduces adverse impact more but also reduces accuracy more; synthetic vs real oversampling offers similar fairness benefits but different implementation complexity.
- Failure signatures: No improvement in adverse impact despite oversampling suggests subgroup differences reflect actual performance differences; large accuracy drops suggest oversampling is removing valid predictive signal.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test different oversampling ratios (0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4) on a simple logistic regression model to establish baseline effects
  2. Compare real vs synthetic oversampling on the same model to verify similar effects
  3. Test equalizing sample sizes in addition to adjusting adverse impact ratios to measure marginal benefit

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would oversampling high-performing minorities affect ML model adverse impact in larger datasets with more predictors?

- What evidence would resolve it: Replication study with larger N (tens of thousands) and broader predictor set (including biodata, cognitive ability)

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does oversampling high-performing minorities affect ML model validity and predictive bias differently than adverse impact?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] authors note accuracy decreases but couldn't examine validity/predictive bias due to lack of workplace outcomes
- Why unresolved: Study focused on adverse impact and accuracy but lacked outcome data to assess validity and predictive bias
- What evidence would resolve it: Study with workplace performance data to examine relationships between oversampling, adverse impact, accuracy, validity, and predictive bias

### Open Question 3

- Basis in paper: [explicit] authors suggest addressing subgroup differences at societal level is most direct route to reducing adverse impact
- Why unresolved: This is a long-term societal change that can't be tested experimentally
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking changes in subgroup differences and adverse impact over time as societal interventions are implemented

## Limitations
- Study relies on proprietary self-report scales without providing specific items, making exact replication difficult
- Corpus contains only 8 relevant papers with zero citations, suggesting limited external validation
- Findings based on single service industry dataset, limiting generalizability to other contexts

## Confidence
- High confidence: The core finding that oversampling high-performing minorities slightly reduces adverse impact while also slightly reducing accuracy (supported by statistical analysis across 9,702 models)
- Medium confidence: The mechanism explanation for why oversampling works, as it relies on assumptions about historical discrimination in training data
- Low confidence: Generalizability to other industries or contexts given the single-dataset approach

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the core analysis using publicly available datasets with demographic information (e.g., COMPAS recidivism data) to test external validity
