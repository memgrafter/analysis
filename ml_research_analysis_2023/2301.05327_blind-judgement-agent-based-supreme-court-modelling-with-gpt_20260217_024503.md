# Blind Judgement: Agent-Based Supreme Court Modelling With GPT

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05327
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05327
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 5
- Key outcome: Multi-agent system achieves 60% accuracy predicting Supreme Court decisions, with individual justice models ranging from 65% to 50% accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a multi-agent system that simulates the decision-making of the US Supreme Court justices using separate GPT-2 models trained on each justice's authored opinions. The system takes in a case topic and has each justice model generate an opinion and vote, with votes tallied to reach a majority decision. Testing on 96 cases, the system achieved better-than-random accuracy of 60%, with accuracy correlating with justices' tendency to overturn precedent, suggesting conservative justices are more predictable.

## Method Summary
The authors trained nine separate GPT-2 models, one for each justice active around 2015, on their respective authored opinions from 2003-2016. They collected Supreme Court Database cases (1946-2021) and extracted written opinions (145MiB) from Supreme Court website (2003-2022). The system uses Python dictionary prompts with issue, topic, opinion, and decision fields. Models were trained for 30 epochs with learning rate 2e-4 and Adam optimizer, then tested on 96 withheld cases to evaluate accuracy.

## Key Results
- Multi-agent system achieved 60% overall accuracy on 96 test cases
- Individual justice model accuracies ranged from 65% to 50%
- Accuracy correlated with justices' tendency to overturn precedent (r ≈ 0.56)
- Conservative justices showed higher predictability in voting patterns

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Individual justice models capture unique decision-making patterns from their authored opinions
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning separate GPT-2 models on each justice's opinions allows the models to internalize distinct rhetorical and argumentative styles, which correlate with voting behavior
- Core assumption: A justice's authored opinions reflect their true decision-making patterns and ideological leanings
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We train nine separate models with the respective authored opinions of each supreme justice"
  - [section] "Collect all prompts generated in step 1 for each of the opinions (2003-2016) written by each justice"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence supporting this mechanism, but the training methodology described provides indirect support
- Break condition: If opinions are ghostwritten or heavily edited by clerks, the models may capture clerk patterns rather than justice patterns

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Multi-agent system improves accuracy by preventing cross-contamination of justice-specific patterns
- Mechanism: Separate models prevent one justice's reasoning patterns from influencing another's, maintaining distinct decision-making profiles
- Core assumption: Justice decision-making is sufficiently independent that combining models would dilute individual patterns
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "training multiple models separately prevents cross-contamination when studying multiple data sources"
  - [section] "train nine separate models with the respective authored opinions"
  - [corpus] Weak - the corpus doesn't directly address cross-contamination, but the methodology implies this concern
- Break condition: If justices collaborate extensively or share clerks, the independence assumption may be invalid

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Accuracy correlates with precedent-overturning frequency because conservative justices are more predictable
- Mechanism: Justices who rarely overturn precedent have more consistent decision patterns, making them easier for language models to predict
- Core assumption: Predictability of voting behavior correlates with frequency of overturning precedent
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "accuracy correlated with the justices' tendency to overturn precedent, suggesting conservative justices are more predictable"
  - [section] "We measure a moderate correlation (r ≈ 0.56) between simulated justice accuracy and the frequency with which each respective justice did not agree with the Court overruling or re-interpreting precedent"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence, but the correlation measurement provides supporting data
- Break condition: If conservative justices appear predictable but actually vote based on complex strategic considerations, the correlation may break down

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Case topic → nine GPT-2 justice models → nine opinions/decisions → majority vote calculation → final decision
- Critical path: Case topic → nine justice models → nine opinions/decisions → majority vote calculation → final decision
- Design tradeoffs: Separate models preserve justice-specific patterns but require more computational resources; single model would be more efficient but potentially less accurate
- Failure signatures: Inconsistent vote counts (not exactly nine), model timeouts, generation errors, invalid opinion formats
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test individual model generation with simple case topics to verify each justice model produces coherent opinions
  2. Run full system with a small test case set to verify majority vote calculation works correctly
  3. Compare accuracy of single-agent vs multi-agent system on a small validation set

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would the system's accuracy change if trained on a larger, more diverse dataset that includes opinions from lower courts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions "Larger Training Corpus" as a next step, suggesting that training on a broader corpus of American law could increase system accuracy.
- Why unresolved: The current model is trained only on opinions from the Supreme Court, which may limit its exposure to diverse legal arguments and precedents.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the system's accuracy after training on a dataset that includes lower court opinions and a wider range of legal cases.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the system's accuracy be improved by incorporating repetitive prompting strategies or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests "Improved Prompting" as a next step, mentioning that repetitive prompting strategies and RLHF could enhance the system's performance.
- Why unresolved: The current model uses a standard prompting approach, which may not fully leverage the potential of language models to avoid the long tail of token probabilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing the system's accuracy before and after implementing repetitive prompting strategies or RLHF.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How would the system perform on cases brought before the court after 2016, and would it predict the same rate of precedent overturning as the post-2016 Supreme Court?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes "Investigating Future Cases" as a next step, questioning how the Roberts IV court would fare with cases after 2016 and their rate of precedent overturning.
- Why unresolved: The system is trained on data up to 2016, and its ability to generalize to future cases and predict precedent overturning rates is untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the system on post-2016 cases and comparing its predictions of precedent overturning with actual outcomes.

## Limitations
- Accuracy ceiling and class imbalance: 60% accuracy represents modest improvement that may be partially explained by binary decision nature and class imbalance
- Temporal mismatch: Training data (2003-2016) vs testing period mismatch may introduce temporal drift in judicial behavior
- Narrow case selection: Only 96 test cases provide relatively small sample size for broad conclusions about model performance

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The basic methodology of using separate language models for each justice is technically sound and well-implemented
- **Medium confidence**: The correlation between accuracy and precedent-overturning frequency (r ≈ 0.56) suggests meaningful relationship
- **Low confidence**: The claim that "conservative justices are more predictable" based on this correlation is speculative and requires additional evidence

## Next Checks
1. **Temporal validation**: Test the system on cases from different time periods to assess whether models maintain accuracy across temporal shifts in judicial behavior and legal precedents
2. **Cross-validation analysis**: Perform k-fold cross-validation on the 96-case test set to better understand variance in model performance and determine if observed accuracy is stable
3. **Baseline comparison**: Implement and test simpler baseline approaches (such as majority class prediction or single-model approaches) to quantify actual contribution of multi-agent architecture beyond basic statistical advantages
