# OPD@NL4Opt: An ensemble approach for the NER task of the optimization problem

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.02459
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02459
- Reference count: 1
- Primary result: Achieved 93.3% micro-averaged F1 score, placing second in NL4Opt NER task

## Executive Summary
This paper presents an ensemble approach for the NL4Opt Named Entity Recognition (NER) competition task, which focuses on identifying semantic entities in optimization problem descriptions. The method combines pre-trained language models (BERT variants) with Conditional Random Fields (CRF), employing differential learning rates and adversarial training to enhance performance. A model ensemble via majority voting over multiple random seeds yields a strong 93.3% F1 score. The approach demonstrates that targeted strategies like adjusting CRF learning rates and adversarial training significantly improve entity recognition, particularly for challenging types like OBJ_NAME.

## Method Summary
The method fine-tunes pre-trained language models (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa variants) on the NL4Opt dataset with a CRF layer for sequence labeling. Key innovations include using a 100x higher learning rate for the CRF layer to accelerate task-specific learning, applying adversarial training via Fast Gradient Method (epsilon=1.0) to improve robustness, and creating an ensemble of models trained with different random seeds combined through majority voting on entity spans. The approach addresses the challenge of limited training data (<1000 samples) by leveraging PLM representations and ensemble techniques to reduce variance and improve generalization.

## Key Results
- Achieved 93.3% micro-averaged F1 score, placing second in the NER task
- DeBERTa-large model outperformed other PLM variants
- Differential learning rates (100x for CRF) improved convergence and performance
- Model ensemble via majority voting reduced variance and enhanced results

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Using different learning rates for the CRF layer improves fine-tuning effectiveness.
- Mechanism: Pre-trained language models are already optimized for general language tasks and require smaller learning rates to avoid destabilizing their learned representations. The CRF layer, however, is randomly initialized and needs a larger learning rate to learn task-specific patterns effectively.
- Core assumption: The CRF layer learns sequential dependencies in the NER task that are not captured by the PLM.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "In our experiment, we Increase the learning rate of CRF layer to 100 times that of PLMs."
  - [section] "If the leaning rate is too large, it may not converge well. But CRF layer need larger learning rate because it is not pre trained."
- Break condition: If the CRF layer becomes too dominant or the PLM weights degrade significantly, causing overall performance to drop.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adversarial training with Fast Gradient Method enhances model robustness and generalization.
- Mechanism: By adding perturbations to the input that maximize the adversarial loss, the model learns to be invariant to small input variations, improving its ability to handle noisy or slightly altered inputs during inference.
- Core assumption: The optimization problem descriptions may contain subtle variations or noise that the model should handle gracefully.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We implement this strategy with Fast Gradient Method(Goodfellow et al., 2014), which directly uses the gradient to compute the perturbation and augments the input with this perturbation to maximizes the adversarial loss."
  - [section] "adversarial training strategies to enhance the model generalization and robustness."
- Break condition: If the perturbation magnitude is too large, causing the model to focus on noise rather than meaningful patterns, or if it leads to overfitting on adversarial examples.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Model ensemble via majority voting reduces variance and improves overall performance.
- Mechanism: Training multiple models with different random seeds leads to different local minima. Combining their predictions through majority voting leverages the strengths of each model and mitigates individual weaknesses.
- Core assumption: Different models capture different aspects of the data distribution, and combining them leads to better coverage of the entity space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We train model with different random seeds. Given predictions from different random seeds, we use majority voting to generate the final prediction."
  - [section] "We convert the label sequences into entity spans to perform majority voting."
  - [section] "In this task, the number of samples in train dataset is less than one thousand, therefore, the performance of model fluctuates greatly when using different random seeds."
- Break condition: If the individual models are too similar (high correlation), the ensemble provides little benefit, or if the voting process introduces ambiguity in cases of ties.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-trained Language Model (e.g., BERT, DeBERTa) -> CRF layer -> Sequence labels -> Adversarial training (during training) -> Model ensemble (during inference)
- Critical path: Input text → PLM → CRF → Sequence labels → Adversarial training (during training) → Model ensemble (during inference)
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger PLMs provide better representations but are computationally expensive
  - Higher learning rates for CRF improve convergence but risk destabilizing PLM weights
  - More ensemble models reduce variance but increase inference time and complexity
- Failure signatures:
  - Overfitting: High training F1 but low validation F1
  - Underfitting: Low F1 on both training and validation sets
  - Mode collapse: Ensemble models produce very similar predictions
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train BERT+CRF baseline with standard learning rates to establish performance baseline
  2. Implement differential learning rates (100x for CRF) and compare performance
  3. Add adversarial training (FGM) with epsilon=1.0 and evaluate impact on robustness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different pre-trained language models perform on optimization problem NER compared to their performance on general NER tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares the performance of different pre-trained models (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa variants) on the optimization NER task and finds DeBERTa-large performs best.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't compare these results to general NER benchmarks or explain why optimization NER differs from general NER.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarking the same models on standard NER datasets and analyzing domain-specific challenges in optimization problem descriptions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between adversarial training intensity (epsilon parameter) and model performance for optimization NER?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses Fast Gradient Method with epsilon=1.0 but doesn't explore different epsilon values or their effects.
- Why unresolved: The authors fixed epsilon at 1.0 without exploring the parameter space or providing sensitivity analysis.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying epsilon values and measuring trade-offs between robustness gains and potential performance degradation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do differential learning rates for CRF layers affect convergence and performance across different optimization problem domains?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses 100x higher learning rate for CRF layer but doesn't test other ratios or analyze domain-specific effects.
- Why unresolved: The optimal ratio is assumed rather than experimentally validated, and no analysis of domain-specific impacts is provided.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing various learning rate ratios and performance analysis across the six different domains in the dataset.

## Limitations

- Limited training data (less than 1000 samples) raises concerns about generalization beyond the competition setting
- Differential learning rate strategy lacks theoretical grounding and sensitivity analysis
- Adversarial training uses fixed epsilon=1.0 without exploring the perturbation space or ablation studies
- Ensemble voting mechanism requires careful preprocessing for entity span handling, not fully detailed

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The ensemble approach improves performance over single models (proven by 93.3% vs individual model results)
- **Medium Confidence**: DeBERTa-large is the best-performing single model (based on competition results but lacks direct comparison with all alternatives)
- **Medium Confidence**: Differential learning rates benefit training (empirically observed but not systematically validated)
- **Medium Confidence**: Adversarial training enhances robustness (claimed but not empirically demonstrated through robustness tests)
