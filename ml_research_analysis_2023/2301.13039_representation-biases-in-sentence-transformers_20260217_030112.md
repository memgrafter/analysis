# Representation biases in sentence transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.13039
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13039
- Reference count: 21
- This paper investigates how sentence transformers encode semantic similarity, finding a strong "nominal-participant-set bias" where embedding similarity is driven primarily by shared noun participants rather than predicates or syntactic roles.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how state-of-the-art sentence transformers encode semantic similarity by constructing controlled sentence sets with varied lexical and syntactic structures. Through regression analysis of embedding similarities against linguistic features, the authors reveal that sentence transformers exhibit a strong "nominal-participant-set bias": embedding similarity is primarily determined by overlap in the set of noun participants (subjects and objects), while largely ignoring shared predicates, lengthy nominal modifiers, and syntactic-thematic roles. This bias contrasts sharply with vanilla BERT, which treats all tokens more equally. The findings suggest these biases likely reflect the nature of training data (such as QA pairs and Reddit conversations) and indicate limitations for tasks requiring fine-grained syntactic or semantic role knowledge.

## Method Summary
The study constructs synthetic sentence sets with controlled lexical and syntactic structures, then computes embeddings using sentence transformers (MPNET, DistilRoberta) and vanilla BERT. Pairwise cosine similarities between sentence embeddings are computed and regressed against binary predictors representing various linguistic properties (SameSubj, SamePred, SameObj, etc.). The regression coefficients reveal which properties drive embedding similarity. UMAP visualizations project embeddings into 2D space to visualize the effects of these properties. The experiments are replicated with alternative lexical items to confirm robustness of findings.

## Key Results
- Sentence transformers show strong nominal-participant-set bias: embedding similarity is primarily determined by shared noun participants (subjects and objects)
- STs largely ignore syntactic-thematic roles, treating subjects and objects interchangeably
- STs underweight predicates and lengthy nominal modifiers compared to lexical overlap
- These biases contrast with vanilla BERT, which treats all tokens more equally
- The bias pattern likely reflects training data characteristics (QA pairs, Reddit conversations)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Sentence transformers learn to prioritize entities and their co-occurrence patterns because their training objectives (contrastive learning, sentence-pair tasks) naturally reward matching sets of participants over matching predicates or syntactic structures. The models develop a bias toward entity-based representations that works well for many practical applications but fails to capture finer-grained semantic and syntactic distinctions.

## Foundational Learning

1. **Cosine similarity in embedding space**: Measures angular distance between vectors; why needed for quantifying semantic similarity, quick check: ranges from -1 to 1, with 1 being identical direction

2. **Syntactic vs. semantic roles**: Syntactic roles (subject, object) are structural positions; semantic roles (agent, patient) are functional meanings; why needed to understand what STs ignore, quick check: subject position doesn't always mean agent

3. **Regression analysis with binary predictors**: Statistical method to identify which features correlate with similarity scores; why needed to isolate effects of different linguistic properties, quick check: coefficient sign indicates direction of effect

4. **UMAP dimensionality reduction**: Projects high-dimensional embeddings to 2D while preserving local structure; why needed to visualize clustering patterns, quick check: preserves more global structure than t-SNE

5. **Contrastive learning objectives**: Training method that pulls similar pairs together and pushes dissimilar pairs apart; why needed to understand training data influence, quick check: used in models like MPNET

6. **Token averaging for embeddings**: Simple method to create sentence representations by averaging token embeddings; why needed as baseline comparison, quick check: ignores word order and syntax

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Synthetic sentence generation -> Embedding computation (STs/BERT) -> Cosine similarity calculation -> Regression analysis -> UMAP visualization

**Critical path**: Sentence generation → Embedding computation → Similarity calculation → Statistical analysis (this sequence is essential for causal interpretation)

**Design tradeoffs**: Controlled synthetic sentences provide clean experimental control but may not fully represent natural language complexity; regression analysis provides interpretable coefficients but may miss nonlinear relationships

**Failure signatures**: Poor regression fit (low R²) suggests uncontrolled lexical effects dominate; inconsistent coefficients across replications suggest instability in findings

## Limitations
- Synthetic sentence sets, while controlled, may not fully capture natural language complexity
- Analysis focuses on a specific subset of linguistic features rather than comprehensive coverage
- Study examines only three models, though patterns are consistent across them

## Confidence
- **High**: Core finding about nominal-participant-set bias and its contrast with BERT
- **Medium**: Proposed explanation linking bias to training data characteristics

## Next Checks
