# "It's a Match!" -- A Benchmark of Task Affinity Scores for Joint Learning

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks six task affinity scores to predict multi-task learning (MTL) performance, aiming to identify cooperative tasks for joint learning. The scores evaluated include taxonomical distance, input attribution similarity, representation similarity analysis, label injection (a novel method), gradient similarity, and gradient transference. Tested on the Taskonomy dataset across five Computer Vision tasks, the results show that no single affinity score strongly predicts MTL performance across all task pairs. However, label injection moderately correlates with MTL gains and successfully identifies the best partner task for most tasks, except for one challenging task (Normal). The study highlights the complexity of task affinity prediction due to dependencies on data, models, and hyperparameters, suggesting further research is needed for practical application.

## Method Summary
The study benchmarks six task affinity scores to predict multi-task learning (MTL) performance gains. These scores are evaluated on the Taskonomy dataset across five Computer Vision tasks using Xception-based models with hard parameter sharing for MTL. The affinity scores are categorized into three types: model-agnostic (taxonomical distance, input attribution similarity), STL-based (representation similarity analysis, label injection), and MTL-based (gradient similarity, gradient transference). The evaluation involves exhaustive MTL training for all task pairs, computing affinity scores, and analyzing their correlation with actual MTL performance gains. The study also examines the asymmetry of task cooperation and the impact of computational cost on score selection.

## Key Results
- No single affinity score strongly predicts MTL performance across all task pairs.
- Label injection moderately correlates with MTL gains and successfully identifies the best partner task for most tasks, except for the Normal task.
- Task cooperation is highly asymmetric, and symmetric scores may not capture this nuance.
- The predictive power of affinity scores is tightly coupled to the specific training conditions (data, models, hyperparameters) used in the experiment.

- Core assumption: The correlation between affinity scores and MTL performance gains is strong enough to guide task grouping decisions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does task complexity significantly influence task affinity predictions in MTL, particularly for complex tasks like Normal?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note that the Normal task consistently benefits others but is poorly assisted itself, and hypothesize this is due to its high complexity. They suggest incorporating task complexity metrics like Task2Vec could help address this.
- Why unresolved: The study did not incorporate task complexity metrics in its affinity scoring methodology, and Task2Vec is only defined for homogeneous tasks, not heterogeneous ones like in this study.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing a task complexity metric applicable to heterogeneous tasks and demonstrating its correlation with MTL gains, particularly for complex tasks like Normal.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the predictive power of task affinity scores be improved by considering higher-order task combinations beyond pairwise?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge their analysis is limited to pairwise task affinity scores and note that various effects may be at play when learning more than two tasks simultaneously, citing (Zhang, Hayes, and Kanan 2021).
- Why unresolved: The study only evaluated pairwise affinity scores, and extending to higher-order combinations would require new methodologies and computational resources.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and validating affinity scoring techniques for higher-order task combinations that correlate well with MTL gains across diverse task sets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do advanced MTL architecture designs with task interference mitigation strategies affect the relationship between task affinity scores and MTL performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note their MTL models use simple hard parameter sharing without interference mitigation, stating that tasks may be affine but still interfere during joint learning without such mechanisms.
- Why unresolved: The study used a basic MTL architecture, and the impact of more sophisticated architectures on affinity-performance relationships remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing task affinity scores' predictive power across different MTL architectures, including those with soft parameter sharing and gradient balancing techniques, on the same task sets.

## Limitations
- No single affinity score strongly predicts MTL performance across all task pairs, indicating fundamental limitations in current proxy methods.
- The study is limited to the Taskonomy dataset, Xception architecture, and specific training conditions, reducing generalizability.
- The computational cost of exhaustive MTL training for all task pairs (90 models) creates scalability concerns for broader application.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The experimental methodology and implementation are sound, with clear correlation analyses and ranking comparisons.
- Medium confidence: The claim that no single score strongly predicts MTL gains is well-supported, but the moderate success of label injection needs further validation.
- Low confidence: Generalizability to other datasets, architectures, and tasks beyond the five Computer Vision tasks tested.
