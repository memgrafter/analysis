# From MNIST to ImageNet and Back: Benchmarking Continual Curriculum Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.11076
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11076
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces two novel continual learning benchmarks, M2I and I2M, designed to address the limitations of existing evaluation protocols by providing multiple heterogeneous tasks from six image datasets with varying levels of complexity and quality. The benchmarks are structured according to curriculum learning principles, with tasks presented in increasing (M2I) and decreasing (I2M) order of complexity to evaluate whether current continual learning models can effectively leverage task ordering. The paper provides an extensive experimental evaluation using nine state-of-the-art continual learning strategies across both class-incremental and task-incremental learning scenarios. Results show that popular continual learning strategies yield sub-par performance and high levels of forgetting when challenged with these benchmarks, highlighting the need for more robust strategies that can handle complex real-world scenarios and effectively leverage curriculum task ordering.

## Method Summary
The benchmarks use six heterogeneous image datasets (MNIST, Omniglot, Fashion MNIST, SVHN, CIFAR10, TinyImageNet) treated as separate tasks, with each task containing 10 balanced classes (500 images per class). Models are trained incrementally on each task using Wide VGG9 and EfficientNet-b1 backbones for 50 epochs with SGD and cross-entropy loss. The evaluation compares nine continual learning strategies plus naive and cumulative baselines across both class-incremental and task-incremental scenarios, measuring performance using average accuracy, backward transfer, and forward transfer metrics.

## Key Results
- Popular continual learning strategies show sub-par performance and high forgetting rates when faced with heterogeneous, multi-domain tasks
- Direct curriculum (M2I) generally outperforms inverse curriculum (I2M), suggesting some benefit from task ordering but limited ability to leverage curriculum structure
- Larger model backbones (EfficientNet-b1 vs Wide VGG9) do not significantly improve performance of staple CL strategies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The benchmark design exposes forgetting by introducing heterogeneous datasets with varying complexity and quality.
- Mechanism: By ordering tasks from MNIST to TinyImageNet (M2I) and vice versa (I2M), the model encounters significant distribution shifts that challenge its ability to retain knowledge across domains.
- Core assumption: Models that perform well on homogeneous datasets will fail when exposed to heterogeneous, multi-domain tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The benchmarks are structured according to curriculum learning principles, with tasks presented in increasing (M2I) and decreasing (I2M) order of complexity to evaluate whether current continual learning models can effectively leverage task ordering."
  - [section] "Our benchmarks take this into consideration as they include very complex multi-domain real-world image classification such as TinyImagenet (harder classification), as well as handwritten digit recognition in MNIST (easier classification)."
  - [corpus] Found 25 related papers; weak evidence for direct mechanism confirmation in corpus.
- Break condition: If tasks are not sufficiently heterogeneous or the ordering does not create meaningful complexity gradients.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using balanced class sizes (500 images per class) ensures fair comparison of forgetting across tasks.
- Mechanism: By equalizing the number of samples per class, the benchmark isolates the learning setting and prevents class imbalance from confounding performance metrics.
- Core assumption: Class imbalance in standard benchmarks can artificially inflate or deflate performance metrics, making cross-task comparisons unreliable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "we balance class sizes by taking 500 images from each of them, for both the training and evaluation phases. By doing so, we isolate the learning setting and avoid typical issues that arise in imbalanced learning, which might undermine the analysis of the final results."
  - [abstract] "we provide the CL community with a rigorous and reproducible evaluation protocol for measuring the ability of a model to generalize and not to forget while learning."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence; mechanism is specific to this benchmark design.
- Break condition: If class balancing is not maintained or if dataset characteristics inherently prevent equal sampling.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The curriculum ordering (M2I vs I2M) tests whether models can leverage task similarity and ordering for better forward and backward transfer.
- Mechanism: Models exposed to increasing complexity (M2I) may build foundational skills that aid in learning more complex tasks, while those exposed to decreasing complexity (I2M) may leverage complex knowledge to simplify learning easier tasks.
- Core assumption: Learning on a curriculum of task complexities helps with domain generalization and catastrophic forgetting.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we additionally structure our benchmarks so that tasks are presented in increasing and decreasing order of complexity – according to a curriculum – in order to evaluate if current CL models are able to exploit structure across tasks."
  - [section] "To properly consider curriculum learning, our benchmark considers curriculum learning by devising a task order according to their difficulty."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence; mechanism is novel to this benchmark.
- Break condition: If task ordering does not create meaningful differences in transfer performance or if models cannot detect task boundaries.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Six datasets (MNIST, Omniglot, Fashion MNIST, SVHN, CIFAR10, TinyImageNet) → each treated as separate task → images resized to 64x64 with 3 channels → nine CL strategies + baselines → evaluation metrics (ACC, BWT, FWT)
- Critical path: 1) Preprocess datasets to balance classes and resize images. 2) Implement task ordering (M2I or I2M). 3) Train models incrementally on each task. 4) Evaluate performance on all tasks after each new task is learned. 5) Aggregate results using the defined metrics.
- Design tradeoffs: Using 500 images per class ensures fairness but may not reflect real-world data scarcity. Resizing all images to 64x64 simplifies processing but may lose detail. Treating each dataset as a separate task maximizes heterogeneity but may not reflect all real-world scenarios.
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on later tasks despite good initial performance suggests catastrophic forgetting. High accuracy on only the most recent task indicates overfitting. Inability to leverage task ordering (similar performance in M2I and I2M) suggests the model cannot exploit curriculum structure.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run a Naive (fine-tuning) baseline on M2I to establish a forgetting baseline.
  2. Test a replay-based method (e.g., Replay) on I2M to see if rehearsal mitigates forgetting in reverse curriculum.
  3. Compare EWC and MAS on both M2I and I2M to evaluate regularization methods under curriculum learning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does increasing model backbone size significantly improve continual learning performance in complex, heterogeneous task scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors tested both Wide VGG9 and EfficientNet-b1 backbones and found that larger models did not significantly improve performance of staple CL strategies.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested two specific architectures; it's unclear if other larger architectures or different scaling approaches might yield better results.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with additional larger architectures (e.g., Vision Transformers) and systematic scaling studies comparing parameter efficiency across different architectures in heterogeneous task scenarios.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can curriculum learning be optimally leveraged in continual learning to maximize both forward and backward transfer?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors found that while M2I (direct curriculum) generally outperformed I2M (inverse curriculum), current CL strategies could only partially benefit from task similarity and curriculum ordering.
- Why unresolved: The paper tested only two fixed curriculum orders; optimal curriculum design for maximizing transfer remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing multiple curriculum designs (random orderings, difficulty-based orderings, similarity-based orderings) and measuring their impact on transfer metrics across various CL strategies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What evaluation metrics best capture model behavior in curriculum learning scenarios with heterogeneous task complexity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors noted that current metrics like average accuracy may be biased when MNIST appears as the first task in curriculum learning, and clearer metrics are needed to interpret behavior across different task orderings.
- Why unresolved: The paper used standard CL metrics but acknowledged their limitations in curriculum contexts without proposing alternatives.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of new metrics that account for task difficulty progression, task similarity, and curriculum-specific evaluation scenarios.

## Limitations
- The benchmark's controlled environment with balanced class sizes may not reflect real-world data scarcity and imbalance scenarios
- The paper doesn't benchmark against parameter isolation methods, leaving uncertainty about whether observed forgetting is architecture-dependent
- Limited ablation studies make it difficult to isolate the effects of curriculum ordering from the inherent difficulty of heterogeneous multi-domain learning

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The benchmark construction methodology (balanced class sizes, standardized preprocessing, clear task definitions) is reproducible and well-documented.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that popular CL strategies yield "sub-par performance" is supported by results, though the paper doesn't benchmark against all relevant baselines (e.g., parameter isolation methods).
- **Low confidence**: The mechanism by which curriculum ordering should help is theoretically plausible but not empirically validated in this work—the paper shows ordering creates differences but doesn't prove the mechanism works as intended.

## Next Checks
