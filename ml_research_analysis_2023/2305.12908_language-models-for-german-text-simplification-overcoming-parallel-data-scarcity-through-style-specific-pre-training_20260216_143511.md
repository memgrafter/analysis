# Language Models for German Text Simplification: Overcoming Parallel Data Scarcity through Style-specific Pre-training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12908
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12908
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: Pre-training on unaligned Easy Language data reduces required parallel data while improving downstream simplification performance

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of German text simplification by proposing a two-step approach that leverages monolingual Easy Language data to overcome the scarcity of parallel simplification corpora. The authors fine-tune language models on German Easy Language corpora to adapt them to the style characteristics of simplified German, then use these adapted models as decoders in a sequence-to-sequence architecture for text simplification. Their results demonstrate that this approach reduces the number of trainable parameters, making less parallel data sufficient for training while improving simplification quality measured by SARI scores.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a two-stage approach for German text simplification. First, they fine-tune pre-trained language models (GPT-2) on monolingual German Easy Language corpora to adapt them to the specific style characteristics. Second, they use these fine-tuned language models as decoders in a sequence-to-sequence architecture with an encoder (mBART), training only the encoder-decoder cross attention on parallel data. This approach significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to traditional sequence-to-sequence models. The models are evaluated on German Wikipedia articles and WikiNews, using automatic metrics (SARI, ROUGE-L, BLEU) and human evaluation.

## Key Results
- Models outperform baseline on SARI metric for text simplification
- Fine-tuned language models adapt to Easy Language style characteristics (short sentences, simplified vocabulary, line breaks)
- Pre-training on unaligned data reduces required parallel data while improving downstream task performance
- Reduced trainable parameters make less parallel data sufficient for training

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning language models on monolingual Easy Language data adapts them to the specific style characteristics of Easy Language.
- Mechanism: The fine-tuning process exposes the model to numerous examples of Easy Language text, allowing it to learn and internalize the style conventions, such as short sentences, simplified vocabulary, and line breaks after each sentence.
- Core assumption: The monolingual Easy Language data is representative of the target style and contains sufficient variety to capture the key characteristics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "First, we fine-tuned language models on a corpus of German Easy Language, a specific style of German."
  - [section 4.1] "To fine-tune the models, we used a NVIDIA A100 GPU. We trained for one epoch, with a learning rate of 1e−4, a weight decay of 0.01, and a batch size of eight together with a gradient accumulation of four."
- Break condition: If the monolingual data is too limited in scope or does not capture the full range of Easy Language characteristics, the fine-tuned model may not generalize well to new examples.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using fine-tuned language models as decoders in a sequence-to-sequence architecture reduces the number of trainable parameters, making less parallel data sufficient for training.
- Mechanism: By leveraging pre-trained language models that already understand Easy Language style, the system only needs to train the encoder-decoder cross attention, significantly reducing the number of parameters that need to be updated during training.
- Core assumption: The pre-trained language models have a good understanding of Easy Language and can generate text in that style with minimal additional training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Moreover, with the style-specific pre-training, we reduced the number of trainable parameters in text simplification models. Hence, less parallel data is sufficient for training."
  - [section 4.2] "As our language models already output text in the desired style, no further training of the decoder was necessary. Therefore, we only trained the encoder-decoder cross attention to align the encoding of the complex articles with our language models."
- Break condition: If the pre-trained language models do not adequately capture the Easy Language style, the reduced training may lead to poor simplification quality.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Pre-training on unaligned data can improve performance on downstream tasks while reducing the required parallel data.
- Mechanism: By learning from a large amount of monolingual Easy Language data, the language models develop a strong understanding of the target style. This knowledge can then be leveraged in downstream tasks like text simplification, even with limited parallel data.
- Core assumption: The knowledge gained from pre-training on monolingual data is transferable to the downstream task and can compensate for the lack of parallel data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our results indicate that pre-training on unaligned data can reduce the required parallel data while improving the performance on downstream tasks."
  - [section 5.5] "Our models outperform the baseline on the SARI metric; however, they fall behind when comparing ROUGE-L and BLEU scores."
- Break condition: If the downstream task requires a different skill set than what is learned from the monolingual data, the pre-training may not provide significant benefits.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Monolingual Easy Language data -> Pre-trained language models (e.g., GPT-2) -> Fine-tuning process -> Sequence-to-sequence architecture -> Encoder (e.g., mBART) -> Decoder (fine-tuned language models) -> Encoder-decoder cross attention

- Critical path:
  1. Collect and preprocess monolingual Easy Language data.
  2. Fine-tune pre-trained language models on the monolingual data.
  3. Set up the sequence-to-sequence architecture with the fine-tuned models as decoders.
  4. Train the encoder-decoder cross attention on parallel data.
  5. Evaluate the model on downstream tasks.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using pre-trained models vs. training from scratch
  - Fine-tuning on monolingual data vs. parallel data
  - Reducing trainable parameters vs. model capacity

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on downstream tasks
  - Inability to generate text in the target style
  - Catastrophic forgetting of the original language understanding

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune a pre-trained language model on monolingual Easy Language data and evaluate its perplexity on Easy Language and standard German texts.
  2. Use the fine-tuned model as a decoder in a sequence-to-sequence architecture and train the encoder-decoder cross attention on a small parallel dataset.
  3. Compare the performance of the fine-tuned model with a baseline model on a text simplification task, using metrics such as SARI, ROUGE-L, and BLEU.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to a single German Easy Language corpus (Netz für Alle), raising questions about generalizability to other Easy Language domains and languages
- Impact of monolingual pre-training on downstream performance is not thoroughly investigated across different model sizes, pre-training data sizes, and downstream tasks
- The paper lacks a detailed error analysis of generated simplified texts, which would be valuable for improving the approach

## Confidence
- Generalization to other Easy Language corpora and languages: Medium
- Impact of pre-training data size and model size on performance: Low
- Transferability of monolingual pre-training benefits across different downstream tasks: Low

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate the approach on multiple German Easy Language corpora and other languages to assess generalizability and robustness.
