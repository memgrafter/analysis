# LexGPT 0.1: pre-trained GPT-J models with Pile of Law

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.05431
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05431
- Reference count: 32
- Developed GPT-J-based models pretrained on legal-domain Pile of Law dataset

## Executive Summary
LexGPT 0.1 introduces a series of GPT-J-based language models pretrained on the legal-domain Pile of Law dataset. The models (6B, 1.6B, and 456M parameters) were designed to serve as foundation models for future legal NLP applications. Using domain-specific tokenizers and lower learning rates, the models were pretrained for 350k steps on approximately 60B tokens. While the pretraining process achieved stable training losses, downstream evaluation on LexGLUE benchmark tasks revealed performance below state-of-the-art results, with micro-F1 scores of 83.5% and 83.9% on LEDGAR, and significantly lower accuracies on CaseHOLD.

## Method Summary
The study developed GPT-J-based language models pretrained on the legal-domain Pile of Law dataset. Three model sizes were created: 6B, 1.6B, and 456M parameters. The pretraining used domain-specific tokenizers and a lower learning rate than standard GPT-J pretraining. Models were trained for 350k steps on approximately 60B tokens from the Pile of Law dataset. The fine-tuning approach emphasized a "No Code" methodology intended to make the models accessible to legal professionals without requiring deep technical expertise. Downstream evaluation was conducted on the LexGLUE benchmark tasks, with a focus on LEDGAR and CaseHOLD datasets.

## Key Results
- Pretraining achieved stable training losses over 350k steps on ~60B tokens
- Fine-tuned LexGPT 456M achieved micro-F1 of 83.5% and macro-F1 of 72.4% on LEDGAR
- Fine-tuned LexGPT 1.6B achieved micro-F1 of 83.9% and macro-F1 of 74.0% on LEDGAR
- CaseHOLD accuracies were 49.6% (456M) and 27.6% (1.6B), significantly below state-of-the-art of 75.4%

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The study demonstrates that domain-specific pretraining on legal corpora can create foundation models for legal NLP tasks. The use of domain-specific tokenizers helps capture legal terminology and document structures more effectively than general-purpose tokenizers. The "No Code" fine-tuning approach aims to democratize access to these models for legal professionals by avoiding complex architectural modifications. However, the mechanism's effectiveness is limited by the significant performance gap compared to state-of-the-art models, suggesting that either the pretraining data, model architecture, or fine-tuning approach requires optimization for competitive performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can downstream task performance be enhanced without modifying the model or its source code?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The study highlights that the fine-tuned LexGPT models achieved notably lower performance than state-of-the-art results, and the authors explicitly state this as a research topic for future exploration.
- Why unresolved: The study tested a "No Code" approach to fine-tuning, which limited the ability to modify the model architecture or add task-specific layers, resulting in lower performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments showing whether adding task-specific layers or modifying the model architecture improves performance beyond the "No Code" fine-tuning approach.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can specialized training data formats, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), improve the performance of LexGPT models in downstream tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors suggest that future research could explore whether providing training data in CoT format can enhance the effectiveness of classifiers under the "No Code" condition.
- Why unresolved: The study did not experiment with CoT-formatted training data, and its potential impact on model performance remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of LexGPT models trained with standard versus CoT-formatted data on downstream tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would LexGPT models outperform state-of-the-art models on the original LEDGAR dataset in a multi-label classification setting?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the original LEDGAR dataset is designed for multi-label classification, but the study simplified it to a single-label task, leaving the performance in the original setting unexplored.
- Why unresolved: The study did not test LexGPT models on the multi-label version of LEDGAR, and the authors explicitly state this as a question for future investigation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating LexGPT models on the multi-label LEDGAR dataset and comparing their performance to state-of-the-art models.

## Limitations
- Significant performance gap compared to state-of-the-art models on both LexGLUE and CaseHOLD benchmarks
- Limited downstream evaluation scope focusing only on two tasks despite positioning as foundation model
- "No Code" fine-tuning approach remains untested at scale with actual legal professionals

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The pretraining methodology, dataset composition, and model architecture specifications are clearly documented and reproducible. The training losses and pretraining process are stable and verifiable.

**Medium confidence**: The downstream evaluation methodology is sound, but the limited task coverage and suboptimal performance relative to SOTA create uncertainty about the model's practical utility.

**Low confidence**: The "No Code" fine-tuning claims for legal professionals lack empirical validation beyond basic implementation, and no user studies or usability assessments are provided.
