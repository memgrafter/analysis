# The Edge of Orthogonality: A Simple View of What Makes BYOL Tick

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.04817
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04817
- Reference count: 40
- This work shows that when using a linear predictor, the optimal predictor in BYOL is close to an orthogonal projection, and proposes four new closed-form predictor variants that outperform standard linear trainable predictor BYOL.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a mathematical framework for understanding self-predictive unsupervised learning methods like BYOL by interpreting the linear predictor as an approximate orthogonal projection on the span of online latent features. The authors show that the exponential moving average (EMA) and stop-gradient operations in BYOL serve as an efficient orthonormalization mechanism, equivalent to a computationally trivial retraction step in Riemannian SGD on the Stiefel manifold. Based on this insight, they propose four new closed-form predictor variants that achieve better performance than standard trainable predictors while being more data- and compute-efficient.

## Method Summary
The authors propose four closed-form predictor variants (NE, Visser, NS, and Stiefel) that approximate orthogonal projections without requiring gradient-based training. These predictors use matrix square root iterations (Visser and Newton-Schulz methods) or Stiefel manifold optimization to maintain approximate orthogonality. The predictors are applied to online latent features, and the resulting representations are trained using the standard BYOL loss against EMA-updated target features. The ridge regularization parameter α and iteration counts are tuned for numerical stability and performance.

## Key Results
- Closed-form predictors outperform standard linear trainable predictor BYOL at 100 and 300 epochs on ImageNet top-1 linear accuracy
- The predictors achieve better data and compute efficiency compared to standard trainable predictors
- The NE predictor with α=0.3 achieves 66.9% top-1 accuracy at 100 epochs, outperforming the standard predictor's 66.3%
- The closed-form predictors maintain approximate orthogonality throughout training, enabling stable convergence

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The linear predictor in BYOL functions as an approximate orthogonal projection on the span of online latent features.
- Mechanism: BYOL's optimal linear predictor minimizes reconstruction loss, which corresponds to projecting target latents onto the subspace spanned by online latents. This projection is orthogonal when the predictor is optimal, and the EMA and stop-gradient operations help enforce this orthogonality implicitly.
- Core assumption: The predictor is close to optimal (linear regression solution) and the EMA operation with τ≃1 provides quasi-symmetry.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "when using a linear predictor, the optimal predictor is close to an orthogonal projection"
  - [section] "the optimal predictor is an orthogonal projection... BYOL objective for optimal linear predictor can be seen as a reconstruction loss"

- Break condition: If the EMA parameter τ is too small, the predictor moves away from the orthogonal manifold and may cause collapse.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The BYOL architecture implements a computationally trivial retraction step for Riemannian SGD on the Stiefel manifold.
- Mechanism: The EMA operation on representations and the linear predictor together act as an approximate orthonormalization step, equivalent to a retraction in Riemannian optimization. This keeps the predictor on or near the Stiefel manifold of orthogonal matrices without expensive QR decomposition.
- Core assumption: The optimal linear predictor is an orthogonal projection, and the EMA update provides quasi-symmetry.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "this framework demonstrates the crucial role of the exponential moving average and stop-gradient operator in BYOL as an efficient orthonormalization mechanism"
  - [section] "the coupling of the EMA and predictor make them an approximate orthonormalization step for the latents... computationally free Stiefel retraction"
  - [corpus] Weak - no corpus papers discussing Riemannian geometry perspective on BYOL
- Break condition: Without the EMA operation or with improper τ values, the predictor drifts from orthogonality and performance degrades.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The predictor's rank grows during training, starting as a low-rank projection and approaching the identity matrix, enabling progressive feature space expansion.
- Mechanism: Early in training, the predictor has low rank, projecting onto a small subspace. As training progresses, more eigendirections are uncovered, expanding the subspace until the predictor becomes full rank (identity) at convergence, preventing collapse.
- Core assumption: The predictor is close to optimal throughout training, and the BYOL loss effectively goes to zero at convergence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we show that when using a linear predictor, the optimal predictor is close to an orthogonal projection"
  - [section] "the eigenvalues of the rescaled predictor converge to either 0 or 1... the rank of the predictor grows throughout training"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus papers don't discuss predictor rank evolution
- Break condition: If training is too short or learning rate too high, the predictor may not fully expand to identity, limiting performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Encoder: ResNet-50 backbone producing representations
  - Projector: 2-layer MLP mapping to latent space
  - Predictor: Closed-form matrix function (NE, Visser, NS, Stiefel variants)
  - EMA: Exponential moving average with parameter τ on both predictor and target network
  - Loss: L2 distance between stop-gradiented target projection and online predictor output

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass through online encoder+projector → Zθ
  2. Apply closed-form predictor to Zθ → Pθ(Zθ)
  3. Forward pass through target encoder+projector → Z′ξ
  4. Compute loss between Pθ(Zθ) and Z′ξ
  5. Backpropagate only through online branch
  6. Update predictor via EMA after loss computation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Predictor complexity vs. iteration count: More complex predictors (NS2) need fewer iterations but are computationally heavier per iteration
  - Ridge parameter α: Provides numerical stability but may reduce expressiveness if too large
  - EMA parameter τ: Smaller values provide more stability but may slow learning; larger values risk collapse

- Failure signatures:
  - Rank deficiency in predictor covariance: Indicates numerical instability, try increasing ridge parameter
  - Divergence in training loss: May indicate predictor too far from orthogonality, reduce τ or add ridge
  - Poor performance despite convergence: Predictor may not be exploring enough eigendirections, try different predictor variant

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare NE predictor with varying ridge parameters α ∈ {0.0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9} at 100 epochs
  2. Test NS vs NS2 iteration count sensitivity with n ∈ {3, 5, 7, 9} at 100 epochs
  3. Evaluate Stiefel predictor performance across τ ∈ {0.99, 0.995, 0.999} at 300 epochs

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of ridge parameter alpha affect the performance of closed-form predictors across different training durations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper evaluates predictor performance at 100 and 300 epochs with varying alpha values.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows performance trends with alpha but does not establish an optimal alpha value that generalizes across different training durations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies showing optimal alpha values for different training durations, and analysis of how alpha interacts with predictor type and training length.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the Riemannian interpretation of BYOL be extended to explain the behavior of other self-supervised learning methods?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper establishes a Riemannian perspective for BYOL and its predictors, suggesting potential applicability to other methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on BYOL specifically and does not explore the broader applicability of this interpretation to other self-supervised learning frameworks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies applying the Riemannian framework to other self-supervised methods and analyzing similarities and differences in their behavior.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of different EMA parameters on the stability and performance of closed-form predictors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using EMA to stabilize predictor numerics and provides specific EMA values for different predictors.
- Why unresolved: While the paper uses specific EMA values, it does not explore the full range of EMA parameters or their effects on predictor stability and performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies varying EMA parameters across different predictors and training durations to identify optimal settings.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis assumes optimal linear predictors, but in practice, closed-form predictors are approximate and may deviate from true orthogonality
- The proposed closed-form predictors haven't been extensively validated across diverse downstream tasks beyond linear evaluation
- Computational efficiency gains are demonstrated but not comprehensively benchmarked against standard BYOL implementations

- **Medium confidence**: The Riemannian optimization framework as an efficient orthonormalization mechanism
