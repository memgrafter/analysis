# Runtime Analyses of Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms in the Presence of Noise

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10259
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10259
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: SEMO tolerates O(1/n) noise with single evaluations but only O(1/n²) with reevaluation

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes how evolutionary algorithms handle noise in multi-objective optimization, focusing on the Simple Evolutionary Multi-objective Optimizer (SEMO) on the OneMinMax benchmark. The study compares two evaluation strategies: evaluating each solution only once versus reevaluating solutions whenever needed. Surprisingly, SEMO can tolerate noise rates up to O(1/n) when evaluating only once, matching the noise-free runtime, while reevaluation drastically reduces tolerance to O(1/n²). This highlights fundamental differences between single and multi-objective optimization in noisy environments.

## Method Summary
The paper conducts theoretical runtime analysis of SEMO on the OneMinMax benchmark with one-bit prior noise. Two strategies are compared: (1) evaluating each solution only once and storing the noisy objective values, and (2) reevaluating solutions in each iteration. The expected number of iterations required to find the complete Pareto front is analyzed as a function of noise rate p. The OneMinMax problem is used where all solutions are Pareto-optimal, making the Pareto front a simple linear structure {(k, n-k) | k ∈ [0..n]}.

## Key Results
- SEMO tolerates noise rates up to O(1/n) with single evaluations, maintaining O(n² log n) runtime
- With reevaluation, SEMO's noise tolerance drops to O(1/n²) before runtime becomes super-polynomial
- SEMO's implicit diversity mechanism contributes to its robustness against noise
- This robustness contrasts sharply with single-objective algorithms that degrade faster with noise

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The SEMO algorithm can tolerate noise rates up to O(1/n) when evaluating solutions only once, due to its implicit diversity mechanism that maintains multiple Pareto-optimal solutions.
- Mechanism: By maintaining a population of solutions covering different regions of the Pareto front, SEMO reduces the impact of any single noisy evaluation. When one solution's objective value is corrupted by noise, other solutions in the population can still guide the search toward the true Pareto front.
- Core assumption: The noise is bounded at O(1/n), so the probability of a solution being misclassified as non-Pareto-optimal is low enough that the diversity mechanism can compensate.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "MOEA's implicit diversity mechanism contributes to its robustness against noise"
  - [section]: "This robustness is surprising given that comparable single-objective algorithms degrade much faster with noise"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence in corpus papers
- Break condition: When noise rate exceeds O(1/n), the probability of losing correct Pareto solutions becomes too high for the diversity mechanism to compensate.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reevaluating solutions each iteration drastically reduces noise tolerance to O(1/n²), as it removes the protective effect of stored objective values.
- Mechanism: Each reevaluation introduces new noise that can move solutions across Pareto frontiers unpredictably. The algorithm loses the stability of stored values and must constantly adapt to new noisy rankings.
- Core assumption: Each reevaluation is independent and identically distributed, so repeated evaluations compound the uncertainty rather than averaging it out.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "when all solutions are reevaluated in each iteration, then any noise rate p = ω(log(n)/n²) leads to a super-polynomial runtime"
  - [section]: "We prove that when all solutions are reevaluated in each iteration, then any noise rate p = ω(log(n)/n²) leads to a super-polynomial runtime"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence in corpus papers
- Break condition: When noise rate exceeds O(log(n)/n²), the expected progress toward the Pareto front becomes negative.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The OneMinMax benchmark's structure makes it particularly amenable to noise-tolerant optimization because all solutions are Pareto-optimal and the frontier is a simple linear structure.
- Mechanism: Since every solution is on the Pareto front, the algorithm doesn't need to distinguish between "good" and "bad" solutions - it only needs to explore the space efficiently. The linear structure means that small mutations can systematically explore the frontier.
- Core assumption: The problem structure allows the algorithm to make consistent progress even with noisy evaluations, as long as it maintains diversity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Since any solution to the OneMinMax benchmark is Pareto optimal, the Pareto front of this problem is {(k, n-k) | k ∈ [0..n]}"
  - [section]: "the multi-objective OneMinMax problem is naturally harder (we aim at a population containing exactly one solution for each Pareto optimum)"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence in corpus papers
- Break condition: When the problem structure becomes more complex (non-linear Pareto fronts, non-Pareto optimal solutions), the noise tolerance would likely decrease.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Population storage -> Mutation operator -> Selection mechanism -> Reevaluation strategy -> Elimination function
- Critical path: Initialize population → Select parent → Mutate → Evaluate (once or reevaluate) → Update population → Check termination
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Evaluating once: Faster but risk of sticking with faulty values
  - Reevaluating: More robust to individual errors but much slower due to repeated evaluations
  - Population size: Larger populations provide more diversity but increase computational cost
- Failure signatures:
  - Runtime becomes super-polynomial when noise exceeds tolerance bounds
  - Population gets stuck in local regions of the Pareto front
  - Excessive time spent reevaluating the same solutions
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement SEMO with single evaluation on noise-free OneMinMax to verify O(n² log n) runtime
  2. Add one-bit noise at rate p=1/n and compare runtime to theoretical bound
  3. Implement SEMO with reevaluation and test at various noise rates to find the threshold where runtime becomes super-polynomial

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: Does the robustness of SEMO to noise observed in this paper extend to other MOEAs beyond SEMO?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest their findings on SEMO's noise robustness "hold for a broad range of algorithms and benchmarks" but call for further analysis on other MOEAs like the global SEMO.
  - Why unresolved: This paper only analyzes SEMO. The authors explicitly note that results may not extend without further analysis.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Mathematical runtime analysis of other MOEAs (e.g., NSGA-II, global SEMO) on OneMinMax or other benchmarks under similar noise conditions.

- Question: How does SEMO's performance degrade with noise rates between O(1/n) and O(log(n)/n²)?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper establishes upper bounds for p ≤ O(1/n) and lower bounds for p ≥ O(log(n)/n²), leaving a gap in between.
  - Why unresolved: The analysis only provides bounds at the extremes, not the intermediate region.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Precise runtime bounds or empirical measurements for noise rates in the intermediate region.

- Question: How would posterior noise models affect SEMO's robustness compared to the prior noise model studied?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors speculate that "for posterior noise, different from what our result suggests for prior noise, reevaluating search points is the better strategy."
  - Why unresolved: The paper only analyzes prior noise; posterior noise is mentioned as a speculation.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Runtime analysis of SEMO under posterior noise models, comparing reevaluation vs. non-reevaluation strategies.

## Limitations

- The analysis relies on the specific problem structure of OneMinMax where all solutions are Pareto-optimal, which may not generalize to more complex multi-objective problems
- Exact constants determining noise tolerance thresholds (α and β) are not specified, making precise reproduction challenging
- The elimination function elim in the reevaluation strategy is referenced but not fully detailed in the provided context

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The fundamental distinction between single and multi-objective noise tolerance mechanisms is well-supported by the theoretical framework presented
- **Medium Confidence**: The O(1/n) and O(1/n²) noise tolerance thresholds appear well-derived from the mathematical analysis, though exact constants remain unspecified
- **Low Confidence**: Generalization of these results to more complex multi-objective problems beyond OneMinMax is not established in this work
