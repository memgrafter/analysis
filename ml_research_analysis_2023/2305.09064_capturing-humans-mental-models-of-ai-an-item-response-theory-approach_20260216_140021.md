# Capturing Humans' Mental Models of AI: An Item Response Theory Approach

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09064
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09064
- Reference count: 40
- Participants perceive AI agents as significantly more capable than other humans

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces an Item Response Theory (IRT) framework for modeling humans' mental models of AI agents, extending cognitive science work on human-to-human mental models. The authors conduct experiments where participants assess their own and others' (either AI or human) performance on trivia questions. Key findings include that participants perceive AI agents as significantly more capable than other humans, perceptions of AI abilities are more correlated across different topics than for humans, and mental models develop over time but don't fully converge to actual performance, especially for AI agents. The framework enables analysis of how people differentiate between self and other agents' abilities, with implications for human-AI collaboration.

## Method Summary
The study collected data from 203 Amazon Mechanical Turk participants who answered 16 sets of 12 trivia questions across 4 topics (History of Art, Video Games, Cities, Math), providing self-assessments and other-assessments of either AI agents or human partners. The authors applied IRT framework with multidimensional ability vectors for 4 trivia topics, using Bayesian inference with Stan to compare one-dimensional vs. multidimensional structures and test three hierarchical model structures (undifferentiated, differentiated by ability, fully differentiated) for self-assessment and other-assessment relationships.

## Key Results
- Participants perceive AI agents as significantly more capable than other humans
- Perceptions of AI abilities are more correlated across different topics than for humans
- Mental models develop over time but don't fully converge to actual performance, especially for AI agents

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: IRT provides a principled way to model humans' mental models of AI by capturing latent abilities and problem difficulties from observed assessment data.
- Mechanism: The IRT framework maps observed scores to latent parameters (ability, difficulty) using probabilistic functions, allowing estimation of both self-perceived and other-perceived performance.
- Core assumption: The relationship between observed scores and latent parameters can be modeled as a noisy function that preserves ordinal relationships.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We present an extensive experimental dataset...and introduce a theoretical model-based framework for directly modeling and analyzing humans' mental models of AI agents"
  - [section 5] "we can then make inferences in the reverse direction about the latent abilities 𝑎𝑖 and problem difficulties 𝑑 𝑗"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains related IRT applications but none specifically addressing human-AI mental models
- Break condition: If the relationship between observed scores and latent parameters is too noisy or non-monotonic, the IRT assumptions break down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Multidimensional IRT captures the complexity of mental models by allowing separate ability dimensions for different topics.
- Mechanism: MIRT extends basic IRT by modeling ability as a vector with multiple dimensions, where each dimension corresponds to a different topic area.
- Core assumption: Different abilities can be modeled as correlated latent dimensions rather than a single general intelligence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6.1] "we use a between-items MIRT setup, which assumes that the probability of success for a specific item is affected by only one of the ability dimensions"
  - [section 6.2.1] "the multidimensional model is a better model for both other-human and AI agent assessment"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows IRT used for multidimensional assessment but not specifically for mental models of AI
- Break condition: If abilities across topics are truly independent, the correlation structure becomes meaningless.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The hierarchical structure of self-perception influences how people form mental models of others, with different structures for humans vs AI.
- Mechanism: Three different hierarchical models (undifferentiated, differentiated by ability, fully differentiated) capture different assumptions about how self-perception relates to other-perception.
- Core assumption: People use different cognitive strategies when estimating others' abilities based on whether the other is human or AI.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 7.1] "we test the fit of three different hierarchical IRT structures connecting the true ('underlying') performance, self-assessment, and other-assessment data"
  - [section 7.2.1] "When feedback on performance is provided, the undifferentiated model has the worst fit overall, especially for AI agents"
  - [section 7.2.2] "The variation of 𝛿s between agent types suggests substantial differences between perceptions of other humans and AI agents"
- Break condition: If people actually use the same cognitive strategy regardless of agent type, the differentiated models would show no advantage.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data collection: Trivia question-answering task with self and other assessments
  - Core modeling: IRT framework with multiple model variants (1D vs MIRT, different hierarchies)
  - Analysis: Model comparison using held-out likelihood, WAIC, LOO
  - Validation: Experimental conditions (feedback vs no feedback, human vs AI agents)

- Critical path:
  1. Collect assessment data from participants
  2. Fit IRT models to self-assessment data
  3. Fit IRT models to other-assessment data
  4. Compare models using information criteria
  5. Interpret latent parameters and correlations

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Simplicity vs accuracy: 1D IRT is simpler but MIRT captures more nuance
  - Computational cost: More complex models require more sampling iterations
  - Interpretability: Some model variants may be harder to explain to non-technical stakeholders

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor convergence in MCMC sampling
  - Model selection criteria are inconclusive or contradictory
  - Latent correlations don't make intuitive sense
  - Held-out log-likelihoods are worse than baseline

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fit 1D IRT to self-assessment data and examine convergence diagnostics
  2. Fit MIRT to other-assessment data and compare WAIC scores with 1D version
  3. Run the three hierarchical models on a subset of data and compare next-round predictions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the mental model development process differ between novice and expert users when collaborating with AI agents?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that their experiments were limited to Amazon Mechanical Turk workers who are not necessarily knowledgeable on the task or AI in general. The authors suggest future work should investigate these research questions across other user groups.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments only involved non-expert users, limiting generalizability to expert-AI collaboration scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments with expert users who regularly interact with AI agents on the same task, measuring differences in mental model formation, accuracy, and development over time.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different types of AI explanations (e.g., attention mechanisms, counterfactual examples) affect the development of human mental models of AI agents?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that prior work has shown explanations can affect perceived intelligence and improve predictions of AI behavior, but doesn't investigate this experimentally.
- Why unresolved: The current experimental design didn't include varying types or quality of AI explanations, so causal effects remain unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing mental model development with different explanation types, measuring changes in perceived abilities, correlations between abilities, and accuracy of predictions over time.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does the dimensionality of mental models vary across different task domains beyond trivia question answering?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note their experiments are limited to a single task and setting, and future work should investigate these questions across other settings like image classification.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments only used trivia questions with four distinct categories, so domain-generalizability is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Similar experiments using tasks with different structural properties (e.g., visual vs. verbal, sequential vs. simultaneous) to determine if multidimensional mental models are a general phenomenon or task-specific.

## Limitations
- Limited to trivia question answering, which may not generalize to other AI collaboration domains
- Experiments conducted with non-expert online workers, limiting generalizability to expert-AI scenarios
- Does not investigate how different types of AI explanations affect mental model development

## Confidence
**High Confidence** - The finding that participants perceive AI agents as more capable than humans is well-supported by the data and consistent across multiple analyses.

**Medium Confidence** - The claim that mental models develop over time but don't fully converge to actual performance is supported, though the mechanism for this incomplete convergence isn't fully explained.

**Low Confidence** - The assertion that perceptions of AI abilities are more correlated across different topics than for humans needs additional validation with broader topic sets.

## Next Checks
1. **Domain Transfer Test**: Apply the framework to non-trivia domains (e.g., medical diagnosis, creative tasks) to assess generalizability of the observed mental model patterns.

2. **Longitudinal Validation**: Conduct a follow-up study with the same participants over multiple sessions to directly measure mental model development trajectories.

3. **Comparative Agent Test**: Include multiple AI agents with varying capabilities to determine if the observed correlation patterns hold when AI performance varies systematically across topics.
