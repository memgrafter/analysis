# The HCI Aspects of Public Deployment of Research Chatbots: A User Study, Design Recommendations, and Open Challenges

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04765
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04765
- Reference count: 8
- Key outcome: Abstract anthropomorphic representation significantly improves user perceptions of engagement and emotional awareness (p<0.01) in research chatbot interactions.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates human-computer interaction challenges when deploying research chatbots to the public, identifying key cognitive factors like lack of conversational context, "speaker perception void", and absence of user expectations. Through a mixed-methods user study with 40 participants, the research tested a chatbot interface incorporating explainable AI (XAI), extradiegetic communication, and abstract anthropomorphic representation. The study reveals significant tensions between conversation engagement and user willingness to provide feedback or use XAI features, while demonstrating that abstract face representation improves perceived engagement and emotional awareness. The work concludes with design recommendations for public-facing research chatbot deployments.

## Method Summary
The study employed a mixed-methods approach with 40 participants in remote 90-minute sessions. Participants interacted with a research chatbot interface featuring XAI elements, extradiegetic communication, and abstract anthropomorphic representation. The methodology included think-aloud sessions during initial chat interactions (10-20 minutes), followed by quantitative questionnaires with 14 questions using 5-point Likert scales, and semi-structured interviews. Participants were screened for diversity across gender, location, tech familiarity, and ethnicity to ensure representative sampling.

## Key Results
- Abstract anthropomorphic representation significantly improved user perceptions of engagement and emotional awareness (p<0.01)
- XAI features and feedback mechanisms created tension with conversation flow engagement
- Extradiegetic communication helped compartmentalize user perceptions and clarify chatbot capabilities
- Avoiding "bot" terminology and using system-level explanations were recommended design practices

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Abstract anthropomorphic representation improves perceived engagement and emotional awareness in research chatbot interactions.
- Mechanism: The abstract face provides subtle anthropomorphic cues that anchor user perception, compensating for the lack of conversational context and speaker perception void.
- Core assumption: Users project anthropomorphic qualities onto minimal visual cues, which influences their perception of the chatbot's engagement and emotional awareness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "abstract anthropomorphic representation for the agent has a significant effect on user's perception"
  - [section] "On average, participants in the with face condition rated the agent more favorably... emotional awareness (3.5 vs 2.5) with p<0.01"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; weak corpus support for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the abstract representation is too minimal or users consciously ignore it, the effect may not manifest.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Extradiegetic communication improves user understanding of chatbot capabilities and expectations.
- Mechanism: System-level messages that explain chatbot behavior separate the system's voice from the agent's, helping users form appropriate mental models without conflating expectations.
- Core assumption: Users can distinguish between diegetic (agent) and extradiegetic (system) communication layers, and this distinction helps manage expectations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Extradiegetic communication... enables the interface to clarify, explain, or augment how the chatbot is behaving conversationally"
  - [section] "Participants' reactions show that it compartmentalizes their perception"
  - [corpus] Weak support; corpus doesn't directly address extradiegetic communication
- Break condition: If users fail to distinguish the two communication layers or find extradiegetic messages disruptive.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: XAI features reduce confusion but create a tension with conversation flow engagement.
- Mechanism: Users seek explanations when encountering errors or confusion, but accessing XAI disrupts the conversational flow, creating a trade-off between understanding and engagement.
- Core assumption: Users are motivated to understand chatbot failures but this motivation competes with their desire to maintain conversational engagement.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "offering AI explainability may have an impact on feedback rates"
  - [section] "Participants were most interested in seeking an explanation for the chatbot's message when something did not make sense"
  - [section] "A few design solutions are discussed... including proactive explanations"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; corpus lacks specific XAI studies
- Break condition: If explanations are not timely or relevant to the user's confusion, they may be ignored or resented.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Diegetic vs. Extradiegetic Communication
  - Why needed here: Understanding this distinction is crucial for designing interfaces that properly manage user expectations and perceptions.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between a diegetic chatbot message and an extradiegetic system message?

- Concept: Anthropomorphism in UI Design
  - Why needed here: The study shows that even abstract representations can influence user perception, which is counterintuitive for some designers.
  - Quick check question: How might an abstract face influence user perception differently than a realistic avatar?

- Concept: XAI (Explainable AI) Implementation
  - Why needed here: XAI features can improve user understanding but must be carefully integrated to avoid disrupting conversation flow.
  - Quick check question: What are potential ways to integrate XAI without requiring users to interrupt conversations?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Chat interface (main conversation area)
  - Abstract face representation (visual element)
  - Diegetic message handler (chatbot responses)
  - Extradiegetic message system (system explanations)
  - XAI module ("Why This Message" feature)
  - Feedback system (thumbs up/down)
  - Memory module (stores conversation context)

- Critical path:
  1. User initiates conversation
  2. Chatbot generates response (diegetic)
  3. System may insert extradiegetic messages as needed
  4. User can request XAI explanations
  5. User can provide feedback
  6. System updates memory for future interactions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Abstract vs. realistic representation: Abstract reduces bias but may be less engaging
  - XAI integration: Deep integration improves understanding but risks disrupting flow
  - Extradiegetic vs. diegetic explanations: Extradiegetic is clearer but may feel less natural

- Failure signatures:
  - High bounce rates despite positive feedback
  - Users ignoring XAI features entirely
  - Confusion about which messages are system vs. chatbot
  - Feedback rates dropping with increased engagement

- First 3 experiments:
  1. A/B test abstract vs. realistic face representations on perceived engagement
  2. Test proactive vs. reactive XAI explanations on user understanding
  3. Compare user perception when extradiegetic messages are in different formats (text vs. icons)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the presence of abstract anthropomorphic representation affect user engagement and feedback rates in research chatbot deployments over extended periods (e.g., weeks or months) rather than single-session interactions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that abstract face representation significantly improved user perceptions of engagement and emotional awareness (p<0.01) in a single 90-minute session, but noted that user perceptions likely change with long-term use.
- Why unresolved: The study only examined short-term interactions, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that user perceptions may evolve differently in long-term use scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: A longitudinal study tracking the same users over weeks or months, measuring changes in engagement, feedback rates, and perceptions of the chatbot with and without abstract face representation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the optimal design strategies for integrating XAI features into conversational interfaces without disrupting the natural flow of conversation, particularly for chatbots designed for casual or social interaction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The study identified a tension between conversation engagement and user willingness to provide feedback or use XAI features, noting that "the more engaging the conversation is, the less likely the user would be to break its flow."
- Why unresolved: While the paper suggests potential solutions like proactive explanations or surfacing UI elements between topic changes, it doesn't provide concrete evidence of which strategies work best or under what circumstances.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative user studies testing different XAI integration approaches (proactive vs. on-demand, diegetic vs. extradiegetic) with metrics measuring both user satisfaction and quality of feedback provided.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the terminology used to describe chatbots (e.g., "bot" vs. alternative names) impact user expectations, trust, and willingness to engage in meaningful interactions with research chatbots?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The study found that some participants experienced confusion about the chatbot's capabilities, which the authors partially attributed to the "customer service and strongly utilitarian connotation" of the word "bot."
- Why unresolved: The study observed this effect qualitatively but didn't systematically test how different naming conventions affect user behavior, expectations, or interaction quality.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled experiment where identical chatbots are presented with different names or descriptions, measuring differences in user expectations, interaction depth, and perceived capability alignment.

## Limitations
- Small sample size of 40 participants may limit generalizability
- Study focused specifically on research-oriented chatbot context rather than commercial applications
- Abstract face representation was minimal (static image), results may not extend to complex anthropomorphic designs

## Confidence
**High Confidence:** The core finding that abstract anthropomorphic representation significantly improves perceived engagement and emotional awareness (p<0.01) is well-supported by quantitative measures and statistically significant differences between conditions.

**Medium Confidence:** The tension between conversation engagement and willingness to use XAI features is observed but requires further investigation with larger samples to confirm the relationship strength and explore underlying mechanisms.

**Medium Confidence:** The recommendation to avoid "bot" terminology and use extradiegetic communication is based on qualitative participant feedback but needs systematic testing across different user demographics and contexts.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale replication test:** Conduct the study with 200+ participants across multiple geographic regions to verify the robustness of the abstract face effect and explore demographic variations in response patterns.

2. **XAI integration experiment:** Design a controlled experiment comparing different XAI integration approaches (proactive vs. reactive explanations, inline vs. separate windows) to quantify the trade-off between user understanding and conversation flow disruption.

3. **Terminology impact study:** Systematically test the impact of different interface terminologies ("bot" vs. "assistant" vs. "AI") on user expectations and interaction patterns through a factorial design with 100+ participants.
