# Multi-dimensional discrimination in Law and Machine Learning -- A comparative overview

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05995
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05995
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Survey comparing legal and ML approaches to multi-dimensional discrimination (cumulative, intersectional, sequential)

## Executive Summary
This survey paper addresses the critical problem of multi-dimensional discrimination in AI-driven decision-making, where bias stems from multiple protected attributes simultaneously. The authors provide a comprehensive overview of how cumulative, intersectional, and sequential discrimination are conceptualized in legal frameworks and how these concepts have been adapted in fairness-aware machine learning. The paper highlights the significant challenge of data scarcity when addressing intersectional subgroups and identifies key gaps between legal and ML approaches to multi-attribute fairness.

## Method Summary
This is a survey paper that synthesizes existing literature from both legal and machine learning domains on multi-dimensional discrimination. The method involves systematically reviewing legal definitions of cumulative, intersectional, and sequential discrimination, then mapping these concepts to corresponding fairness metrics and mitigation strategies in ML. The authors analyze the connections and limitations between domains, identifying open research directions without conducting original experiments.

## Key Results
- Multi-dimensional discrimination manifests as cumulative, intersectional, or sequential bias across protected attributes
- Data scarcity exponentially increases as protected attributes multiply, severely limiting intersectional fairness approaches
- Current ML fairness metrics often fail to capture the nuanced legal definitions and social contexts of discrimination

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The survey bridges legal and ML conceptions of multi-dimensional discrimination, creating a cross-domain framework for identifying and addressing bias. By juxtaposing legal definitions of cumulative, intersectional, and sequential discrimination with ML operationalizations (e.g., subgroup fairness metrics), the work surfaces gaps and misalignments in how each domain handles multi-attribute bias.

### Mechanism 2
The paper highlights the exponential data scarcity problem when addressing intersectional subgroups in ML. It demonstrates that as protected attributes multiply, subgroup sizes shrink, leading to imbalanced data and unreliable fairness metrics (e.g., Statistical Parity Subgroup Fairness).

### Mechanism 3
The paper introduces sequential discrimination as a distinct, understudied type of multi-dimensional bias relevant to ML pipelines. By framing sequential discrimination as a chain of decision stages (e.g., hiring pipeline steps), it motivates the development of stage-aware fairness algorithms that propagate fairness constraints forward.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific protected attributes beyond race, gender, and age should be prioritized for multi-dimensional fairness research, and how should their definitions be operationalized in ML contexts?
- Basis in paper: Authors note the need for "expansion of fairness frameworks to more protected grounds" and discuss challenges in defining and labeling protected attributes
- Why unresolved: The paper highlights that while EU law covers six grounds (race, gender, religion, disability, age, sexual orientation), ML research needs to determine which additional attributes are most critical and how to properly define them in technical systems while respecting legal and social contexts
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies identifying the most impactful protected attributes across different domains, along with methodological frameworks for operationalizing these attributes in ML systems

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can fairness trade-offs between subgroups be mathematically formalized and resolved when improving fairness for one protected attribute may decrease fairness for others?
- Basis in paper: Authors state "With an increasing number of protected attributes, including subgroups, it becomes more likely that increasing fairness for one attribute limits or decreases fairness for others"
- Why unresolved: Current fairness metrics focus on single attributes or subgroups in isolation, lacking frameworks for handling multi-attribute trade-offs that reflect real-world complexity
- What evidence would resolve it: Novel mathematical frameworks that can quantify and optimize fairness trade-offs across multiple protected attributes simultaneously, validated through real-world applications

### Open Question 3
- Question: What practical methods can effectively address data scarcity for intersectional subgroups in fairness-aware ML, particularly for small minority groups where statistical tests may be unreliable?
- Basis in paper: Authors discuss the exponential growth of subgroups with multiple protected attributes and note that "statistical tests applied in ECJ jurisprudence are not always suitable for identifying discrimination of small minority groups"
- Why unresolved: Current approaches struggle with sparse data in intersectional subgroups, and synthetic data generation risks introducing new biases while data collection faces legal and practical constraints
- What evidence would resolve it: Novel statistical methods that can reliably detect discrimination in small subgroups, along with practical techniques for generating representative synthetic data or leveraging transfer learning to address data scarcity

## Limitations
- The paper is a survey without original empirical validation of proposed frameworks
- Mapping between legal and ML concepts may oversimplify complex social and legal contexts
- Effectiveness of synthetic data solutions for intersectional subgroup fairness remains untested

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The characterization of cumulative, intersectional, and sequential discrimination types as distinct categories is well-supported by both legal and ML literature. The data scarcity problem for intersectional subgroups is empirically observable and widely documented.

- **Medium Confidence**: The mapping between legal concepts and ML fairness metrics is reasonable but may miss important context-specific nuances. The theoretical framework for sequential discrimination is sound, but its real-world applicability requires further validation.

- **Low Confidence**: The effectiveness of synthetic data solutions for intersectional subgroup fairness has not been empirically validated. The assumption that sequential discrimination compounds multiplicatively may not hold in all real-world decision systems.
