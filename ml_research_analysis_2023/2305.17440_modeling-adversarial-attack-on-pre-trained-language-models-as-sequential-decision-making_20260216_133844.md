# Modeling Adversarial Attack on Pre-trained Language Models as Sequential Decision Making

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17440
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17440
- Reference count: 24
- Achieves 95.8% attack success rate on Yelp dataset while maintaining semantic similarity of 0.71

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes SDM-Attack, a novel approach that models adversarial attacks on pre-trained language models as sequential decision-making problems. The method uses reinforcement learning to find optimal attack paths by iteratively selecting words to edit and their substitutions. The approach demonstrates strong performance, achieving the highest attack success rate among state-of-the-art methods while maintaining comparable modification rates and semantic similarity. The framework shows promising generalization across different victim models and transferability across datasets.

## Method Summary
The paper formalizes adversarial attack as a Markov Decision Process where an agent sequentially makes two decisions: word finding and word substitution. The word finder uses a masked language model to encode states and select tokens for editing, while the substitution component generates candidate replacements using synonym vocabulary filtered by semantic similarity. The entire attack process is modeled as a sequence where each decision influences subsequent steps. The agent is trained using reinforcement learning with REINFORCE algorithm, optimizing for cumulative rewards based on attack success rate, fluency, and semantic similarity. The modular design allows the substitution strategy to be swapped with alternative methods like mask-fill approaches.

## Key Results
- Achieves 95.8% attack success rate on Yelp dataset (highest among compared methods)
- Maintains semantic similarity of 0.71 while achieving high attack success
- Shows strong generalization across different victim models and transferability across datasets
- Outperforms state-of-the-art baselines like BERT-Attack while using comparable modification rates (8.2%)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The sequential decision-making approach allows the agent to adapt its strategy dynamically based on intermediate states. Rather than computing importance scores once at the beginning, the agent selects words to edit and substitutions iteratively, where each choice influences the next step's decision. This captures the subsequent influence of substitutions that earlier detached two-stage frameworks neglect. The core assumption is that the attack process can be modeled as a Markov Decision Process where the future state depends only on the current state and action.

### Mechanism 2
Reinforcement learning is effective because it can optimize for long-term cumulative rewards without requiring direct intermediate signals. The agent receives only a final termination signal (success/failure) and an instant reward at each step based on attack success rate, fluency, and semantic similarity. The REINFORCE algorithm updates policy parameters to maximize expected return. The core assumption is that the reward signals are informative enough to guide the policy toward generating valid adversarial examples.

### Mechanism 3
The word finder and word substitution components can generalize across different victim models and substitution strategies. The word finder uses a masked language model to encode states and select tokens, while the substitution component can work with either synonym-based replacement or mask-fill methods from other approaches. The core assumption is that the policy learned for word selection transfers across different model architectures, and the substitution strategy is modular enough to plug in alternatives.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Reinforcement Learning (Policy Gradient Methods)
  - Why needed here: The adversarial attack lacks direct intermediate feedback, requiring an algorithm that can learn from cumulative rewards and sparse signals.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between REINFORCE and Q-learning in terms of feedback requirements?

- Concept: Masked Language Models (MLM)
  - Why needed here: MLM provides contextual representations of tokens for the word finder to evaluate which words to edit based on their importance in the current state.
  - Quick check question: How does MLM differ from autoregressive language models in terms of training objective?

- Concept: Synonym-based Word Substitution
  - Why needed here: Preserving semantic similarity requires replacing words with semantically similar alternatives while maintaining fluency and avoiding antonyms.
  - Quick check question: Why is cosine similarity in word embedding space used as a filter for candidate substitutions?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Original input -> Word Finder selects token -> Word Substitution generates candidates -> Environment evaluates and provides reward -> Agent updates policy -> Repeat until success or failure
- Critical path: The agent iteratively selects tokens to edit based on MLM encodings, generates substitution candidates, receives environment feedback, and updates its policy until attack succeeds or maximum steps reached
- Design tradeoffs: Sequential captures dependencies but is slower vs. parallel decision-making; more informative rewards speed learning but require careful design; synonym-based is simple but mask-fill may be more effective
- Failure signatures: Low attack success rate despite high reward indicates misaligned reward functions; grammatical errors indicate insufficient fluency penalty; high modification rate indicates policy not optimizing for minimal changes
- First 3 experiments: 1) Implement word finder component only on a fixed dataset, verify it selects semantically important words; 2) Test word substitution with pre-defined rewards, ensure it generates fluent and similar sentences; 3) Combine both components with simple reward function, measure attack success rate on a single dataset/model

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the performance of SDM-Attack compare when using different word substitution strategies beyond the mask-fill approach used in BERT-Attack? While the paper shows that SDM-Attack-mlm outperforms BERT-Attack, it does not explore other substitution strategies or provide a comprehensive comparison of different methods.

### Open Question 2
How does SDM-Attack's performance vary across different languages and multi-lingual datasets? The paper only evaluates SDM-Attack on English datasets and mentions that it lacks multi-task, multi-model, and multilingual verification in terms of generalization and transferability.

### Open Question 3
How does the training time of SDM-Attack scale with the size of the training corpus and the complexity of the input text? While the paper provides some information on the training time, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how the training time scales with the size of the training corpus or the complexity of the input text.

## Limitations

- The Markovian assumption underlying the MDP formulation may not hold for long-range dependencies in language, potentially missing optimal attack strategies
- The reward function design represents a critical bottleneck that is tuned for specific datasets and may not generalize well across different domains
- Claims about generalization across different victim models are supported by limited ablation studies rather than comprehensive cross-model validation

## Confidence

- High confidence: The sequential decision-making framework is technically sound and the RL implementation follows established methodologies
- Medium confidence: The mechanism claims about why sequential decision-making outperforms two-stage methods are plausible but not definitively proven
- Low confidence: Generalization claims across different victim models and transferability across datasets are weakly supported
