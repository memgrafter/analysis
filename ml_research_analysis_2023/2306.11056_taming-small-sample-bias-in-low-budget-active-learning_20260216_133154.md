# Taming Small-sample Bias in Low-budget Active Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.11056
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11056
- Reference count: 3
- Primary result: CHAIN framework reduces small-sample bias in low-budget active learning by up to 4 points in accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical problem of small-sample bias in low-budget active learning, where models trained on few queried examples suffer from inaccurate predictions and poor uncertainty estimates. The authors propose CHAIN (Curriculum Firth biAs reductIoN), a query-strategy-agnostic framework that incorporates Firth bias reduction—a penalized likelihood technique—to reduce this bias. Instead of manually tuning the regularization coefficient, CHAIN uses bilevel optimization to automatically adjust it during training and further improves it with a curriculum that adapts the coefficient based on training progress. Experiments on three benchmark datasets (CIFAR10, CIFAR100, Fashion MNIST) with multiple query strategies and both deep learning and logistic regression settings show that CHAIN consistently outperforms baselines, including grid search and fixed bilevel optimization.

## Method Summary
CHAIN combines Firth bias reduction with bilevel optimization to automatically tune the regularization coefficient λ. The Firth regularizer adds a log-determinant penalty of the Fisher information matrix to the loss function, encouraging uniform class assignment probabilities and reducing small-sample bias. Bilevel optimization treats λ as a hyperparameter, optimizing it via an outer loop while training model parameters in an inner loop. A curriculum schedule periodically adjusts λ during training—stronger regularization early when bias is high, reduced later for better fitting. The framework is designed to be query-strategy-agnostic and works with both deep learning and logistic regression models.

## Key Results
- CHAIN outperforms baselines (FBR+GS, FBR+BO) by up to 4 points in accuracy on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and Fashion MNIST
- Automatic bilevel optimization of the Firth coefficient eliminates manual tuning while improving performance
- Curriculum adjustment of λ during training provides consistent gains across all query strategies tested
- Performance improvements are robust across both deep learning (ResNet-18 features) and logistic regression models
- CHAIN maintains effectiveness across different budget sizes (M ∈ {10, 20, 50, 100}) with total budget of 500 samples

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Firth bias reduction reduces small-sample bias by penalizing the log-determinant of the Fisher information matrix
- Mechanism: The Firth regularizer adds a term proportional to the KL divergence between the uniform class distribution and the model's predicted probabilities. This encourages the model to avoid overconfident predictions when data is scarce, counteracting the bias inherent in maximum likelihood estimation with small samples.
- Core assumption: The bias in MLE is proportional to O(N^-1) and can be effectively reduced by encouraging uniform class assignment probabilities through Jeffrey's prior.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we address this small-sample bias issue in low-budget AL by exploring a regularizer called Firth bias reduction, which can provably reduce the bias during the model training process"
  - [section] "Taking the log of both sides of Eq.5 yields the log-posterior as a sum of the cross-entropy loss and the Firth bias reduction term"
- Break condition: When the training set size becomes large enough that the bias term becomes negligible, the regularization may unnecessarily constrain the model and hurt performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Bilevel optimization automatically tunes the Firth coefficient without expensive grid search
- Mechanism: The bilevel optimization framework treats the Firth coefficient λ as a hyperparameter to be optimized. The inner loop optimizes model parameters β given a fixed λ, while the outer loop updates λ based on validation performance. This allows λ to be adapted to the specific dataset and training stage without manual tuning.
- Core assumption: The optimal λ varies across datasets, query rounds, and training stages, and can be efficiently approximated through gradient-based optimization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Instead of tuning the coefficient for each query round, which is sensitive and time-consuming, we propose the curriculum Firth bias reduction (CHAIN) that can automatically adjust the coefficient to be adaptive to the training process"
  - [section] "Specifically, we formulate the optimization of both the model parameters and the Firth coefficient as a bilevel optimization"
- Break condition: When the validation set is too small or unrepresentative, the bilevel optimization may converge to suboptimal λ values.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Curriculum adjustment of the Firth coefficient improves performance by adapting regularization strength throughout training
- Mechanism: The coefficient λ is not fixed but updated periodically during training based on the current model state. Early in training when bias is high, stronger regularization is applied. As training progresses and the model becomes more stable, the regularization is reduced to allow better fitting of the data.
- Core assumption: The impact of small-sample bias varies across training stages, requiring different levels of regularization at different times.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose a simple curriculum of the coefficient that adaptively changes the coefficient for the model in different training stages"
  - [section] "Therefore, the strength of Firth regularization (i.e., the Firth coefficient) should be adaptively adjusted rather than fixed"
- Break condition: If the curriculum schedule is not well-calibrated, it may either under-regularize early (failing to address bias) or over-regularize late (hurting generalization).

  - Why needed here: The automatic tuning of the Firth coefficient relies on bilevel optimization, which is a non-trivial optimization framework

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Query strategy module (entropy, coresets, BADGE, random) -> Model training module (cross-entropy loss + Firth regularizer) -> Bilevel optimization module (inner loop for β, outer loop for λ) -> Curriculum scheduler (periodic λ updates during training) -> Validation module (for bilevel optimization updates)

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize unlabeled dataset and budget
  2. Randomly sample initial training set
  3. For each query round:
     - Train model with current training set
     - Apply curriculum updates to λ during training
     - Use query strategy to select next batch of examples
     - Add new examples to training set
  4. Output final model

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Frequency of λ updates (T2 parameter): More frequent updates allow better adaptation but increase computational cost
  - Number of bilevel optimization steps (T1 parameter): More steps improve λ quality but increase overhead
  - Batch size scaling: Larger batches reduce noise but may not fit in memory with small training sets

- Failure signatures:
  - Negative λ values: Indicates the model is trying to sharpen predictions rather than regularize
  - High variance in λ across runs: Suggests instability in bilevel optimization
  - Performance degradation with curriculum: May indicate poor scheduling or overfitting to validation set

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline comparison: Run CHAIN with entropy query strategy on CIFAR10 with M=20, compare to standard MLE training
  2. Bilevel optimization ablation: Compare fixed λ (from pre-training bilevel optimization) vs curriculum updates on CIFAR100
  3. Query strategy robustness: Test CHAIN with all four query strategies on Fashion MNIST to verify query-strategy-agnostic claim

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but several areas remain unexplored based on the experimental setup and methodology:

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the curriculum of Firth coefficient (CHAIN) compare to other regularization techniques beyond Firth bias reduction for small-sample bias mitigation in low-budget active learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on Firth bias reduction and its curriculum, but does not explore other regularization techniques.
- Why unresolved: The paper is centered on Firth bias reduction, leaving other potential regularization methods unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments with other regularization techniques like L1/L2 regularization, dropout, or data augmentation on the same datasets and query strategies.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the theoretical justification for the observed trend of the Firth coefficient decreasing across AL rounds during deep model fine-tuning, but initially increasing then decreasing during logistic regression?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper observes this trend but only provides a hypothesis about early stopping in logistic regression.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides a hypothesis but does not offer a rigorous theoretical explanation for the different trends in deep models versus logistic regression.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis connecting the coefficient trends to the bias-variance tradeoff and model capacity across different model architectures.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does CHAIN perform when combined with non-uncertainty-based query strategies like diversity-based or hybrid methods that don't rely heavily on model predictions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper only tests CHAIN with uncertainty-based, representation-based, and hybrid strategies, but not specifically with diversity-based methods.
- Why unresolved: The experiments focus on a limited set of query strategies, leaving the performance with diversity-based methods untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments combining CHAIN with diversity-based query strategies like CoreSet with different distance metrics or other hybrid methods on the benchmark datasets.

## Limitations

- Limited scalability evidence: Experiments focus on small datasets (CIFAR10/100, Fashion MNIST) without testing on larger, more complex domains
- Implementation details unclear: Bilevel optimization specifics (GIMLI algorithm) and curriculum scheduling mechanisms are not fully specified
- Query strategy validation scope: Only four query strategies tested, leaving uncertainty about performance with other methods like diversity-based approaches
- Theoretical analysis gap: The paper lacks rigorous theoretical justification for why the curriculum schedule should work across different model architectures

## Confidence

- High confidence in the theoretical foundation of Firth bias reduction and its ability to reduce small-sample bias in MLE estimation
- Medium confidence in the effectiveness of bilevel optimization for coefficient tuning, given the limited implementation details provided
- Medium confidence in the curriculum approach's benefits, as the scheduling mechanism could be more precisely defined
- Low confidence in the general applicability claim without testing on more diverse datasets and query strategies
