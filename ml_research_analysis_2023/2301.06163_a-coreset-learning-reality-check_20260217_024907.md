# A Coreset Learning Reality Check

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2301.06163
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06163
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Most coreset methods for logistic regression fail to outperform simple uniform random sampling across diverse datasets and metrics

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of six coreset and subsampling methods for logistic regression, comparing them against uniform random sampling across 8 datasets and 3 performance metrics. Surprisingly, most methods fail to outperform the naive baseline, with the k-means coreset being the notable exception when properly tuned. The authors identify methodological flaws in prior evaluations that may have led to overestimation of coreset effectiveness, and demonstrate that increasing regularization makes uniform sampling more competitive. The study calls for more diverse evaluation benchmarks in future coreset research.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates six coreset methods (k-means, leverage, Lewis, monotonic, OSMAC-MSE, OSMAC-VC) plus uniform baseline for logistic regression across 8 real-world datasets ranging from 24K to 4.5M rows. Each method is tested at 25 different subsample sizes (200-100K) with 50 replications per configuration. Performance is measured using three metrics: relative NLL error, coefficient MSE, and relative ROC. The authors implement all methods from scratch to ensure fair comparison, using L2 regularization (λ = 10^-5) and one-hot encoding for categorical features.

## Key Results
- Most coreset methods fail to outperform uniform random sampling across datasets and metrics
- K-means coreset performs among the best when properly tuned (R = 1.0, k = 6)
- Increasing regularization strength makes uniform sampling more competitive, consistent with theoretical predictions
- Methodological issues in prior evaluations may have led to overestimation of coreset effectiveness

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Coreset methods fail to outperform uniform sampling because their theoretical sensitivity bounds are too loose for practical datasets, causing them to select similar or redundant samples.
- Mechanism: Coreset algorithms rely on sensitivity scores to weight sampling probabilities. If these sensitivity estimates are conservative (overestimates), the resulting sampling distribution approaches uniform, erasing any theoretical advantage.
- Core assumption: The gap between theoretical sensitivity bounds and actual sensitivity is large enough to negate the benefit of informed sampling.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "In many cases, methods do not outperform simple uniform subsampling."
  - [section] "As R → ∞ the importance weights converge to the uniform distribution, so their results are not representative of the effectiveness of the k-means method."
  - [corpus] Weak - related works focus on coreset construction but don't directly address sensitivity looseness.
- Break condition: When sensitivity estimation techniques become tight enough relative to actual influence, coreset methods should outperform uniform sampling.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Prior evaluations overestimated coreset performance by using limited datasets and baselines, creating a false impression of superiority.
- Mechanism: Studies with few datasets and weak baselines (often only one competitor) could by chance show improvement on those specific datasets. Subsequent papers built on these flawed comparisons without including the naive baseline.
- Core assumption: The performance variance across datasets is high enough that limited evaluations can produce misleading conclusions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Some limitations of previous studies are: Each method is benchmarked with at most one or two competitors on a small selection of real-world datasets."
  - [section] "Furthermore, most studies only included one or two baselines... It only takes one paper to draw conclusions of improvement against a baseline for later works to follow suit."
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers don't discuss methodological flaws in prior evaluations.
- Break condition: When evaluations consistently use diverse datasets and include uniform sampling as a baseline, performance differences should become clear.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The choice of evaluation metric (NLL, MSE, ROC) doesn't significantly change which method performs best, suggesting the fundamental issue is with the subsampling distribution rather than metric-specific behavior.
- Mechanism: If a method consistently underperforms across different metrics, the problem lies in how it selects samples rather than in metric-specific optimization. The uniform method's robustness across metrics indicates its sampling distribution is adequate for various objectives.
- Core assumption: The performance consistency across metrics indicates a fundamental sampling issue rather than metric-specific optimization problems.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The evaluation metric has not consistent across these works, with some assessing objective function error, some measuring the error of estimating β itself, etc."
  - [section] "Methods such as Leverage, Lewis, and OSMAC MSE are very effective on KDD cup relative to the uniform baseline, but not so much on others... This shows that accurately estimating βMLE with βC leads to effective logistic regression subsampling regardless of the metric of interest."
  - [corpus] Weak - related works don't analyze cross-metric consistency.
- Break condition: When a method shows metric-specific strengths, it would indicate the sampling distribution is capturing metric-relevant information.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Logistic regression optimization and regularization
  - Why needed here: The paper evaluates subsampling methods for logistic regression, requiring understanding of how L2 regularization affects model stability and sensitivity.
  - Quick check question: How does the regularization parameter λ affect the sensitivity of logistic regression and why does this matter for coreset construction?

- Concept: Sensitivity sampling and coreset theory
  - Why needed here: Coreset methods rely on sensitivity scores to determine sampling probabilities. Understanding how these are computed and bounded is crucial for interpreting why they fail.
  - Quick check question: What is the relationship between sensitivity bounds and the minimum coreset size required for (1+ε)-approximation?

- Concept: Non-parametric statistical testing
  - Why needed here: The paper uses Dunn tests to determine if performance differences are statistically significant across multiple metrics.
  - Quick check question: Why is the Bonferroni correction applied when testing across three metrics, and what is the resulting significance threshold?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Dataset preprocessing -> Six coreset implementations + uniform baseline -> Logistic regression fitting with regularization -> Performance evaluation across three metrics -> Statistical comparison framework -> Experiment orchestration
- Critical path: For each dataset → for each subsample size → for each method → run 50 replications → compute median performance → statistical comparison → aggregate results
- Design tradeoffs: The paper chose to implement all methods from scratch rather than using existing libraries to ensure fair comparison and control over implementation details. This increased development time but eliminated implementation bias.
- Failure signatures: If a method shows high variance across replications or performs poorly only on certain datasets, this indicates sensitivity to data characteristics or implementation issues rather than fundamental limitations.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run all methods on a small dataset (e.g., census) with subsample sizes 200-1000 to verify implementation correctness and identify any obvious errors.
  2. Compare method performance on a dataset where one method is known to work well (e.g., KDD cup for leverage-based methods) to establish baseline expectations.
  3. Test the effect of regularization strength on uniform sampling performance to verify the theoretical relationship between λ and coreset effectiveness.

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to logistic regression; results may not generalize to other model classes
- Implementation approximations for some methods (e.g., Lewis iterative procedure) may affect comparability
- Only 8 datasets used, which may not represent full diversity of real-world scenarios

## Confidence

**Major Uncertainties:**
- The analysis focuses exclusively on logistic regression; performance patterns may not extend to other model classes like SVM or neural networks
- Implementation details for some coreset methods (particularly Lewis iterative procedure) required practical approximations that may affect comparability
- The paper assumes sensitivity bounds are overly loose but doesn't quantify the gap between theoretical and empirical sensitivities

**Confidence Labels:**
- **High confidence**: Claims about uniform sampling competitiveness when regularization is strong; methodological flaws in prior evaluations; k-means coreset remains competitive when properly tuned
- **Medium confidence**: Generalization that most seminal coreset methods are no better than naive baselines (limited to logistic regression scope)
- **Low confidence**: Broader implications for coreset theory across machine learning domains
