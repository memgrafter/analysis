# Revisiting the Gumbel-Softmax in MADDPG

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.11793
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11793
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Alternative gradient estimators (GST, STGS-T) significantly outperform baseline Gumbel-Softmax in MADDPG for discrete-action multi-agent grid-world tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the choice of gradient estimator impacts MADDPG performance in discrete-action multi-agent settings. MADDPG requires gradient estimation for discrete actions, typically handled by the biased Gumbel-Softmax estimator. The authors compare four alternative estimators—STGS-T, TAGS, GRMC-K, and GST—against the baseline STGS-1 across nine grid-world tasks. Results show that alternative estimators can significantly improve MADDPG performance, with GST achieving up to 55% higher returns and faster convergence compared to baseline, particularly in larger grid-world environments. While STGS-T offers moderate improvements with minimal computational overhead, GST provides the best performance despite being 2.5× slower.

## Method Summary
The study compares four gradient estimators for discrete action sampling in MADDPG: STGS-T (straight-through Gumbel-Softmax with lower temperature), TAGS (temperature-annealed Gumbel-Softmax), GRMC-K (Monte Carlo-based estimator with Rao-Blackwellization), and GST (Gapped Straight-Through estimator). These were benchmarked against the baseline STGS-1 estimator across nine grid-world tasks from the Papoudakis et al. benchmarking suite. The experiments used 5 random seeds, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, and evaluation every 50,000 time steps. Performance metrics included maximum returns, average returns, compute time, and gradient variance across mini-batches.

## Key Results
- GST estimator achieved up to 55% higher returns compared to baseline STGS-1 in challenging grid-world tasks
- STGS-T provided moderate improvements with minimal computational overhead compared to baseline
- GST converged faster than baseline but required 2.5× more compute time
- TAGS performed poorly, potentially due to suboptimal temperature annealing hyperparameters
- Performance gains were most pronounced in larger grid-world environments (15x15) versus simpler tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Replacing the biased Gumbel-Softmax estimator with GST reduces gradient bias, enabling better policy updates in discrete MADDPG.
- Mechanism: The GST estimator uses deterministic perturbations (gap creation and logit alignment) instead of Gumbel noise, producing lower-variance, less-biased gradient estimates for discrete actions.
- Core assumption: The bias in the original Gumbel-Softmax estimator is the primary reason MADDPG underperforms in grid-world tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This method, however, is statistically biased, and a recent MARL benchmarking paper suggests that this bias makes MADDPG perform poorly in grid-world situations, where the action space is discrete."
  - [section 3.1] "Relaxing the space in this way, however, introduces statistical bias into the gradient computation."
- Break condition: If the discrete action space becomes so large that the gap creation in GST becomes computationally prohibitive, or if the deterministic perturbations fail to capture necessary exploration.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Lower temperature in gradient estimation reduces bias at the cost of exploration, improving convergence in later training stages.
- Mechanism: By annealing the temperature parameter in the STGS-T and TAGS estimators, the algorithm shifts from exploration to exploitation, reducing bias while maintaining sufficient exploration early in training.
- Core assumption: Early exploration is sufficient with high temperature, and later training benefits more from reduced bias than additional exploration.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "Since exploration is usually desirable in the beginning of a training procedure, we propose setting the temperature to be high early-on, and then annealing it to be lower over time."
  - [section 5.1] "We suspect this outcome is because informative gradients are not as crucial in simple tasks. That is, the gradient estimation is not a problematic aspect of the training, and limitations arise elsewhere in the mechanics of MADDPG."
- Break condition: If the annealing schedule is too aggressive, causing premature convergence to suboptimal policies, or if the environment requires sustained exploration.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Rao-Blackwellization in GRMC reduces gradient variance, allowing safe training at lower temperatures with reduced bias.
- Mechanism: The GRMC estimator computes the expected gradient given the sampled action, providing a variance-reduced version of the STGS estimator while maintaining the same bias.
- Core assumption: Lower variance gradients enable stable training at reduced temperatures, which in turn reduces bias without sacrificing convergence stability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.4] "Though guarantees are only made about the latter, the authors of the GRMC argue that with a lower variance, one can safely train the estimator at lower temperatures—i.e. with a lower bias."
  - [section 5.1] "The Rao-Blackwellisation procedure of GRMC𝐾 also sees better returns and faster convergence."
- Break condition: If the computational cost of Monte Carlo sampling becomes prohibitive, or if the variance reduction is insufficient to offset the remaining bias at low temperatures.

  - Why needed here: MADDPG requires gradient-based updates but discrete actions don't have gradients; Gumbel-Softmax relaxes discrete distributions to continuous ones

- Concept: Bias-variance tradeoff in gradient estimators
  - Why needed here: Different estimators balance bias and variance differently, affecting MADDPG's learning performance in discrete action spaces
  - Quick check question: How does the GRMC estimator maintain the same bias as STGS while reducing variance?

- Concept: Temperature annealing for exploration-exploitation balance
  - Why needed here: Early training requires exploration while later training benefits from exploitation; temperature controls this tradeoff in discrete gradient estimation
  - Quick check question: Why might annealing temperature hurt performance in some tasks despite reducing bias?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Under what conditions does the Gapped Straight-Through (GST) estimator significantly outperform the baseline STGS-1 estimator in MADDPG?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows GST achieving up to 55% higher returns in challenging tasks, particularly in larger grid-world environments, but only marginal improvements in simpler tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper notes that GST performs well in complex tasks but doesn't provide a clear threshold or criteria for when the performance gap becomes significant.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic testing across tasks of varying complexity with quantitative metrics defining "simple" vs "challenging" tasks, showing the exact point where GST's advantage emerges.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the temperature-annealing scheme in TAGS compare to the fixed lower temperature approach in STGS-T across different MARL environments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found TAGS performed poorly compared to STGS-T, but acknowledges this might be due to hyperparameters chosen.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested one annealing scheme and one set of hyperparameters, leaving open whether better parameter tuning could make TAGS competitive.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive experiments with multiple annealing schedules, start/end temperatures, and comparison against optimized STGS-T across diverse environments.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between gradient variance in policy networks and the performance improvements observed with alternative estimators like GST?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows GST produces higher gradient variance across mini-batches in policy networks compared to STGS-1, and hypothesizes this indicates more informative gradients.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents preliminary evidence but doesn't establish a causal relationship or quantify how much gradient variance contributes to performance gains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying gradient variance independently while keeping other factors constant, measuring the direct impact on returns and convergence speed.

## Limitations

- Computational overhead of GST (2.5× slower than baseline) may limit practical applicability in larger-scale problems
- Temperature annealing effects on bias-variance tradeoff remain theoretical without direct empirical validation
- Performance improvements are primarily demonstrated in grid-world environments, with uncertain generalization to other MARL settings

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Performance improvements of GST and STGS-T over baseline STGS are well-supported by experimental results across multiple tasks
- **Medium confidence**: Claims about bias reduction mechanisms are theoretically sound but lack direct empirical validation in the MARL setting
- **Low confidence**: Generalization to non-grid-world environments and continuous action spaces remains untested
