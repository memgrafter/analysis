# Sebis at SemEval-2023 Task 7: A Joint System for Natural Language Inference and Evidence Retrieval from Clinical Trial Reports

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.13180
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13180
- Reference count: 9
- Placed 3rd out of 40 teams in SemEval-2023 Task 7

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a system for natural language inference and evidence retrieval from clinical trial reports, developed for the SemEval-2023 Task 7. The system addresses the challenge of automating the process of inferring new knowledge and finding evidence from clinical trial reports, which is becoming increasingly difficult due to the large volume of reports generated daily. The proposed approach involves two systems: a pipeline system that models the two tasks separately, and a joint system that learns the two tasks simultaneously with a shared representation and a multi-task learning approach. The final system combines their outputs in an ensemble system, achieving 3rd place in the competition.

## Method Summary
The system uses two approaches: a pipeline system with sequential learning and a joint system with multi-task learning and shared representation. Both systems utilize BERT-based models fine-tuned on clinical trial data. The pipeline system processes evidence retrieval and textual entailment separately, while the joint system shares an encoder between both tasks. The final ensemble system combines outputs from both approaches by averaging prediction probabilities. Domain-specific models (ClinicalBERT, BioBERT, UmlsBERT) were tested and compared against general-purpose BERT variants.

## Key Results
- The joint system achieved better evidence retrieval performance than the pipeline system
- ClinicalBERT outperformed other domain-specific models on this task
- The ensemble system combining pipeline and joint approaches achieved the best overall performance
- The system placed 3rd out of 40 teams in the competition

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Joint modeling with shared representation improves both evidence retrieval and textual entailment simultaneously
- Mechanism: Multi-task learning with shared encoder captures inter-task dependencies by forcing the model to jointly optimize evidence selection and entailment prediction
- Core assumption: Evidence quality directly impacts entailment accuracy and vice versa
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The second one is a joint system that learns the two tasks simultaneously with a shared representation and a multi-task learning approach"
  - [section] "The joint systems turned out to perform slightly better on the evidence retrieval task. This was expected considering that it considers the full document when doing the evidence retrieval task"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence of multi-task learning effectiveness for this specific task combination
- Break condition: If the inter-task dependencies are weak or the tasks conflict, joint training could degrade performance compared to separate pipelines

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Domain-specific pre-trained language models outperform general-purpose models on clinical trial data
- Mechanism: Pre-training on biomedical/clinical text creates task-specific representations that capture domain terminology and relationships
- Core assumption: Clinical trial reports share distributional properties with biomedical literature used for pre-training
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The domain-specific biomedical and clinical BERT models outperformed the vanilla BERT model on both tasks, which shows the benefit of pre-training large language models on specialized text"
  - [section] "ClinicalBERT outperforms the BioBERT, which is an expected outcome since it works with clinical trial reports"
  - [corpus] Missing - no citation or comparison data for model performance differences
- Break condition: If clinical trial language significantly differs from biomedical literature, domain-specific models may not provide advantages

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Ensemble systems combining pipeline and joint approaches achieve better overall performance
- Mechanism: Averaging predictions from complementary systems reduces individual model biases and captures different aspects of the problem
- Core assumption: Pipeline and joint systems make different types of errors that can be complementary
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The final system combines their outputs in an ensemble system"
  - [section] "Considering that the outputs of the two systems are different in certain predictions because of different output probabilities of classes, we decided to average the probabilities"
  - [section] "Table 2: Best performing model results on the test set" showing ensemble achieving best results
- Break condition: If both systems share the same systematic errors, ensembling provides minimal benefit

## Foundational Learning

- Multi-task learning
  - Why needed here: Allows simultaneous optimization of evidence retrieval and textual entailment, leveraging their dependencies
  - Quick check question: What are the two primary loss functions being optimized in the joint system?

- Domain adaptation
  - Why needed here: Clinical trial reports contain specialized terminology and structures that general models don't capture well
  - Quick check question: Which base model performed best on this task and why might that be?

- Evidence selection as sequence classification
  - Why needed here: Each sentence needs to be evaluated independently for evidence relevance while considering document context
  - Quick check question: How does the joint system differ from the pipeline system in representing evidence candidates?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input processing → Base language model (BERT variants) → Evidence selection module → Entailment prediction module → Output aggregation
  Pipeline: Evidence module → Entailment module (sequential)
  Joint: Shared encoder → Parallel evidence and entailment heads → Combined output
  Ensemble: Weighted average of pipeline and joint predictions

- Critical path:
  Claim and document → Encoding → Evidence identification → Evidence aggregation → Entailment prediction → Final verdict
  Performance bottleneck: Document encoding (up to 1024 tokens) in joint system

- Design tradeoffs:
  Pipeline vs joint: Pipeline allows independent optimization but loses inter-task benefits; joint captures dependencies but is computationally heavier
  Domain-specific vs general models: Specialized models better capture terminology but may overfit to training distribution
  Evidence truncation: Limiting to 20 sentences improves precision but may miss relevant evidence

- Failure signatures:
  Low precision, high recall: Model selecting too many sentences as evidence (common with quantifier claims)
  Contradictions with commonsense: Missing real-world knowledge not present in clinical text
  Numerical reasoning errors: Difficulty with percentages and quantities in adverse events

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare pipeline vs joint performance on dev set with same base model to validate multi-task benefits
  2. Test different base models (BioBERT, ClinicalBERT, DeBERTa) on evidence retrieval task to identify optimal encoder
  3. Implement ensemble with different weighting schemes (0.5/0.5, 0.4/0.6, 0.3/0.7) to find optimal combination ratio

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies several challenges that could be addressed in future work, including improving handling of commonsense reasoning, numerical reasoning, and multi-hop reasoning, as well as incorporating additional medical and world knowledge. While the paper suggests these areas for improvement, it does not provide specific strategies or techniques for addressing these challenges.

## Limitations
- The ensemble approach is presented as a "black box" combination without exploring optimal weighting strategies
- Claims about domain-specific model superiority lack comparative statistics and empirical validation
- The corpus analysis returned only 25 related papers with moderate relatedness scores
- The system struggles with numerical reasoning and commonsense knowledge not present in clinical text

## Confidence
- **High**: The basic system architecture (pipeline vs joint approaches) and the use of domain-specific BERT variants are well-supported by the text
- **Medium**: The claim about ensemble effectiveness is supported by test results but lacks ablation studies or error analysis
- **Low**: Claims about inter-task dependencies benefiting joint training lack empirical validation or comparison to baseline single-task models

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation Study on Multi-task Learning**: Compare pipeline and joint system performance on the development set using identical base models to quantify the actual benefit of shared representations

2. **Error Analysis of Ensemble Components**: Analyze cases where pipeline and joint systems disagree to determine if their errors are truly complementary or if they share systematic biases

3. **Domain Adaptation Validation**: Conduct a controlled experiment comparing general vs domain-specific models on a subset of claims that require specialized clinical knowledge versus general reasoning
