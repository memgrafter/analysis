# Spelling convention sensitivity in neural language models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.03457
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03457
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: Large language models show partial sensitivity to British vs. American spelling conventions, primarily through lexical associations rather than sub-word generalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether neural language models trained on diverse English text corpora learn to maintain British or American spelling conventions. The authors find that while training corpora exhibit substantial but imperfect spelling consistency (particularly biased toward American English), models like T5 and GPT2 show only partial sensitivity to these conventions. T5 demonstrates a preference for spelling consistency, stronger for American than British variants, but this consistency is driven by lexical dependencies rather than sub-word patterns, as evidenced by poor generalization to nonce words. Fine-tuning on balanced synthetic data reduces American English bias but also weakens overall consistency.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes spelling convention consistency across five corpora (C4, WebText, Wikipedia, BNC, and Billion Word Benchmark) using British-American spelling variant pairs. Models T5 and GPT2 are probed using controlled templates containing these variants, measuring conditional probabilities and prediction accuracy. The researchers also fine-tune T5 on a synthetic version of the British National Corpus with balanced spelling conventions to test whether this reduces American English bias. Results are evaluated using mutual information between spelling conventions and consistency in predictions across templates.

## Key Results
- T5 exhibits a preference for spelling consistency, though weaker for British than American variants (0.86 probability for US forms after US primers vs 0.39 for UK forms after US primers)
- GPT2 shows similar patterns of limited sensitivity to spelling conventions
- Consistency is driven by lexical associations rather than sub-word generalization, as evidenced by poor performance on nonce words
- Fine-tuning T5 on balanced synthetic data reduces American English bias but also reduces overall consistency

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The model learns spelling consistency from co-occurrence patterns in training data.
- Mechanism: When British and American variants consistently appear together in training strings, the model builds associative links between them. This allows the model to predict one variant given the presence of another from the same convention.
- Core assumption: Training data exhibits sufficient consistency in spelling conventions for the model to extract meaningful patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Using a set of probe words unique to either British or American English, we first establish that training corpora exhibit substantial (though not total) consistency."
  - [section 4]: Analysis shows C4 corpus has 74.6% US-matched pairs and 14.7% UK-matched pairs, demonstrating substantial but imperfect consistency.
  - [corpus]: The C4 corpus shows substantial consistency (74.6% US-matched pairs), providing evidence for this mechanism, though the 14.7% UK-matched pairs indicate the patterns are not perfect.
- Break condition: If training data becomes highly inconsistent with mixed spelling conventions in the same strings, the associative links would weaken or become unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The model shows stronger consistency for American English due to training data bias.
- Mechanism: Higher frequency of American English variants in training data creates stronger learned associations for American spelling patterns. The model assigns higher probabilities to American forms due to this frequency bias.
- Core assumption: Training data distribution significantly influences model behavior through frequency effects.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "They then probe T5 and GPT2 models... Results show that T5 exhibits a preference for spelling consistency, though weaker for British than American variants."
  - [section 5.1]: T5 assigns 0.86 probability to US forms after US primers vs 0.39 probability to UK forms after US primers, showing clear preference for American English.
  - [corpus]: C4 corpus has 74.6% US-matched pairs vs 14.7% UK-matched pairs, explaining the model's preference for American English through training data frequency.
- Break condition: If training data is balanced or weighted toward British English, the preference would shift accordingly.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The model learns lexical dependencies rather than sub-word spelling patterns.
- Mechanism: The model associates specific British and American word pairs rather than learning general spelling rules. This explains why consistency doesn't generalize to nonce words with British/American spelling patterns.
- Core assumption: The model relies on memorized lexical associations rather than abstract spelling rules.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "T5... appears to internalize this consistency, though only with respect to observed lexical items (not nonce words with British/American spelling patterns)."
  - [section 5.5]: When tested with nonce forms, T5 shows attenuated consistency patterns compared to real words, suggesting reliance on lexical rather than sub-word patterns.
  - [corpus]: The analysis shows specific word pairs co-occur consistently in training data, supporting the idea that the model learns these specific associations.
- Break condition: If the model could generalize to nonce words following the same spelling patterns, this mechanism would be disproven.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: To what extent can language models learn sub-word generalizations for spelling conventions beyond lexical dependencies, and how does this ability vary across different model architectures (e.g., T5 vs. GPT2)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper found that T5's sensitivity to spelling consistency is largely driven by lexical dependencies rather than sub-word features, as evidenced by the limited generalization to nonce words. The authors suggest that future work should investigate methods to mine information about language variation from large corpora and LLMs.
- Why unresolved: The current study only scratched the surface of sub-word generalization, focusing primarily on lexical dependencies. The paper hints at the potential for future work to explore sub-word features but does not provide a comprehensive analysis.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed study comparing the ability of different model architectures (e.g., T5, GPT2, and other models) to generalize spelling conventions to nonce words and sub-word patterns. This could involve creating nonce words with varying degrees of sub-word similarity to known spelling variants and measuring the models' sensitivity to these patterns.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different types of web-scraped corpora (e.g., C4, WebText, Wikipedia) vary in their consistency of spelling conventions, and how does this variation impact the performance of language models trained on these corpora?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that C4, WebText, and Wikipedia have different levels of spelling consistency, with C4 being the most consistent. The authors also noted that the bias towards American English in these corpora impacts the models' performance, particularly for British English.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides a snapshot of spelling consistency across different corpora, it does not delve into the reasons behind these variations or their specific impact on model performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive analysis of the sources of spelling inconsistency in different web-scraped corpora, including factors such as the diversity of contributors, the types of content, and the prevalence of automated editing. Additionally, a study comparing the performance of language models trained on these corpora on tasks that require spelling consistency, such as text generation and next-word prediction.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can fine-tuning language models on synthetic data with perfect spelling consistency improve their ability to maintain spelling conventions in generated text, and how does this approach compare to other methods of addressing bias in training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that fine-tuning T5 on a synthetic version of the British National Corpus with perfect spelling consistency reduced the model's preference for American English but also reduced overall consistency. The authors suggest that this approach may not be sufficient to achieve reliable spelling consistency.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explored one method of fine-tuning (synthetic data) and did not compare it to other potential approaches, such as data augmentation or bias correction techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative study of different methods for addressing bias in training data, including synthetic data, data augmentation, and bias correction techniques. This could involve training multiple models on different datasets and evaluating their performance on tasks that require spelling consistency, such as text generation and next-word prediction.

## Limitations
- Limited probe word set (36 pairs) may not capture full complexity of spelling variation
- Templates used for probing may not reflect all natural usage patterns where spelling consistency matters
- SentencePiece tokenization may affect how spelling patterns are represented and learned

## Confidence
- Core finding (models show partial spelling convention sensitivity): Medium confidence, limited by probe word set size and binary classification approach
- Fine-tuning results (synthetic balanced data): Medium confidence, as synthetic scenario may not reflect real-world adaptation
- Mechanism (lexical vs. sub-word learning): Medium confidence, though nonce word results strongly support lexical dependency interpretation
