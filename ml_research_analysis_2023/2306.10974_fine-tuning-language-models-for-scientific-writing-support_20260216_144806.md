# Fine-Tuning Language Models for Scientific Writing Support

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10974
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10974
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Transformer models achieve low MSE in scientificness scoring, up to 90% F1 in section classification, and high BLEU/METEOR in paraphrasing scientific sentences.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive approach to scientific writing support using fine-tuned language models. The authors develop three distinct models: regression models for scientificness scoring, multi-label classifiers for section assignment, and paraphrasers for style improvement. Using BERT, SciBERT, T5, and BART, they demonstrate strong performance across all tasks on arXiv papers and synthetic data. The work highlights the effectiveness of transformer models in understanding and generating scientific text, with context playing a crucial role in section classification.

## Method Summary
The method involves fine-tuning pre-trained language models on three scientific writing tasks. For scientificness scoring, BERT, SciBERT, and WideMLP regression models are trained to predict continuous scores between 0 and 1. Section classification uses multi-label classifiers (BERT, SciBERT, WideMLP) to assign sentences to paper sections, with experiments on context lengths of 1-3 sentences. Paraphrasing employs T5 and BART models fine-tuned on synthetic parallel data created by corrupting scientific sentences. Models are trained using AdamW optimizer with varying learning rates (1e-5 to 5e-5), dropout rates (0.1 to 0.5), and weight decay (0.001 to 0.05).

## Key Results
- Scientificness models achieve MSE < 2% across all tested architectures
- Section classification reaches up to 90% F1-score with 3-sentence context
- Paraphrasing models produce outputs with high BLEU/METEOR scores close to gold standards
- Context length significantly improves section classification performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Scientificness scoring works by learning to distinguish sentence features that correlate with academic writing conventions.
- Mechanism: Regression models are trained on labeled scientific (0.9) vs non-scientific (0.1) sentences, learning to map textual features to a continuous score.
- Core assumption: The presence of technical terms, formal structure, and domain-specific vocabulary reliably indicates scientificness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We train regression models to discern non-scientific from scientific sentences by determining a sentence's scientificness."
  - [section] "All models achieve an MSE smaller than 2%. The experiment on the influence of <equation> and <reference> tokens (see Table 2) shows that transformer models rank sentences containing such tokens higher than sentences without such tokens."
  - [corpus] Weak - the corpus composition is not detailed enough to verify that non-scientific text is representative.
- Break condition: If non-scientific datasets are not diverse enough or scientificness is too subjective, the regression score becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Section classification improves with context because surrounding sentences provide structural and topical cues.
- Mechanism: Multi-label classifiers (BERT, SciBERT, WideMLP) take input sequences of 1-3 sentences and predict section labels based on both local and global context.
- Core assumption: Adjacent sentences in scientific papers tend to share section labels and provide disambiguating information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We study the impact of context, i. e., surrounding sentences, on the section classification performance."
  - [section] "We observe that the classification performance increases with more context provided (from 1-sentence to 3-sentences)."
  - [corpus] Weak - no explicit validation that the extracted sections are correctly labeled or complete.
- Break condition: If context length exceeds BERT's 512 token limit or if sentences are poorly extracted, classification accuracy drops.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Paraphrasing models can improve scientific writing style by learning from parallel datasets of original and corrupted sentences.
- Mechanism: T5 and BART models are fine-tuned to reconstruct original scientific sentences from corrupted versions (insertions, deletions, modifications).
- Core assumption: High-quality scientific text can be recovered from controlled degradations, and the models learn generalizable style improvements.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We fine-tune the language models BART [19] and T5 v1.1 [29] small, base, and large. We evaluate the sentence paraphrasing on these models as well as using Pegasus [42] and GPT-2 [28]."
  - [section] "The BLEU and METEOR scores improve with larger model sizes, i. e., the generated sentences are closer to the original sentences when larger models are used."
  - [corpus] Weak - the quality and diversity of the synthetic parallel corpus is not validated.
- Break condition: If the corruption process does not simulate realistic writing errors, the model cannot generalize to real writing issues.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Regression modeling for continuous score prediction
  - Why needed here: The scientificness task requires a continuous score rather than binary classification to capture varying degrees of scientific style.
  - Quick check question: What loss function is appropriate for training a regression model to predict scientificness scores between 0 and 1?

- Concept: Multi-label classification
  - Why needed here: Scientific sentences can belong to multiple sections (e.g., "introduction and related work"), requiring models to predict multiple labels simultaneously.
  - Quick check question: How does the threshold parameter λ affect the assignment of multiple labels in multi-label classification?

- Concept: Text-to-text transfer learning
  - Why needed here: Paraphrasing requires generating new text while preserving meaning, which is naturally framed as a sequence-to-sequence problem.
  - Quick check question: Why is beam search with width 5 used during paraphrase generation, and what trade-off does it represent?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: LaTeX extraction -> sentence splitting -> token replacement (<equation>, <reference>) -> filtering -> train/val/test split -> model fine-tuning -> evaluation
- Critical path: Data preprocessing -> model fine-tuning -> evaluation on held-out test sets
- Design tradeoffs: 
  - Using pre-trained models vs training from scratch (WideMLP)
  - Context length vs computational cost and model limitations
  - Synthetic corruption vs real parallel data for paraphrasing
- Failure signatures:
  - High MSE in scientificness indicates poor feature learning or label noise
  - Low F1 in section classification suggests insufficient context or label ambiguity
  - Low BLEU in paraphrasing means the model fails to reconstruct original meaning
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train BERT on scientificness task with 1% of data to verify learning capability
  2. Test section classification with 1-sentence input vs 3-sentence input on validation set
  3. Fine-tune T5 small on IDM-DS dataset and evaluate on held-out test set

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the scientificness scoring model be improved to better handle non-scientific text containing equations and citations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that adding equation and reference tokens to non-scientific text pushes the scientificness score towards more scientificness, but WideMLP relies less on these tokens than BERT and SciBERT.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows the effect of these tokens but does not explore ways to improve the model's handling of such cases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the model on a larger and more diverse dataset of non-scientific text with equations and citations, and comparing its performance to other models that may handle these cases better.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of the section classification model change when using a larger context size, such as a full paragraph or document?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors show that using a larger context size (up to three sentences) improves the performance of the section classification model, but they do not test the model on larger contexts.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the impact of using a larger context size on the model's performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the model on larger contexts and comparing its performance to the current model.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the paraphrasing model be improved to generate more diverse and creative paraphrases while maintaining the scientific style?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the paraphrasing model makes comparatively few alterations and produces output sentences close to the gold standard, but they do not explore ways to improve the model's creativity.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore ways to improve the model's ability to generate more diverse and creative paraphrases while maintaining the scientific style.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the model on a larger and more diverse dataset of scientific text and comparing its performance to other models that may generate more diverse and creative paraphrases.

## Limitations

- The scientificness scoring evaluation lacks rigorous validation of ground truth labels and corpus composition
- Paraphrasing evaluation relies entirely on synthetic data without real-world validation or human preference studies
- The paper does not address whether improvements translate to meaningful writing quality enhancements

## Confidence

- Scientificness Scoring: Medium confidence - The low MSE values are promising, but weak validation of ground truth labels limits confidence
- Section Classification: High confidence - Systematic evaluation with clear performance improvements provides strong evidence
- Paraphrasing: Low confidence - Exclusive reliance on synthetic data without real-world validation makes practical utility uncertain

## Next Checks

1. **Scientificness Label Validation**: Conduct inter-annotator agreement studies on a subset of scientific and non-scientific sentences to establish ground truth reliability, and perform ablation studies removing specific non-scientific datasets to determine which sources contribute most to model performance.

2. **Real-World Paraphrasing Evaluation**: Collect a dataset of actual scientific writing errors from peer review feedback or editing services, and evaluate the paraphrasing models on their ability to correct these real issues rather than synthetic corruptions.

3. **Cross-Domain Generalization Test**: Evaluate the trained models on scientific writing from different domains (e.g., biology vs computer science) or publication venues not represented in the training data to assess the generalizability of the learned scientific writing conventions.
