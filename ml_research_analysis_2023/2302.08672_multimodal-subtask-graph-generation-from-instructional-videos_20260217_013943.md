# Multimodal Subtask Graph Generation from Instructional Videos

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of modeling causal dependencies between subtasks from instructional videos, particularly when faced with missing steps and annotation noise. The authors propose Multimodal Subtask Graph Generation (MSG2), a novel approach that integrates visual and textual data to predict subtask states and infer subtask graphs. MSG2 employs a complexity-regularized inductive logic programming method to handle noisy data and generates subtask graphs that are more accurate than prior approaches.

## Method Summary
MSG2 tackles the problem of subtask graph generation from instructional videos by first predicting subtask states using multimodal fusion of visual frames and text transcripts, then inferring causal dependencies using complexity-regularized inductive logic programming. The method preprocesses video data by extracting CLIP features and verb phrases, trains a subtask state prediction module with a transformer-based architecture, and generates subtask graphs that capture causal relationships while being robust to noise and missing annotations.

## Key Results
- MSG2 improves next subtask prediction accuracy by 85% on ProceL and 30% on CrossTask datasets compared to video transformer models
- The method generates subtask graphs that are closer to human-annotated graphs than prior approaches
- MSG2 is more robust to incomplete and noisy data due to its complexity regularization and multimodal state prediction

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: MSG2 improves subtask graph generation by predicting missing subtask states using multimodal fusion of visual frames and text transcripts.
- Mechanism: The subtask state prediction network jointly processes visual and language data via multi-head attention and a transformer, producing state labels (not started, in progress, completed) that fill gaps in noisy annotations.
- Core assumption: Visual and textual modalities provide complementary cues for inferring missing subtask progression states.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] MSG2 uses a multimodal subtask state prediction module that makes use of video and text data to accurately infer the missing subtasks to improve the graph inference.
  - [section 4.1] Inspired by MERLOT (Zellers et al., 2021), we jointly process visual frames and language information to model subtask states.
- Break condition: If visual or textual cues are too ambiguous or conflicting, the state prediction may introduce new errors rather than correct existing noise.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Complexity-regularized inductive logic programming handles noisy and incomplete data better than standard ILP.
- Mechanism: Instead of maximizing accuracy (which leads to trivial always-true predictions given label bias), MSG2 optimizes precision and penalizes precondition complexity, avoiding overfitting to noise and producing non-trivial, more accurate subtask graphs.
- Core assumption: Noise in data often introduces spurious dependencies, so simpler preconditions are more likely to be correct.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] We modify Equation (6) and maximize the precision as described in Equation (7). The denominator penalizes the precondition being overly optimistic, which helps mitigate the effect of positive label bias in the data.
- Break condition: If the regularization weight is too high, it may oversimplify preconditions and miss true dependencies.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Recency weighting improves graph generation by prioritizing more recent evidence of subtask eligibility.
- Mechanism: When inferring preconditions, MSG2 assigns higher weight to data samples where a subtask became eligible more recently, improving the signal-to-noise ratio in the ILP training data.
- Core assumption: Recent evidence of eligibility is more reliable than older evidence in instructional videos where subtask order can vary.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section B.b] We propose to assign the weight to each data sample according to the recency; i.e., we assign higher weight if a subtask has become eligible more recently.
- Break condition: If videos are edited non-chronologically, recency weighting may mislead the precondition inference.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Video frames + ASR text transcripts → CLIP feature extractor → Frame and sentence embeddings → Multi-head attention + transformer → Joint multimodal embeddings → Subtask state prediction head → Predicted states for each subtask → Complexity-regularized ILP → Subtask graph with causal dependencies → Downstream: Next subtask prediction using the inferred graph
- Critical path: CLIP features → attention fusion → transformer → state prediction → ILP graph generation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using CLIP vs. training video-specific encoders: CLIP offers strong zero-shot transfer but may miss video-specific cues.
  - Complexity regularization vs. accuracy: Simpler preconditions avoid overfitting but risk missing real dependencies.
  - Multimodal fusion vs. single modality: Fusion improves robustness but adds complexity and training cost.
- Failure signatures:
  - If state prediction is noisy, graph generation will inherit and amplify errors.
  - If regularization is too strong, the generated graph will be too sparse to be useful.
  - If attention fusion fails, visual and textual cues will not complement each other, hurting state inference.

It also states that further improvements in multimodal subtask state modeling can help address these errors.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide specific methods or experiments to improve subtask state prediction beyond the current CLIP-based approach.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing MSG2's subtask state prediction accuracy against other multimodal approaches (e.g., using different vision-language models, incorporating additional modalities like audio or motion features) on the ProceL and CrossTask datasets.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the complexity-regularized inductive logic programming method handle noisy and incomplete data compared to other approaches?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that MSG2's complexity regularization term helps it be more robust to incomplete and noisy data compared to MSGI and MSGI+ baselines. It mentions that MSGI+ overfits to noise and predicts overly complicated preconditions, while MSGI ignores data points where precondition is unknown.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed comparison of MSG2's complexity-regularized ILP method against other ILP approaches or different regularization techniques for handling noisy data in subtask graph generation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing MSG2's graph generation accuracy against other ILP methods with different regularization strategies on datasets with varying levels of noise and incompleteness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the layer-wise precondition inference method prevent cycles in the subtask graph, and are there cases where it might fail?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explains that layer-wise precondition inference assigns subtasks to layers based on transition purity and infers preconditions in order of increasing depth to prevent cycles. However, it also mentions that this approach cannot guarantee to extract all parent-child relationships, especially when the precondition involves an OR relationship.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide examples or experiments demonstrating cases where the layer-wise approach might fail to prevent cycles or extract all parent-child relationships. It also does not discuss potential limitations or alternative methods for handling complex precondition structures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Case studies or experiments on tasks with complex precondition structures (e.g., involving OR relationships or parallel subtasks) to evaluate the effectiveness of the layer-wise approach in preventing cycles and capturing all parent-child relationships. Comparison with alternative methods for subtask graph inference, such as constraint satisfaction or reinforcement learning approaches.

## Limitations
- The complexity-regularized ILP method is not fully specified, particularly regarding the search algorithm and complexity measure used.
- The subtask state prediction module's exact architecture and training data augmentation techniques are not detailed.
- The method's performance on videos with significant non-chronological editing or highly variable subtask orderings is unclear.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The claim that multimodal fusion via attention improves subtask state prediction accuracy is well-supported by the evidence provided.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that complexity-regularized ILP handles noisy data better than standard ILP is plausible given the evidence, but the specific benefits may depend on the choice of regularization parameters and the nature of the noise in the data.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that recency weighting improves graph generation is supported by the proposed mechanism, but its effectiveness may vary depending on the characteristics of the instructional videos and the extent of non-chronological editing.
