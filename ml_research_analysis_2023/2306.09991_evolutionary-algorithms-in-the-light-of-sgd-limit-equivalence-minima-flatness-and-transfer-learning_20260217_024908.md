# Evolutionary Algorithms in the Light of SGD: Limit Equivalence, Minima Flatness, and Transfer Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.09991
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09991
- Reference count: 24
- This paper establishes formal equivalence between GO-EA and SGD in the low learning rate limit, with empirical validation on large ANNs.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a formal equivalence between a class of evolutionary algorithms (GO-EAs) and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in the low learning rate limit. The GO-EA class consists of algorithms that evaluate fitness changes from single parameter perturbations without aggregation. This equivalence reveals that population size in GO-EA corresponds to uniform anisotropic noise in SGD. The work demonstrates successful transfer of SGD hyperparameters to GO-EA, explores the relationship between minima flatness and feature redundancy, and investigates transfer learning dynamics. Experiments validate these findings on ConvNet architectures trained on MNIST.

## Method Summary
The paper trains ConvNet architectures on MNIST using both SGD and GO-EA algorithms to compare convergence behavior, minima flatness, and transfer learning performance. GO-EA operates by sampling population members, evaluating fitness changes from single parameter perturbations, and selecting the best update without aggregation. The theoretical framework establishes convergence to SGD gradients under low learning rate and high population conditions. Experiments measure minima flatness through random perturbation sweeps, analyze update vector properties, and test transfer learning after 8-class occlusion. The GitHub repository provides implementation details for reproduction.

## Key Results
- GO-EA updates converge to SGD gradients in mean under low learning rate and high population conditions
- Population size in GO-EA acts as uniform anisotropic noise in SGD, reducing curse of dimensionality
- Minima flatness relates more to feature redundancy than generalization ability

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GO-EA updates converge to SGD gradients in mean under low learning rate and high population.
- Mechanism: The algorithm samples update vectors uniformly in parameter space. With locally smooth loss, small steps, and infinite population, the best sampled vector converges to the true gradient direction.
- Core assumption: Loss surface is locally smooth (Lipschitz continuous gradient) and population size → ∞.
- Evidence anchors: Theorem 1 proof uses smoothness assumption and shows convergence in mean; abstract states formal equivalence in low learning rate limit.
- Break condition: If loss surface is non-smooth (e.g. ReLU networks with sparse activations), the local smoothness assumption fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Population size in GO-EA acts as uniform anisotropic noise in SGD, reducing the curse of dimensionality.
- Mechanism: Large population increases chance of sampling near the true gradient direction. For overparametrized models, valid update vectors are scattered, so only proximity to any valid vector is needed.
- Core assumption: Overparametrization makes loss landscape smoother and minima more connected, leading to scattered valid updates.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states population size corresponds to uniform anisotropic noise; empirical investigations show SGD updates can be orthogonal.
- Break condition: If model is underparametrized or dataset is small, valid updates may align more, requiring larger populations.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Flat minima arise from feature redundancy rather than generalization ability.
- Mechanism: Redundancy allows multiple parameter perturbations without changing output, creating flat regions. Transfer learning performance depends on feature overlap, not flatness.
- Core assumption: Flatness measures error correction capability, not generalization.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states minima flatness relates more to feature redundancy than generalization; hypothesis about error-correcting representations.
- Break condition: If redundancy doesn't improve robustness (e.g. task requires precise features), flatness may not correlate with redundancy.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Population size vs. computation: Larger populations better approximate gradients but cost more
  - Batch size in SGD: Smaller batches increase noise but may improve generalization
  - Model overparametrization: Increases landscape smoothness but also computational cost

- Failure signatures:
  - GO-EA not converging: Population too small or loss surface too non-smooth
  - SGD overfitting: Batch size too large, reducing noise benefits
  - Flatness measurements inconsistent: Perturbation magnitude too large for local approximation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the equivalence between GO-EA and SGD be extended to non-differentiable settings beyond hard attention in LLMs?
- Basis in paper: The paper suggests that leveraging existing knowledge regarding SGD in a non-differentiable setting opens up new possibilities for training large models with non-differentiable layers, specifically mentioning hard attention in LLMs.
- Why unresolved: The paper only speculates about the potential of GO-EAs in non-differentiable settings but does not provide concrete evidence or experiments beyond the MNIST task.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental validation of GO-EAs on non-differentiable tasks such as hard attention in LLMs or other non-differentiable architectures would provide concrete evidence for the broader applicability of the equivalence.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the precise relationship between minima flatness and feature redundancy in overparameterized models?
- Basis in paper: The paper hypothesizes that minima flatness is determined by the redundancy of features used by the trained ANN to recognize patterns, but the experiments only show correlation, not causation.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates that flatter minima have more redundant feature encoding, it does not establish a causal link or quantify the exact relationship between flatness and redundancy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments that manipulate feature redundancy (e.g., through dropout or architectural changes) while measuring both flatness and generalization performance would help establish causality.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does mixing update vectors in GO-EA ever provide benefits in specific scenarios?
- Basis in paper: The paper hypothesizes that mixing update vectors in GO-EA is unnecessary and potentially counterproductive, but the experiments only test a basic greedy algorithm without vector mixing.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests a simple GO-EA algorithm without vector mixing, so it cannot rule out potential benefits of mixing in other scenarios or with different loss landscapes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing GO-EA with and without vector mixing on diverse tasks and loss landscapes would determine if mixing ever provides benefits.

## Limitations

- The theoretical framework relies on smoothness assumptions that may not hold for networks with non-smooth activation functions like ReLU
- Empirical validation is limited to relatively small ConvNet architectures on MNIST, raising questions about scalability
- The relationship between flatness and feature redundancy, while intuitively appealing, lacks strong empirical evidence

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The theoretical equivalence between GO-EA and SGD in the low learning rate limit, supported by Theorem 1 and consistent with established optimization theory
- **Medium confidence**: The population size-noise equivalence in GO-EA and SGD, with reasonable empirical support but limited theoretical formalization
- **Low confidence**: The claim that flatness relates primarily to feature redundancy rather than generalization, based on intuitive reasoning but lacking strong empirical validation
