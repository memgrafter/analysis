# Balanced Supervised Contrastive Learning for Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.16687
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16687
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Proposes BSC loss with unified classifier initialization achieving state-of-the-art performance on FSCIL benchmarks

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses few-shot class-incremental learning (FSCIL) by proposing a balanced supervised contrastive (BSC) loss that emphasizes augmented image pairs to create more generalizable representations. The method introduces a unified classifier initialization strategy using mean feature vectors for both base and incremental classes, addressing the imbalance between base and new class classifiers. Through a two-stage training process combining pre-training with BSC loss and fine-tuning with knowledge distillation, the approach achieves significant improvements in preventing forgetting while learning new tasks across multiple datasets.

## Method Summary
The proposed method employs a two-stage training process: pre-training the feature extractor with balanced supervised contrastive (BSC) loss to learn generic representations, followed by fine-tuning with cross-entropy and cs-kd loss to acquire domain-specific knowledge. The BSC loss modifies standard supervised contrastive learning by emphasizing augmented image pairs through a balancing scale factor α, which controls the proportion of augmented images used for representation learning. Classifiers for both base and incremental sessions are initialized as the mean feature vector of their respective classes, ensuring consistent decision boundaries. The approach uses ResNet18 as the backbone and trains with 5-way 5-shot settings on CIFAR100 and miniImageNet, with 10-way 5-shot on CUB200.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art performance on CUB200, CIFAR100, and miniImageNet datasets across diverse metrics
- Significantly improves new task learning ability (NLA) while maintaining strong base task maintaining ability (BMA)
- Reduces performance drop (PD) compared to existing FSCIL methods
- Demonstrates effectiveness of balanced supervised contrastive learning for both viewable and unviewable classes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Balanced Supervised Contrastive (BSC) loss improves feature extractor generalization by increasing the proportion of augmented image pairs relative to same-class pairs in the contrastive learning objective.
- Mechanism: The BSC loss splits positives into two groups: positives-aug (augmented versions of the same image) and positives-diffsrc (different images of the same class). By applying a balancing scale factor α to emphasize positives-aug, the loss encourages features to capture domain-agnostic visual patterns while still maintaining class-specific information.
- Core assumption: Augmented image pairs provide stronger generalization signals than same-class pairs from different images because they enforce invariance to transformations while preserving semantic content.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose the balanced supervised contrastive (BSC) loss to control the proportion of augmented images used for representation learning, thereby balancing the effectiveness of the extracted representations for both viewable and unviewable classes."
  - [section 4.1]: Describes how BSC splits positives and applies balancing scale factors to emphasize augmented images.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Initializing both base and incremental session classifiers as the mean feature vector of their respective classes ensures consistent decision boundaries across sessions.
- Mechanism: By initializing classifiers as the average of features for each class, the method creates a unified initialization scheme that prevents classifier imbalance between base and incremental classes. This avoids situations where one set of classifiers dominates the decision space.
- Core assumption: Classifier initialization consistency is critical for maintaining balanced classification performance across base and incremental classes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "In terms of classifiers, we analyze and emphasize the importance of unifying initialization methods for both the base and incremental session classifiers."
  - [section 4.3]: Explains the classifier initialization strategy for incremental sessions using mean feature vectors.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The two-stage training process (pre-training for generic representations, then fine-tuning for domain-specific knowledge) prevents overfitting while maintaining generalization ability.
- Mechanism: Pre-training with BSC loss focuses on learning domain-agnostic visual patterns through contrastive learning. Fine-tuning then adapts these generic representations to the specific dataset using cross-entropy and knowledge distillation losses, transferring domain knowledge without losing generalization.
- Core assumption: Separating generic representation learning from domain-specific adaptation allows better control over the trade-off between generalization and specialization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Since the pre-training stage is focused on enhancing the generalization of representations rather than acquiring knowledge for the base session itself, we add a fine-tuning stage to update the feature extractor and base classifiers to acquire domain-specific knowledge."
  - [section 4.2]: Details the fine-tuning process with cs-kd loss for domain knowledge transfer.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Feature extractor (ResNet18) -> Projection head (2-layer MLP) -> Classifiers (mean feature vectors) -> Training pipeline (Pre-training -> Fine-tuning -> Incremental sessions)

- Critical path: Feature extractor training → Classifier initialization → Incremental learning
  The feature extractor must be pre-trained with BSC loss before any classifier initialization, and classifier initialization must occur before fine-tuning to ensure consistent decision boundaries.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Augmentation balance vs. class discrimination: Higher α in BSC emphasizes augmentation invariance but may reduce class-specific information
  - Pre-training duration vs. fine-tuning adaptation: Longer pre-training improves generalization but may require more fine-tuning to adapt to domain specifics
  - Classifier initialization method vs. flexibility: Mean initialization ensures consistency but may be suboptimal if feature quality is poor

- Failure signatures:
  - NLA much higher than BMA: Indicates the model is overfitting to new classes while forgetting base classes
  - Both NLA and BMA low: Suggests poor feature extractor quality or insufficient training
  - PD high but absolute accuracy low: May indicate the model is maintaining relative performance but starting from a low baseline

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test BSC with different α values (1.0, 1.5, 2.0) on CIFAR100 to find the optimal balance between augmentation emphasis and class discrimination
  2. Compare BSC with standard SupCon and SimCLR on miniImageNet to validate the effectiveness of the balanced approach
  3. Run ablation study removing the fine-tuning stage to quantify the importance of domain knowledge transfer

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the optimal value of α in BSC loss vary across different datasets and tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that increasing α value during feature extractor pre-training may help shift the attention from acquiring information specifically from the given annotations to increasing the generality of the extracted features, and they observe that as α increases, NLA increases while BMA decreases. They also identify the optimal α value to achieve the optimal PD value.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides results for a limited range of α values (1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0, 4.0) on the CIFAR100 dataset. It is unclear how the optimal α value generalizes to other datasets or tasks, or if there is a systematic way to determine the optimal α value for a given problem.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study of the effect of α on BSC performance across multiple datasets and tasks, potentially including a theoretical analysis of the relationship between α and the trade-off between NLA and BMA.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the proposed BSC loss compare to other self-supervised and supervised contrastive learning methods in terms of the generality and effectiveness of the learned representations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares BSC to SupCon, PaCo, and THANOS, and shows that BSC outperforms these methods in terms of NLA. However, it does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other self-supervised and supervised contrastive learning methods, such as SimCLR, MoCo, or BYOL.
- Why unresolved: There are many other contrastive learning methods that could potentially be applied to FSCIL, and it is unclear how BSC compares to these methods in terms of the generality and effectiveness of the learned representations.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive comparison of BSC with other self-supervised and supervised contrastive learning methods on a wide range of FSCIL benchmarks, including metrics for both the generality and effectiveness of the learned representations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed method perform on more challenging FSCIL settings, such as those with larger domain shifts between sessions or a larger number of classes and sessions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the proposed method may have limitations in more diverse settings, such as incremental sessions with datasets from different data distributions or settings with more incremental classes and sessions.
- Why unresolved: The experiments in the paper are limited to relatively simple FSCIL settings, and it is unclear how the proposed method would perform in more challenging scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on FSCIL benchmarks with larger domain shifts between sessions, a larger number of classes and sessions, or other challenging settings, such as open-set recognition or continual learning with task boundaries.

## Limitations

- The optimal value of the balancing factor α in BSC loss is not systematically explored across different datasets and tasks, limiting the generalizability of the findings.
- The proposed method has not been tested on more challenging FSCIL settings with larger domain shifts between sessions or a larger number of classes and sessions.
- The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other self-supervised and supervised contrastive learning methods, making it difficult to assess the relative effectiveness of BSC in learning generalizable representations.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The experimental methodology and evaluation metrics are clearly specified and reproducible. The claim that BSC loss improves representation generalization through augmentation emphasis is supported by theoretical foundations of contrastive learning.
- **Medium confidence**: The effectiveness of unified classifier initialization depends on the quality of feature representations, which is dataset-dependent. The two-stage training approach shows promise but requires careful hyperparameter tuning.
- **Low confidence**: Claims about the superiority of BSC over all existing FSCIL methods require validation across more diverse datasets and incremental scenarios beyond the three tested.
