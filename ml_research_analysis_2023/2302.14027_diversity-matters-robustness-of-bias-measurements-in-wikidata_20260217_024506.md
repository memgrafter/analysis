# Diversity matters: Robustness of bias measurements in Wikidata

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.14027
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14027
- Reference count: 38
- Key outcome: Societal biases in knowledge graphs vary significantly depending on the choice of demographic data and embedding algorithm used.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how gender bias in Wikidata occupations varies across 13 demographics and two embedding methods (TransE and ComplEx). The authors find that embedding algorithms not only reflect but actively transform inherent data bias, producing different bias rankings than raw data metrics. The rankings of biased occupations differ significantly between TransE and ComplEx, with Jaccard similarities often below 0.2. Additionally, ComplEx exhibits lower demographic overlap than TransE, suggesting it captures more culturally specific occupations. The findings highlight that embedding choice and demographic context critically affect bias detection, challenging the assumption of universal bias metrics in knowledge graph applications.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes gender bias in Wikidata occupations across 13 demographics using two embedding methods—TransE and ComplEx. The authors first extract demography-specific subgraphs from Wikidata, then train embeddings on the giant KG. They apply both a data bias metric and Fisher embedding bias metric to compute bias scores for each occupation within each demographic. The resulting bias rankings are compared using Jaccard similarity and rank deviation metrics to quantify how embedding algorithms transform inherent data bias.

## Key Results
- Embedding algorithm choice fundamentally alters bias ranking, with TransE and ComplEx producing characteristically different occupation orderings
- Demographic context shifts bias measurement sensitivity, with minimal overlap between biased occupation rankings across different cultures
- Data bias is algorithmically transformed rather than merely mirrored, as evidenced by positive/negative rank deviations between data bias and embedding bias rankings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Embedding algorithm choice fundamentally alters bias ranking.
- Mechanism: Different scoring functions (L1 vs dot product vs tensor decomposition) induce different latent vector geometries, which in turn reorder occupations by gender association.
- Core assumption: Embeddings preserve and amplify underlying data bias structure in algorithm-specific ways.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the rankings of biased occupations differ significantly between TransE and ComplEx, with Jaccard similarities often below 0.2"
  - [section 6.2] "TransE pulls up very generic occupations at the top of the list. In contrast, demography-specific occupations are ranked at higher positions in the list pulled up by the ComplEx embedding"
  - [corpus] "Embedding algorithm choice fundamentally alters bias ranking" - weak but consistent with claim
- Break condition: If embeddings are post-processed for fairness (e.g., adversarial debiasing), the intrinsic bias ordering would collapse.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Demographic context shifts bias measurement sensitivity.
- Mechanism: The subset of entities and relations for a given demography changes the statistical distribution of gender-occupation associations, leading to different bias score thresholds and rankings.
- Core assumption: Gender-occupation co-occurrence frequencies are not stable across cultures or geographies.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the similarity of the biased occupations across demographics is minimal which reflects the socio-cultural differences"
  - [section 6.3] "we found very little overlap between the two rankings of biased occupations obtained by the two embedding methods... For all values of 𝐾 (20, 50, and 80) we observed a similar trend of minimal overlap"
  - [corpus] "Demographic context shifts bias measurement sensitivity" - moderate evidence; study compares across 13 demographics explicitly
- Break condition: If occupations are universally gender-neutral by definition, the sensitivity to demographics would vanish.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Data bias is not merely mirrored but algorithmically transformed.
- Mechanism: The embedding training objective (negative sampling, loss function, optimization) modifies the original co-occurrence statistics into a transformed representation space, producing different bias rankings.
- Core assumption: Embedding learning is not a lossless transformation; it actively reweights relational evidence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "inherent data bias is altered by algorithmic bias, with embeddings introducing different bias rankings than raw data metrics"
  - [section 6.1] "the two rankings generated by the data bias metric and the embedding bias metric are characteristically different... A positive (negative) value of rank deviation indicates that occupations are ranked at a lower (higher) position in the second rank list"
  - [corpus] "Data bias is not merely mirrored but algorithmically transformed" - strong evidence; multiple algorithms tested
- Break condition: If embeddings are trained to preserve exact triple-wise scores, the transformation would be minimal.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Wikidata extraction pipeline → Demography-specific KG → Embedding training (TransE/ComplEx/DistMult) → Bias scoring (data bias metric + Fisher metric) → Ranking & similarity comparison
- Critical path:
  1. Build demography-specific subgraphs from Wikidata dump
  2. Train embeddings on the giant KG
  3. Apply Fisher bias metric per demography
  4. Compute similarity metrics across embeddings and demographics
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using English labels simplifies processing but may lose nuance in non-English entities
  - Training on the giant KG ensures shared representation but may dilute demographic specificity
  - Negative sampling size impacts training speed vs. embedding quality
- Failure signatures:
  - Low Hits@10 indicates poor link prediction, likely causing unreliable bias scores
  - Very high similarity (>0.5) across embeddings/demographics may signal insufficient model capacity or over-smoothing
  - Zero Jaccard similarity might suggest data sparsity or extreme cultural divergence
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify embedding quality: Check MRR/Hits@10 for each algorithm; ensure >0.7 Hits@10 before bias analysis
  2. Baseline bias comparison: Run data bias metric on all 13 demographics; confirm expected male/female occupation trends
  3. Embedding bias consistency: For one demography, compare bias rankings from TransE vs. ComplEx; expect Jaccard < 0.2

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the biases introduced by KG embedding algorithms compare when using multi-valued sensitive attributes beyond gender (e.g., ethnicity, religion, nationality)?
- Basis in paper: explicit - The authors note that future work will extend their approach to multi-valued sensitive attributes to demonstrate fine-grained bias distribution globally.
- Why unresolved: The current study only uses gender as the sensitive attribute. The authors explicitly state they will explore multi-valued attributes in future work.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting similar experiments with other sensitive attributes and comparing the resulting bias rankings and overlaps across different demographics and embedding algorithms.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between the socio-cultural and geographical proximity of demographics and the similarity of their biased occupation rankings across different embedding algorithms?
- Basis in paper: explicit - The authors manually inspect pairs of demographics to determine if their similarity is due to socio-cultural or geographical closeness, finding examples of both patterns.
- Why unresolved: While the authors provide some examples, they do not systematically quantify or model the relationship between demographic proximity and ranking similarity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing a quantitative model that measures socio-cultural and geographical distances between demographics and correlates these with the similarity of their biased occupation rankings across embedding algorithms.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the biases in KG embeddings affect the performance of downstream applications across different demographics and embedding algorithms?
- Basis in paper: explicit - The authors note that conclusions drawn from KG studies and downstream application performance would vary based on embedding algorithm and demographic data variance, highlighting this as a serious concern.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on measuring biases in embeddings but does not examine how these biases actually impact downstream task performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments that use the biased embeddings in specific downstream tasks (e.g., recommendation systems, question answering) and measuring performance differences across demographics and embedding algorithms.

## Limitations
- The study's findings rely on English-language labels, potentially missing cultural nuances encoded in native language labels for non-English demographics.
- The threshold (t) for determining biased occupations is not specified, which could affect the stability of bias rankings across different datasets.
- Negative sampling size and optimization parameters for embedding training are not detailed, leaving room for variability in results.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Embedding algorithm choice fundamentally alters bias ranking (supported by multiple direct quotes and empirical comparisons).
- **Medium Confidence**: Demographic context shifts bias measurement sensitivity (evidence is consistent but relies on cross-demographic comparisons).
- **Medium Confidence**: Data bias is algorithmically transformed (supported by rank deviation analysis, but transformation mechanism is not fully detailed).
