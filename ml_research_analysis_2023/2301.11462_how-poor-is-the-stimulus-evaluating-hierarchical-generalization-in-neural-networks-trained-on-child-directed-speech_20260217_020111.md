# How poor is the stimulus? Evaluating hierarchical generalization in neural networks trained on child-directed speech

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.11462
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11462
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Neural networks trained on child-directed speech fail to learn correct hierarchical rules for English yes/no questions, instead relying on linear generalizations or lexical-specific rules.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether neural networks trained on child-directed speech can learn the hierarchical rule for English yes/no questions or if they instead rely on non-hierarchical generalizations. The authors train LSTMs and Transformers on CHILDES corpus data and evaluate their generalization on yes/no questions, which require hierarchical structure. They find that while models capture surface statistics well (perplexity ~20), they fail to learn the correct hierarchical rule. Instead, they prefer linear generalizations or lexical-specific rules. Models trained to transform declaratives into questions also fail, even when given access to both next-word prediction and question formation tasks. The results suggest that human-like hierarchical generalization from text alone requires stronger inductive biases than those of standard neural architectures, supporting the idea that innate hierarchical biases may be necessary for language acquisition.

## Method Summary
The authors train LSTM and Transformer models on the CHILDES corpus (9.6 million words of North American English, excluding child utterances) using next-word prediction. They evaluate model performance using perplexity on general text and targeted evaluation on yes/no question datasets generated using context-free grammars. For yes/no questions, they create 6 variants of each declarative sentence by preposing/deleting first or main auxiliaries, then measure model preferences for hierarchical (prepose main, delete main) versus linear (prepose first, delete main) question types. They also train models to transform declaratives into questions and test whether dual-task training improves hierarchical learning.

## Key Results
- Models achieve low perplexity (~20) on CHILDES text, indicating strong capture of surface statistics
- Despite strong general performance, models fail to learn correct hierarchical rules for yes/no questions
- Models systematically prefer incorrect linear generalizations or lexical-specific rules over the correct hierarchical rule
- Dual-task training (next-word prediction + question formation) does not improve hierarchical generalization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Models trained on CHILDES text fail to learn hierarchical yes/no question formation rules because their architecture lacks inherent structural bias.
- Mechanism: LSTMs and Transformers process sequences purely based on statistical co-occurrence without built-in hierarchical representations. Without explicit syntactic structure, models default to surface-level generalizations like linear order or lexical identity.
- Core assumption: Hierarchical structure is not sufficiently encoded in word sequences alone to override default linear processing mechanisms.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we find that, though they perform well at capturing the surface statistics of child-directed speech... both model types generalize in a way more consistent with an incorrect linear rule than the correct hierarchical rule."
  - [section] "LSTMs and Transformers are the best at modeling the probabilistic structure of linguistic data. Therefore, if child-directed speech contains clear evidence for the hierarchical nature of yes/no questions... it is likely that LSTMs and Transformers would be among the set of general-purpose learners that could use this evidence to make hierarchical generalizations"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows related work on hierarchical biases but no direct evidence about CHILDES-specific signal strength.
- Break condition: If additional input modalities (visual, prosodic) or architectural changes (stack-augmented networks, recursive structures) enable hierarchical generalization.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Even with access to both next-word prediction and question formation tasks, models cannot learn the correct hierarchical rule for yes/no questions.
- Mechanism: Dual-task training exposes models to both surface statistics and sentence-pair transformations, but the architectural limitation prevents learning the correspondence between declaratives and questions based on hierarchical structure.
- Core assumption: The CHILDES training data contains sufficient examples of yes/no questions for a learner with appropriate inductive biases to extract the hierarchical rule.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Models trained to transform declaratives into questions also fail, even when given access to both next-word prediction and question formation tasks."
  - [section] "Our results provide evidence that this conclusion does not extend to models trained on child-directed speech" (referring to prior work showing large corpus benefits)
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains related work but no specific evidence about CHILDES question distribution adequacy.
- Break condition: If models are given explicit hierarchical structure (e.g., parse trees) during training or use architectures with built-in hierarchical processing.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Models' failure to learn hierarchical generalization supports the poverty of the stimulus argument for innate linguistic biases.
- Mechanism: Since low-bias neural architectures fail to extract hierarchical rules from CHILDES data despite strong in-distribution performance, this suggests children must have stronger inductive biases to achieve human-like generalization.
- Core assumption: The CHILDES corpus provides data comparable in quantity and quality to what children actually receive.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "These results suggest that human-like generalization from text alone requires stronger biases than the general sequence-processing biases of standard neural network architectures"
  - [section] "The size of our training set was plausibly within the range from which children can acquire HIERARCHICAL Q... By age 3, American children receive approximately 10 to 33 million words of input... the 8.5 million words of our training set is close to the lower end of that range"
  - [corpus] Moderate - corpus shows related work on poverty of stimulus but no direct evidence about CHILDES word count adequacy.
- Break condition: If alternative explanations (e.g., different evaluation metrics, lexical specificity) account for model failures without requiring innate biases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific architectural features or inductive biases would enable neural networks to learn the hierarchical generalization for yes/no questions when trained on child-directed speech?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that current LSTMs and Transformers fail to learn the hierarchical rule despite capturing surface statistics well, suggesting that stronger inductive biases may be necessary.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not identify specific architectural modifications or inductive biases that would solve this problem, leaving it as an open research direction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing various neural architectures with different built-in hierarchical structures (e.g., tree-structured networks, models with explicit syntactic representations) trained on the same CHILDES data, measuring their ability to learn the correct hierarchical rule for yes/no questions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the quantity of training data affect neural networks' ability to learn hierarchical generalizations from child-directed speech?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that the 8.5 million words used in their experiments is close to the lower end of the range children receive (10-33 million words), and that future work should investigate ways to augment the data.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested one quantity of training data, leaving open the question of whether more data would enable better hierarchical generalization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training experiments varying the amount of CHILDES data from minimal to maximum available, measuring performance on yes/no question tasks and comparing results to child language acquisition benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What role do visual and prosodic cues play in enabling hierarchical generalization that cannot be captured from text alone?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that children receive multimodal input (prosody, visual information, meaning) that correlates with syntactic structure, which was absent from their text-only experiments.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested text-based models, leaving open whether multimodal learning would enable hierarchical generalization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training neural networks on multimodal child-directed speech data (text + prosody + visual context), then evaluating their ability to learn hierarchical rules for yes/no questions compared to text-only models.

## Limitations

- The evaluation relies on CFG-generated yes/no question datasets that may not fully capture the complexity and variability of natural yes/no questions in child-directed speech.
- The study assumes CHILDES provides representative data comparable to what children actually receive, though the corpus composition and word count adequacy remain uncertain.
- Alternative explanations for model failures (e.g., lexical specificity, evaluation bias) are not fully ruled out, leaving open the possibility that different training approaches could enable hierarchical learning without requiring innate biases.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Models trained on CHILDES achieve low perplexity (~20) but fail to learn correct hierarchical rules for yes/no questions, preferring linear generalizations or lexical-specific rules instead.

**Medium Confidence**: The CHILDES corpus size (8.5 million words) plausibly falls within the range of children's linguistic input, making the poverty of the stimulus argument relevant for explaining model failures.

**Low Confidence**: The conclusion that neural networks cannot learn hierarchical generalization from text alone requires stronger inductive biases than standard architectures provide - this assumes no architectural modifications or additional input modalities could enable such learning.

## Next Checks

1. **Test with alternative architectures**: Evaluate whether stack-augmented networks or models with explicit hierarchical structure (e.g., recursive neural networks) can learn the correct yes/no question rules from the same CHILDES data.

2. **Cross-linguistic validation**: Apply the same methodology to languages with different question formation rules to determine if the failure generalizes across linguistic structures or is specific to English.

3. **Augmented input experiments**: Test whether adding prosodic information, visual context, or semantic cues alongside text enables models to learn hierarchical generalization without changing the core architecture.
