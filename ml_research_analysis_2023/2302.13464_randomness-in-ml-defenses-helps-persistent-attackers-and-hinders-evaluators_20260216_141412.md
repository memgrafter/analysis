# Randomness in ML Defenses Helps Persistent Attackers and Hinders Evaluators

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.13464
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13464
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 23
- Key outcome: Randomness is neither necessary nor sufficient for designing robust ML defenses; deterministic variants can match empirical robustness while being more evaluable

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the conventional wisdom that randomness improves adversarial robustness in machine learning defenses. Through systematic analysis of three randomized defenses (Randomized Smoothing, EBM-Def, and DiffPure), the authors demonstrate that deterministic variants can achieve similar empirical robustness. The work introduces the "Nag Factor" threat model, showing that repeated queries actually improve attacker success against random defenses compared to deterministic ones. Additionally, the authors present Subspace Grid-sweep, a new evaluation framework that better identifies adversarial regions by leveraging the deterministic nature of defenses.

## Method Summary
The authors construct deterministic variants of three randomized defenses by fixing random seeds, then compare their empirical robustness against white-box attacks. They evaluate defenses using PGD attacks and AutoAttack, and introduce Subspace Grid-sweep to systematically search for adversarial regions in lower-dimensional subspaces. The framework compares grid-sweep, random sampling, and gradient-guided attacks to identify cases where defenses may be using gradient obfuscation rather than genuinely reducing adversarial vulnerability.

## Key Results
- Deterministic variants of randomized defenses achieve similar empirical robustness to their random counterparts
- The Nag Factor threat model shows repeated querying increases attacker success probability against random defenses
- Subspace Grid-sweep identifies adversarial regions that gradient-based methods miss, revealing potential gradient obfuscation in some defenses
- Robustness in examined defenses emerges from deterministic mechanisms rather than randomness itself

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Deterministic defenses can match the empirical robustness of randomized defenses against white-box attacks.
- Mechanism: Robustness emerges from deterministic mechanisms like ensemble averaging or deterministic refinement rather than from randomness. The deterministic variant maintains the core robustness properties while eliminating randomness that can be exploited by repeated queries.
- Core assumption: The deterministic variant preserves the key structural properties that provide robustness (e.g., self-ensembling, energy-based refinement, diffusion denoising).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Overall, we demonstrate that deterministic defenses have similar or equal empirical robustness to the originally published randomized defenses."
  - [section] "As these examples show, robustness exhibited by these defenses emerges from a deterministic characteristic or mechanism and not as the result of any randomness in the defense."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus focuses on other defenses like backdoor detection, not directly comparable.
- Break condition: If the deterministic variant fails to preserve critical structural elements that provide robustness, or if randomness itself contributes unique robustness properties not captured by the deterministic version.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Repeated querying reduces the effectiveness of randomized defenses compared to deterministic ones.
- Mechanism: In randomized defenses, each query uses different random transformations or corruptions, allowing an attacker to eventually find an input that succeeds across all possible randomness variations. Deterministic defenses use the same transformations for each query, so success or failure is consistent.
- Core assumption: The randomness in defenses creates variability that can be exploited through repeated attempts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "For a classiﬁer f let p(x,y) = Prr∼R[fθ (x; r) = y] be the probability the stochastic classiﬁer correctly assigns the label y to example x with randomness r."
  - [section] "Therefore, we are guaranteed that pN (x,y)≤ q(x,y), with the inequality being a strict inequality any time p(x,y)< 1."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't directly address repeated query attacks.
- Break condition: If the randomness in the defense creates such strong noise that even multiple attempts cannot overcome it, or if the defense includes mechanisms to detect and block repeated queries.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Subspace grid-sweep provides more comprehensive adversarial region discovery than gradient-based methods.
- Mechanism: By directly searching a discretized subspace with a grid pattern and random sampling, the method can find adversarial regions that gradient-based methods miss, especially in cases of gradient obfuscation.
- Core assumption: Adversarial regions can be discovered through systematic grid search and random sampling in lower-dimensional subspaces.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "By evaluating with several datapoints, comparing these different methods of ﬁnding adversarial regions should be able to reveal whether gradient-guided methods are having difﬁculty ﬁnding adversarial regions that grid-sweep or random sampling can ﬁnd."
  - [section] "If this is the case, then it is likely the defense is not reducing the prevalence of adversarial regions, but simply making them harder to ﬁnd via gradients (i.e., obfuscated gradients)."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus focuses on other attack/defense types not directly related to subspace grid-sweep.
- Break condition: If the dimensionality of the subspace is too low to capture relevant adversarial regions, or if the discretization is too coarse to detect subtle adversarial perturbations.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input preprocessing (random vs deterministic transformations) -> Base classifier -> Aggregation mechanism (for randomized defenses) -> Attack evaluation framework

- Critical path:
  1. Create deterministic variant of randomized defense
  2. Evaluate both versions against PGD attacks
  3. Implement subspace grid-sweep for comprehensive robustness evaluation
  4. Compare results and identify robustness sources

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Randomness vs determinism: Randomness provides theoretical certificates but can be exploited by repeated queries; determinism is more predictable but may lose theoretical guarantees.
  - Grid-sweep vs gradient-based attacks: Grid-sweep is more comprehensive but computationally expensive; gradient-based attacks are efficient but can be misled by gradient obfuscation.

- Failure signatures:
  - Deterministic variant shows significantly lower robustness than randomized version (suggests randomness was contributing to robustness)
  - Gradient-based attacks find all adversarial regions that grid-sweep finds (suggests no gradient obfuscation)
  - Repeated queries do not improve attack success rate on randomized defenses (suggests strong randomness effects)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement deterministic smoothing and compare against randomized smoothing with PGD attacks on CIFAR-10
  2. Create deterministic variants of EBM-Def and DiffPure and evaluate their robustness
  3. Apply subspace grid-sweep to a known defense with obfuscated gradients (like k-Winners-Take-All) to validate the method

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does randomness in ML defenses provide any theoretical security guarantees that cannot be replicated by deterministic methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that Randomized Smoothing loses its robustness certificate when made deterministic, yet empirical robustness remains similar
- Why unresolved: The paper shows empirical equivalence but does not explore whether there are theoretical security properties that randomness uniquely provides beyond what deterministic methods can achieve
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of theoretical security guarantees between randomized and deterministic defenses across multiple threat models

### Open Question 2
- Question: Under what specific conditions does the Nag Factor threat model manifest most severely?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces the Nag Factor where repeated querying increases success probability against random defenses, but doesn't quantify the threshold at which this becomes problematic
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the existence of the vulnerability but doesn't provide a detailed analysis of when and how severely this vulnerability manifests across different types of defenses
- What evidence would resolve it: Quantitative analysis mapping attack success probability to number of queries across various defense types and configurations

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does Subspace Grid-sweep performance scale with input dimensionality and subspace dimensionality?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper introduces Subspace Grid-sweep as a method for evaluating deterministic defenses but only tests on relatively low-dimensional subspaces
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates effectiveness on tested subspaces but doesn't establish scalability limits or provide guidance on optimal subspace dimensionality selection
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of Subspace Grid-sweep performance across varying input and subspace dimensions with computational cost analysis

## Limitations
- The deterministic variants may not fully capture all properties of the original randomized defenses
- The Subspace Grid-sweep framework requires further validation across diverse defense types and dimensionality settings
- The study focuses primarily on white-box attacks, with limited exploration of other threat models
