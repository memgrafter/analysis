# Ask an Expert: Leveraging Language Models to Improve Strategic Reasoning in Goal-Oriented Dialogue Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17878
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17878
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: BlenderBot models using "Ask an Expert" approach achieve 10% improvement in human evaluation scores over baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a framework called "Ask an Expert" that improves dialogue models by incorporating structured advice from large language models (LLMs) during training and inference. The approach involves prompting LLMs to provide reasoning about emotional status, causes, and suggestions, which is then fed into the dialogue model as context. The framework is evaluated on the ESConv dataset for mental health support, showing that models using expert reasoning produce more engaging, empathetic, and helpful responses compared to baselines.

## Method Summary
The method fine-tunes BlenderBot 2.7B on both general conversational data (BlendedSkillTalks) and mental health support data (ESConv) with LLM-generated reasoning annotations. During training, the model receives concatenated input of seeker utterances and structured reasoning from the LLM expert. At inference time, the model interfaces with LLM experts to obtain ad-hoc reasoning annotations. The approach uses text-generation style prompts to elicit reasoning processes covering emotional status, cause identification, and suggestion generation.

## Key Results
- BBMHR models (BlenderBot with mental health reasoning) outperform BBMH and BB models on most evaluation dimensions
- BBMHR + davinci achieves the best performance overall with 10% improvement in human evaluation scores
- The improvement scales with the size of the LLM expert, with even smaller experts outperforming models without expert advice

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The dialogue model learns to selectively use expert advice through training with both dialogue and expert reasoning as context.
- Core assumption: The model can distinguish between useful and irrelevant expert advice and selectively integrate it into responses.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Advice is solicited via a structured dialogue with the expert, and the model is optimized to selectively utilize (or ignore) it given the context and dialogue history."
  - [section]: "The dialogue model is trained to optimize dialogue quality while working together with the expert suggestions, and can therefore learn how best to make use of advice in a context-specific manner."
- Break condition: If the reasoning process is too noisy or irrelevant, the model may learn to ignore it entirely, negating the benefit.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Larger LLM experts provide better reasoning processes, leading to improved dialogue model performance.
- Core assumption: Larger models have better knowledge and reasoning capabilities that translate into more useful advice.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The benefit scales with the size of the LLM expert, and even smaller experts outperform models without expert advice."
  - [section]: "We find a strong correlation with the degree of improvement on these metrics and the size of the model."
- Break condition: If the dialogue model cannot effectively process or utilize the richer reasoning, performance gains may plateau or diminish.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Prompt design style affects the quality and relevance of reasoning processes generated by the LLM expert.
- Core assumption: The format of the prompt influences how well the LLM generates useful reasoning information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Results of our initial experiments comparing the two prompt styles can be found in Appendix A. The remainder of the experiments in this paper use TG-style prompts."
- Break condition: If prompt design is suboptimal, the LLM may generate reasoning that is irrelevant or unhelpful to the dialogue model.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Prompt engineering for structured knowledge extraction
  - Why needed here: The quality of reasoning processes depends heavily on how prompts are designed to elicit specific types of information from the LLM expert.
  - Quick check question: How would you modify a prompt to encourage the LLM to generate more specific suggestions rather than general advice?

- Concept: Multi-task learning for domain adaptation
  - Why needed here: The dialogue model is fine-tuned on both general conversational data and domain-specific mental health support data with reasoning annotations.
  - Quick check question: Why is it beneficial to train on both general dialogue data and specialized mental health support data with reasoning?

- Concept: Context window management in transformer models
  - Why needed here: The approach requires concatenating dialogue history with reasoning processes, which must fit within the model's context window.
  - Quick check question: How would you handle a situation where the combined dialogue history and reasoning process exceeds the context window limit?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Dialogue model (BlenderBot 2.7B) -> Expert LLM (GPT variants) -> Prompt templates -> Training pipeline -> Inference pipeline

- Critical path:
  1. At each dialogue turn, the current context is sent to the expert LLM with appropriate prompts
  2. The expert generates reasoning (emotion, cause, suggestion) through multi-turn conversation
  3. The reasoning is formatted and concatenated with the dialogue context
  4. The dialogue model processes this combined input to generate the next response
  5. During training, this process is repeated with collected expert annotations

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Expert model size vs. deployment feasibility: Larger experts provide better reasoning but are harder to deploy
  - Prompt complexity vs. inference speed: More detailed prompts yield better reasoning but increase latency
  - Multi-turn expert conversation vs. single-turn: Multi-turn allows for richer reasoning but increases computation

- Failure signatures:
  - Short, non-empathetic responses at conversation openings (insufficient context for expert)
  - Repetitive strategies that ignore expert reasoning (overfitting to training patterns)
  - Degradation in performance as conversation history grows (context window limitations)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare dialogue model performance with and without expert consultation on a small validation set
  2. Test different prompt styles (QA vs. TG) to determine which yields more useful reasoning
  3. Evaluate performance across different expert model sizes (GPT1, GPT2, GPT3 variants) to establish scaling relationships

## Limitations

- Context Window Constraints: The approach requires concatenating dialogue history with expert reasoning, which can quickly exceed transformer context window limits.
- Domain-Specific Performance: The framework shows strong results on mental health support but lacks evidence for performance on other dialogue domains.
- Real-time Expert Dependency: The inference-time expert consultation creates a dependency on LLM availability and introduces latency.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The claim that incorporating expert reasoning improves dialogue quality is well-supported by human evaluation results showing 10% improvement over baselines.
- Medium Confidence: The claim that benefits scale with LLM expert size has supporting evidence but lacks systematic ablation studies across a broader range of model sizes.
- Low Confidence: The assertion that prompt design style (TG vs QA) significantly affects reasoning quality is based on preliminary experiments mentioned only in Appendix A.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation Study on Context Window Management**: Systematically test the approach's performance as conversation length increases to quantify the degradation point and evaluate different context truncation strategies.

2. **Cross-Domain Transfer Evaluation**: Apply the trained models to dialogue datasets from different domains (customer service, general conversation, technical support) to assess generalization beyond the mental health domain.

3. **Expert Availability Stress Testing**: Evaluate model performance when expert consultation is delayed, partially available, or fails entirely to understand the robustness of the framework under realistic deployment conditions.
