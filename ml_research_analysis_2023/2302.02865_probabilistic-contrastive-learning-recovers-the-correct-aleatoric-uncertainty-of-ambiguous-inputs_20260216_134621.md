# Probabilistic Contrastive Learning Recovers the Correct Aleatoric Uncertainty of Ambiguous Inputs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02865
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02865
- Reference count: 25
- Key outcome: MCInfoNCE recovers true posteriors up to rotation, enabling uncertainty-aware embeddings

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of learning representations that capture aleatoric uncertainty in ambiguous inputs by extending nonlinear Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to probabilistic generative processes. The authors propose Monte Carlo InfoNCE (MCInfoNCE), a contrastive learning objective that trains encoders to predict probabilistic embeddings—distributions over possible latents—instead of point estimates. They prove that MCInfoNCE recovers the correct posteriors of the data-generating process, including the true level of aleatoric uncertainty, up to a rotation of the latent space. Empirically, the predicted uncertainties align with human annotator disagreement and information loss (e.g., image cropping) in CIFAR-10H, with rank correlations of 0.33 and 0.70 respectively.

## Method Summary
The method extends InfoNCE to handle probabilistic embeddings by minimizing cross-entropy between predicted and true marginal distributions. The encoder predicts vMF (von Mises-Fisher) posterior parameters (location and concentration) for each input, representing both the most likely latent and associated uncertainty. Training uses Monte Carlo sampling to approximate the contrastive loss, comparing predicted distributions to ground truth generated by a known process. The approach is evaluated on synthetic data with controlled posteriors and CIFAR-10H, measuring alignment between predicted and true uncertainties.

## Key Results
- MCInfoNCE recovers correct vMF parameters up to rotation with high rank correlation in controlled experiments
- Predicted certainties correlate with human annotator disagreement (rank correlation 0.33) and image cropping information loss (rank correlation 0.70) on CIFAR-10H
- Credible intervals computed from predicted posteriors enable uncertainty-aware image retrieval with qualitative success

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MCInfoNCE recovers the correct posterior distributions of the generative process up to a rotation.
- **Mechanism:** The loss function minimizes the cross-entropy between the predicted marginal distribution of the encoder and the true marginal distribution implied by the generative process. By analyzing the form of the marginal as a function of the predicted location and concentration parameters, it can be shown that the only way to minimize the loss is for the predicted parameters to match the true ones (up to a rotation).
- **Core assumption:** The generative process is modeled as a conditional distribution P(x|z) that results in vMF posteriors P(z|x), and the encoder parametrizes distributions of the same family.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "We prove that MCInfoNCE attains its global minimum when the encoder recovers the true posteriors of the data-generating process, up to a rotation of the latent space."
  - [section]: Proposition 4.1 and Theorem 4.4 provide the formal proof that the loss is minimized when the predicted marginals match the true marginals, which implies the predicted posteriors are correct up to a rotation.
- **Break condition:** If the generative process does not result in vMF posteriors, or if the encoder uses a different distribution family, the proof does not directly apply. However, empirical results suggest some robustness.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The predicted certainty values (concentration parameters) reflect the true level of aleatoric uncertainty in the data.
- **Mechanism:** The concentration parameter κ(x) of the vMF posterior represents the inverse variance, so a lower κ(x) indicates higher uncertainty. By training with MCInfoNCE, the encoder learns to predict κ(x) values that match those of the generative process, which encode the true aleatoric uncertainty due to information loss.
- **Core assumption:** The aleatoric uncertainty in the data is captured by the generative process posteriors, and the vMF family is appropriate for modeling this uncertainty.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "We prove that MCInfoNCE attains its global minimum when the encoder recovers the true posteriors... including its level of aleatoric uncertainty."
  - [section]: Empirical results in Table 1 and Figure 2 show that MCInfoNCE learns the correct κ(x) values up to a high rank correlation, and Table 4 shows these predicted certainties correlate with human annotator disagreement and information reduction.
- **Break condition:** If the true aleatoric uncertainty is not well-modeled by the vMF family, or if the contrastive learning setup does not provide sufficient signal, the predicted certainties may not accurately reflect the true uncertainty.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The predicted posteriors enable practical applications like computing credible intervals for image retrieval.
- **Mechanism:** Since the encoder predicts a full distribution P(z|x) instead of a point estimate, we can define credible intervals in the latent space that contain the true latent with a given probability. These intervals can be visualized by finding images whose modal latent values fall within the interval.
- **Core assumption:** The predicted posteriors are accurate and the vMF distribution is appropriate for defining intervals in the latent space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "In addition to providing calibrated uncertainty estimates, these posteriors allow the computation of credible intervals in image retrieval."
  - [section]: Figure 6 shows examples of credible intervals for different query images, demonstrating how the intervals capture the uncertainty in the latents.
- **Break condition:** If the predicted posteriors are inaccurate, the credible intervals will not correctly capture the uncertainty. Additionally, if the latent space dimensionality is too high, computing and visualizing intervals may become challenging.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Independent Component Analysis (ICA)
  - Why needed here: ICA provides the theoretical framework for understanding how contrastive learning can recover the latent variables that generated the data. The paper builds on recent results showing that InfoNCE can invert a deterministic, injective generative process.
  - Quick check question: What is the main goal of ICA, and how does it relate to the task of recovering latent variables from observations?

- **Concept:** Contrastive Learning
  - Why needed here: Contrastive learning is the training paradigm used to learn the encoder that predicts the latent distributions. The paper extends the InfoNCE loss to handle probabilistic embeddings.
  - Quick check question: How does the InfoNCE loss encourage the encoder to produce similar embeddings for positive pairs and dissimilar embeddings for negative pairs?

- **Concept:** Probabilistic Embeddings
  - Why needed here: Instead of predicting point estimates in the latent space, the encoder predicts distributions (vMF posteriors) that capture both the most likely latent and the uncertainty around it. This is crucial for handling ambiguous inputs.
  - Quick check question: What is the advantage of predicting a distribution over the latent space instead of a single point, especially in the context of ambiguous inputs?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Generative process (MLPs) -> Encoder (MLPs) -> MCInfoNCE loss -> Evaluation metrics
- **Critical path:**
  1. Sample observations x and their corresponding latents z from the generative process
  2. Pass x through the encoder to get predicted vMF parameters
  3. Sample from the predicted and true distributions to compute the MCInfoNCE loss
  4. Backpropagate the loss to update the encoder parameters
  5. Evaluate the predicted parameters against the true parameters and on downstream tasks
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Latent space dimensionality: Higher dimensions may capture more complex data but increase computational cost and make rejection sampling less efficient
  - Number of Monte Carlo samples: More samples give a better approximation of the loss but increase computation time
  - Number of negative samples: More negatives can improve the quality of the learned representations but also increase computation
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High RMSE between predicted and true κ(x) values, especially for high uncertainty cases
  - Low rank correlation between predicted and true parameters
  - Poor performance on downstream tasks like image retrieval or uncertainty quantification
  - Instability during training, such as exploding or vanishing gradients
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Verify that MCInfoNCE recovers the correct vMF parameters on a simple synthetic dataset with known posteriors
  2. Test the robustness of MCInfoNCE to misspecified latent space dimensionality or non-vMF generative posteriors
  3. Evaluate the quality of the predicted uncertainties on a real dataset like CIFAR-10H, comparing to human annotator disagreement and information loss

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does MCInfoNCE perform when the generative process has a posterior distribution family that is fundamentally different from vMF (e.g., mixture distributions or heavy-tailed distributions)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper tests MCInfoNCE on Gaussian and Laplace posteriors, showing reasonable performance, but does not explore more complex distributions like mixtures or heavy tails.
- Why unresolved: The controlled experiments focus on unimodal distributions (vMF, Gaussian, Laplace), leaving uncertainty about performance on multimodal or heavy-tailed posteriors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing MCInfoNCE predictions against known posteriors of mixture models or heavy-tailed distributions, measuring both location and concentration accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between MCInfoNCE and the Expected Likelihood Kernel (ELK) loss, and under what conditions do they converge to the same solution?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that ELK also recovers correct posteriors in controlled experiments but notes this is the "first confirmation" and calls for "future theoretical investigations."
- Why unresolved: While empirical results suggest ELK works, the paper does not provide theoretical justification for why ELK should recover correct posteriors up to rotation.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal proof showing conditions under which MCInfoNCE and ELK have equivalent global minima, or counterexamples demonstrating their divergence.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the bias in the marginal likelihood approximation affect downstream tasks like image retrieval or uncertainty-aware prediction, and what sampling strategies minimize this bias?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies that the MC approximation introduces bias in estimating ˆκ(x), particularly for high concentration values, and shows this bias decreases with more samples.
- Why unresolved: The paper quantifies the bias but does not investigate its practical impact on retrieval performance or task-specific utility.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies measuring retrieval performance or uncertainty calibration quality as a function of MC sample count, identifying the point of diminishing returns.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on vMF posteriors, which may not hold for arbitrary real-world data
- Limited downstream evaluations on CIFAR-10H; performance on other uncertainty-sensitive tasks untested
- Computational overhead of Monte Carlo sampling may limit scalability

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** The theoretical proof that MCInfoNCE recovers correct posteriors up to rotation in the controlled setting (Proposition 4.1, Theorem 4.4)
- **Medium confidence:** The empirical alignment of predicted uncertainties with human disagreement (rank correlation 0.33) and information loss (rank correlation 0.70) on CIFAR-10H
- **Medium confidence:** The practical utility of credible intervals for image retrieval, as demonstrated qualitatively but not exhaustively quantified
