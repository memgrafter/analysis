# Prompting Neural Machine Translation with Translation Memories

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05380
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05380
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: BLEU score increases up to 8.63 points on TM specialized translation tasks and 2.94 points on commercial NMT systems

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a method for integrating translation memories (TMs) into neural machine translation (NMT) systems by treating retrieved TMs as prompts during decoding. The approach concatenates source TM with input sentences and generates target TM in a forced manner before generating the final translation, all without modifying the NMT model or training process. Experiments demonstrate significant improvements in translation quality, particularly when highly similar TMs are available (similarity > 0.8) and when using strong NMT models.

## Method Summary
The method involves concatenating retrieved TM source sentences with input sentences using specific templates, then forcing the NMT decoder to generate the corresponding TM target tokens before continuing with the actual translation. This is done at test time without retraining the NMT model. The approach uses fuzzy matching to retrieve the most similar TM entries and employs different concatenation templates for sentence-level and fragment-level TMs. The key innovation is leveraging TM knowledge through prompt-based decoding rather than model modification or fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- Up to 8.63 BLEU point improvements on TM specialized translation tasks
- 2.94 BLEU point improvements on commercial NMT systems
- Effectiveness scales with NMT model strength and TM similarity (>0.8)
- Significant improvements across multiple language pairs and datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The NMT model benefits from additional context without retraining when similar TM sentences are prepended to the input. The encoder receives a longer input that starts with the TM source sentence, providing additional context. During decoding, the target TM and template tokens are forced, giving the model a clear target for the TM portion before generating the actual translation. Core assumption: The pre-trained NMT model can effectively use the concatenated input and forced decoding without architectural changes.

### Mechanism 2
Higher similarity between the input sentence and TM leads to greater translation quality improvements. When the TM is highly similar to the input, the forced generation of the target TM provides highly relevant context that the model can leverage for better translation of the input portion. Core assumption: The forced generation of highly similar TM target text provides useful context for translating the input sentence.

### Mechanism 3
Stronger NMT models benefit more from TM prompting than weaker models. Stronger models with better translation capabilities can better utilize the additional context provided by the TM, leading to greater improvements in translation quality. Core assumption: The translation quality improvement from TM prompting scales with the baseline model's strength.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Input sentence and retrieved TM -> TM retriever -> Template generator -> NMT model -> Forced decoder -> Output translation

- **Critical path**: 1) Retrieve the most similar TM sentence pair for the input sentence, 2) Concatenate the TM source with the input sentence using the appropriate template, 3) Force decode the TM target and template tokens, 4) Generate the remaining tokens to complete the translation

- **Design tradeoffs**: Using a pre-trained model vs. fine-tuning (avoids computational cost but may not fully leverage TM data), Sentence-level vs. fragment-level TM (more context vs. alignment errors)

- **Failure signatures**: Poor translation quality when TM similarity is low, degraded performance on lengthy sentences due to encoder context window limitations, no improvement or degradation when using weak NMT models

- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Verify that the concatenation template is correctly applied and the TM target is being forced during decoding, 2) Test the TM retriever's ability to find highly similar sentences and measure the impact of similarity on translation quality, 3) Compare the performance of the approach with different NMT model strengths to validate the scaling of improvements

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What is the optimal threshold for translation memory (TM) similarity that balances translation quality improvement with the risk of introducing noise, particularly for different NMT model strengths? The paper finds that for strong NMT models, a similarity threshold of 0.8 yields significant improvements, while lower similarity scores (below 0.6) can introduce noise and degrade performance. This remains unresolved because the optimal threshold may vary depending on the model's architecture, training data, and the specific language pair.

### Open Question 2
How can the proposed method be extended to effectively handle long input sentences and their corresponding TMs without compromising translation quality? The paper acknowledges that the method's performance degrades significantly for long sentences (above 60 tokens) even when highly similar TMs are available, due to the doubled input length when concatenating source TM with input sentences. This remains unresolved as the paper only identifies the problem but does not propose concrete solutions or investigate alternative approaches.

### Open Question 3
What are the relative contributions of different template designs (direct concatenation, comma, semicolon, conjunction, parenthesis) to translation quality improvement across different language pairs and TM similarity levels? The paper tests five different concatenation templates for sentence-level TMs and finds that their effectiveness varies across datasets, with parentheses and direct concatenation often performing best, but the relative contributions are not systematically analyzed. This remains unresolved as the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of why certain templates work better than others.

## Limitations

- Performance heavily depends on TM similarity, degrading significantly below 0.8 threshold
- No comprehensive error analysis for cases where no sufficiently similar TM exists
- Doubling input length may exceed encoder context window for long sentences

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core claim that concatenating source TM with input sentences and forcing TM target generation during decoding improves translation quality when high-similarity TMs are available.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that stronger NMT models benefit more from TM prompting than weaker models, though the experimental validation could be more comprehensive.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that the method avoids model retraining while still achieving significant improvements, though fine-tuning with TM data could potentially achieve even better results.

## Next Checks

1. **Similarity Threshold Analysis**: Conduct detailed study of TM similarity thresholds (0.5-1.0 in 0.1 increments) across different domain types to precisely identify minimum effective threshold and quantify performance drop below this point.

2. **Context Window Limitation Study**: Systematically evaluate performance on sentences of varying lengths (short: 10-20 tokens, medium: 20-40 tokens, long: 40+ tokens) to quantify impact of doubling input length on translation quality.

3. **Retrieval Error Impact Analysis**: Implement controlled experiments with varying degrees of retrieval errors (10%, 30%, 50%) to measure robustness to imperfect TM matches and establish practical guidelines for TM database quality requirements.
