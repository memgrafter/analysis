# Exploring the Performance of Pruning Methods in Neural Networks: An Empirical Study of the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15479
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15479
- Reference count: 21

## Executive Summary
This paper provides an empirical comparison of various pruning methods for finding winning tickets under the lottery ticket hypothesis. The authors evaluate different pruning strategies including one-shot versus iterative pruning, Fisher pruning versus L1 unstructured pruning, and their performance across varying network widths and sparsity levels. They also introduce batched Fisher pruning as an efficient alternative. The study aims to understand when and why different pruning methods succeed or fail in identifying subnetworks that can be trained to match or exceed the performance of the original dense networks.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a comprehensive empirical study comparing pruning methods on vision tasks including MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Tiny-ImageNet. They evaluate one-shot versus iterative pruning strategies, compare Fisher pruning with L1 unstructured pruning, and introduce batched Fisher pruning with a batch size of 100 for improved efficiency. The experiments span varying network widths and sparsity levels, examining performance across different architectures. The study systematically measures accuracy retention across pruning percentages and investigates failure modes, particularly focusing on bottleneck formation in random pruning at high sparsity levels.

## Key Results
- One-shot and iterative pruning show similar performance up to 50% pruning, but iterative pruning outperforms at higher sparsity levels
- Fisher pruning and L1 unstructured pruning demonstrate comparable performance, with L1 slightly better on narrow networks
- Random pruning fails at high sparsity due to bottleneck formation that prevents effective information flow
- Batched Fisher pruning with batch size 100 improves both accuracy and computational efficiency

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The lottery ticket hypothesis suggests that dense neural networks contain subnetworks (winning tickets) that can be trained in isolation to match or exceed the performance of the original network. Pruning methods work by iteratively removing less important weights while preserving the essential structure needed for learning. The effectiveness of different pruning strategies depends on how well they identify and preserve these critical connections. Iterative pruning allows the network to adapt gradually to increasing sparsity, while structured methods like Fisher pruning use second-order information to make more informed decisions about weight importance.

## Limitations
- Evaluation focuses primarily on vision tasks with relatively small-scale models, limiting generalizability to larger architectures
- One-shot pruning comparison only extends to 50% sparsity, potentially missing critical differences at higher levels
- Batched Fisher pruning analysis limited to single batch size of 100, without exploring optimal batch size trade-offs
- Does not investigate learning rate rewinding or other hyperparameters that influence lottery ticket effectiveness
