# Investigating the Combination of Planning-Based and Data-Driven Methods for Goal Recognition

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05608
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05608
- Reference count: 9
- One-line primary result: A hybrid approach combining planning-based and data-driven methods outperforms purely planning-based and purely data-driven goal recognition methods, especially with limited observations.

## Executive Summary
This work investigates the application of planning-based plan recognition approaches in real-world settings, specifically addressing their limitations when recognizing human goals due to non-optimal human behavior. The authors propose an extension through a classification-based method trained on observed behavior data to overcome these limitations. Empirical evaluation demonstrates that the hybrid approach not only outperforms purely planning-based and purely data-driven methods but also achieves more reliable goal recognition with fewer observations.

## Method Summary
The research combines two state-of-the-art planning-based goal recognition approaches (PRAP-based methods RG and GM) with a Naive Bayes Model (NBM) trained on observed behavior data. The hybrid approach uses model stacking to combine predictions from both symbolic planning and data-driven learning. The evaluation uses three datasets: the CMU-MMAC Kitchen Dataset, an artificially extended version of this dataset, and a logistics domain dataset. Performance is measured using mean goal recognition accuracy across varying fractions of observed actions.

## Key Results
- The hybrid approach outperforms both purely planning-based and purely data-driven goal recognition methods
- The hybrid method achieves more reliable goal recognition, especially when only a small number of observations are available
- Both hybrid approaches (weighted sum and tiebreaking) were at least as good as the GM and NBM approaches for small training set sizes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The hybrid approach compensates for individual weaknesses of planning-based and data-driven methods. PRAP methods provide robustness from domain knowledge but miss statistical correlations, while NBM captures these correlations from data but lacks reasoning depth. Combining them balances these limitations.

Core assumption: The two models provide complementary information that, when combined, yields superior performance.

### Mechanism 2
Early goal recognition is possible because the hybrid approach leverages non-optimal but statistically relevant actions. The NBM learns from training data that certain actions are statistically associated with specific goals, even if these actions aren't necessary in optimal plans.

Core assumption: Human behavior includes carrying out actions that are not strictly necessary to reach the current goal.

### Mechanism 3
Scalability to more goals is improved because the hybrid approach handles increased complexity better than individual methods. As goals increase, PRAP's computational complexity grows exponentially while NBM's grows linearly with features.

Core assumption: The NBM can learn to distinguish between a larger number of goals from training data, even if PRAP struggles with increased complexity.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: PRAP method (GM or RG) -> NBM -> Meta-model -> Final goal probabilities
- Critical path: 1. Observe agent's actions and state 2. PRAP generates goal probabilities 3. NBM generates goal probabilities 4. Meta-model combines predictions 5. Output final goal probabilities
- Design tradeoffs: PRAP vs NBM (interpretability vs statistical correlation capture), weighted sum vs tiebreaking (flexibility vs simplicity)
- Failure signatures: PRAP failure (deviation from optimal plans), NBM failure (weak statistical correlations), Hybrid failure (ineffective combination)
- First 3 experiments: 1. Evaluate PRAP method on synthetic dataset 2. Evaluate NBM on real-world dataset 3. Evaluate hybrid approach on combined dataset

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How can the hybrid goal recognition approach be extended to handle complex temporal dependencies between actions and goals that are not captured by current models?
- Basis in paper: The paper discusses limitations in capturing statistical relations between actions and goals that are not necessary for optimal plans
- Why unresolved: Current approach focuses on combining methods but doesn't explicitly address complex temporal relationships
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results demonstrating improved performance of an extended hybrid model incorporating temporal dependencies

### Open Question 2
How can the hybrid goal recognition approach be adapted to handle scenarios where multiple agents are involved in achieving goals?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on single-agent goal recognition, suggesting potential for extension to multi-agent scenarios
- Why unresolved: Current approach doesn't address complexities introduced by multiple agents like coordination and shared goals
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing effectiveness in multi-agent goal recognition compared to single-agent scenarios

### Open Question 3
What are the optimal methods for combining symbolic and data-driven approaches in the hybrid goal recognition model to maximize performance across diverse domains?
- Basis in paper: The paper presents two meta-models but suggests potential for optimization
- Why unresolved: Current meta-models are manually designed and may not be optimal for all domains
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of different combination methods including automated learning approaches

## Limitations
- The computational complexity of the hybrid approach is not thoroughly discussed, which could limit real-time applications
- The assumption that human behavior includes non-optimal actions may not hold in all domains
- The focus on a specific kitchen environment limits generalizability to other domains

## Confidence
- Superiority of hybrid approach: Medium
- Generalizability across domains: Low
- Computational efficiency: Low
