# A Crosslingual Investigation of Conceptualization in 1335 Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.08475
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08475
- Reference count: 26
- Key outcome: Conceptualizer method achieves up to 87% accuracy in assigning languages to their correct family based on conceptual similarity

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Conceptualizer, a novel method for investigating conceptualization across 1,335 languages using a parallel Bible corpus. The approach constructs a directed bipartite graph between source language concepts and target language strings using forward and backward passes that identify highly associated strings. The method is evaluated on one concept across all languages and on 32 Swadesh concepts, showing good alignment accuracy. The paper also demonstrates that crosslingual stability of a concept (its tendency to have 1-1 mappings) is predicted by concreteness, and proposes a new conceptual similarity measure for languages that is complementary to genealogical and typological measures.

## Method Summary
Conceptualizer creates a directed bipartite graph between source language concepts and target language strings by exhaustively searching for high correlations using chi-squared scores. The method uses forward and backward passes to identify associated strings, with a coverage threshold of 0.9 to ensure meaningful associations. For each focal concept, it searches all ngrams in the target language that occur in the same verses, selecting the ngram with the highest chi-squared correlation score. The method is evaluated on one concept across all languages and on 32 Swadesh concepts using the NoRaRe database for validation.

## Key Results
- Conceptualizer achieves up to 87% accuracy in assigning languages to their correct family based on conceptual similarity
- Crosslingual stability of concepts is predicted by concreteness, with concrete concepts showing more 1-1 mappings
- The method demonstrates good alignment accuracy for both the 'bird' concept across all languages and 32 Swadesh concepts

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Conceptualizer identifies highly associated target-language ngrams through a forward pass using chi-squared association scores
- Mechanism: For each focal concept in English, Conceptualizer searches all ngrams in the target language that occur in the same verses, selecting the ngram with the highest chi-squared correlation score to the concept's verse set. This process iterates until a coverage threshold is met.
- Core assumption: The chi-squared score reliably captures semantic association between ngrams and the concept across different languages and verse contexts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Conceptualizer creates a bipartite directed alignment graph between source language concepts and sets of target language strings"
  - [section] "We use the χ2 score χ2(l,t,V) as a measure of correlation: we test, for all t, whether the two categorial variables t ∈ v and v ∈ V are independent"
- Break condition: If the verse corpus is too sparse or misaligned, chi-squared scores become unreliable and fail to identify true semantic associations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Backward pass validates and enriches concept alignments by finding English ngrams associated with the target-language hits
- Mechanism: After identifying target-language strings highly associated with a focal concept, Conceptualizer performs a backward search in English to find ngrams correlated with those target strings. If the backward hits match the original focal concept, the alignment is considered stable; if not, it reveals divergent conceptualization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Conceptualizer creates a bipartite directed alignment graph... that we then use for analysis and prediction"
  - [section] "We can represent a source concept... as the set of verses V in which it occurs. In contrast to standard alignment algorithms, we exhaustively search all stringst of the target languagel for high correlation withV"
- Break condition: If target-language strings are ambiguous or have multiple unrelated meanings, the backward pass may produce misleading associations that don't reflect true conceptualization.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Concept stability across languages is predicted by concreteness, with concrete concepts showing more 1-1 mappings
- Mechanism: The paper defines stability as the proportion of recurrent paths in the bipartite graph that return to the original concept. It correlates this stability measure with concreteness ratings from (Brysbaert et al., 2014), finding that concrete concepts are more stable across languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We define crosslingual stability of a concept as the degree to which it has 1-1 correspondences across languages, and show that concreteness predicts stability"
  - [section] "We define the stability of a focal concept F as: σ(F) = |{c|(F,c) ∈ G ∧ (c,F) ∈ G}| / |{c|(F,c) ∈ G}|"
- Break condition: If semantic extension processes (metaphor, metonymy) affect concrete concepts similarly to abstract ones, the concreteness-stability correlation may break down.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Bipartite graph representation of concept alignments
  - Why needed here: The bipartite graph structure is fundamental to Conceptualizer's approach, allowing representation of both forward and backward alignments between source concepts and target-language strings
  - Quick check question: Can you explain why a directed bipartite graph is used instead of a simple alignment matrix?

- Concept: Statistical association measures (chi-squared)
  - Why needed here: Chi-squared scores are used to identify strings in the target language that are statistically associated with the focal concept's verse occurrences
  - Quick check question: What assumptions does chi-squared make about the independence of verse occurrences and string presence?

- Concept: Crosslingual stability as a measure of concept consistency
  - Why needed here: Stability quantifies how consistently a concept maps across languages, forming the basis for analyzing the relationship between concreteness and crosslingual consistency
  - Quick check question: How would you compute stability for a concept that has many polysemous mappings across languages?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Parallel Bible Corpus -> Conceptualizer algorithm -> Bipartite alignment graph -> Stability metrics and language similarity vectors

- Critical path: Forward pass -> Coverage check -> Backward pass -> Graph construction -> Stability/similarity computation
  - Each concept requires both forward and backward passes for each target language before graph construction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Coverage threshold (α = 0.9) vs. computational efficiency: Higher coverage ensures better associations but increases runtime
  - String length restrictions (1-8 chars for target, 3+ for English) vs. completeness: Prevents combinatorial explosion but may miss longer multi-word expressions
  - Maximum iterations (M = 5) vs. accuracy: Limits runtime but may miss additional relevant associations

- Failure signatures:
  - Low coverage (<0.9) after maximum iterations indicates data sparsity or concept divergence
  - High false positive rate in backward pass suggests target-language ambiguity
  - Poor stability scores despite high concreteness ratings may indicate cultural/contextual differences

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run Conceptualizer on 'bird' concept across all languages and manually validate the backward pass hits against independent translation resources
  2. Compare chi-squared association scores against alternative measures (pointwise mutual information, dice coefficient) on a subset of concept-language pairs
  3. Test stability predictions by comparing concreteness ratings with human judgments of crosslingual concept consistency for a sample of 20 concepts

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does concreteness reliably predict stability of concepts across languages, or are there other factors at play?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper hypothesizes that concreteness contributes to stability and finds some evidence for this, but also notes that many concepts do not follow this pattern.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation only uses a binary classification (stable vs unstable) based on a single threshold. A more fine-grained analysis could reveal whether concreteness is a reliable predictor or if other factors like metaphor and metonymy play a larger role.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed regression analysis predicting stability from concreteness, metaphor, metonymy, etc. would show the relative importance of each factor. Qualitative analysis of concept associations could also reveal common semantic extensions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How well does Conceptualizer perform on languages outside the 1,335 in the PBC corpus?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only evaluates Conceptualizer on the 1,335 PBC languages. It is unclear how well it would generalize to other languages, especially those with different linguistic features or scripts.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not test Conceptualizer on any languages outside the PBC corpus. It is possible that languages with different features could pose challenges.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating Conceptualizer on a diverse set of languages not in the PBC corpus, including low-resource languages with different features, would reveal its generalization ability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How much does Conceptualizer rely on the specific Bible translations used in the PBC corpus? Would it work as well on other parallel corpora?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses the PBC corpus as its data source, but does not explore how well Conceptualizer would work on other parallel corpora. The Bible translations may have features that make them particularly amenable to Conceptualizer.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests Conceptualizer on the PBC corpus. It is possible that other parallel corpora with different features (e.g. more varied genres, different translation styles) could pose challenges.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating Conceptualizer on a variety of parallel corpora with different features would reveal how much it relies on the specific characteristics of the PBC corpus.

## Limitations
- Limited empirical validation of chi-squared's effectiveness compared to alternative association measures for semantic association detection
- Lack of independent validation for backward pass results against datasets outside the Bible corpus
- Dependence on external concreteness ratings rather than corpus-derived measures of concept abstractness

## Confidence
- **High**: The overall framework of using directed bipartite graphs for crosslingual concept alignment
- **Medium**: The effectiveness of chi-squared scores for semantic association detection
- **Medium**: The concreteness-stability correlation predictions
- **Low**: The generalizability of results beyond the Bible corpus domain
