# Dialectical language model evaluation: An initial appraisal of the commonsense spatial reasoning abilities of LLMs

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models possess commonsense spatial reasoning abilities through a dialectical evaluation approach. The authors conduct qualitative investigations with several LLMs, including ChatGPT and Bard, on spatial reasoning tasks involving part-whole relationships, object rotation, directions, size comparisons, and object permanence. The study reveals that while LLMs often provide fluent responses, they frequently demonstrate fundamental misunderstandings of spatial concepts, particularly when reasoning is required beyond pattern matching. The research highlights the value of interactive evaluation methods in revealing these limitations and suggests directions for future work in both improving LLM capabilities and developing more robust evaluation frameworks.

## Method Summary
The authors employ a dialectical evaluation methodology where they engage LLMs in conversational dialogues to probe spatial reasoning capabilities. The approach involves generating initial prompts related to spatial concepts, analyzing responses for correctness, and then using follow-up questions to test understanding and consistency. This interactive method allows evaluators to build on previous exchanges, probe for deeper understanding, and identify the boundaries of the model's spatial commonsense knowledge. The study focuses on qualitative investigation rather than quantitative measurement, using specific task categories including part-whole relationships, object rotation, directions, size comparisons, and object permanence.

## Key Results
- LLMs frequently demonstrate fundamental misunderstandings of spatial concepts when reasoning beyond simple pattern matching
- Dialectical evaluation methods reveal limitations that static benchmarks often miss
- Spatial reasoning tasks requiring intuitive physics models prove particularly challenging for current LLMs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Dialectical evaluation exposes LLM reasoning limitations that static benchmarks miss.
- Mechanism: By engaging in interactive dialogues, evaluators can probe consistency, follow reasoning chains, and detect contradictions or gaps in understanding.
- Core assumption: LLMs will respond to follow-up questions in a way that reveals their internal reasoning limitations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Dialoguing with the system gives the opportunity to check for consistency and get more reassurance of these boundaries beyond anecdotal evidence."
  - [section 3] "The effort in the dialogues is put to find a variation or continuation where a system fails, to properly map this contour of spatial common sense."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains related evaluation papers but none specifically about dialectical methods for spatial reasoning.
- Break condition: If LLMs could simulate consistent reasoning chains without actual understanding, dialectical probing would fail to expose limitations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Context accumulation in conversational LLMs enables sophisticated probing of commonsense reasoning.
- Mechanism: The model's memory of previous exchanges in a session allows evaluators to build on responses and test understanding through elaboration.
- Core assumption: LLMs maintain coherent context across multiple exchanges in a single session.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Modern language models allow for a dialectic evaluation by the accumulation of the previous questions and answers as context."
  - [section 2] Multiple examples where follow-up prompts are used to probe understanding after initial responses.
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors discuss evaluation but not specifically context-based dialectical approaches.
- Break condition: If LLMs treat each exchange independently or context degrades rapidly, this probing mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Spatial reasoning requires intuitive physics models that current LLMs lack.
- Mechanism: Spatial concepts like containment, rotation, and object permanence require internal models of physical reality that pattern matching cannot provide.
- Core assumption: Spatial reasoning fundamentally requires understanding physical constraints rather than linguistic patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "A cell could be in the organ, but the organ could be located in a different part of the body" - confused spatial containment reasoning.
  - [section 2.2] "C3.5 incorrectly describes the shape of a Z and then gets the wrong answer" - fails basic spatial transformations.
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors discuss commonsense reasoning but not specifically the physics-model requirement.
- Break condition: If LLMs can learn spatial reasoning through pattern matching alone, this mechanism fails to explain their limitations.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Mereology (part-whole relationships)
  - Why needed here: Spatial reasoning fundamentally relies on understanding how objects relate through part-whole relationships.
  - Quick check question: If a wheel is part of a car, and the car is part of a garage, what is the spatial relationship between the wheel and the garage?

- Concept: Qualitative spatial reasoning
  - Why needed here: The paper focuses on qualitative rather than metric spatial reasoning, which requires understanding relationships without precise measurements.
  - Quick check question: If point A is to the left of point B, and point C is to the right of point B, what is the relationship between A and C?

- Concept: Object permanence
  - Why needed here: The paper tests whether LLMs understand that objects continue to exist when out of sight, a fundamental spatial concept.
  - Quick check question: If you put your keys on a table, then move them to your pocket, where would someone expect to find them if they didn't see you move them?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Prompt generator -> LLM interface -> Response analyzer -> Dialogue manager -> Follow-up question generator
- Critical path: Generate initial prompt → Receive response → Analyze for correctness → Generate follow-up probe → Continue dialogue until boundary found
- Design tradeoffs: Interactive evaluation provides deeper insight but requires human time vs automated testing is scalable but shallow
- Failure signatures: Consistent wrong answers with confident explanations, contradictory responses to similar questions, inability to follow logical reasoning chains
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test basic spatial containment: "If X is part of Y and Y is part of Z, what is the relationship between X and Z?"
  2. Test rotation understanding: "If I rotate a letter M 180 degrees, what letter does it look most like?"
  3. Test object permanence: "You see a green car approach a sofa and disappear, then a red car appears. What happened?"

## Limitations
- Limited sample size: The study tested only a small set of LLMs (ChatGPT variants and Bard) on a restricted set of spatial reasoning tasks
- Qualitative methodology constraints: The approach relies heavily on human judgment for follow-up questions and interpretation of responses
- Task selection bias: The spatial reasoning tasks were selected based on researchers' judgment of what constitutes "commonsense" spatial understanding

## Confidence
- High Confidence: LLMs demonstrate fundamental misunderstandings of spatial concepts when reasoning beyond pattern matching
- Medium Confidence: Dialectical evaluation methods are more effective than static benchmarks at revealing LLM reasoning limitations
- Medium Confidence: Spatial reasoning requires intuitive physics models rather than pattern matching

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a systematic comparison with static benchmarks using both dialectical and traditional multiple-choice formats for the same spatial reasoning tasks
2. Apply the same dialectical evaluation methodology to non-spatial commonsense reasoning domains (e.g., temporal reasoning, social reasoning) to determine whether limitations are specific to spatial reasoning
3. Have human participants answer the same spatial reasoning questions to establish a performance baseline and determine whether LLM errors represent fundamental limitations
