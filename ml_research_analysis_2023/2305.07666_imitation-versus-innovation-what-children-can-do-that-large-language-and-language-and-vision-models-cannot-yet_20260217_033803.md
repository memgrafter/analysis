# Imitation versus Innovation: What children can do that large language and language-and-vision models cannot (yet)?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07666
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07666
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 18
- Key outcome: Large language models excel at imitation but struggle with innovation, while children can both imitate and innovate in tool use and causal learning tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the distinction between imitation and innovation in AI systems and human children. It argues that large language models (LLMs) are cultural technologies that excel at imitation, while human children can innovate and solve novel problems. The authors conducted experiments comparing the performance of LLMs and children in tool innovation and causal learning tasks. The results showed that while LLMs can identify superficial commonalities between objects, they struggle to select functionally relevant objects for novel tasks. Similarly, LLMs have difficulty discovering novel causal relationships and using them to design interventions, whereas young children can do so effectively.

## Method Summary
The authors designed experiments to compare children's and LLMs' ability to imitate versus innovate. They presented children (ages 3-7) and adults with physical problems requiring tool use, asking them to select objects that were either superficially similar, functionally relevant, or irrelevant to the task. The same tasks were presented to several LLMs (OpenAI GPT-3.5 Turbo, text-davinci-003, Claude, FLAN-T5, Bloomz, and DALLE-2) using text-based prompts. Additionally, the authors tested children and LLMs on causal learning tasks involving "blicket detector" scenarios. Performance was scored based on the selection of functionally relevant objects or accurate causal inferences.

## Key Results
- LLMs consistently selected superficially similar but functionally irrelevant objects in tool innovation tasks, while children showed a preference for functionally relevant objects.
- LLMs struggled to discover novel causal relationships and use them to design interventions, whereas young children could infer causal structures and apply them effectively.
- The study highlights the gap between current AI models' ability to imitate patterns from training data and their inability to innovate or solve novel problems requiring causal understanding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Large language models are cultural technologies that excel at imitation through statistical pattern extraction from text and image corpora.
- Mechanism: LLMs learn to reproduce linguistic and visual patterns by predicting the next token or image element in sequences. This process captures associative and distributional regularities in the training data, enabling them to imitate human-like text generation and image synthesis.
- Core assumption: The statistical patterns in language and images encode sufficient information to reproduce human cultural outputs without requiring grounded, causal understanding of the world.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We argue that these artificial intelligence models are cultural technologies that enhance cultural transmission in the modern world, and are efficient imitation engines."
  - [section] "Deep learning models trained on large datasets today excel at imitation in a way that far outstrips earlier technologies and so represent a new phase in the history of cultural technologies."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Children's imitation is mediated by goal representation and causal understanding, allowing them to innovate beyond observed patterns.
- Mechanism: Children decompose observed actions into goals and causal steps, enabling them to reproduce intentional actions and discard causally irrelevant details. This supports innovation by applying functional analogies to new problems.
- Core assumption: Early cognitive mechanisms for goal and causal reasoning allow children to flexibly apply learned knowledge to novel situations, unlike purely associative learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Children can meaningfully decompose observed visual and motor patterns in relation to the agent, target object, movement path and other salient features of events."
  - [section] "Children distinctively copy intentional actions... discarding apparently failed attempts, mistakes, and causally inefficient actions when they seek to learn skills from observing other people."
  - [corpus] "Top related titles: Children's Mental Models of Generative Visual and Text Based AI Models"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Innovation requires extracting knowledge from active interaction with the external world, not just from cultural transmission.
- Mechanism: Novel causal learning and tool innovation depend on acting on the environment to discover affordances and causal relationships. This process cannot be fully captured by passive statistical learning from existing data.
- Core assumption: Certain cognitive capacities, especially those involving causal inference and novel problem solving, require embodied, active exploration beyond what large-scale language and vision data provide.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We might, however, also wonder whether young children can themselves perform this kind of innovation, or whether it depends on explicit instruction and experience."
  - [section] "Discovering novel functions in everyday tools is not about finding the statistically nearest neighbor from lexical co-occurrence patterns. Rather, it is about appreciating the more abstract functional analogies and causal relationships between objects that do not necessarily belong to the same category or are associated in text."

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Causal learning and intervention
  - Why needed here: The paper contrasts children's ability to discover novel causal structures with LLMs' struggles in this domain. Understanding how causal learning works is key to grasping the innovation gap.
  - Quick check question: Can you explain how children might infer a new causal relationship by acting on a system, as opposed to just observing it?

- Concept: Tool innovation vs. tool use
  - Why needed here: The experiments compare selecting functionally relevant substitutes for tools (innovation) versus associating typical tools (imitation). Distinguishing these tasks is central to the study.
  - Quick check question: Why would choosing a plant pot over a coffee lid to hold a hot cup be considered innovation rather than imitation?

- Concept: Statistical learning and distributional semantics
  - Why needed here: LLMs rely on extracting patterns from large text/image corpora. Understanding how this enables imitation but not innovation is critical.
  - Quick check question: How does predicting the next word in a sentence relate to the ability (or inability) to invent new uses for objects?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Child experiment setup -> Data collection -> LLM prompt generation -> Model querying -> Response scoring -> Comparison analysis
- Critical path: Design tasks → Run with children → Query LLMs with same prompts → Score responses → Compare results → Draw conclusions
- Design tradeoffs: Using language-only prompts for multimodal tasks (tool use) may disadvantage models that need visual input; using real-world interaction for children vs. text-only for models introduces modality bias
- Failure signatures: LLMs consistently choosing superficially similar but functionally irrelevant objects; children showing variability in innovation but consistent imitation; models generating plausible but incorrect causal explanations
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replication of the tool substitution task with a new set of everyday objects to test robustness
  2. Ablation study varying the type of prompt (text-only vs. multimodal) to isolate the effect of input modality
  3. Active learning version where models can query the environment (simulated) to discover causal structure, comparing with passive training

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can large language models be improved to match human children's performance in tool innovation tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that current large language models struggle with tool innovation tasks, selecting functionally irrelevant objects over functionally relevant ones.
- Why unresolved: While the paper highlights the limitations of current models, it does not explore potential improvements or modifications that could enhance their performance in such tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Demonstrating improved performance in tool innovation tasks by large language models after incorporating specific modifications or training techniques.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific aspects of human cognition enable children to excel in causal learning tasks compared to large language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how children can infer novel causal relationships and use them to design interventions, while large language models struggle with this.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the gap in performance but does not delve into the underlying cognitive mechanisms that contribute to children's superior causal learning abilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Identifying and isolating the specific cognitive processes or representations that allow children to excel in causal learning tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the data and learning techniques used in training large language models compare to the embodied and multimodal learning experiences of human children?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that children learn through embodied, multimodal exploration, while large language models are trained on vast amounts of text and image data.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed comparison of the learning processes and data used in training large language models versus the embodied learning experiences of children.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting studies that directly compare the learning processes and data used in training large language models with the embodied learning experiences of children.

## Limitations
- The study relies on text-only prompts for multimodal tasks, potentially disadvantaging LLMs compared to children who can physically interact with objects.
- The corpus analysis reveals minimal prior literature directly addressing the imitation-innovation distinction, limiting theoretical grounding.
- Specific experimental protocols and prompts are not fully detailed, making exact replication challenging.

## Confidence
- High confidence: LLMs excel at statistical pattern imitation from cultural data but struggle with novel causal inference.
- Medium confidence: Children's innovation relies on goal representation and causal understanding, as the cognitive mechanisms described are well-established but the experimental evidence is limited.
- Low confidence: Active interaction is uniquely necessary for innovation, given the lack of systematic comparison with actively learning AI systems.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the tool innovation experiment with new object sets and control for cultural knowledge cues in prompts to verify the robustness of LLM vs. child performance differences.
2. Conduct an ablation study varying input modality (text-only vs. multimodal) to isolate the effect of presentation format on model and child performance.
3. Test LLMs with active learning capabilities in causal tasks to determine if embodied interaction narrows the innovation gap.
