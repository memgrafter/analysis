# Using Language Models For Knowledge Acquisition in Natural Language Reasoning Problems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.01771
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01771
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 3
- Large language models perform better at extracting knowledge from text than directly solving reasoning problems

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the effectiveness of large language models (LLMs) for solving natural language reasoning problems. The authors compare two approaches: directly asking LLMs to solve problems versus using them to extract knowledge from text and then applying theorem proving. Experiments were conducted on ChatGPT and GPT-4 using logic puzzles adapted from Smullyan's "Ladies or Tigers?" series and other sources. The results demonstrate that both models struggle with direct reasoning but perform significantly better when used as knowledge acquisition tools to translate natural language into logical formulas. GPT-4 shows superior performance compared to ChatGPT in both tasks.

## Method Summary
The study employs two main experimental approaches with large language models. First, models are directly asked to solve logic puzzles by providing answers. Second, models are prompted to translate natural language statements into propositional logic formulas, which are then processed by a theorem prover to derive solutions. The experiments use logic puzzles from Smullyan's "Ladies or Tigers?" series, an AI course exam question about friends sharing a car, and an Alpine Club puzzle about skiers and mountain climbers. Manual verification is used to check the correctness of generated logical formulas.

## Key Results
- Both ChatGPT and GPT-4 struggle with direct reasoning on logic puzzles, often providing incorrect or conditional answers
- When used as knowledge acquisition tools to translate natural language statements into logical formulas, both models perform significantly better
- GPT-4 shows superior performance compared to ChatGPT in both direct reasoning and knowledge extraction tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LLMs can effectively translate natural language statements into logical formulas when given explicit symbolic mappings
- Mechanism: By providing explicit symbolic mappings, the LLM leverages its language understanding capabilities to create formal representations that can be processed by theorem provers
- Core assumption: The LLM has sufficient language understanding to map natural language constructs to logical operators when given clear symbolic guidance
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "when used as a knowledge acquisition tool to translate natural language statements into logical formulas, the models performed significantly better"
  - [section] "To make the task more specific, we provide logical symbols by which the statements are to be encoded"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the natural language contains ambiguity that cannot be resolved with the given symbols, or if the LLM's language understanding is insufficient for the specific domain

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Breaking complex reasoning problems into smaller, explicit steps improves LLM performance
- Mechanism: By decomposing problems into individual statements and encoding each separately, the cognitive load on the LLM is reduced and errors are less likely to compound
- Core assumption: The LLM's reasoning errors are primarily due to processing complexity rather than fundamental misunderstanding
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ChatGPT and GPT4 did much better when asked to translate the texts in the puzzles to logic sentences"
  - [section] "This motivates us to ask ChatGPT to encode the statements one at a time"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the problem requires holistic understanding that spans multiple statements, or if the LLM cannot maintain consistency across separate encodings

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Theorem provers can compensate for LLMs' reasoning limitations when given correctly encoded knowledge
- Mechanism: The LLM handles language understanding and knowledge extraction, while the theorem prover handles logical inference and reasoning
- Core assumption: The logical encoding produced by the LLM is sufficiently accurate for the theorem prover to work correctly
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the potential is clearly there. It can certainly be improved by some further training or fine-tuning"
  - [section] "The formula entails P2 but not P1" (showing correct reasoning when encoding is correct)
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the encoding contains errors that lead the theorem prover astray, or if the problem requires reasoning that cannot be captured in propositional logic

  - Why needed here: The paper relies on translating natural language statements into propositional logic formulas that can be processed by theorem provers
  - Quick check question: Can you translate "If A is true, then B is true" into propositional logic?

- Concept: Theorem proving basics
  - Why needed here: The encoded formulas need to be evaluated for logical entailment to solve the original problems
  - Quick check question: What does it mean for a formula to entail another formula in propositional logic?

- Concept: Knowledge acquisition vs. reasoning
  - Why needed here: The paper distinguishes between LLMs' ability to extract knowledge from text versus their ability to perform reasoning
  - Quick check question: Why might it be easier for an LLM to extract facts from text than to reason about those facts?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Natural language text → LLM (knowledge acquisition) → Propositional logic formulas → Theorem prover → Problem solution
- Critical path: The LLM must correctly encode the knowledge; the theorem prover must correctly reason about the encoded knowledge
- Design tradeoffs: Accuracy of LLM encoding vs. complexity of theorem prover; explicit symbol provision vs. LLM autonomy
- Failure signatures: Incorrect final answers despite correct knowledge extraction; inconsistent encodings across multiple attempts; failure to handle certain logical constructs
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test LLM's ability to encode simple statements with provided symbols (e.g., "A is red" → X1)
  2. Test LLM's ability to encode compound statements (e.g., "If A is red, then B is green" → X1 → Y2)
  3. Test end-to-end pipeline with a simple problem: encode statements, run theorem prover, verify solution matches expected answer

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific training methods or fine-tuning approaches could improve LLMs' ability to translate natural language statements into formal logic?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest that LLMs could be improved by "some further training or fine-tuning" to better translate natural language to logic
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates current limitations but doesn't explore specific training methodologies or architectures that could address these shortcomings
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing improved translation accuracy after implementing specific training/fine-tuning techniques

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of LLMs in knowledge acquisition tasks compare to specialized natural language understanding systems designed for formal representation extraction?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors compare LLMs to theorem provers but don't compare them to specialized NLU systems for formal representation
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses solely on comparing direct reasoning vs. knowledge extraction using LLMs, without benchmarking against domain-specific alternatives
- What evidence would resolve it: Head-to-head comparisons of LLM-based extraction versus specialized NLU systems on identical tasks

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the underlying mechanisms causing LLMs to make assumptions about unstated information in logical reasoning problems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that both ChatGPT and GPT4 "took the liberty to assume" things not stated in the problem text
- Why unresolved: The paper observes this behavior but doesn't investigate the root causes or patterns in when/where these assumptions occur
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic analysis of assumption patterns across different problem types and model versions

## Limitations

- Experimental scope is narrow, focusing on specific types of logic puzzles rather than a broader range of reasoning problems
- Manual verification of logical formulas introduces potential human bias and inconsistency
- Comparison between ChatGPT and GPT-4 may be influenced by different release dates and training updates rather than inherent architectural differences

## Confidence

- High confidence: LLMs perform better at knowledge extraction than direct reasoning when explicit symbolic mappings are provided
- Medium confidence: GPT-4 outperforms ChatGPT in both direct reasoning and knowledge extraction tasks
- Low confidence: The specific mechanisms proposed for why LLMs struggle with direct reasoning and succeed with knowledge extraction

## Next Checks

1. Replicate across diverse problem types: Test the knowledge extraction approach on mathematical word problems, commonsense reasoning tasks, and other non-logical reasoning domains to verify generalizability

2. Automated verification pipeline: Develop automated methods to verify the correctness of LLM-generated logical formulas to eliminate manual verification bias and enable larger-scale experiments

3. Fine-tuning experiments: Conduct controlled fine-tuning experiments on the LLM to improve its direct reasoning capabilities and compare performance against the knowledge extraction approach on the same problems
