# Contextual Trust

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08900
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08900
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 14
- Key outcome: Proposes contextual trust as a four-place relation (A, B, X, C) where trust depends on unquestioning attitude and incorporation of action as means to ends

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that trust is inherently context-sensitive and proposes a four-place relational theory of contextual trust. The author claims that trust occurs when a truster adopts an unquestioning attitude toward a trustee performing an action within a specific context, and incorporates that action as a means to one of their ends. The framework is then applied to Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), suggesting that model transparency and explainability influence trust by modifying the truster's contextual belief sets.

## Method Summary
This theoretical paper develops a framework for contextual trust by analyzing existing philosophical theories and proposing a four-place relational model. The author examines how traditional three-place trust theories (truster, trustee, action) can be enhanced by incorporating context as a fourth dimension. The method involves philosophical argumentation and conceptual analysis, with an application to XAI transparency mechanisms. No experimental methods or data analysis are employed.

## Key Results
- Trust is fundamentally a four-place relation including context (A, B, X, C) rather than the traditional three-place model
- Context consists of structured belief sets about the truster, trustee, action, and trustee's past performance
- Model transparency and explainability in XAI influence trust by modifying contextual belief sets
- Different trusters form different beliefs from the same transparency information, leading to varied trust outcomes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Contextual trust is defined as a four-place relation including a truster, trustee, action, and context, rather than the traditional three-place relation.
- Mechanism: The context (C) encodes structured beliefs about the truster (A), the trustee (B), the action (X), and B's past performance at X in varying contexts. Trust exists when A adopts an unquestioning attitude toward B doing X in context C, and A incorporates B's doing X as a means to one of A's ends.
- Core assumption: Trust is inherently truster-subjective and cannot be reduced to a purely rationalist calculation of outcomes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "I propose that the answer to 'What does it mean for A to trust B to do X in context C?' has two conditions. First, A must take B's doing X as a means towards one of A's ends. Second, A must adopt an unquestioning attitude concerning B's doing X in context C."
  - [section]: "We replace the general question 'What does it mean to trust?' with a more specific and tractable one, 'What does it mean for A to trust B to do X in context C?'"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct neighbor papers mention the four-place relational formulation, but the term "contextual" appears in neighbor titles suggesting relevance.
- Break condition: If A's beliefs about B, X, or B's past performance are demonstrably incorrect, the trust relation may dissolve even if the unquestioning attitude remains.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Context-sensitivity is not merely a linguistic convenience but a difference-making feature of trust.
- Mechanism: Contextual factors belong to structured sets (beliefs about A, beliefs about B, beliefs about X, beliefs about B's history of doing X). These factors are not arbitrary but relevant to A's decision-making about trust.
- Core assumption: Not every fact about A or B is relevant to trust; only those facts that A herself views as relevant are included in context C.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Since A ultimately decides whether to trust B to X... any relevant contextual factors must be determined via relevance to A herself."
  - [section]: "Context C, like truster A and trustee B, is constructed in a structured and restricted fashion... Context can be considered similarly to belief sets in the context of Bayesian inference."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no neighbor papers discuss the structured nature of contextual factors, though "trust" appears in neighbor abstracts.
- Break condition: If the belief set C becomes empty or uninformative (e.g., A has no relevant beliefs about B or X), the contextual trust relation cannot be meaningfully evaluated.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Transparency and explainability in XAI influence trust by changing contextual belief sets (C).
- Mechanism: Functional, structural, and run transparency provide information that modifies A's beliefs about B (the model), X (the prediction task), or B's past performance. Trust is then reassessed in the new context.
- Core assumption: Different trusters (laypeople vs. domain experts) will form different beliefs from the same transparency information, leading to different trust outcomes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Functional and structural transparency correspond to contextual factors in C of the second type... run transparency corresponds to the third and fourth types of contextual factors in C."
  - [section]: "Gaining transparency into a model changes a potential truster's beliefs about that model. As a result, the truster can consider trust in a new context."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no neighbor papers explicitly link transparency to belief modification, though "trust" and "AI" appear in neighbor titles.
- Break condition: If the transparency information is misleading or misinterpreted, A's belief set C may be corrupted, leading to misplaced trust or distrust.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Truster (A): The agent whose trust is being evaluated; must be a human agent
  - Trustee (B): The agent being trusted; can be human or non-human (e.g., AI system)
  - Action (X): The specific action over which trust is given
  - Context (C): A structured set of A's beliefs about B, X, and B's history of doing X
  - Unquestioning Attitude: A dispositional state where A suspends deliberation about B doing X in context C
  - Ends-Orientation: A's incorporation of B's doing X as a means to one of A's goals
- Critical path: A decides to trust B to do X in context C if (1) A adopts an unquestioning attitude toward B doing X in C, and (2) A incorporates B's doing X as a means to one of A's ends
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Granularity of actions vs. context-sensitivity: Finer-grained actions may subsume contextual factors but lose naturalness
  - Structured vs. unstructured context: Structured context is more informative but requires careful belief set construction
  - Human vs. non-human trustees: Allowing non-human trustees increases applicability but complicates goodwill conditions
- Failure signatures:
  - If A lacks relevant beliefs about B or X, context C is empty and trust cannot be evaluated
  - If A's unquestioning attitude is coerced rather than voluntary, the trust relation may be invalid
  - If transparency information is misleading, A's belief set C may be corrupted
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test whether participants trust an AI model more after receiving functional transparency information vs. no information
  2. Vary the granularity of the action (e.g., "drive" vs. "drive in the city at night") and measure changes in trust
  3. Manipulate A's beliefs about B's past performance and measure effects on trust in context C

## Limitations

- The theory assumes a specific interpretation of "unquestioning attitude" that may not align with all philosophical traditions on trust
- The claim that context is structured and restricted is philosophically contentious; alternative views (e.g., that context is "everything else") are not fully engaged
- The XAI application is speculative and requires empirical testing to validate the proposed mechanisms

## Confidence

- Medium: Application to XAI transparency mechanisms
