# Improving the Quality of Neural Machine Translation Through Proper Translation of Name Entities

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07360
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07360
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 17
- This paper proposes a preprocessing approach to improve neural machine translation (NMT) accuracy for name entities, achieving very high accuracy: 99.86% for person names, 99.63% for location names, and 99.05% for organization names.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of accurately translating named entities (person names, location names, and organization names) in neural machine translation from English to Hindi. The authors propose a preprocessing approach that combines knowledge base lookup with phoneme-based transliteration enhanced by Hidden Markov Models. The system achieves exceptionally high accuracy rates across all entity types, demonstrating that dedicated preprocessing of named entities can significantly improve NMT quality. The approach handles cases where direct translation is insufficient by using phonetic mapping and probabilistic alignment to generate Hindi equivalents.

## Method Summary
The method involves identifying name entities using Stanford NER, then attempting knowledge base lookup for direct translation of organizations and locations. For entities not found in the knowledge base, the system extracts phonemes from English names using rule-based classification of characters as vowels or consonants, generates probability tables, and applies Hidden Markov Models to align and disambiguate these phonemes to Hindi equivalents. The Hindi phonemes are then merged to produce the final translated entity. This preprocessing approach ensures named entities are handled consistently before entering the NMT pipeline, addressing the context and world knowledge limitations of NMT systems.

## Key Results
- Person names: 99.86% accuracy
- Location names: 99.63% accuracy
- Organization names: 99.05% accuracy
- Overall accuracy: 99.52% across 29,283 name entities

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Phoneme-based transliteration combined with HMM disambiguation improves accuracy for names not found in the knowledge base.
- Mechanism: The algorithm decomposes English name entities into phonemes using rule-based vowel/consonant classification, then applies a Hidden Markov Model to align and disambiguate these phonemes to Hindi equivalents using transition and likelihood probabilities.
- Core assumption: Phoneme sequences capture enough linguistic structure to enable accurate cross-lingual mapping when direct dictionary lookup fails.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The phoneme extraction was done through a set of rules where the characters in phonemes had two classes (vowels and consonants)...we had also developed a knowledge base where most of the organization names and location are translated from English to Hindi."
  - [section] "Once the English and Hindi phonemes are extracted, their probability table is generated for implementation of hidden Markov model (HMM) as it is a very handy mechanism in identifying context and disambiguating accordingly."
- Break condition: If phoneme decomposition rules do not reflect actual pronunciation or if phoneme mappings are highly ambiguous, the HMM alignment will fail to disambiguate correctly.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Knowledge base lookup for high-frequency entity names avoids transliteration errors.
- Mechanism: The system first checks if the entity is in the pre-built English-to-Hindi knowledge base before attempting phoneme-based transliteration.
- Core assumption: Many named entities (especially organizations and locations) have standardized translations that differ from phonetic equivalents.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using the Hidden Markov Model, we generated equivalent Hindi phoneme. Finally, merged the phonemes and generated the output text in Hindi...if the extracted name entity is an organization name or location, then search for their exact names in knowledge base."
- Break condition: If the knowledge base is incomplete or outdated, entities will fall back to the less accurate phoneme-based method.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Preprocessing named entities before NMT input prevents translation errors and improves overall translation quality.
- Mechanism: By identifying and correctly translating/transliterating named entities before they enter the NMT pipeline, the system ensures these critical tokens are handled consistently rather than relying on the NMT model's limited contextual understanding.
- Core assumption: NMT models perform poorly on named entities due to lack of world knowledge and context awareness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Since we were translating the name entities, we needed a resource for identifying name entities. Thus, we use d Stanford's name entity recognizer (NER) for this purpose."
  - [section] "This is because, the NMT systems have lack of understanding of context and world knowledge which leads to inconsistent translation quality."
- Break condition: If the preprocessing step introduces errors or if entity identification is inaccurate, the overall translation quality may degrade.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Hidden Markov Models for sequence alignment
  - Why needed here: HMMs provide a probabilistic framework to map English phonemes to Hindi phonemes while handling ambiguities in pronunciation.
  - Quick check question: How does the HMM use transition and likelihood probabilities to find the most probable Hindi phoneme sequence for a given English phoneme sequence?

- Concept: Rule-based phoneme extraction from text
  - Why needed here: The system needs to convert orthographic characters into phonetic units before applying HMM alignment.
  - Quick check question: What are the two classes of characters used in the phoneme extraction algorithm, and how are they combined into phonemes?

- Concept: Named Entity Recognition (NER)
  - Why needed here: The system must first identify which tokens in the input text are named entities before applying the translation/transliteration process.
  - Quick check question: Why is it important to distinguish between person names, location names, and organization names when applying this system?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input text processor -> NER module -> Knowledge base lookup -> Phoneme extractor -> HMM aligner -> Output generator
- Critical path: NER -> Knowledge base check -> (Phoneme extraction -> HMM alignment) -> Output
  The system first attempts knowledge base lookup; only if that fails does it proceed to phoneme-based transliteration.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Knowledge base coverage vs. phoneme algorithm coverage: A comprehensive knowledge base prevents transliteration errors but requires maintenance; the phoneme algorithm handles unseen entities but may produce less natural results.
  - Rule-based phoneme extraction vs. learned phonetic models: Rules are deterministic and explainable but may not capture all pronunciation variations; learned models could be more accurate but require training data.
- Failure signatures:
  - High knowledge base miss rate indicates incomplete entity coverage
  - HMM alignment producing nonsensical phoneme sequences suggests rule-based phoneme extraction is inadequate
  - NER module failing to identify entities means preprocessing step is ineffective
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test knowledge base lookup accuracy on a sample of organization and location names, measuring coverage and correctness
  2. Evaluate phoneme extraction rules on a diverse set of English names to verify they produce phonetically meaningful sequences
  3. Assess HMM alignment quality by comparing output phoneme sequences against human-transliterated references for ambiguous cases

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the long-term impact of integrating a named entity translation module on overall neural machine translation performance across different language pairs and domains?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on Hindi and demonstrates high accuracy but does not discuss long-term performance across languages or domains.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation is limited to one language pair and a fixed set of entities; no longitudinal or cross-lingual studies are presented.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation across multiple language pairs, domains, and over time with updates to the knowledge base and transliteration model.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the phoneme extraction rule set handle edge cases like loanwords, compound names, or entities with non-standard spellings?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The phoneme extraction algorithm is rule-based and may not be robust to irregular or ambiguous inputs not covered in the rule set.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides examples but does not test the system's behavior on edge cases or rare orthographic patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: A test suite containing diverse edge cases, with manual verification of transliteration accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the effect of expanding the knowledge base size and diversity on the accuracy and fluency of translations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge that expanding the knowledge base could improve performance, but do not quantify this effect.
- Why unresolved: No experiments were conducted to measure accuracy gains from incrementally larger or more diverse knowledge bases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing accuracy and fluency with varying knowledge base sizes and entity coverage.

## Limitations

- The exceptionally high accuracy claims (99%+) require cautious interpretation due to lack of detailed evaluation methodology and potential evaluation bias.
- The system's dependence on Stanford NER for entity identification introduces potential error that is not quantified in the reported results.
- The rule-based phoneme extraction approach may not generalize well to non-Indo-European names or edge cases with non-standard spellings.

## Confidence

- High confidence: The general approach of preprocessing named entities before NMT input is well-established in related literature
- Medium confidence: The HMM-based phoneme alignment mechanism is plausible but not fully validated with quantitative evidence
- Low confidence: The specific 99%+ accuracy claims without detailed evaluation methodology

## Next Checks

1. **Independent Evaluation:** Have bilingual Hindi-English speakers manually verify a random sample of 100 translated entities (20 person names, 40 locations, 40 organizations) to establish ground truth accuracy separate from the reported metrics.

2. **Knowledge Base Coverage Analysis:** Systematically measure the percentage of test entities found in the knowledge base versus those requiring phoneme-based transliteration, and evaluate accuracy separately for each category to identify potential bias.

3. **Cross-Lingual Generalization:** Test the phoneme extraction and HMM alignment components on English names from languages with different phonological structures (e.g., names of Chinese or Arabic origin) to assess whether the current rule-based approach generalizes beyond Indo-European names.
