# Evaluating the Effectiveness of Pre-trained Language Models in Predicting the Helpfulness of Online Product Reviews

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.10199
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10199
- **Reference count**: 38
- **Primary result**: Pre-trained language models (RoBERTa and XLM-R) outperform traditional feature-based baselines by up to 23% in RMSE for predicting online review helpfulness

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether state-of-the-art pre-trained language models can effectively predict the helpfulness of online product reviews. The study compares RoBERTa (monolingual) and XLM-R (multilingual) against a baseline using handcrafted features like LIWC on Amazon review data across four product categories. Results show that both pre-trained models significantly outperform the baseline, with RoBERTa generally achieving better performance than XLM-R for English reviews. The findings demonstrate that fine-tuning pre-trained models is a superior approach for review helpfulness prediction, with implications for improving e-commerce recommendation systems.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate RoBERTa and XLM-R pre-trained language models for review helpfulness prediction on Amazon review datasets across four product categories. They implement a baseline using Random Forest with handcrafted features (LIWC, review length, star rating). The pre-trained models use a CLS token embedding as input to a regression head, with experiments testing different layer freezing strategies (2, 4, 6, or all layers). The models are evaluated using regression metrics (RMSE, MAE, PCC) and ranking metrics (Spearman's rho, Kendall's tau, NDCG) to assess both prediction accuracy and ranking performance.

## Key Results
- Pre-trained language models outperform the handcrafted feature baseline by up to 23% in RMSE
- RoBERTa achieves better performance than XLM-R for English review helpfulness prediction
- Adding supplementary features like product ratings and review length further improves model performance
- Fine-tuning only the top layers (2-6 layers) is sufficient for effective prediction while reducing computational cost

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning only the top layers of pre-trained language models is sufficient for effective review helpfulness prediction.
- Mechanism: The top layers of pre-trained models capture task-specific features, while lower layers encode general linguistic features. Freezing lower layers reduces overfitting and computational cost without sacrificing performance.
- Core assumption: Lower layers of pre-trained models already encode universal linguistic features that generalize well to review helpfulness prediction.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Motivated by prior works such as [16,8] that suggest freezing a subset of layers may be beneficial depending on the task and dataset"
  - [section]: "Fine-tuning fewer layers can speed up training while potentially reducing the chances of overfitting"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: RoBERTa outperforms XLM-R for English review helpfulness prediction.
- Mechanism: RoBERTa's monolingual training on English data provides better representation quality for English text compared to XLM-R's multilingual training, which spreads capacity across many languages.
- Core assumption: Monolingual models can achieve better performance than multilingual models when applied to a single language they were trained on.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Results show that both monolingual and multilingual models outperform the baseline by up to 23% in RMSE"
  - [section]: "According to the results, we can confidently say that models based on pre-trained language models outperform the baseline"
  - [section]: "our results suggest that pre-trained multilingual models may not be used for fine-tuning only one language"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Adding additional features (star rating, review length) improves model performance.
- Mechanism: Supplementary features provide information beyond textual content that correlates with helpfulness, allowing the model to capture patterns that text alone might miss.
- Core assumption: Review metadata (rating, length) contains predictive signal for helpfulness that complements textual features.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Additionally, incorporating extra features like product ratings and review length further improves model performance"
  - [section]: "The results from our experiments show that adding the additional features can improve the performance of both RoBERTa and XLM-R-based models"

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do RoBERTa and XLM-R perform on helpfulness prediction for reviews in languages other than English?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that while XLM-R is a multilingual model, it does not consistently outperform RoBERTa on English reviews, raising questions about its effectiveness for other languages.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the models on English reviews, so their performance on other languages remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on multilingual review datasets to compare the models' performance across different languages.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of layers to fine-tune in pre-trained language models for review helpfulness prediction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors conduct ablation tests on freezing different numbers of layers and find that fine-tuning fewer layers can improve performance and reduce computational complexity.
- Why unresolved: The optimal number of layers may vary depending on the dataset size, task complexity, and language model architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across different datasets, tasks, and model architectures to determine the impact of fine-tuning different numbers of layers.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the ranking performance of language models compare to their regression performance in review helpfulness prediction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors evaluate both regression metrics (RMSE, MAE, PCC) and ranking metrics (SPC, KC, NDCG) but do not directly compare the two types of performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper reports results for both regression and ranking metrics but does not analyze the relationship between them or determine which type of performance is more important for practical applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of regression and ranking performance across different datasets and evaluation scenarios to determine the trade-offs and relative importance of each type of metric.

## Limitations
- The study only evaluates models on English-language reviews despite testing a multilingual model (XLM-R)
- Experiments are limited to four product categories, restricting generalizability across different domains
- The paper does not investigate model robustness to adversarial or deceptive reviews

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The core finding that pre-trained language models outperform traditional feature-based baselines (up to 23% RMSE improvement) is well-supported by experimental results across multiple metrics and product categories.
- **Medium confidence**: The conclusion that monolingual models (RoBERTa) outperform multilingual models (XLM-R) for English-only tasks is supported by the data but requires further validation across more languages and domains.
- **Low confidence**: The specific optimal number of layers to fine-tune (2-6 layers) and the universal benefit of adding metadata features (rating, length) need broader validation across different datasets and review types.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-lingual validation**: Test both RoBERTa and XLM-R on multilingual review datasets to determine if the performance gap between monolingual and multilingual models persists when applied to their respective training languages.
2. **Adversarial robustness testing**: Evaluate model performance on datasets containing deceptive or fake reviews to assess whether pre-trained models maintain effectiveness when faced with intentionally misleading content.
3. **Domain generalization study**: Apply the best-performing models to review helpfulness prediction tasks in non-Amazon contexts (e.g., Yelp, TripAdvisor, specialized forums) to test whether the observed performance improvements transfer across different e-commerce ecosystems.
