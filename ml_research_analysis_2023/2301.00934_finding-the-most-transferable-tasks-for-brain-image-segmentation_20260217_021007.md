# Finding the Most Transferable Tasks for Brain Image Segmentation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.00934
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00934
- Reference count: 29
- One-line primary result: A framework that incorporates modality and RoI shape similarity filtering significantly improves transferability metric performance for brain image segmentation source selection.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the task selection problem for transfer learning in brain image segmentation by proposing a framework that leverages prior knowledge about modality and region of interest (RoI) characteristics. The authors demonstrate that transferring from different tasks within the same imaging modality often outperforms transferring from the same task across different modalities, challenging conventional assumptions about source task selection. By incorporating RoI shape similarity filtering alongside modality analysis, the framework improves the performance of state-of-the-art transferability metrics like H-score and OTCE, leading to better source task selection for segmentation tasks.

## Method Summary
The method involves three main stages: Modality Analysis filters candidate source tasks by imaging modality to ensure domain consistency, RoI Analysis computes SSIM-based shape similarity between source and target tasks to identify structurally similar segmentation targets, and Transferability Estimation applies analytical metrics (H-score or OTCE) on the filtered subset to rank source tasks. The approach uses pre-trained 2D U-Net models on binary segmentation tasks derived from brain MRI datasets, with fine-tuning performed only on the decoder while freezing the encoder. Experiments are conducted on three brain image segmentation datasets (FeTS 2021, iSeg-2019, and WMH) with various binary segmentation tasks.

## Key Results
- Same-modality transfer (different tasks) consistently outperforms same-task cross-modality transfer in Dice scores
- RoI shape similarity (SSIM) between source and target tasks correlates with improved transfer performance
- The proposed framework improves Spearman's footrule scores for transferability metric rankings compared to direct metric application

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Same-modality transfer outperforms cross-modality transfer even when source and target tasks differ.
- Mechanism: Convolutional features learned from one imaging modality are better adapted to another task within the same modality because underlying intensity distributions and noise characteristics are similar, allowing the encoder to preserve task-relevant patterns.
- Core assumption: The input domain P(θs(Xs)) = P(θs(Xt)) holds when both tasks use the same imaging modality.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] states "transferring from different tasks under the same modality is often more successful than transferring from the same task under different modalities"; Table I shows average Dice scores favoring same-modality transfer.
- Break condition: If source and target tasks involve fundamentally different tissue types with non-overlapping intensity ranges even within the same modality.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Stronger RoI shape similarity between source and target tasks correlates with better transfer performance within the same modality.
- Mechanism: SSIM in label space captures structural similarity of segmentation targets; when spatial layout and shape of regions of interest align, the decoder can reuse shape priors more effectively.
- Core assumption: Segmentation labels encode sufficient spatial structure to make SSIM a meaningful proxy for transfer difficulty.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] states "within the same modality, transferring from the source task that has stronger RoI shape similarity with the target task can significantly improve the final transfer performance"; Table II and III show RoI-Sim scores correlate with Dice improvements.
- Break condition: If labels are noisy or poorly aligned spatially, SSIM may not reflect true shape similarity.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Incorporating prior knowledge (modality and RoI filtering) into transferability estimation metrics improves ranking accuracy over using metrics alone.
- Mechanism: By pruning the candidate pool to tasks with matching modalities and higher RoI similarity, the remaining set is more homogeneous, making analytical metrics more discriminative and less prone to noise from domain shifts.
- Core assumption: Analytical transferability metrics are sensitive to domain gaps; filtering reduces such gaps and thus improves metric reliability.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] states "the proposed framework improves the performance of state-of-the-art transferability metrics like H-score and OTCE on the source selection problem"; Table VI shows improved Spearman's footrule scores.
- Break condition: If the filtered subset is too small or not representative, metrics may overfit to a narrow set.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Modality Analysis -> RoI Analysis -> Transferability Estimation -> Ranking -> Selection
- Critical path: Modality Analysis → RoI Analysis → Transferability Estimation → Ranking → Selection
- Design tradeoffs: Using only modality filtering risks including poor shape matches; using only SSIM risks mixing modalities with incompatible feature spaces. The two-step filter balances both concerns but increases preprocessing complexity.
- Failure signatures: Low correlation between filtered ranking and ground truth Dice; high Spearman's footrule scores; poor performance when source/target tasks are from different datasets with large domain shifts.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify that cross-task same-modality transfer outperforms same-task cross-modality transfer using Dice scores.
  2. Compute RoI-Sim between tasks of the same modality and check correlation with transfer performance.
  3. Apply the full framework on a multi-partition dataset and compare ranking accuracy with baseline (no filtering).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can transferability metrics be further refined to handle larger gaps between source and target tasks in the medical domain?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that "current transferability estimation metrics are not sufficient to handle the large gaps between source and target tasks and thus require further refinement, particularly in the medical domain."
- Why unresolved: While the authors propose a framework that incorporates prior knowledge to improve transferability metrics, they acknowledge that these metrics still need further refinement to handle the unique challenges of the medical domain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments on a wider range of medical image segmentation tasks and datasets, and comparing the performance of different transferability metrics with and without the proposed framework, would provide evidence for the effectiveness of further refinements.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of transferability metric affect the source selection process in the proposed framework?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors use H-score and OTCE as transferability metrics in their experiments and show that incorporating prior knowledge improves their performance. However, they do not explore other transferability metrics or compare their performance.
- Why unresolved: Different transferability metrics may have varying strengths and weaknesses, and their performance may be affected differently by the incorporation of prior knowledge.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing the performance of different transferability metrics, with and without the proposed framework, on various medical image segmentation tasks would provide insights into the impact of the choice of metric on the source selection process.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed framework perform on other medical image datasets beyond brain images?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that they envision the framework can be used on other medical image datasets besides brain images.
- Why unresolved: The authors only conduct experiments on brain image datasets, and it is unclear how the framework would perform on other types of medical images, such as chest X-rays or retinal images.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying the proposed framework to other medical image datasets and evaluating its performance on source selection would provide evidence for its generalizability to other medical imaging domains.

## Limitations
- The study relies on controlled experimental conditions with specific datasets and task definitions
- The assumption that same-modality transfer is universally superior may not hold with heterogeneous anatomical structures or multi-site data
- The effectiveness of SSIM as a shape similarity metric for RoI filtering lacks strong external validation from broader literature

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The experimental observation that same-modality transfer outperforms cross-modality transfer for brain segmentation tasks (supported by direct quantitative evidence in Table I)
- **Medium confidence**: The correlation between RoI shape similarity and transfer performance (supported by internal correlation analysis but limited external validation)
- **Medium confidence**: The improvement in transferability metric performance through prior filtering (supported by ranking metrics but dependent on specific dataset characteristics)

## Next Checks
1. Test the framework on multi-site brain MRI datasets with varying acquisition protocols to assess robustness to domain shift beyond simple modality differences
2. Validate the SSIM-based RoI similarity metric against alternative shape comparison methods (e.g., Dice coefficient, Hausdorff distance) to ensure findings are not metric-specific
3. Evaluate the framework's performance when source and target tasks have partially overlapping but non-identical anatomical regions to test limits of the RoI filtering approach
