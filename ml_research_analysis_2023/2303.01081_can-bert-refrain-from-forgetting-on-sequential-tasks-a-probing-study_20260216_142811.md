# Can BERT Refrain from Forgetting on Sequential Tasks? A Probing Study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.01081
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01081
- Reference count: 28
- Key outcome: BERT retains high-quality representations for previously learned tasks without memory replay, challenging catastrophic forgetting assumptions

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the conventional belief that pre-trained language models suffer from catastrophic forgetting when learning sequential tasks. Through a systematic probing study, the authors demonstrate that BERT can maintain high-quality representations for previously learned tasks even without memory replay mechanisms. The research introduces a novel method to track BERT's encoding ability across different tasks and training stages, revealing that the model preserves its representation quality remarkably well. The study also decomposes catastrophic forgetting into intra-task and inter-task components, providing new insights into how PLMs manage knowledge retention during sequential learning.

## Method Summary
The study employs a sequential learning approach where BERT is trained on a stream of tasks without memory replay, with periodic checkpoints saved every 5,000 training examples. For each checkpoint, a re-initialized linear probing decoder is attached and trained on held-out data from each task while the BERT encoder remains frozen. This allows measurement of representation quality through classification accuracy on previous tasks. The method tracks how encoding ability changes over time and compares performance with and without episodic memory replay at different intervals (10k, 30k, 60k, 115k examples). The approach uses five text classification datasets and four question answering datasets, with 115,000 training examples and 7,600 testing examples for text classification tasks.

## Key Results
- BERT maintains high probing accuracy for previous tasks without memory replay, contradicting catastrophic forgetting assumptions
- Sparse memory replay (every 30,000-60,000 examples) significantly improves performance by reducing inter-task forgetting
- The study identifies intra-task forgetting (minimal in BERT) and inter-task forgetting (significant without replay) as distinct phenomena

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
BERT's representations retain task-specific structure even after learning new tasks without replay. The learned representation sub-spaces for each class remain topologically ordered—nearby vectors in the original task space stay close after learning new tasks. This assumes representation vectors for the same class cluster tightly and maintain relative spatial relationships across tasks. Evidence shows BERT can generate high-quality representations for previously learned tasks under sparse replay or no replay. The mechanism breaks if new task learning significantly rotates or distorts representation sub-spaces, breaking topological ordering.

### Mechanism 2
Catastrophic forgetting can be decomposed into intra-task and inter-task forgetting. Intra-task forgetting is minimal because BERT preserves high-quality class representations; inter-task forgetting occurs when representations of different tasks overlap in the shared space. This assumes class-level representation cones remain non-overlapping within a task but may overlap across tasks. Evidence shows representation sub-spaces of different classes within one task are distinguishable to each other, while previous task sub-spaces tend to overlap with current ones without replay. The mechanism breaks if representation cones for classes within the same task begin to overlap, increasing intra-task forgetting.

### Mechanism 3
Sparse memory replay realigns representation sub-spaces to decoder weight vectors. During replay, representation cones rotate closer to their corresponding decoder column vectors, reducing inter-task confusion. This assumes decoder weights remain relatively stable while replay adjusts representation directions. Evidence shows memory replay obliges the vectors of previous tasks rotating to their corresponding column-vectors in decoder efficiently. The mechanism breaks if decoder weights change significantly during replay, diminishing the realignment benefit.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Text encoder (BERT-base) -> Task decoder (linear layer for classification, span boundary decoders for QA) -> Memory replay module (optional) -> Probing decoder (re-initialized linear layer)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Sequentially train BERT on task stream with/without replay
  2. Save encoder checkpoints periodically
  3. Attach new probing decoder to each checkpoint
  4. Train decoder on held-out task data (encoder frozen)
  5. Evaluate decoder performance to assess representation quality

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Fixed encoder vs. fine-tuned: Freezing encoder isolates representation quality from decoder adaptation
  - Single-layer probing decoder vs. complex: Simpler decoders reduce confounding and match prior work for comparability
  - Replay frequency: Higher frequency reduces inter-task forgetting but increases computational cost

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Probing accuracy drops to near-zero after learning new tasks → catastrophic forgetting dominates
  - Intra-task probing scores remain high but macro-averaged accuracy low → inter-task confusion
  - No improvement from replay → replay interval too sparse or memory too small

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Train BERT sequentially on text classification tasks without replay; probe each checkpoint to measure representation retention
  2. Repeat experiment with varying replay intervals (10k, 30k, 60k, 115k examples) to quantify replay impact
  3. Visualize representation spaces via t-SNE before/after replay to observe cone realignment and overlap reduction

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does the intrinsic ability of PLMs to maintain knowledge across tasks apply equally to all types of NLP tasks, including more complex or domain-specific tasks? The paper investigates text classification and extractive question answering tasks, finding that BERT and other PLMs maintain encoding ability for previous tasks even without memory replay. This remains unresolved because the study focuses on relatively simple tasks that may not fully represent the diversity of NLP tasks. Complex or domain-specific tasks might have different requirements that could affect the PLM's ability to retain knowledge. Conducting similar experiments on a wider range of NLP tasks, such as machine translation, summarization, or domain-specific tasks like medical or legal text processing, would provide insights into the generalizability of the findings.

### Open Question 2
What is the theoretical limit to the number of tasks a PLM can learn sequentially while maintaining performance on previous tasks, and how does this limit vary with model size and architecture? The paper mentions that the number of tasks is limited and suggests exploring a longer sequence of tasks in the future. It also investigates various PLM scales and architectures but does not determine a theoretical limit. This remains unresolved because the study does not explore the maximum number of tasks a PLM can handle before performance degradation becomes significant, and the impact of model size and architecture on this limit is not fully explored. Systematic experiments increasing the number of tasks and varying model sizes and architectures would help identify the theoretical limits and the factors influencing them.

### Open Question 3
How does the intrinsic ability of PLMs to retain knowledge interact with different training strategies, such as curriculum learning or meta-learning, in a continual learning setting? The paper focuses on sequential task learning without exploring how different training strategies might enhance or interact with the PLM's intrinsic ability to retain knowledge. This remains unresolved because the study does not investigate the potential synergies between the PLM's intrinsic ability and other training strategies that could further improve continual learning performance. Experiments comparing the performance of PLMs using different training strategies, such as curriculum learning or meta-learning, in a continual learning setting would provide insights into potential interactions and improvements.

## Limitations
- The probing methodology assumes simple linear decoders adequately measure representation quality, potentially missing complex task dependencies
- Findings based on BERT-base may not generalize to larger models or different pre-training objectives
- Study focuses on sequential task learning without addressing parallel or multi-task scenarios where representation overlap might differ

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The core finding that BERT retains task-specific representation quality without catastrophic forgetting is well-supported by the probing experiments. The geometric analysis of representation cones and the distinction between intra-task and inter-task forgetting are methodologically sound.

**Medium Confidence:** The mechanism explaining how sparse replay realigns representation sub-spaces to decoder weights is theoretically plausible but could benefit from more rigorous mathematical formalization. The decomposition of forgetting into intra-task and inter-task components provides useful framework but may oversimplify complex representation dynamics.

**Low Confidence:** Claims about BERT's intrinsic ability to preserve knowledge without any forgetting may be overstated, as the study shows some inter-task confusion still occurs. The generalizability to other PLMs and task types remains uncertain.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-model validation:** Replicate the probing study with other transformer architectures (RoBERTa, XLNet, GPT variants) to determine if the findings extend beyond BERT.

2. **Representation geometry analysis:** Conduct detailed analysis of representation cone angles and overlap metrics across tasks to quantify the extent of inter-task forgetting and test the proposed realignment mechanism more rigorously.

3. **Large-scale task transfer:** Test the probing methodology on more diverse and challenging task sequences, including cross-domain transfer and multi-modal tasks, to assess real-world applicability of the findings.
