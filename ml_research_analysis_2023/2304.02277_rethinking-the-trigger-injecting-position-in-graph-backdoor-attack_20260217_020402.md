# Rethinking the Trigger-injecting Position in Graph Backdoor Attack

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.02277
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02277
- **Reference count**: 34
- **Key outcome**: LIAS strategy achieves higher attack success rates (>97% in most cases) compared to MIAS for graph backdoor attacks on GNNs

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the position of trigger injection affects the effectiveness of graph backdoor attacks on Graph Neural Networks. By comparing two strategies—injecting triggers into most important areas (MIAS) versus least important areas (LIAS)—the authors demonstrate that LIAS generally achieves higher attack success rates while maintaining negligible clean accuracy drop. The study uses explanation techniques to understand why LIAS performs better, revealing that successfully misclassified samples have higher recall scores between clean and poisoned feature importance orderings.

## Method Summary
The authors implement a feature-trigger backdoor attack on node classification tasks using Cora and CiteSeer datasets with GCN and GAT models. They employ GNNExplainer to identify representative features for each sample, then inject triggers into either the most important (MIAS) or least important (LIAS) features. The poisoned training data contains 10% of samples relabeled to the target class with modified features. After training backdoored models, they evaluate attack success rate (ASR) and clean accuracy drop (CAD), then use GNNExplainer again to compute recall scores between original and new feature importance orderings to explain the attack effectiveness differences.

## Key Results
- LIAS strategy achieves ASR above 97% in most experimental conditions, slightly outperforming MIAS
- Both MIAS and LIAS maintain negligible clean accuracy drop (CAD)
- GCN on Cora with MIAS shows an exception where unsuccessful misclassifications still have high recall scores
- Successfully misclassified nodes generally have significantly higher recall scores than unsuccessfully misclassified nodes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LIAS outperforms MIAS because it injects triggers into features the model considers unimportant, reducing interference with original class patterns
- Core assumption: The model's learned feature importance hierarchy remains stable between clean and poisoned training
- Evidence anchors: ASR results showing LIAS superiority, observation that LIAS causes less disruption to original patterns

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Recall score effectively measures how well the backdoored model recognizes the trigger pattern
- Core assumption: GNNExplainer provides consistent feature importance rankings reflecting the model's decision-making
- Evidence anchors: Correlation between high recall scores and successful misclassification

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: GCN on Cora with MIAS shows an exception where high recall scores don't guarantee attack success due to dual recognition pathways
- Core assumption: GCN architecture has sufficient capacity to learn multiple classification pathways
- Evidence anchors: Exception observed where unsuccessfully misclassified nodes still have high recall scores

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Cora/CiteSeer datasets → clean train/test split → poisoned sample generation → clean GNN training → poisoned training with trigger injection → backdoored model training → ASR and CAD computation → GNNExplainer application → feature importance ranking → recall score calculation

- **Critical path**: Dataset preparation → clean model training → poisoned dataset generation (with GNNExplainer) → backdoored model training → attack evaluation → explanation analysis

- **Design tradeoffs**: MIAS vs LIAS represents a tradeoff between stealth (CAD) and attack effectiveness (ASR)

- **Failure signatures**: Low ASR with high CAD suggests too disruptive trigger injection; high ASR with very low recall scores suggests model isn't properly recognizing the trigger

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run MIAS and LIAS attacks on Cora dataset with GCN, measure ASR and CAD
  2. Compute recall scores for successfully vs unsuccessfully classified samples
  3. Extend target and predicted masks by 2x trigger length and recompute recall scores

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the performance difference between MIAS and LIAS hold for graph classification tasks?
- Basis: Authors explicitly state they plan to extend work to graph classification tasks
- Why unresolved: Not yet experimentally verified
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results on graph classification benchmarks

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does extension rate γ affect recall scores for successfully and unsuccessfully misclassified nodes in MIAS?
- Basis: Authors mention γ > 1 can be used but only test with γ=2
- Why unresolved: Only tested with one extension rate
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments with various extension rates

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would alternative explanation techniques produce different insights about backdoor attack behavior?
- Basis: Paper uses GNNExplainer exclusively while acknowledging other methods exist
- Why unresolved: Only one explanation technique was used
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis using multiple explanation techniques

## Limitations
- Assumes GNNExplainer's feature importance rankings remain stable after poisoning
- Focuses only on binary feature vectors and two specific GNN architectures
- Does not test generalizability to other graph structures, feature types, or GNN variants

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: LIAS consistently outperforms MIAS in ASR across most experimental conditions
- **Medium confidence**: Explanation that LIAS works better due to injecting into less important features
- **Medium confidence**: Correlation between high recall scores and successful misclassification
