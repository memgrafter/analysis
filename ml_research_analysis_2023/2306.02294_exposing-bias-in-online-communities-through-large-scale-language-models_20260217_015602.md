# Exposing Bias in Online Communities through Large-Scale Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02294
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02294
- Reference count: 18
- Primary result: Fine-tuning language models on community conversations reveals measurable biases that vary across online communities and demographic dimensions.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a scalable method for measuring bias in online communities by fine-tuning large language models on their conversations and evaluating the resulting models for bias using sentiment and toxicity classifiers. The approach examines six diverse communities (including Reddit forums and religious discussion boards) across five demographic dimensions, finding that bias varies significantly in type and intensity between communities. The results demonstrate that fine-tuned models are more toxic and negative than baseline models, suggesting that biases present in training data transfer to the model. This methodology offers a customizable framework for bias analysis that can adapt to different communities and bias metrics.

## Method Summary
The method fine-tunes GPT-Neo 1.3B on six social media datasets (1.4M–2.8M examples each) for 2 epochs, then evaluates bias by prompting the models with conversational templates containing demographic keywords. Generated text is analyzed using VADER for sentiment and Detoxify for toxicity across five dimensions: gender, race, sexual orientation, religion, and socioeconomic class. The approach builds on placeholder templates to capture conversational structures and uses 266 unique prompts to elicit representative text for bias analysis.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned models show significantly higher toxicity and negative sentiment than baseline GPT-Neo models
- Bias patterns vary substantially across the six studied communities, with some showing strong religious bias while others exhibit gender or racial biases
- The method successfully transfers community-specific biases to language models, validating the approach for bias measurement
- Different bias dimensions manifest differently across communities, suggesting context-dependent bias expression

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning a pre-trained language model on community-specific data transfers the biases present in that data to the model.
- Mechanism: The model learns statistical associations between social groups and sentiment/toxicity labels during fine-tuning. When prompted with different demographics, it generates text reflecting those learned associations.
- Core assumption: The training data contains measurable bias that the model can learn and reproduce in generations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "This work utilises the flaw of bias in language models to explore the biases of six different online communities."
  - [section]: "We examine the bias of online communities by probing correspondingly fine-tuned language models."
- Break condition: If the fine-tuning data lacks sufficient bias examples, or if the model overfits to niche topics and ignores demographic prompts.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sentiment and toxicity classifiers can quantify representational bias by measuring differences in sentiment/toxicity across demographics.
- Mechanism: The model generates text for prompts containing different social groups. Automated classifiers assign sentiment/toxicity scores to these generations. Differences in scores across groups indicate bias.
- Core assumption: Sentiment and toxicity classifiers are reliable indicators of representational bias in generated text.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The bias of the resulting models is evaluated by prompting the models with different demographics and comparing the sentiment and toxicity values of these generations."
  - [section]: "Sentiment analysis aims to determine the sentiments and emotions expressed in a given text... Toxicity detection is often utilised to quantify harmful biases in conjunction with sentiment analysis."
- Break condition: If classifiers are biased themselves or if the generated text is too ambiguous to properly classify.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using conversational templates and varied demographic keywords provides a more comprehensive bias analysis than fixed datasets.
- Mechanism: Templates mimic social media post structures and allow prompting for different social groups. Multiple keywords per demographic capture various angles. This approach enables targeted analysis of bias dimensions.
- Core assumption: Template-based prompting can elicit representative text for different social groups.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Building upon the placeholder templates created by Sheng et al. (Sheng et al. 2019), we introduce templates that capture conversational and social media post structures."
  - [section]: "We construct six different placeholder templates... and a set of demographic keywords that were influenced by Sheng et al. (Sheng et al. 2019) and Barikeri et al. (Barikeri et al. 2021)."
- Break condition: If templates fail to elicit relevant text or if keyword choices are ambiguous/confused.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: GPT-Neo 1.3B (pre-trained model) -> Fine-tuning on community data -> Prompting with templates + keywords -> Sentiment/toxicity classification -> Bias analysis
- Critical path: Fine-tuning -> Prompting -> Classification -> Analysis
- Design tradeoffs: Using larger models vs. computational cost; relying on automated classifiers vs. potential biases in classifiers; template-based vs. fixed dataset approaches.
- Failure signatures: Model overfits to niche topics; classifiers are biased; templates fail to elicit relevant text.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune GPT-Neo on a small sample of community data and verify it generates relevant text.
  2. Test prompting with templates and keywords on a fine-tuned model and check if it elicits representative text for different groups.
  3. Run sentiment/toxicity classification on generated text and verify it captures differences across demographics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do biases from pre-training datasets impact fine-tuned language models, and can these biases be amplified during the fine-tuning process?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the potential for biases from pre-training datasets to be amplified during fine-tuning, especially if the bias is related to a topic present in the fine-tuning data.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges this possibility, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the extent to which pre-training biases are amplified or mitigated during fine-tuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative study analyzing the biases in pre-trained models versus fine-tuned models, focusing on the degree of amplification or mitigation of specific biases.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How effective are the current sentiment and toxicity classifiers in accurately capturing and measuring biases in language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges the limitations of using sentiment and toxicity classifiers for bias analysis, noting that these classifiers can suffer from bias themselves and may not capture all forms of bias.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses these classifiers but does not provide a comprehensive evaluation of their effectiveness or explore alternative bias metrics that could offer a more nuanced understanding of bias.
- What evidence would resolve it: An evaluation of the performance of sentiment and toxicity classifiers in detecting biases, along with a comparison to other potential bias metrics, would provide insights into their effectiveness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different online communities contribute to the development of biases in language models, and what factors influence the manifestation of these biases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explores the biases of six online communities by fine-tuning language models with their conversations and evaluating the resulting models for bias.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies differences in bias between communities, it does not delve into the underlying factors that contribute to these differences or explore how community characteristics influence bias development.
- What evidence would resolve it: A deeper analysis of the characteristics of each community, such as the topics discussed, the demographics of users, and the nature of interactions, could provide insights into the factors that influence bias development.

## Limitations

- The study relies on automated classifiers (VADER and Detoxify) that may contain their own biases, potentially conflating classifier bias with dataset bias
- The six studied communities may not be representative of all online spaces, limiting generalizability of findings
- The 2-epoch fine-tuning approach may miss subtler biases that could emerge with longer training

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core methodology of using fine-tuned models to transfer and measure dataset biases is sound and well-established in the literature.
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of template-based prompting and the reliability of automated classifiers for bias detection.
- **Low Confidence**: The completeness of bias capture across all relevant dimensions and the generalizability of findings to communities beyond the six studied.

## Next Checks

1. **Classifier Validation Study**: Conduct a controlled test where known biased and unbiased text samples are evaluated by VADER and Detoxify to quantify their sensitivity and potential biases across different demographic groups.

2. **Template Ablation Experiment**: Systematically vary the template structure and keyword selection to measure their impact on generated text characteristics, comparing results across different template variants.

3. **Extended Fine-tuning Analysis**: Train models for varying numbers of epochs (1, 2, 4, 8) on a subset of communities and track how bias measurements evolve to determine if the 2-epoch choice captures the full bias spectrum.
