# Prediction under Latent Subgroup Shifts with High-Dimensional Observations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.13472
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13472
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: Partial RPM maintains high prediction accuracy (~90%) as observation dimensionality increases, while structured VAE methods degrade

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of prediction under latent subgroup shifts in graphical models when dealing with high-dimensional observations like images. The core problem is that unobserved confounding latent variables can shift between source and target environments, and traditional latent shift adaptation methods struggle to scale to high-dimensional data due to reconstruction requirements. The authors propose a "partial" Recognition-Parametrised Model (RPM) that parametrizes factors involving high-dimensional observations in recognition form while keeping generative factors for categorical variables, thus avoiding explicit reconstruction.

The method demonstrates strong empirical performance across numerical simulations and real image data. In simulations, the partial RPM maintains approximately 90% prediction accuracy even as observation dimensionality increases from 2 to 20 dimensions, while structured VAE methods degrade to baseline performance. On CIFAR-10 image data, the partial RPM successfully adapts to latent shifts when sufficient training data is available, with accuracy improving as dataset size increases. The key innovation is that by avoiding reconstruction of high-dimensional observations, the model can focus only on information shared through the latent variable.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces a partial Recognition-Parametrised Model (RPM) for prediction under latent subgroup shifts. The method parametrizes factors involving high-dimensional observations in recognition form while keeping generative factors for categorical variables. This approach avoids the need to reconstruct observations, allowing the model to focus only on information shared through the latent variable. The partial RPM is designed to handle scenarios where observations are high-dimensional (like images) but the underlying latent structure is low-dimensional and categorical.

## Key Results
- In numerical simulations, partial RPM maintains high prediction accuracy (~90%) as observation dimensionality increases from 2 to 20 dimensions
- Structured VAE methods degrade to baseline performance under increasing dimensionality
- On CIFAR-10 image data, partial RPM successfully adapts to latent shifts with sufficient training data, with accuracy improving as dataset size increases
- Source-trained models without adaptation fail to adapt to latent shifts

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The partial RPM works by strategically avoiding reconstruction of high-dimensional observations. Instead of modeling the full generative process for high-dimensional data (which requires expensive reconstruction), it parametrizes the recognition distribution for high-dimensional observations while maintaining generative factors for the categorical latent variables. This allows the model to capture the shared information between environments through the latent variable without being burdened by the complexity of reconstructing high-dimensional data. The method leverages the assumption that high-dimensional observations contain irrelevant noise beyond what's needed to predict the target variable through the latent structure.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: X (high-dim observations) -> Z (latent) -> Y (target) with U (latent subgroup) influencing both Z and Y

Critical path: The model learns P(Y|X,U) through the recognition distribution of high-dimensional observations while maintaining generative structure for the categorical latent variable U. The partial RPM avoids modeling P(X|U) directly, instead focusing on the conditional distribution P(Y|X,U).

Design tradeoffs: The key tradeoff is between model expressiveness and computational feasibility. By avoiding reconstruction of high-dimensional observations, the method gains scalability but may miss some information in X that could be relevant for prediction. The approach assumes that sufficient predictive information flows through the latent variable U.

Failure signatures: The method may fail when: (1) the latent variable U doesn't capture all relevant structure for predicting Y, (2) there's insufficient data in the target environment to learn the recognition distribution, (3) the partial parametrization discards information in X that is actually predictive of Y beyond what's captured by U.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper doesn't explicitly call out open questions, but several key uncertainties emerge from the work:

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the partial RPM's performance scale when the latent variable U has more than 3 categories (kU > 3) while maintaining high-dimensional observations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper tests with kU = 3 in CIFAR-10 experiments but doesn't explore larger latent spaces, though the authors note that structured VAE models require higher dimensional latents to capture more structure.
- Why unresolved: The paper only demonstrates with kU = 3, and it's unclear whether the partial RPM's advantage over reconstruction-based methods persists as latent dimensionality increases, especially since the method avoids explicit reconstruction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying kU from 3 to 10+ while keeping observation dimensionality fixed, comparing partial RPM against structured VAE baselines on the same tasks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the partial RPM maintain its advantage over structured VAE methods when proxy variable W contains relevant information (not just noise) alongside irrelevant dimensions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The numerical simulations add irrelevant dimensions to X, but the paper doesn't test scenarios where W also contains both relevant and irrelevant information, which would be more realistic.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments isolate the effect of irrelevant dimensions in X, but real-world proxy variables likely contain mixed signal, and it's unclear how the partial RPM's approach of avoiding reconstruction handles this complexity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments where W has both informative and noisy dimensions, comparing partial RPM performance against structured VAE methods as the ratio of relevant to irrelevant dimensions in W varies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the partial RPM's adaptation performance change when the shift in latent distribution between source and target is extreme (e.g., P(U) = [0.99, 0.01] vs Q(U) = [0.01, 0.99])?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that in source P, π = softmax([1.0, 0.1, 0.1]) and in target Q, π = softmax([0.1, 0.1, 1.0]), but doesn't test extreme shifts where one category dominates in one distribution and is rare in the other.
- Why unresolved: The current CIFAR-10 experiment uses moderate shifts, but extreme distributional changes could challenge the method's ability to identify and adapt to the latent structure, particularly if certain latent states become very rare in one environment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments systematically varying the entropy of the latent distribution shift, measuring adaptation accuracy as the KL divergence between P(U) and Q(U) increases.

## Limitations
- Performance critically depends on sufficient training data availability in the target environment
- Theoretical guarantees focus on prediction consistency rather than identification of true causal structure
- Method may lose information by avoiding full generative modeling of high-dimensional observations
- Limited evaluation on real-world datasets beyond CIFAR-10

## Confidence
- High confidence in the core methodological contribution of partial RPM formulation and its ability to scale to high-dimensional observations
- Medium confidence in simulation results showing robustness to increasing dimensionality, though these use simplified Gaussian models
- Medium confidence in CIFAR-10 image results, given the promising but data-dependent performance patterns observed
