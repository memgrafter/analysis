# Dynamic Scheduled Sampling with Imitation Loss for Neural Text Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.13753
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13753
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: DySI improves translation quality and robustness by dynamically adjusting sampling probability based on training-time accuracy and incorporating imitation loss.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes Dynamic Scheduled Sampling with Imitation Loss (DySI) to address exposure bias in neural text generation. The method dynamically adjusts the probability of using model-generated tokens during training based on training-time accuracy, while also incorporating an imitation loss to encourage the model to behave similarly whether given ground-truth or self-generated tokens as input. Experiments on machine translation and language modeling show that DySI significantly improves translation quality and robustness to perturbations and toxic text compared to baseline teacher forcing and scheduled sampling methods.

## Method Summary
DySI trains a neural text generation model using a dynamic scheduler that determines the number of positions to replace with model-generated tokens based on training-time accuracy. The model computes accuracy from teacher-forced predictions and samples uniformly from 0 to accuracy * sequence length, scaled by β. An imitation loss using KL divergence aligns the operative decoder's distribution with the teacher-forced decoder's distribution, regularizing the model to mimic soft predictions. The method requires two forward passes per training instance - one teacher-forced and one operative - to compute accuracy and imitation loss.

## Key Results
- DySI achieves 0.2-0.3 BLEU improvements over baseline teacher forcing on WMT'14 En-De and En-Fr translation tasks
- The method demonstrates improved robustness under nucleus sampling, length normalization, and toxic text perturbations
- DySI shows consistent improvements across different hyperparameter settings of β and α

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The dynamic scheduler adjusts sampling probability based on training-time accuracy, not training steps.
- Mechanism: During each training instance, the model's teacher-forced predictions are compared to ground truth. The accuracy is computed as the proportion of correct predictions. The number of positions to replace with model-generated tokens is sampled uniformly from 0 to accuracy * sequence length, scaled by β.
- Core assumption: Training accuracy is a more reliable indicator of model readiness than training steps, which vary with batch size and task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Dy namic Scheduled Sampling with Imitation Loss (D YSI), which maintains the schedule based solely on the training time accuracy"
  - [section]: "We propose a scheduler that solely depends on training time accuracy. By tracking training progress though accuracy, we avoid having to perform a costly heuristic search to ﬁnd a suitable scheduler for each different problem setup."
- Break condition: If accuracy is not a good proxy for model readiness (e.g., early in training when accuracy is near zero), the dynamic schedule may under-sample model-generated tokens.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The imitation loss aligns the operative decoder's behavior with the teacher-forced decoder's behavior using KL divergence.
- Mechanism: The teacher-forced decoder generates a distribution over the vocabulary at each step. The KL divergence between this "expert" distribution and the operative decoder's distribution is minimized, regularizing the operative decoder to mimic the expert's soft predictions.
- Core assumption: Matching the full distribution (not just the argmax) provides richer supervision and better generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "introducing an imitation loss, which attempts to make the behavior of the decoder indistinguishable from the behavior of a teacher-forced decoder"
  - [section]: "To match the operative decoder behavior to the teacher-forced decoder behavior, we bring the intuition of imitation learning. In particular, the operative decoder can be seen as a learner, which tries to imitate the behavior of an expert at each decoding step."
- Break condition: If the teacher-forced distribution is poorly calibrated (e.g., overconfident), KL divergence may reinforce bad behavior.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Random sampling of positions to replace with model-generated tokens prevents confirmation bias and exposes the model to diverse contexts.
- Mechanism: Instead of selecting high-confidence positions, N positions are chosen uniformly at random from the sequence. This ensures the operative decoder encounters varied states, including mistakes, improving robustness.
- Core assumption: Exposure to diverse, even erroneous, contexts during training helps the model generalize better at inference.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "As a sampling strategy, we choose N positions in the sequence randomly and uniformly. A random selection as opposed to selection based on high conﬁdence avoids conﬁrmation bias... and potentially exposes the model to more varied input-output samples, which in turn helps the behavior cloning."
- Break condition: If the model is too early in training and generates mostly wrong tokens, random sampling may introduce too much noise.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Teacher-forced decoder -> Dynamic scheduler -> Operative decoder -> Imitation loss module

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass through teacher-forced decoder to get distributions and predictions.
  2. Compute training accuracy from predictions.
  3. Dynamic scheduler samples N positions based on accuracy.
  4. Generate mixed input sequence for operative decoder.
  5. Forward pass through operative decoder.
  6. Compute MLE loss and imitation loss.
  7. Backward pass to update operative decoder parameters.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Two forward passes (teacher-forced and operative) increase training time but improve robustness.
  - Random sampling avoids confirmation bias but may introduce noise early in training.
  - Imitation loss provides richer supervision but requires storing and computing distributions.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low BLEU scores despite high training accuracy: imitation loss may be misaligned or too strong.
  - High variance in validation performance: random sampling may be too noisy; consider confidence-based sampling.
  - Slow convergence: two forward passes may be too expensive; consider reducing sequence length or batch size.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline: Train with teacher forcing only; measure BLEU and M AUVE scores.
  2. DySI without imitation loss: Use dynamic scheduler but no imitation loss; compare to baseline.
  3. DySI with imitation loss: Full DySI; compare to baseline and step 2.

## Limitations
- The dynamic scheduler's reliance on training-time accuracy may under-sample model-generated tokens early in training when accuracy is low, potentially slowing convergence.
- The imitation loss assumes teacher-forced distributions are well-calibrated, but early in training these distributions may be overconfident and reinforce poor behavior.
- Random sampling strategy may introduce excessive noise when the model's predictions are largely incorrect, potentially destabilizing training.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The experimental results showing DySI outperforming scheduled sampling and baseline teacher forcing on WMT translation tasks (BLEU improvements of 0.2-0.3 points) are well-supported by the data.

**Medium Confidence**: The robustness claims are supported by experiments, but the mechanisms by which DySI specifically improves robustness are not fully explored.

**Low Confidence**: The claim that accuracy-based scheduling is superior to step-based scheduling across all problem setups is not fully validated, as the paper only tests on translation and language modeling tasks.

## Next Checks

3. **Distribution calibration check**: Compare the entropy and calibration of teacher-forced distributions across training epochs to ensure that the imitation loss is not reinforcing overconfident predictions, which could harm generalization.
