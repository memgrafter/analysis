# Learning with Rejection for Abstractive Text Summarization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.08531
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08531
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: Rejection learning improves factuality (DAE from 34.6% to 43.4%) while increasing abstractiveness on XSUM dataset

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of hallucination in abstractive summarization systems caused by noise in training data, where reference summaries contain unsupported information not present in source documents. The authors propose a rejection learning approach that introduces a rejection class during training, allowing the model to learn to identify and reject potentially hallucinated tokens rather than forcing them to match noisy reference summaries. They further develop a regularized decoding objective that penalizes candidate summaries based on the rejection probabilities learned during training.

Experiments on the XSUM dataset show that their method significantly improves factuality (DAE score from 34.6% to 43.4%, FACTCC from 22.8% to 32.3%) while simultaneously increasing abstractiveness, outperforming five baseline models including loss truncation and RL-based approaches. The method achieves 77.6% accuracy in rejecting non-factual hallucinations on human-annotated data.

## Method Summary
The approach introduces a rejection class during training that allows the model to optionally reject potentially noisy or hallucinated tokens instead of forcing them to match reference summaries. During training, a rejection loss is applied to entity tokens for the first m steps to fully recover cross-entropy training. At inference time, a regularized decoding objective penalizes candidate summaries based on the rejection probability learned during training. The method is evaluated on the XSUM dataset using BART-LARGE as the backbone model, with experiments showing improved factuality and abstractiveness compared to multiple baselines.

## Key Results
- FACTCC score improved from 22.8% to 32.3% compared to baseline BART model
- DAE score increased from 34.6% to 43.4%, indicating better factuality
- Method achieved 77.6% accuracy in rejecting non-factual hallucinations on human-annotated data
- Improved abstractiveness (more novel n-grams) while maintaining or improving factuality metrics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Rejection learning allows the model to identify and reject tokens associated with noisy or hallucinated information during training.
- **Mechanism:** By introducing a rejection class, the model learns to assign high probability to rejecting unsupported tokens rather than forcing them to match noisy reference summaries. This is based on the observation that noisy samples have high training loss.
- **Core assumption:** Noisy samples in the training data have abnormally high loss compared to clean samples.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "we propose a training objective for abstractive summarization based on rejection learning, in which the model learns whether or not to reject potentially noisy tokens"
  - [section] "Inspired by rejection learning for image classification (Grandvalet et al., 2008; Cortes et al., 2016; Thulasidasan et al., 2019), we introduce a new rejection class at training time that offers the model an option to reject confusing tokens rather than assigning high probability to all reference tokens"
- **Break condition:** If noisy samples don't consistently have higher loss than clean samples, the rejection learning would fail to distinguish between them.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Regularized decoding uses the rejection probability learned during training to penalize non-factual candidate summaries during inference.
- **Mechanism:** The decoding objective is modified to include a regularization term based on the rejection probability, making summaries with high rejection probability less likely to be generated.
- **Core assumption:** Rejection probability learned during training correlates with the likelihood of hallucination in generated text.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We further propose a regularized decoding objective that penalizes non-factual candidate summaries during inference by using the rejection probability learned during training"
  - [section] "When the rejection class is assigned a high probability at time step t, the generated content is likely to be hallucinated and non-factual at this step"
- **Break condition:** If the rejection probability doesn't correlate with actual hallucination during inference, the regularization would be ineffective.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The combination of rejection learning and regularized decoding improves factuality while simultaneously increasing abstractiveness of generated summaries.
- **Mechanism:** By rejecting noisy information and penalizing non-factual candidates, the model produces more factual summaries that are less extractive (more abstract).
- **Core assumption:** Factual summaries can be more abstract than extractive summaries, and this method achieves both goals simultaneously.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "we show that our method considerably improves the factuality of generated summaries in automatic and human evaluations when compared to five baseline models, and that it does so while increasing the abstractiveness of the generated summaries"
  - [section] "Our method improves the model's factuality while simultaneously increasing the abstractiveness of the generated summaries"
- **Break condition:** If improving factuality necessarily requires being more extractive (as some prior work suggests), this combined benefit would not be achievable.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  - Backbone: Pre-trained BART-LARGE model
  - Training component: Rejection loss objective with adjustable regularization weight α
  - Inference component: Regularized decoding with rejection probability penalty
  - Evaluation component: Multiple factuality metrics (FACTCC, DAE, ENTFA) and abstractiveness metrics (novel n-grams, coverage)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Train model with rejection loss objective
  2. Use trained model for inference with regularized decoding
  3. Evaluate generated summaries using factuality and abstractiveness metrics

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Higher α values in rejection loss lead to better factuality but may over-reject factual information
  - Larger λ values in regularized decoding improve factuality but may reduce ROUGE scores due to increased abstractiveness
  - Beam size affects factuality - our method benefits more from larger beam sizes compared to baseline models

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Factuality doesn't improve despite training with rejection loss (check if noisy samples have high loss)
  - ROUGE scores drop significantly without corresponding factuality improvement (check if rejection is too aggressive)
  - Rejection rate is too high for factual entities (adjust α parameter)

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Train baseline BART model and evaluate factuality scores to establish baseline
  2. Train model with rejection loss (α=1.0) without regularized decoding to isolate effect of rejection learning
  3. Train model with both rejection loss and regularized decoding (λ=2.0) to evaluate full method effectiveness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How well would the rejection learning approach generalize to other text generation tasks like machine translation or knowledge-grounded dialogue generation where training datasets also contain noise?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note in the Limitations section that "it remains to be seen whether a similar advantage in factuality can be obtained" when applying their method to other NLG tasks such as machine translation and knowledge-grounded dialogue generation.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests the rejection learning approach on abstractive summarization, so there is no empirical evidence of its effectiveness on other generation tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing the rejection learning approach improves factuality in machine translation or dialogue generation tasks would demonstrate its generalizability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the rejection learning approach ever incorrectly reject clean but difficult-to-learn samples that have high loss because they are simply challenging for the model?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention in the Limitations section that "it is not clear whether there also exist 'difficult' but clean samples that also have high loss because they are simply difficult for the model to learn."
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate whether the rejection learning approach mistakenly rejects clean samples that are just hard to learn.
- What evidence would resolve it: An analysis showing that the rejection learning approach does or does not incorrectly reject clean but difficult samples would clarify this limitation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal regularization weight α in Equation 3 that balances rejecting non-factual hallucinations and avoiding rejecting factual entities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that "the rejection rate for factual entities drops from 15% to 6.8%" when increasing α from 1.0 to 2.0, but also note that "the rejection rate for non-factual entities also drops to 68.7%."
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests α values of 1.0 and 2.0, so the optimal value is not determined.
- What evidence would resolve it: An ablation study testing different α values and measuring their impact on rejecting non-factual hallucinations versus factual entities would identify the optimal regularization weight.

## Limitations

- The method assumes noisy samples consistently exhibit higher loss than clean samples, but this assumption isn't thoroughly validated across different noise types
- Experimental evaluation is limited to XSUM dataset, raising questions about generalizability to other summarization datasets
- Human evaluation only assessed rejection accuracy on 100 samples, which may not be representative of the full distribution

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims:**
- The rejection learning training objective can be implemented and trained on XSUM data
- The method shows measurable improvement in factuality metrics (FACTCC, DAE) compared to baseline models
- The rejection mechanism can identify and reject hallucinated tokens with reasonable accuracy (~77.6%)

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- The combination of rejection learning and regularized decoding provides synergistic benefits
- The improvement in factuality comes without significant degradation in ROUGE scores
- The method increases abstractiveness while improving factuality

**Low Confidence Claims:**
- The approach generalizes to other summarization datasets beyond XSUM
- The rejection probability learned during training consistently correlates with hallucination risk across different domains
- The mechanism for identifying noisy samples through high loss is robust to different noise patterns

## Next Checks

1. **Validate the high-loss assumption**: Systematically analyze the loss distribution of clean vs. noisy samples during training to empirically confirm that noisy samples consistently exhibit higher loss. This should include different types of noise (entity errors, semantic inconsistencies, unsupported claims) to ensure the assumption holds broadly.

2. **Cross-dataset generalization study**: Evaluate the method on at least two additional summarization datasets (e.g., CNN/DailyMail, Newsroom) to assess whether the factuality improvements and abstractiveness gains transfer beyond XSUM. This would help determine if the approach is dataset-specific or more generally applicable.

3. **Correlation analysis between rejection probability and hallucination**: Conduct a detailed study measuring the correlation between rejection probability and actual hallucination across different entity types, summary lengths, and beam sizes. This should include both automatic metrics and human evaluation to establish whether the learned rejection probability is a reliable indicator of hallucination risk.
