# TOME: A Two-stage Approach for Model-based Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11161
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 14
- Key outcome: TOME improves retrieval performance on MS MARCO and Natural Questions by using tokenized URLs as identifiers and a two-stage generation architecture, achieving Hits@1 of 77.5% on MS MARCO dev set

## Executive Summary
TOME addresses key challenges in model-based retrieval by proposing a two-stage generation approach that uses tokenized URLs as identifiers. The method decomposes the prediction task into passage generation and URL generation stages, reducing the discrepancy between training and inference. By leveraging natural language components in URLs that align with PLM pre-training vocabulary, TOME achieves improved retrieval performance compared to existing model-based methods while maintaining better training efficiency through model scaling strategies.

## Method Summary
TOME implements a two-stage generation architecture using T5-based models for both passage generation and URL generation. The approach tokenizes URLs into natural language components that align with PLM pre-training vocabulary, then trains two separate models: first to generate relevant passages from queries, and second to predict the corresponding URL from generated passages. Training strategies include query augmentation, passage length reduction, and model scaling to handle larger corpora efficiently. The method is evaluated on MS MARCO and Natural Questions datasets using the Hits@1 metric.

## Key Results
- Achieves Hits@1 of 77.5% on MS MARCO dev set, outperforming existing model-based retrieval methods
- Demonstrates improved training efficiency through model scaling, with T5-3B converging faster than smaller variants
- Shows effectiveness of tokenized URLs as identifiers, reducing pre-training to fine-tuning discrepancy
- Validates two-stage architecture reduces training-inference discrepancy while maintaining retrieval accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Using tokenized URLs as identifiers aligns better with the vocabulary learned during PLM pre-training than randomly generated or manually constructed identifiers.
- Mechanism: Tokenized URLs contain natural language components (domains, paths, words) that overlap with the vocabulary space of the PLM, reducing the pre-training to fine-tuning discrepancy.
- Core assumption: URLs contain meaningful text tokens that are naturally present in web/text corpora used for pre-training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we suggest using tokenized URLs (or URIs) as text identifiers, which are widely available for web pages or Wikipedia pages... URLs typically contain more meaningful tokens in the form normal text and widely exist in real-world text corpora"
  - [section 3.2.1] "URLs typically contain more meaningful tokens in the form normal text and widely exist in real-world text corpora, making them more suitable to modeling and prediction using generative PLMs"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citation of pre-training corpus composition studies, but URLs are known to be common in web data.
- Break condition: If the URL structure contains many non-text tokens (e.g., query parameters, encoded characters) that are not well represented in pre-training.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The two-stage generation architecture reduces the training-inference discrepancy by separating the query-to-passage and passage-to-URL generation tasks.
- Mechanism: During training, the first stage learns to generate relevant passages from queries, while the second stage learns to map passages to URLs. During inference, only queries are needed as input, with the first stage generating passages that guide the second stage.
- Core assumption: The second stage can tolerate errors from the first stage due to the longer context and stronger memorization capability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "our approach decomposes the prediction task into two consecutive stages, namely passage generation and URL generation... This two-stage architecture can reduce the discrepancy between training and inference"
  - [section 3.2.2] "Such a two-stage generation approach can more effectively capture the semantic relatedness between queries and identifiers by both reducing the training-inference discrepancy and enriching the generation context"
  - [corpus] Case study evidence shows URL generation model can handle imperfect passage outputs (Table 4).
- Break condition: If the first stage generates completely irrelevant passages that provide no useful context for URL prediction.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Scaling model size improves training efficiency and convergence for model-based retrieval with longer generation targets.
- Mechanism: Larger PLMs have better memorization capacity and can handle the complexity of generating longer passages, leading to faster convergence.
- Core assumption: The training difficulty increases with corpus size and passage length, requiring larger models to maintain performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "we scale the model size accordingly and employ a larger PLM when necessary... Besides the improved capacity, we find that using a larger model size is also useful in improving the convergence rate"
  - [section 5.4] Figure 2(b) shows T5-3B converges faster than T5-large and T5-base
  - [corpus] No direct citation of model scaling studies for generative retrieval, but consistent with general PLM scaling literature.
- Break condition: If model scaling leads to overfitting on smaller corpora or if computational constraints prevent using larger models.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Pre-training vs. Fine-tuning Discrepancy
  - Why needed here: Understanding why randomly generated docids fail while tokenized URLs succeed requires knowing how PLMs learn vocabulary during pre-training versus what they encounter during fine-tuning.
  - Quick check question: What percentage of tokens in a typical URL are likely to have been seen during standard web corpus pre-training?

- Concept: Autoregressive Generation in Sequence-to-Sequence Models
  - Why needed here: The entire approach relies on generating identifiers token-by-token, requiring understanding of how probability distributions are computed at each step.
  - Quick check question: In a sequence-to-sequence model, how is the probability of generating the next token conditioned on previous tokens and the input?

- Concept: Memorization vs. Generalization in Large Language Models
  - Why needed here: Model-based retrieval fundamentally requires memorizing the entire corpus, unlike traditional retrieval which relies on generalization.
  - Quick check question: What architectural features of PLMs make them particularly good at memorization tasks compared to smaller models?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Query Encoder -> Passage Generation Model -> Generated Passage -> URL Generation Model -> Tokenized URL -> Tokenizer -> Final URL string

- Critical path:
  1. Query → Passage Generation Model → Generated Passage
  2. Generated Passage → URL Generation Model → Tokenized URL
  3. Tokenized URL → Tokenizer → Final URL string

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single-stage vs. two-stage: Simpler architecture vs. better training-inference alignment
  - URL vs. random docids: Better vocabulary alignment vs. potentially longer generation sequences
  - Model scale: Better performance vs. higher computational cost

- Failure signatures:
  - Training divergence in passage generation stage indicates corpus memorization issues
  - Low Hits@1 despite good passage generation suggests URL generation stage problems
  - Slow convergence across both stages may indicate model scale inadequacy

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation: Replace tokenized URLs with random docids while keeping two-stage architecture to isolate identifier design impact
  2. Scaling test: Train single-stage model with tokenized URLs on increasing corpus sizes to measure memorization limits
  3. Context sensitivity: Generate passages of varying lengths (maxlen=32 vs 128) to test optimal context size for URL generation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How does the performance of TOME scale when using even larger corpora beyond the MS MARCO Full dataset?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the limitations of model-based retrieval when scaling up to larger corpora and mentions a performance gap compared to dense retrievers.
- Why unresolved: The experiments conducted in the paper do not explore corpus sizes larger than the MS MARCO Full dataset.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with TOME on even larger corpora and comparing its performance to dense retrievers would provide insights into its scalability limitations.

- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that tokenized URLs are used as identifiers and suggests that other types of documents can use tokenized URIs as identifiers.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the use of different types of document identifiers beyond URLs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with TOME using different types of document identifiers (e.g., URIs for different document types) and comparing the performance would provide insights into the impact of identifier choice.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of model size (e.g., T5-large vs. T5-3B) affect the training efficiency and convergence of TOME on different corpus scales?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper investigates the scaling laws of TOME by examining different model sizes and mentions that larger models converge faster but may have lower learning efficiency on larger corpora.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the impact of model size on training efficiency and convergence across different corpus scales.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with TOME using different model sizes (e.g., T5-large, T5-3B) on various corpus scales and analyzing the training efficiency and convergence would provide insights into the optimal model size for different scenarios.

## Limitations

- Training efficiency concerns limit scalability to web-scale retrieval, with performance degrading significantly beyond 5M passages
- Evaluation focuses primarily on Hits@1 metric, lacking comprehensive analysis of ranking quality, diversity, and multi-metric performance
- Computational resource requirements for large PLMs (T5-3B) raise deployment feasibility concerns in resource-constrained environments

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims**:
- The two-stage generation architecture provides training-inference alignment benefits. Well-supported by experimental evidence and theoretical justification.
- Tokenized URLs as identifiers perform better than random docids. Clear evidence of superior performance and vocabulary alignment.

**Medium Confidence Claims**:
- Model scaling improves convergence and performance. Experiments show faster convergence, but computational tradeoffs not fully explored.
- The proposed training strategies contribute meaningfully to performance. Mentioned but lack comprehensive ablation studies.

**Low Confidence Claims**:
- TOME's general applicability to all retrieval scenarios. Limited evaluation to specific datasets doesn't test diverse query types or domains.
- The approach's scalability to web-scale retrieval. Testing up to 5M passages is far smaller than real-world scenarios.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation Study on Training Strategies**: Conduct comprehensive ablation experiments to isolate the impact of each proposed training strategy (query augmentation, passage length reduction, model scaling) on final performance by training models with different combinations of strategies.

2. **Scalability Testing Beyond 5M Passages**: Design experiments that systematically test TOME's performance as corpus size increases beyond the current 5M passage limit, measuring training time, convergence behavior, and performance degradation patterns.

3. **Multi-metric Evaluation on Diverse Query Sets**: Expand evaluation beyond Hits@1 to include comprehensive ranking metrics (MRR, NDCG, recall@k) and test on diverse query types including ambiguous queries, long-tail queries, and queries requiring temporal or geographical reasoning.
