# Mediated Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08419
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08419
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Constrained mediators induce cooperation in MARL by respecting agent boundaries and ensuring commitment is an equilibrium.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes mediators as a mechanism design solution for achieving cooperation in mixed multi-agent reinforcement learning environments. Unlike traditional approaches that force agents to cooperate, mediators act as benevolent intermediaries that facilitate cooperation only when it is in agents' best interests. The framework ensures that emergent behaviors are equilibria by enforcing incentive compatibility (IC) and encouragement (E) constraints through constrained policy optimization. Experiments demonstrate that constrained mediators successfully induce socially beneficial equilibria in prisoner's dilemma, public good games, and their sequential variants, while naive mediators fail due to incentive misalignment.

## Method Summary
The method involves joint training of self-interested agents and a mediator using actor-critic frameworks. Agents can choose to commit to the mediator for k time steps, after which the mediator acts on their behalf. The mediator's policy is trained to maximize social welfare subject to IC and E constraints enforced via Lagrange multipliers and dual gradient descent. A centralized critic estimates values for all agents (both in and out of the coalition) to enable counterfactual reasoning. The approach is tested in matrix games (Prisoner's Dilemma, Public Good Game) and their iterative versions, comparing naive versus constrained mediators and varying commitment window sizes.

## Key Results
- Constrained mediators successfully induce cooperation in PD and PGG, while naive mediators fail due to incentive misalignment
- Commitment window size k affects cooperation strength, with larger k allowing more social welfare gains
- Mediators effectively deter free-riding in PGG by enforcing the E constraint through counterfactual value estimation
- The framework respects agent boundaries by ensuring commitment is voluntary and self-interested

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Mediators can transform non-equilibrium games into equilibria by acting as trusted intermediaries that enforce incentive-compatible policies.
- **Mechanism:** The mediator observes the current coalition and state, then selects joint actions for committed agents. By incorporating Lagrange multipliers that penalize deviation from IC and E constraints, the mediator ensures that agents' payoffs from committing are at least as good as acting independently. This transforms the strategic landscape so that committing becomes a dominant strategy.
- **Core assumption:** Agents are myopically rational and update their policies to increase the probability of actions that yield higher expected returns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "cooperation also requires agents' identities and boundaries to be respected by making sure that the emergent behaviour is an equilibrium"
  - [section 3.2] "The mediator’s policy should satisfy certain constraints. In- tuitively, self-interested agents only commit if committing serves their best selfish interests."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus contains related works on social welfare but lacks direct discussion of mediator-based equilibrium enforcement.
- **Break condition:** If the mediator's policy fails to satisfy the IC or E constraints (e.g., due to poor credit assignment or insufficient exploration), agents will stop committing and revert to defection.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Commitment window size (k) allows mediators to trade off between immediate social welfare gains and long-term stability.
- **Mechanism:** When k > 1, agents commit for multiple time steps. This reduces the frequency at which constraints must be satisfied, giving the mediator more flexibility to pursue socially beneficial policies. The mediator can therefore achieve higher social welfare when k is larger, at the cost of reduced adaptability to changing conditions.
- **Core assumption:** The environment is stationary enough that a policy committed for k steps remains beneficial throughout that period.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Commitment window size (k) also affects cooperation strength, with larger k allowing more social welfare gains."
  - [section 3.2] "When 𝑘 > 1, the constraints only need to be satisfied on average over the periods of 𝑘 time-steps, since agents commit for these periods."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence - corpus papers focus on communication and reward shaping, not commitment windows.
- **Break condition:** If the environment changes rapidly, a large k may lock agents into suboptimal policies for too long, reducing overall performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The mediator's value function estimates for all agents (both in and out of the coalition) enable counterfactual reasoning that enforces the E constraint.
- **Mechanism:** By training a centralized critic that outputs values for all agents, the mediator can estimate what an agent's value would be if it were in or out of the coalition. This allows the mediator to compute the E constraint (preventing free-riding) and adjust its policy accordingly via dual gradient descent on the Lagrange multiplier.
- **Core assumption:** The centralized critic can accurately estimate counterfactual values for agents not in the current coalition.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "To find 𝜆𝑖𝑐 𝑖 (𝑜𝑖,𝑡 ), we employ dual gradient descent... This constraint implies that the same 𝜆𝑖𝑐 𝑖 (𝑜𝑖,𝑡 ) is used in the dual objective."
  - [section 3.1] "The mediator's critic receives coalition 𝐶 and observations of all agents... This formulation requires centralized access to all observations and rewards."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus discusses communication and reward sharing but not centralized value estimation for counterfactual reasoning.
- **Break condition:** If the critic's value estimates are inaccurate (e.g., due to function approximation error), the E constraint may be violated, leading to persistent free-riding.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Agents -> Mediator -> Environment -> Agents
- **Critical path:**
  1. Agents select actions (including commit if allowed).
  2. If agents commit, mediator acts for them for k steps.
  3. Environment returns next state and rewards.
  4. Collect transition (state, action, reward, next state, coalition info).
  5. Update agent and mediator policies via policy gradient.
  6. Update Lagrange multipliers via dual gradient descent.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Centralized critic for mediator enables counterfactual reasoning but requires access to all agents' observations and rewards during training.
  - Factorized mediator policy scales to larger coalitions but may limit expressiveness compared to a fully joint policy.
  - Commitment window k trades off between immediate social welfare gains (larger k) and adaptability to changing conditions (smaller k).

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Agents never commit: IC or E constraints not satisfied, mediator policy needs adjustment.
  - Mediator policy oscillates: Lagrange multipliers not converging, learning rate may be too high.
  - Social welfare plateaus: Mediator policy stuck in local optimum, exploration or credit assignment may be insufficient.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Implement and train agents and mediator on Prisoner's Dilemma. Verify that naive mediator fails to induce cooperation, while constrained mediator succeeds.
  2. Implement Two-step PD with k=1 and k=2. Verify that k=2 allows higher social welfare due to reduced constraint satisfaction frequency.
  3. Implement PGG with N=3,5,10 agents. Verify that constrained mediator deters free-riding and achieves higher social welfare than naive mediator.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1:** How can cryptographic technologies be leveraged to allow mediators to function without centralized execution while still preventing agents from misreporting their commitment status?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper notes that mediators are currently implemented with centralized execution to prevent misreporting, but mentions this as an area for future research.
  - **Why unresolved:** Current implementations require centralized control to verify commitment status, but this creates a single point of failure and limits scalability.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Demonstration of a cryptographic protocol that allows decentralized verification of commitment status while maintaining the incentive compatibility properties of mediators.

- **Open Question 2:** What is the optimal commitment window size (k) for different types of games, and how does it affect the trade-off between social welfare maximization and constraint satisfaction?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper introduces k as a hyperparameter affecting mediator power, with larger k allowing more social welfare gains, but doesn't systematically explore optimal k values.
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper only briefly mentions k=1, k>1, and k=inf cases without determining optimal values or understanding the underlying dynamics.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies across diverse game types showing how k affects convergence speed, final social welfare, and constraint satisfaction, potentially leading to guidelines for choosing k.

- **Open Question 3:** How do mediators perform in large-scale, high-dimensional environments compared to traditional MARL approaches for unconditional cooperation?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper notes this as future work, having only tested mediators in relatively simple matrix and iterative games.
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper's experimental focus on simple games means we don't know if mediators scale effectively or face new challenges in complex environments.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct comparison of mediator performance versus state-of-the-art unconditional cooperation methods in benchmark MARL environments like Starcraft II or Mujoco multi-agent tasks.

## Limitations

- The framework requires centralized access to all agents' observations and rewards during training, which may not be feasible in many real-world applications.
- The approach assumes myopic rationality, which may not accurately capture more complex agent behaviors with longer planning horizons.
- The paper does not address potential issues with credit assignment in the mediator's policy, which could lead to suboptimal or unstable learning.

## Confidence

**High confidence:** The mechanism by which mediators enforce incentive-compatible policies and the role of commitment window size (k) in trading off between immediate social welfare gains and long-term stability. The experimental results in the Prisoner's Dilemma and Public Good Game provide strong evidence for these claims.

**Medium confidence:** The mediator's ability to estimate counterfactual values for agents not in the current coalition and use this information to enforce the E constraint. While the paper provides a clear mechanism for this, the accuracy of the centralized critic's value estimates is not thoroughly evaluated.

**Low confidence:** The generalizability of the results to more complex, real-world environments with larger state and action spaces, or to settings where the assumptions of centralized training and myopic rationality do not hold. The paper focuses on relatively simple matrix games and does not explore the scalability or robustness of the approach.

## Next Checks

1. **Credit Assignment Analysis:** Investigate the impact of credit assignment errors in the mediator's policy on the satisfaction of IC and E constraints. This could involve adding noise to the rewards or observations during training and measuring the resulting changes in commitment rates and social welfare.

2. **Robustness to Non-Myopic Agents:** Extend the experiments to include agents with longer planning horizons or more complex decision-making processes (e.g., using Monte Carlo Tree Search or planning-based policies). Measure the mediator's ability to enforce IC and E constraints in these settings.

3. **Scalability Evaluation:** Implement the mediator framework in a more complex environment with larger state and action spaces (e.g., a multi-agent grid world or a cooperative navigation task). Evaluate the scalability of the approach in terms of sample efficiency, training time, and the ability to find high-performing policies.
