# Adversarial AI in Insurance: Pervasiveness and Resilience

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.07520
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07520
- Reference count: 6
- Key outcome: Examines Adversarial AI attacks in insurance where modified input data can deceive AI systems to produce false outputs, particularly creating zero-shot or few-shot classification events.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the vulnerability of AI systems in the insurance sector to adversarial attacks, where carefully crafted input modifications can cause misclassification and produce outputs in previously unseen categories. The authors analyze typical insurance AI applications including product design, risk assessment, underwriting, and claims processing, demonstrating how these systems can be compromised through image, audio, and text data attacks. They show that adversarial attacks often create zero-shot or few-shot classification events by introducing categories the model has not been trained on. The paper proposes prevention methods including Romera-Paredes and Torr's linear model approach for zero-label treatment, data augmentation with CNN as in Hu et al., and multi-source transfer learning networks. The authors emphasize that proper validation procedures and multiple testing are crucial when implementing AI systems in insurance, given the pervasiveness of adversarial AI risks.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes multiple defense approaches against Adversarial AI attacks in insurance AI applications. The methods include implementing Romera-Paredes and Torr's linear model approach for handling zero-label cases through attribute-class relationship modeling, applying Hu et al.'s data augmentation algorithm with CNN for few-shot fault diagnosis using resampling techniques, and developing Yang et al.'s multi-source transfer learning network to handle rare events by transferring knowledge from multiple datasets through a single diagnostic module. The approach focuses on building resilience against attacks that create zero-shot or few-shot classification events by introducing previously unseen categories.

## Key Results
- Adversarial attacks on insurance AI systems can create zero-shot or few-shot classification events by introducing previously unseen categories
- Attacked outputs can appear statistically better than non-attacked ones, deceiving both the system and human evaluators
- Zero-label prevention methods like Romera-Paredes and Torr's linear model can mitigate adversarial attacks by modeling unseen categories
- Proper validation procedures and multiple testing are crucial when implementing AI systems in insurance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adversarial attacks can create zero-shot or few-shot classification events by introducing previously unseen categories
- Mechanism: The attack modifies input data in a way that produces outputs fitting into categories the model has not been trained on, bypassing normal classification boundaries
- Core assumption: The AI system relies on labeled training data and does not have mechanisms to handle unseen categories robustly
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "They show that adversarial attacks often create zero-shot or few-shot classification events by introducing previously unseen categories"; [section] "The first thing that comes to mind is to include the class name in the list of classes, even though there are no training samples for this class..."
- Break condition: If the model incorporates robust zero-shot or few-shot learning techniques during training, the attack loses effectiveness

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adversarial perturbations can make attacked outputs appear statistically better than non-attacked ones, deceiving both the system and human evaluators
- Mechanism: The attack is designed to maximize misclassification probability, which can paradoxically increase confidence metrics for the false output
- Core assumption: Confidence scores correlate with accuracy, and attackers can exploit this by optimizing for high confidence in misclassification
- Evidence anchors: [section] "The astonishing fact is that the level of confidence in the erroneous, final classification is much greater than the one of the original image (99.3% instead of 57.7%)"; [section] "The appalling feature of such an attack is that the attacked and non-attacked images are indistinguishable by a human eye, and also the statistical properties of the AI algorithm, once attacked, may be better than before the attack..."
- Break condition: If validation procedures include blind testing without confidence score reliance, the deception is exposed

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Zero-label prevention methods can mitigate adversarial attacks by explicitly modeling unseen categories
- Mechanism: By incorporating attribute-class relationships in training and inference, the model can generalize to unseen categories without requiring labeled examples
- Core assumption: The relationship between attributes and classes can be learned independently of specific training samples for each class
- Evidence anchors: [section] "A very simple approach has been suggested by Romera-Paredes and Torr (2015)... Though so simple, the approach is shown by the Authors to outperform the previously-suggested approaches by 17%"; [section] "The first thing that comes to mind is to include the class name in the list of classes, even though there are no training samples for this class..."
- Break condition: If the attribute-class relationships are too complex or non-linear for the linear model to capture, the method fails

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal method for validating and verifying AI systems that incorporate zero-shot learning components to prevent adversarial attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper emphasizes that adversarial attacks often create zero-shot classification events and that zero-label countermeasures are crucial for prevention, yet the validation of such systems remains an open challenge
- Why unresolved: Zero-shot learning systems face unique validation challenges because they must handle categories never seen during training, making traditional validation metrics insufficient
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison studies of different validation methodologies (cross-validation, domain adaptation, simulated data) specifically applied to zero-shot learning systems under adversarial conditions, showing which approaches most effectively detect and prevent attacks

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can insurance companies effectively implement multi-source transfer learning networks (MSTLN) to detect and prevent adversarial attacks across diverse insurance applications?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes MSTLN as a sophisticated approach for industrial applications, but does not explore its practical implementation or effectiveness in insurance contexts
- Why unresolved: While MSTLN shows promise for knowledge transfer from multiple datasets, its specific application to insurance domains and its ability to detect adversarial attacks in real-world insurance scenarios remains untested
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation studies demonstrating MSTLN's performance in detecting adversarial attacks across multiple insurance applications (health, property, auto) with varying levels of labeled data availability

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the most effective cooperative strategies between insurance companies and other stakeholders (like auto manufacturers) to build resilience against adversarial AI attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that resilience to attacks on self-driving cars benefits both producers and insurance companies, suggesting potential for cooperation, but does not detail specific strategies
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the need for cooperation but does not provide concrete frameworks or evidence of successful collaborative approaches to prevent adversarial AI attacks
- What evidence would resolve it: Case studies or pilot programs demonstrating successful cooperation frameworks between insurance companies and other stakeholders, showing measurable improvements in adversarial attack prevention and resilience
