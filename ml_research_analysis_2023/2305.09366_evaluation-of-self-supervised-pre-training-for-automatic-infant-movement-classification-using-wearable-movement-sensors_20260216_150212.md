# Evaluation of self-supervised pre-training for automatic infant movement classification using wearable movement sensors

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09366
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09366
- Reference count: 26
- Primary result: Self-supervised pre-training with data2vec improves infant movement classification accuracy by up to 25 percentage points on limited labeled data.

## Executive Summary
This study demonstrates that self-supervised learning (SSL) can substantially improve automatic classification of infant movements using wearable sensors. By pre-training a transformer model on unlabeled MAIJU data with the data2vec algorithm, classification accuracy improved by up to 25 percentage points compared to non-pretrained models, especially when training data was limited. Further gains were achieved by context-selective quality screening of pre-training data to exclude periods of minimal movement or sensor dropout, improving UAF1 scores from 80.5% to 82.1%. These results show that SSL with appropriate data filtering is effective for enhancing out-of-hospital infant motor assessment.

## Method Summary
The method involves pre-training a transformer model using the data2vec self-supervised learning algorithm on large unlabeled MAIJU sensor datasets, followed by fine-tuning on smaller labeled datasets. Raw accelerometer and gyroscope data are preprocessed with interpolation, bias removal, and median filtering, then windowed into 2.3-second frames. A CNN feature encoder produces 160-dimensional latent representations that feed into a 12-layer transformer encoder. The data2vec algorithm uses a student-teacher architecture with masking and exponential moving average updates. Fine-tuning occurs in two stages: first adding ELU layers with weighted categorical cross-entropy loss, then full network fine-tuning with data augmentation.

## Key Results
- Pre-trained transformer models achieved 25 percentage point improvement in UAF1 score compared to non-pretrained models on 5% labeled data
- Context-selective quality screening improved UAF1 from 80.5% to 82.1% by filtering out low-movement periods and sensor dropouts
- Data augmentation during pre-training helped performance only when combined with quality screening
- Pre-training benefits diminished as labeled training data increased from 5% to 100%

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Self-supervised pre-training with data2vec improves classification performance on limited labeled data.
- Mechanism: The data2vec model learns contextualized representations by predicting masked latent features in unlabeled data. When fine-tuned on small labeled datasets, these representations provide a strong prior, reducing the need for large amounts of labeled data.
- Core assumption: The unlabeled data distribution is sufficiently similar to the labeled data distribution so that learned representations are transferable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "pre-training the classifier with unlabeled data leads to a robust accuracy increase of subsequent classification models"
  - [section] "pre-training is crucial for models with a large number of trainable parameters, especially if the amount of labeled training data is small"
  - [corpus] Weak correlation; corpus contains related infant movement studies but not direct SSL mechanism evidence.
- Break condition: If the unlabeled data distribution diverges significantly from the labeled data (e.g., different infant ages, movement types), learned representations may not transfer effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Context-selective quality screening of pre-training data improves classification performance.
- Mechanism: Filtering out low-movement or sensor-dropout periods removes uninformative data, focusing the pre-training on segments that better represent meaningful infant movements. This results in richer, more relevant feature representations.
- Core assumption: Periods of minimal movement or sensor dropout are not representative of the motor performance evaluation task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "selecting context-relevant pre-training data leads to substantial further improvements in the classifier performance"
  - [section] "we seek an answer to whether classification model performance is improved by context-selective quality-screening of the data used for pre-training"
  - [corpus] Weak correlation; corpus does not provide direct evidence of quality screening impact.
- Break condition: If quality screening removes too much data, leaving insufficient volume for effective pre-training, or if it inadvertently excludes rare but important movement classes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Data augmentation during pre-training enhances model robustness and performance.
- Mechanism: Augmentation methods (sample dropout, sensor dropout, sensor rotation) introduce variability that helps the model generalize better to unseen data and prevents overfitting to specific sensor patterns.
- Core assumption: The augmented variations are realistic enough to represent possible variations in real-world sensor data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "data augmentation during pre-training helped in achieving a better performance level, but only when data screening was used"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; corpus neighbors do not discuss augmentation in this context.
- Break condition: If augmentation introduces unrealistic distortions, the model may learn to ignore important signal patterns or overfit to augmented noise.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Raw sensor data -> Preprocessing (interpolation, bias removal, smoothing, windowing) -> Feature encoder (CNN) -> 160-dimensional latent representations -> Transformer encoder blocks (12 layers) -> Contextualized embeddings -> Data2vec SSL training (student/teacher networks, masking, EMA updates) -> Fine-tuning stage 1: Add FC layers -> Categorical probabilities -> Fine-tuning stage 2: Full network fine-tuning with labeled data -> Evaluation: Confusion matrix, UAF1 score

- Critical path:
  1. Preprocess raw sensor data into 2.3-second frames
  2. Feed into CNN feature encoder to get 160-dim embeddings
  3. Pass embeddings through transformer for contextualized representations
  4. Apply data2vec SSL training with masking and EMA
  5. Fine-tune with labeled movement categories
  6. Evaluate classification accuracy

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model size vs. overfitting: Large transformer models can overfit small labeled datasets; pre-training mitigates this.
  - Data screening strictness vs. data volume: Stricter screening improves data quality but reduces available training data.
  - Augmentation strength vs. realism: Too much augmentation can distort signals; too little may not help generalization.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low variance in learned embeddings -> Possible representation collapse, often due to too much flat data or aggressive augmentation.
  - Poor performance on fine-tuning -> Insufficient or irrelevant pre-training data, or mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning distributions.
  - Overfitting on small labeled datasets -> Need for stronger regularization or more pre-training data.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a baseline transformer without pre-training on 5% labeled data; observe high overfitting and low UAF1.
  2. Pre-train transformer with data2vec on full unlabeled data (no screening); fine-tune on 5% labeled data; compare UAF1 improvement.
  3. Pre-train transformer with data2vec on quality-screened unlabeled data; fine-tune on 5% labeled data; measure impact of screening on performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of pre-trained Transformer models compare to other neural network architectures (e.g., WaveNet) when the amount of labeled training data is very small?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares pre-trained Transformer models to a WaveNet baseline, showing that WaveNet performs better when labeled data is limited, but the Transformer outperforms it with sufficient pre-training and data screening.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a direct comparison of Transformer models to other architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) under extremely limited labeled data conditions, nor does it explore how different architectures respond to varying amounts of pre-training data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing multiple neural network architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs, Transformers) trained with varying amounts of labeled data and different SSL pre-training strategies.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between data screening strictness and pre-training dataset size for maximizing model performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that quality + playtime screening yields the best results, but also notes that this drastically reduces the pre-training dataset size from 1333 hours to 387 hours. It's unclear if further screening would improve or harm performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the screening thresholds (e.g., percentage of dropped sensors allowed, playtime requirements) and measuring the trade-off between dataset size and model performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different SSL pre-training algorithms (e.g., contrastive learning, masked prediction) compare for infant movement classification?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses data2vec (masked prediction) for SSL pre-training but notes that other methods like wav2vec 2.0 and contrastive predictive coding could be used.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests one SSL algorithm (data2vec), leaving open the question of whether other SSL approaches might yield better performance for this specific task.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison of multiple SSL algorithms (e.g., data2vec, wav2vec 2.0, contrastive learning, bootstrapping) using the same dataset and model architecture.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the inclusion of contextual information (e.g., caregiver handling, sleep periods) affect the model's ability to classify infant movements?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that long-term recordings include periods of minimal movement or sensor dropout, and that playtime screening improves performance, suggesting contextual information is important.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore how explicitly incorporating contextual information (e.g., caregiver presence, sleep states) into the model might improve classification accuracy or whether excluding such information could lead to overfitting to specific contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments that either explicitly encode contextual information into the model or systematically exclude periods with known contextual factors (e.g., caregiver handling) to measure their impact on classification performance.

## Limitations
- Benefits primarily demonstrated on small labeled datasets (5-20% splits), unclear if advantages persist with larger labeled data
- Data2vec algorithm implementation details not fully specified, including masking probabilities and EMA parameters
- Only one SSL method (data2vec) and one model architecture (transformer) evaluated, limiting generalizability

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Classification accuracy improvements (UAF1 score increases of 25 percentage points) when using pre-trained models on limited labeled data.
- **Medium Confidence**: The specific mechanism by which data2vec SSL learns transferable representations.
- **Low Confidence**: The universal applicability of context-selective quality screening.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-validation with different SSL methods: Replicate the study using alternative SSL approaches (e.g., SimCLR, Masked Autoencoders) to determine if observed improvements are specific to data2vec or generalizable across SSL methods.
2. Larger labeled dataset evaluation: Test the pre-training approach on larger labeled datasets (50-100% of available labeled data) to assess whether benefits persist or diminish as labeled data increases.
3. External dataset validation: Evaluate the pre-trained model on movement data from different infant populations, sensor configurations, or assessment protocols to test robustness and generalizability of learned representations.
