# How to Allocate your Label Budget? Choosing between Active Learning and Learning to Reject in Anomaly Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.02909
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02909
- Reference count: 3
- This paper proposes BALLAD, a dynamic label allocation strategy that outperforms single-strategy baselines by 20% in average cost per test example.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of optimally allocating a fixed label budget between Active Learning (AL) and Learning to Reject (LR) strategies in anomaly detection. The key insight is that AL requires biased labels for training while LR needs i.i.d. labels for evaluation, making direct comparison difficult. BALLAD introduces a multi-round dynamic allocation framework that uses reward functions to estimate the expected gain from labeling examples for either strategy. The framework demonstrates significant improvements over static allocation approaches across 18 benchmark datasets.

## Method Summary
BALLAD operates through an iterative process where at each round, it estimates the potential benefit of labeling examples for AL versus LR using two proposed reward metrics. The entropy-based reward measures uncertainty in model predictions, while the cosine similarity reward assesses the diversity of selected examples. Based on these rewards, BALLAD dynamically allocates the limited label budget between the two strategies. The framework alternates between phases of label acquisition and model updating, allowing it to adapt to changing conditions and distributions throughout the anomaly detection process.

## Key Results
- BALLAD achieves 20% lower average cost per test example compared to baselines allocating all labels to either AL or LR
- The entropy reward leads to more balanced allocation between AL and LR strategies
- Experiments conducted across 18 benchmark datasets demonstrate consistent performance improvements

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
BALLAD works by recognizing that the optimal allocation between AL and LR depends on the current state of the model and data distribution. The reward functions provide a quantitative basis for comparing the marginal benefit of additional labels in each strategy. By dynamically adjusting allocation based on these estimates, BALLAD can respond to changing conditions and avoid over-investing in either strategy when diminishing returns are observed.

## Foundational Learning
- Active Learning fundamentals: Understanding when and how AL improves model performance with limited labels is crucial for evaluating its marginal benefit.
- Learning to Reject basics: Knowing how LR operates and its requirements for i.i.d. labels helps explain why it needs different allocation patterns.
- Reward function design: The choice of reward metrics directly impacts allocation decisions and overall system performance.
- Multi-round allocation strategies: Understanding sequential decision-making helps explain how BALLAD adapts over time.
- Anomaly detection challenges: Recognizing the unique difficulties of anomaly detection contextualizes why label allocation is particularly important.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Data -> Feature Extraction -> Reward Calculation -> Allocation Decision -> Label Acquisition -> Model Update -> Performance Evaluation

**Critical Path**: The core loop consists of calculating rewards, making allocation decisions, acquiring labels, and updating models. This iterative process drives the system's ability to optimize budget usage.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The framework trades computational complexity for improved label efficiency. The multi-round approach requires more computation per iteration but can achieve better overall performance with limited labels.

**Failure Signatures**: If reward functions poorly estimate true benefits, the system may over-allocate to one strategy, leading to suboptimal performance. Computational bottlenecks in reward calculation can slow down the iterative process.

**First Experiments**: 1) Test single-round versus multi-round allocation on synthetic data. 2) Compare entropy versus cosine reward performance in isolation. 3) Evaluate sensitivity to initial budget allocation percentages.
