# Analysis and Comparison of Two-Level KFAC Methods for Training Deep Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.18083
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18083
- Reference count: 40
- Two-level KFAC methods incorporating layer interactions do not significantly improve optimization performance compared to standard KFAC

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether incorporating layer interactions through two-level KFAC methods can improve the optimization performance of Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature (KFAC) for training deep neural networks. KFAC approximates the Fisher Information Matrix as block-diagonal, ignoring interactions between layers. The authors propose several two-level methods inspired by domain decomposition techniques that add coarse correction terms to account for these interactions. Through experiments on deep auto-encoders, CNNs, and linear networks, they find that while two-level methods provide updates closer to exact natural gradient descent, this does not translate to better optimization performance. The results suggest that KFAC's block-diagonal approximation is sufficiently robust for practical optimization.

## Method Summary
The authors propose two-level KFAC methods that incorporate layer interactions by adding a coarse correction term to the standard KFAC update. Inspired by domain decomposition techniques, these methods use different coarse spaces to capture interactions between layers that are ignored in standard KFAC's block-diagonal approximation. The coarse correction is computed using a Galerkin approach, where the coarse space is spanned by the dominant eigenvectors of the layer-wise Fisher matrices. Several variants are explored, differing in how the coarse space is constructed and how the correction term is computed. The methods are evaluated on deep auto-encoders, convolutional neural networks, and linear networks to assess whether accounting for layer interactions improves optimization performance.

## Key Results
- Two-level KFAC methods provide updates closer to exact natural gradient descent but do not improve optimization performance
- Standard KFAC's block-diagonal approximation remains robust even when layer interactions are explicitly accounted for
- Experiments across deep auto-encoders, CNNs, and linear networks show consistent results
- The extra-diagonal blocks of the Fisher Information Matrix do not contribute to improved optimization in tested scenarios

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism behind KFAC's effectiveness despite ignoring layer interactions lies in its approximation of the Fisher Information Matrix as block-diagonal. This simplification captures the dominant curvature information within each layer while treating cross-layer interactions as negligible. The two-level methods attempt to restore these interactions through coarse correction terms, but the experiments reveal that these additional terms, while mathematically closer to the exact natural gradient, do not provide practical optimization benefits. This suggests that the primary optimization challenges in deep learning are captured by the block-diagonal structure, and the ignored interactions are either too small to matter or are implicitly handled through other mechanisms in the training process.

## Foundational Learning

**Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature (KFAC)**: A second-order optimization method that approximates the Fisher Information Matrix using Kronecker products for computational efficiency. Why needed: Enables scalable natural gradient descent for deep networks. Quick check: Verify that KFAC reduces memory complexity from O(n²) to O(n) where n is the number of parameters.

**Fisher Information Matrix (FIM)**: The expected outer product of gradients, capturing the curvature of the loss landscape. Why needed: Provides curvature information for natural gradient descent. Quick check: Confirm that FIM is positive semi-definite and captures parameter sensitivity.

**Domain Decomposition Methods**: Numerical techniques that solve large problems by breaking them into smaller subproblems and combining solutions. Why needed: Provides theoretical foundation for two-level correction approach. Quick check: Understand how coarse spaces capture global interactions between subdomains.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Input -> Forward Pass -> Gradient Computation -> KFAC/Fisher Approximation -> Parameter Update -> Output

**Critical Path**: The optimization loop follows: compute activations and gradients → approximate Fisher matrices → compute KFAC update → apply parameter update → repeat

**Design Tradeoffs**: KFAC trades off exact curvature information for computational efficiency through block-diagonal approximation. Two-level methods add computational overhead to capture interactions but this doesn't yield optimization benefits.

**Failure Signatures**: If two-level methods showed improved convergence but with higher variance, it would suggest layer interactions are important but difficult to capture. The actual result (no improvement) indicates interactions are genuinely unimportant.

**First Experiments**: 1) Compare training loss curves for standard KFAC vs two-level methods on a simple linear network. 2) Measure wall-clock time per iteration for each method. 3) Analyze the magnitude of coarse correction terms across different network depths.

## Limitations
- Experiments limited to auto-encoders, CNNs, and linear networks; may not generalize to Transformers or other architectures
- The conclusion that extra-diagonal blocks don't matter is based on specific tasks and may not hold for all problem domains
- Does not investigate edge cases where KFAC's approximation might break down
- Analysis doesn't explore why layer interactions don't improve optimization despite being closer to exact natural gradient

## Confidence
- Block-diagonal approximation in KFAC is sufficiently robust: High confidence
- Extra-diagonal blocks of FIM do not contribute to improved optimization: Medium confidence
- Two-level methods providing closer-to-exact updates doesn't translate to better optimization: Medium confidence
