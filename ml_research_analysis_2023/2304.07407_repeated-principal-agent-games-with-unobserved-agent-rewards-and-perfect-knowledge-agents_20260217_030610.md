# Repeated Principal-Agent Games with Unobserved Agent Rewards and Perfect-Knowledge Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.07407
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07407
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Achieves O(√T log T) regret bound in a principal-agent game with hidden agent rewards using a novel feasibility estimator and ϵ-greedy algorithm.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a framework for repeated principal-agent games in a multi-armed bandit setting where the principal cannot observe the agent's rewards. The key innovation is a novel estimator that infers the agent's reward vector from observed actions and incentives using a linear feasibility problem. A data-driven ϵ-greedy algorithm is designed that balances exploration and exploitation to achieve a regret bound of O(√T log T) relative to an oracle policy. The method is validated through numerical experiments on a transportation planning problem.

## Method Summary
The method constructs a policy that achieves low regret by first estimating the agent's expected rewards for each bandit arm through a linear feasibility problem. The principal uses this estimator to design adaptive incentives for the agent. The algorithm combines exploration (random incentive sampling) and exploitation (incentive optimization based on current estimates) phases. The estimation problem is formulated as finding a feasible solution to linear constraints derived from observed actions and incentives, while the exploitation phase uses concentration bounds to compute incentives that drive the agent toward actions maximizing the principal's net reward.

## Key Results
- The feasibility estimator consistently recovers the agent's reward vector using only observed actions and incentives
- The ϵ-greedy algorithm achieves O(√T log T) regret bound against an oracle policy
- Numerical experiments on transportation planning confirm theoretical results and show good performance as action space size increases
- The agent can extract finite information rent through strategic reward misrepresentation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The principal can consistently estimate the agent's reward vector using only the observed actions and incentives, even without direct reward observations.
- Mechanism: The estimator formulates the problem as a linear feasibility problem where constraints ensure that for each observed incentive-action pair, the chosen action maximizes the agent's total utility (agent reward + incentive). This forms a random polytope whose diameter bounds the estimation error.
- Core assumption: The agent is a perfect-knowledge utility maximizer with a fixed reward vector, and the incentive space is rich enough to distinguish between different reward vectors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We construct a policy that achieves a low regret... by first constructing an estimator for the agent's expected reward for each bandit arm."
  - [section 2.2]: Formal definition of the feasibility estimator that uses only incentive and action data.
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - neighboring papers also study inverse optimization in bandit settings but focus on different assumptions about agent knowledge.
- Break condition: If the agent is not a perfect-knowledge utility maximizer, or if the incentive space is too constrained to differentiate reward vectors, the estimator may not be identifiable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The ϵ-greedy algorithm achieves O(√T log T) regret by balancing exploration and exploitation based on estimation uncertainty.
- Mechanism: During exploration phases, the principal randomly samples incentives to reduce estimation uncertainty. During exploitation, the principal uses the current reward estimates plus a confidence bonus (βt) to compute incentives that drive the agent toward actions that maximize the principal's expected net reward.
- Core assumption: The principal's rewards follow sub-Gaussian distributions, and the estimator provides valid concentration bounds on the estimation error.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "A data-driven ϵ-greedy algorithm is then designed, combining exploration and exploitation phases."
  - [section 3.1]: Detailed pseudocode showing the exploration-exploitation trade-off and the use of βt as a confidence bonus.
  - [corpus]: Moderate evidence - standard bandit algorithms use similar exploration-exploitation trade-offs, but the specific application to hidden reward settings is novel.
- Break condition: If the concentration bounds on the estimator are violated (e.g., due to heavy-tailed rewards), the regret bound may not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The agent can extract a finite information rent from the principal by strategically misrepresenting their reward vector.
- Mechanism: The agent solves an optimization problem to find a "pretended" reward vector that maximizes their own utility while still making the principal believe they are maximizing the principal's net reward. This allows the agent to extract extra incentives beyond what they would receive if truthful.
- Core assumption: The agent has full knowledge of both their own rewards and the principal's rewards, and the principal cannot observe the agent's true rewards.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4]: Formalization of the agent's optimization problem and numerical examples showing how the agent can increase their information rent.
  - [abstract]: "There is no way for the principal to prohibit the agent from this misbehavior which allows them to extract a strictly positive information rent from the principal."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - the concept of information rent is well-established in principal-agent theory, but the specific application to hidden reward bandit settings is not directly addressed in neighboring papers.
- Break condition: If the agent is less knowledgeable about the principal's rewards, or if the principal can observe the agent's true rewards, the information rent may be reduced or eliminated.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Estimator -> ϵ-greedy algorithm -> Regret analysis -> Numerical experiments

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize by making the agent select each action once with maximum incentive
  2. At each time step, update the agent reward estimate using the feasibility estimator
  3. Update the principal reward estimate using the sample mean
  4. With probability ϵt, explore by randomly sampling incentives; otherwise, exploit by computing incentives based on current estimates and confidence bounds
  5. Observe the agent's action and the principal's reward, and repeat

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Exploration rate: Higher exploration reduces estimation uncertainty but decreases immediate reward
  - Confidence bound tightness: Tighter bounds allow for more aggressive exploitation but may lead to overfitting
  - Incentive magnitude: Larger incentives make the agent's action more predictable but may reduce the principal's net reward

- Failure signatures:
  - High regret: Indicates that the exploration-exploitation trade-off is not effective, possibly due to poor estimation or too little exploration
  - Unstable estimates: Suggests that the estimator is not converging, possibly due to insufficient exploration or a non-utility-maximizing agent
  - Information rent extraction: If the agent is able to consistently extract high information rent, it may indicate that the principal's estimates are systematically biased

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify that the estimator can correctly identify the agent's reward vector in a simple 2-action setting with known rewards
  2. Test the ϵ-greedy algorithm on a 3-action setting and compare the cumulative regret to the theoretical bound
  3. Vary the exploration rate (m parameter) and observe its effect on the estimation accuracy and regret

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Question 1**: How does the principal's performance degrade if the agent is not a perfect-knowledge utility maximizer, but instead has imperfect knowledge of their own reward model?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses this as a future direction in the conclusion, noting that the dynamic interaction between the principal's and agent's learning processes would add substantial complexity.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only analyzes the case where the agent has perfect knowledge of their reward model and can take the true utility-maximizing action at every period.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Numerical experiments comparing the principal's regret when the agent has perfect vs. imperfect knowledge of their reward model, or theoretical analysis of how the regret bounds change under imperfect agent knowledge.

- **Question 2**: How does the principal's performance change when dealing with multiple utility-maximizing agents instead of a single agent?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions this as a future direction, noting that studying a multi-agent setting where the principal needs to design incentives for each individual selfish-agent would require a completely different approach and analysis.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only considers a single agent scenario, and extending to multiple agents would introduce new challenges such as potential agent communication and coordination.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Numerical experiments comparing the principal's regret in single-agent vs. multi-agent scenarios, or theoretical analysis of how the regret bounds scale with the number of agents.

- **Question 3**: Can the principal's estimation problem be solved more efficiently if they have some prior knowledge about the agent's reward model, such as knowing the discrete set of possible mean reward values?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions in Section 4 that achieving the maximum information rent would require a significant amount of sophistication from the agent, and that reducing this information rent could be possible in other principal-agent designs where more information about the agent's utility model is accessible by the principal.
  - Why unresolved: The paper assumes the agent's rewards belong to a continuous space and the principal has no prior knowledge about the reward model. However, in practice, the principal may have some prior information that could be leveraged to improve estimation efficiency.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Numerical experiments comparing the principal's estimation error and regret when they have varying levels of prior knowledge about the agent's reward model, or theoretical analysis of how the regret bounds change under different prior knowledge assumptions.

## Limitations

- The method assumes the agent is a perfect-knowledge utility maximizer, which may not hold in practice due to bounded rationality or strategic complexity.
- The information rent extraction mechanism relies on strong assumptions about the agent's knowledge and computational capabilities, with no rigorous bounds provided on the information rent.
- The numerical experiments validate the method on a specific transportation planning problem, but the generalizability to other domains and sensitivity to problem parameters remain unclear.

## Confidence

- **Theoretical regret bounds**: Medium confidence - Formal proofs and concentration inequalities provide a solid foundation, but practical performance may be sensitive to hyperparameter choices.
- **Information rent extraction**: Low confidence - Theoretically sound but relies on strong assumptions with limited empirical validation.
- **Numerical experiment results**: Medium confidence - Validates method on specific problem but lacks sensitivity analysis and generalizability assessment.
