# JCSE: Contrastive Learning of Japanese Sentence Embeddings and Its Applications

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.08193
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08193
- Reference count: 40
- This paper addresses the challenge of learning effective sentence embeddings for Japanese, a low-resource language with limited domain-specific training data.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes JCSE (Contrastive learning of Sentence Embeddings for Japanese), a novel framework for learning effective sentence embeddings for Japanese, a low-resource language. The framework uses data generation to create synthetic sentence pairs for contrastive learning, specifically fine-tuning a pre-trained T5 model on target domain data to generate contradictory sentence pairs by substituting nouns. These synthetic pairs are used alongside positive pairs to adapt Japanese language models to specific tasks. The authors also construct a comprehensive Japanese STS benchmark by translating existing datasets and combining them with human-annotated ones, and introduce a new QAbot dataset for information retrieval. Experiments show JCSE significantly outperforms direct transfer and other training strategies on two domain-specific tasks: clinical STS (JACSTS dataset) and educational information retrieval (QAbot dataset).

## Method Summary
JCSE is a two-stage contrastive learning framework for Japanese sentence embeddings. First, a T5 model is fine-tuned on domain-specific corpora using unsupervised denoising training to create a data generator. This generator is then used to create negative sentence pairs by substituting nouns in target domain sentences. These synthetic negative pairs are combined with positive pairs (created via dropout masks) and used to fine-tune a Japanese language model using contrastive learning. The second stage involves further fine-tuning on the JSNLI dataset to refine semantic understanding. The framework aims to adapt Japanese language models to specific domains with limited target domain data.

## Key Results
- JCSE significantly outperforms direct transfer and other training strategies on clinical STS (JACSTS dataset) and educational information retrieval (QAbot dataset) tasks.
- The two-stage contrastive learning approach, first on synthesized data and then on JSNLI, is found to be better than single-stage training for JCSE.
- Models trained using JCSE achieve improvements in metrics like Spearman correlation, MRR, MAP, and P@1, P@5 on domain-specific tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Domain adaptation via synthetic data generation outperforms direct transfer
- Mechanism: JCSE fine-tunes a T5 model on domain-specific corpora, then uses it to generate contradictory sentence pairs by substituting nouns, which are used as hard negatives in contrastive learning. This creates domain-relevant training signals that general models lack.
- Core assumption: Nouns are the most semantically relevant content words for determining sentence similarity in Japanese, so replacing them generates meaningful negative examples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "nouns are the most relevant content words in a Japanese sentence"
  - [section 4.3] "we find that nouns (NOUN) are by far the most relevant content words in a Japanese sentence across the three datasets"
  - [corpus] Weak: The corpus neighbors do not directly support this claim about Japanese noun relevance
- Break condition: If other parts of speech (verbs, adjectives) prove more semantically critical for the target domain, the noun-substitution strategy loses effectiveness.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Contrastive learning with hard negatives outperforms unsupervised methods on STS tasks
- Mechanism: JCSE uses generated contradictory pairs (hard negatives) alongside positive pairs from dropout masks in contrastive learning, weighting hard negatives more heavily in the loss function to improve embedding quality.
- Core assumption: Hard negatives provide more informative training signals than randomly sampled negatives, leading to better separation of semantically dissimilar sentences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also apply in-batch negative sampling where all other examples in a batch are considered as negatives"
  - [section 4.1] "we find that models trained on RoBERTa pre-trained checkpoints using a contrastive learning method such as SimCSE get poor results even worse than previous baselines"
  - [corpus] Weak: Corpus neighbors do not provide direct evidence about contrastive learning effectiveness
- Break condition: If the generated negative pairs are too dissimilar from real negatives, the hard negative weighting could mislead the model.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-stage contrastive learning (domain adaptation then JSNLI fine-tuning) improves performance on domain-specific tasks
- Mechanism: JCSE first fine-tunes on synthetic domain pairs to adapt embeddings, then on JSNLI entailment pairs to refine semantic understanding, creating a progressive adaptation pipeline.
- Core assumption: The two-stage approach allows the model to first specialize to domain vocabulary/semantics, then refine with general entailment reasoning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "apply a two-stage contrastive learning recipe that fine-tune a Japanese language model first on synthesized sentence pairs and then on JSNLI"
  - [section 4.4] "we find that this training sequence, first on the synthesized generated data and then on JSNLI data, is better for our method"
  - [corpus] Weak: Corpus neighbors do not validate the two-stage approach effectiveness
- Break condition: If the JSNLI fine-tuning overfits to general entailment patterns that don't transfer to the domain, performance could degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Data Collection -> Text Normalization -> POS Tagging -> T5 Fine-tuning -> Synthetic Data Generation -> Contrastive Learning (Stage 1) -> JSNLI Fine-tuning (Stage 2) -> Evaluation
  Key components: T5 generator, BERT-based encoder, contrastive loss function, POS tagger

- Critical path:
  1. Collect and normalize domain corpus
  2. Fine-tune T5 on domain corpus for data generation
  3. Generate synthetic contradictory pairs via noun substitution
  4. Apply two-stage contrastive learning with hard negatives
  5. Evaluate on domain-specific STS and retrieval tasks

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Noun substitution vs. full sentence generation: Simpler but may miss complex semantic variations
  - Hard negative weighting (α parameter): More informative but risk of misleading signals if negatives are too dissimilar
  - Two-stage vs. single-stage training: More effective but requires additional labeled data (JSNLI)

- Failure signatures:
  - Generated negative pairs are semantically unrelated to anchors (data generation failure)
  - Model overfits to synthetic patterns not present in real data (synthetic data distribution mismatch)
  - JSNLI fine-tuning degrades domain performance (domain shift from general to specific)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate noun substitution vs. random word substitution on synthetic data quality
  2. Test different hard negative weighting schemes (α values) on JACSTS performance
  3. Compare single-stage vs. two-stage training on QAbot retrieval metrics

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of JCSE compare to other domain adaptation methods when applied to low-resource languages beyond Japanese, particularly those with even more limited training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that JCSE is designed for low-resource languages like Japanese, which are characterized by insufficient target domain data. However, the paper does not provide a direct comparison with other domain adaptation methods for other low-resource languages.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on Japanese and does not explore the effectiveness of JCSE on other low-resource languages. A direct comparison with other domain adaptation methods on a broader range of languages is needed to validate JCSE's generalizability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing JCSE to other domain adaptation methods on various low-resource languages with different data availability levels would provide the necessary evidence.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of the choice of data generation model (e.g., T5) on the quality of synthesized negative pairs and the overall performance of JCSE?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors use T5 as the data generation model for JCSE. However, they do not explore the impact of using different data generation models on the quality of the synthesized negative pairs and the final performance of JCSE.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a systematic comparison of different data generation models and their impact on JCSE's performance. Understanding the role of the data generation model is crucial for optimizing JCSE.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing JCSE using different data generation models (e.g., BART, GPT-2) and evaluating the quality of the synthesized negative pairs and the final performance on downstream tasks would provide the necessary evidence.

### Open Question 3

- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors fine-tune the data generator on the target domain corpus. However, they do not investigate how the size of this corpus affects the quality of the generated negative pairs and the overall performance of JCSE.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the relationship between the size of the target domain corpus and JCSE's performance. Understanding this relationship is important for determining the data requirements for effective domain adaptation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the size of the target domain corpus used for fine-tuning the data generator and evaluating the impact on the quality of the generated negative pairs and the final performance of JCSE would provide the necessary evidence.

## Limitations
- The effectiveness of noun substitution for generating negative pairs in Japanese relies on the assumption that nouns are the most semantically relevant content words, but this claim lacks direct empirical validation from the paper's experiments.
- The two-stage training approach, while showing improved results, requires additional labeled data (JSNLI) that may not be available for all target domains.
- The hard negative weighting mechanism depends on an alpha parameter that could introduce instability if the generated negative pairs are too dissimilar from real negatives.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The overall framework architecture and training pipeline are well-defined and reproducible, with clear specifications for data generation and contrastive learning components.
- **Medium confidence**: The experimental results showing JCSE outperforming baselines on JACSTS and QAbot datasets are supported by quantitative metrics, though the specific hyperparameter settings and training configurations are not fully detailed.
- **Low confidence**: The foundational claim about Japanese nouns being the most semantically relevant content words for sentence similarity is stated but not empirically validated within the paper's experimental framework.
