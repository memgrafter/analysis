# Two derivations of Principal Component Analysis on datasets of distributions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.13503
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13503
- Reference count: 1
- Primary result: Distributional PCA formulation for datasets where each point is a mean-covariance pair

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Distributional PCA, a method for dimensionality reduction when data points are probability distributions characterized by mean vectors and covariance matrices rather than simple point estimates. The authors present two mathematically equivalent derivations: one based on maximizing expected projected variance and another minimizing 2-Wasserstein reconstruction error. The approach generalizes standard PCA, reducing to it when all covariances approach zero, and provides a principled framework for handling uncertain or hierarchical data.

## Method Summary
Distributional PCA operates on datasets where each observation consists of a mean vector and covariance matrix, treating each data point as a probability distribution rather than a single location. The method constructs a matrix from the sum of all mean products and covariances, then extracts principal components as the leading eigenvectors of this matrix. Two equivalent optimization perspectives are provided: maximizing the expected variance of projections and minimizing the total squared 2-Wasserstein distance between original and reconstructed distributions under linear projection. This dual formulation mirrors the classical PCA framework while extending it to distributional data.

## Key Results
- Principal components are defined as leading eigenvectors of a matrix formed from sums of mean products and covariances
- Two equivalent derivations provided: variance maximization and Wasserstein error minimization
- Method reduces to standard PCA when all covariances approach zero
- Visual demonstration shows distinct behavior compared to PCA on means and samples

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by incorporating both location (mean) and spread (covariance) information into the dimensionality reduction process. By treating each data point as a distribution rather than a single point, it captures uncertainty structure that standard PCA ignores. The two derivation approaches converge because both are optimizing the same underlying objective - finding projections that best preserve the distributional characteristics of the data. The 2-Wasserstein distance naturally captures the geometry of the space of distributions with finite second moments, making it an appropriate metric for reconstruction error.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input distributions (mean, cov) -> Construct matrix M -> Eigen-decomposition -> Principal components

Critical path: The construction of matrix M from the dataset is the critical step, followed by eigendecomposition. The computational bottleneck is typically the eigendecomposition of potentially large matrices.

Design tradeoffs: 
- Assumes distributions are well-characterized by mean and covariance (may not hold for multimodal distributions)
- Uses 2-Wasserstein distance which is computationally intensive but geometrically meaningful
- Maintains PCA's linear projection framework while extending to distributional data

Failure signatures:
- Poor performance when distributional assumptions are violated (e.g., heavy tails, multimodality)
- Computational issues with very large datasets due to matrix operations
- Instability when covariance matrices are near-singular

First experiments:
1. Apply to synthetic Gaussian data with known covariance structures to verify theoretical properties
2. Compare reconstruction error against standard PCA on the same data
3. Test sensitivity to varying levels of uncertainty across the dataset

## Limitations
- Limited empirical validation across diverse real-world datasets
- Assumes mean-covariance pairs fully capture distributional information
- Computational complexity of 2-Wasserstein metric may limit scalability
- No comparison with alternative uncertainty-aware dimensionality reduction methods

## Confidence
High: The mathematical formulation appears sound and follows established PCA principles. The two derivation approaches are logically consistent with each other.

Medium: The theoretical claim that the method reduces to standard PCA when covariances approach zero needs empirical verification. The visualization example is limited in scope.

Low: The practical advantages over alternative approaches for handling distributional data are not demonstrated through comprehensive experiments. Scalability implications remain unexplored.
