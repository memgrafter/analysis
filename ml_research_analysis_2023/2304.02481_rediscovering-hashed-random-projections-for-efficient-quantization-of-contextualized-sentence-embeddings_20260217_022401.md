# Rediscovering Hashed Random Projections for Efficient Quantization of Contextualized Sentence Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.02481
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02481
- Authors: [Not specified in input]

- Primary result: Compresses contextualized sentence embeddings by up to 98.96% with 94-99% performance retention on classification and semantic similarity tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes an efficient quantization approach for contextualized sentence embeddings using randomly initialized hyperplane projections. The method transforms floating-point embeddings into binary vectors through random projections followed by Heaviside quantization, achieving up to 98.96% memory reduction. Experiments across English and German sentence classification tasks show that the quantized embeddings retain 94-99% of original performance, with effectiveness maintained across various projection sizes.

## Method Summary
The method applies randomly initialized hyperplane projections (HRP) to pre-trained sentence embeddings, followed by Heaviside quantization to create binary vectors. A random hyperplane matrix Whrp ∈ R^(ds×dt) with entries drawn from N(0,1) projects sentence embeddings into lower-dimensional space, which are then binarized using the Heaviside step function. The approach is evaluated on multiple English and German classification tasks and semantic similarity benchmarks using various multilingual sentence embedding models including SBert, MiniLM, LaBSE, and m-USE.

## Key Results
- Achieves up to 98.96% memory footprint reduction through binary vector quantization
- Maintains 94-99% of original performance across English and German classification tasks
- Embeddings remain effective across different projection sizes (256-2048 dimensions)
- Semantic similarity tasks are less affected by HRP than classification tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Randomly initialized hyperplane projections can approximate hamming distance in nearest neighbor clustering
- Mechanism: Random hyperplane hashing maps continuous vectors into binary space where hamming distance approximates similarity
- Core assumption: The random hyperplane matrix has entries drawn from a normal distribution and preserves pairwise similarity structure
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Indyk and Motwani (1998) as well as Charikar (2002) have shown that hashes from randomly initialized hyperplane projections can approximate the hamming distance in nearest neighbor clustering" and [section 3] "we randomly initialize a hyperplane Whrp ∈ Rds×dt ∼ N(0, 1) to project a sentence embedding"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Quantization via Heaviside step function preserves classification performance while reducing memory footprint
- Mechanism: The Heaviside function binarizes continuous projections, enabling compression while retaining class-discriminative information
- Core assumption: The linear classifier can recover performance from binarized inputs if the binarization preserves class boundaries
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "To further reduce their size by up to 98.96%, we quantize the resulting floating-point representations into binary vectors" and [section 3] "We then further apply the Heaviside (Weisstein, 2002) step function H(·) to quantize the resulting vector"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Sentence-BERT aggregation methods can work well with hashed random projections and minimal computational overhead
- Mechanism: SBert's pooling strategies (mean, max, CLS) aggregate contextualized embeddings in a way that remains compatible with HRP transformations
- Core assumption: The aggregation preserves the semantic structure needed for the downstream task even after dimensionality reduction
- Evidence anchors: [section 5] "In contrast, we find that recent aggregation methods for contextualized word embeddings such as SBert (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) can work well with HRP and a minimal computational overhead"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-trained sentence embedding model -> Random hyperplane projection layer -> Heaviside quantization function -> Linear classifier or similarity computation
- Critical path: 1. Generate sentence embeddings from pre-trained model 2. Apply HRP transformation: x0/1_SBert = H(xSBert · Whrp) 3. Feed quantized embeddings to downstream model (linear classifier or similarity computation) 4. Evaluate performance and memory usage
- Design tradeoffs: Projection dimension vs. memory footprint vs. accuracy: Higher dimensions preserve more information but increase memory usage; Random initialization vs. learned projections: Random is faster and requires no training data but may be less optimal; Fixed vs. adaptive quantization: Fixed (Heaviside) is simple but may not be optimal for all embedding distributions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different random initialization seeds affect the performance of hashed random projections (HRP)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions running 10 different weight initializations using different pseudo-random number generator seeds.
- Why unresolved: While the paper reports average performance across multiple runs, it doesn't deeply analyze how sensitive the HRP performance is to initialization seeds or whether certain seeds consistently produce better results.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed statistical analysis comparing performance variance across different initialization seeds, and potentially identifying patterns in which seeds yield better results.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific aspects of sentence embeddings are most affected by quantization and dimensionality reduction?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that semantic similarity tasks are less affected by HRP than classification tasks, suggesting that certain types of information are more robust to compression.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates performance retention but doesn't investigate which semantic or syntactic features are most preserved or lost during quantization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed feature analysis comparing compressed vs. uncompressed embeddings, potentially using probing tasks to identify which linguistic properties are best preserved.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would learned projection matrices outperform randomly initialized ones for HRP?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses randomly initialized hyperplane projections and mentions this as a limitation, suggesting that "A more informed approach using dimensional reduction methods could lead to better projections."
- Why unresolved: The paper deliberately uses random projections for efficiency, but doesn't explore whether training the projection layer could yield better compression without significantly increasing computational cost.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments between random and learned projection matrices, measuring both performance and computational overhead.

## Limitations

- The empirical validation is limited to specific multilingual models and tasks, not exploring diverse domains or embedding distributions
- The binarization via Heaviside function may not be optimal for all embedding distributions and adaptive quantization strategies are not compared
- The paper does not thoroughly investigate the impact of different pooling strategies on HRP performance
- No exploration of whether learned projection matrices might outperform random ones for specific domains

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The memory reduction claims (up to 98.96%) are straightforward calculations based on bit representation changes. The experimental methodology for classification tasks (5-fold cross-validation, 10 random seeds) is well-specified and reproducible.

**Medium Confidence**: The performance retention claims (94-99%) are empirically demonstrated but may vary with different embedding models, languages, or task types not covered in the study. The assumption that random projections preserve semantic structure for all sentence embedding models needs further validation across diverse domains.

**Low Confidence**: The assertion that SBert aggregation methods work well with HRP transformations without extensive empirical comparison to other aggregation strategies. The paper does not thoroughly investigate the impact of different pooling strategies (mean, max, CLS) on HRP performance.

## Next Checks

2. **Adaptive Quantization Comparison**: Implement and compare Heaviside quantization with adaptive thresholding methods (e.g., percentile-based, learned thresholds) to determine if fixed binarization is optimal for preserving semantic similarity.

3. **Learned vs. Random Projections**: Train a small projection matrix on a subset of the target task data and compare its performance to random projections, measuring the tradeoff between training time/computational overhead and accuracy gains.
