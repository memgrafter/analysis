# Are Machine Rationales (Not) Useful to Humans? Measuring and Improving Human Utility of Free-Text Rationales

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07095
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07095
- Reference count: 40
- Only a small percentage of LM-generated rationales are useful to humans, with many misleading them to incorrect answers.

## Executive Summary
This work evaluates human utility of free-text rationales generated by large language models (LMs) through human studies. It finds that only a small percentage of LM-generated rationales are actually useful for humans, with many misleading them to incorrect answers. Task performance and similarity to gold rationales do not correlate well with human utility. However, the ability of rationales to help humans generalize to similar unseen questions is a good indicator of their human utility. Based on this, an automated score (GEN-U) is proposed that correlates better with human utility and can be used to improve LMs' ability to generate more useful rationales without sacrificing task performance.

## Method Summary
The study uses StrategyQA and OBQA datasets with rationales generated by GPT-3, T5-3B, and T5-large. Human MTurk studies annotate rationale utility (useful, not useful, unsure). The key innovation is using LM generalization to unseen questions as a proxy for human utility. Generalization questions (rephrases, counterfactuals, similar reasoning) are generated and used to compute GEN-U scores. T5-large is then fine-tuned using Quark RL with GEN-U as reward to improve rationale quality.

## Key Results
- Only a small percentage of LM-generated rationales are useful to humans, with many misleading to incorrect answers
- Task performance and BERTScore similarity do not correlate well with human utility
- GEN-U, an automated score based on LM generalization ability, correlates better with human utility and can improve LM rationale generation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Human utility of free-text rationales is best measured by their ability to help humans generalize to similar unseen questions.
- Mechanism: If a rationale helps humans answer paraphrased, counterfactual, or similar reasoning questions correctly, it indicates the rationale transferred useful knowledge rather than just aligning with a gold answer.
- Core assumption: Knowledge transfer is a stronger indicator of human utility than task accuracy or similarity to gold rationales.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "While we observe that certain properties of rationales like conciseness and novelty are correlated with their human utility, estimating them without human involvement is challenging. We show that, by estimating a rationale's helpfulness in answering similar unseen instances, we can measure its human utility to a better extent."
  - [section 3] "We observe that gold rationales form an upper bound for generalization difference, across all types of generalization questions and types of rationale utility. Useful rationales are able to help humans generalize better to new instances, whereas non-useful rationales often mislead humans to make incorrect choices."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Task performance of the LM generating rationales does not correlate well with human utility.
- Mechanism: High task accuracy can result from rationales that leak answers or contain irrelevant information, which may mislead humans rather than help them learn.
- Core assumption: Task performance and human utility are distinct evaluation criteria; a rationale can be effective for LM prediction without being useful for human understanding.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Existing metrics like task performance of the LM generating the rationales, or similarity between generated and gold rationales are not good indicators of their human utility."
  - [section 2] "We observe Theill's U for GPT-3, T5-3B and T5-Large were 0.111 (0.092), 0.034 (0.029) and 0.005 (0.016) for StrategyQA (OBQA) respectively... This also demonstrates that even though T5-Large, which was fine-tuned on the entire training set had the highest task performance, it has the lowest correlation with human utility."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: An automated score (GEN-U) based on LM generalization ability can effectively approximate human utility of rationales.
- Mechanism: GEN-U measures how well an LM answers generalization questions before and after being shown a rationale. If the rationale improves the LM's ability to answer similar questions, it indicates high human utility.
- Core assumption: LM generalization ability is a good proxy for human utility, and GEN-U can reliably estimate this ability without human involvement.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also translate this finding into an automated score, GEN-U, that we propose, which can help improve LMs' ability to generate rationales with better human utility, while maintaining most of its task performance."
  - [section 4] "We find that Theill's U = 0.22, which is indicates that GEN-U is a better estimate that F's task accuracy or BERTScore similarity between generated and gold rationales."

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Task performance vs. human utility distinction
  - Why needed here: To understand why high-performing rationales may not be useful for humans and avoid conflating LM effectiveness with human understanding.
  - Quick check question: Can you give an example where a rationale helps an LM answer correctly but misleads a human?

- Concept: Generalization ability as a proxy for knowledge transfer
  - Why needed here: To grasp why the ability to answer unseen but similar questions is a stronger indicator of useful rationales than exact task performance.
  - Quick check question: How would you design a test to check if a rationale helps with generalization rather than just memorization?

- Concept: Automated evaluation metrics for interpretability
  - Why needed here: To appreciate the challenge of evaluating human utility without expensive human studies and the need for proxy metrics like GEN-U.
  - Quick check question: What are the limitations of using LM performance or text similarity as proxies for human utility?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Self-rationalizing LM (F) -> Generalization question generator -> GEN-U scorer (uses F_I and F_IR) -> Quark updater -> Updated LM

- Critical path: Generate rationales → Evaluate generalization ability (GEN-U) → Update LM with Quark → Measure human utility

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using LM generalization as proxy for human utility vs. direct human evaluation (cost vs. accuracy)
  - Automated question generation vs. manual curation (scalability vs. quality)
  - Reward-based fine-tuning vs. supervised learning (flexibility vs. stability)

- Failure signatures:
  - GEN-U scores do not correlate with human utility on new data
  - Updated LM loses task performance while gaining human utility
  - Generated generalization questions are too similar to originals or have incorrect labels

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement GEN-U and test correlation with human utility on a small subset
  2. Use Quark to update a baseline LM and measure changes in GEN-U and human utility
  3. Analyze failure cases where GEN-U and human utility diverge to improve the metric

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the specific granular-level properties of rationales that correlate with human utility, and how can these properties be reliably estimated without human involvement?
- Basis in paper: Explicit
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies that certain properties like conciseness and novelty are correlated with human utility, but estimating these properties without human involvement remains challenging.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of automated methods or metrics that can accurately estimate these properties and demonstrate their correlation with human utility.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the GEN-U metric be improved to have a higher correlation with human utility, and what alternative methods could be explored to simulate human generalization?
- Basis in paper: Explicit
- Why unresolved: While GEN-U shows better correlation with human utility compared to task accuracy or BERTScore, its overall correlation is still low, indicating room for improvement.
- What evidence would resolve it: Creation of a more accurate metric with higher correlation to human utility, possibly by refining GEN-U or exploring other methods to simulate human generalization.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the process of generating generalization questions be fully automated, eliminating the need for manual validation and human involvement?
- Basis in paper: Inferred
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions that generating generalization questions currently requires manual validation by humans, which is time-consuming and limits the scalability of the approach.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of a fully automated method for generating high-quality generalization questions and obtaining their gold answers, without requiring human validation.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can the findings from this study be applied to other tasks, such as closed-book question answering, where human annotators cannot "guess" answers and the task is more challenging for both LMs and humans?
- Basis in paper: Inferred
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the StrategyQA dataset, which involves multi-choice question answering. The applicability of the findings to other tasks, especially those where humans have limited prior knowledge, is not explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting similar studies on other tasks, such as closed-book question answering, to evaluate the human utility of rationales and validate the generalizability of the findings.

## Limitations
- GEN-U correlation with human utility is still relatively low despite being better than alternatives
- Study only covers two datasets (StrategyQA and OBQA) limiting generalizability
- Manual validation of generalization questions is time-consuming and not fully automated

## Confidence

- High confidence: The finding that task performance and BERTScore similarity do not correlate well with human utility, supported by strong empirical evidence across multiple models and datasets.
- Medium confidence: The claim that generalization ability is a good indicator of human utility, based on moderate correlation observed in experiments but requiring validation on additional tasks.
- Low confidence: The effectiveness of GEN-U as a universal proxy for human utility, given it was only validated on two datasets and may not generalize to more complex reasoning tasks.

## Next Checks

1. Test GEN-U correlation with human utility on a broader range of tasks, particularly those requiring more complex reasoning or domain-specific knowledge to establish generalizability.

2. Conduct ablation studies removing either the question generation or human evaluation components to quantify their relative contributions to the final results.

3. Implement an online A/B test where users interact with systems using rationales optimized via GEN-U versus traditional approaches to measure real-world impact on user understanding and task performance.
