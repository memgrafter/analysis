# Sound Explanation for Trustworthy Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.06134
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06134
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Proved no attribution method can satisfy all desirable properties simultaneously; proposed sound explanation approach achieving AUC 0.826 for PDAC and 0.922 for HCC prediction

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the fundamental limitations of attribution-based interpretability methods in machine learning. Through rigorous theoretical analysis, the authors prove that no attribution algorithm can simultaneously satisfy four desirable properties: specificity, additivity, completeness, and baseline invariance. Instead, they propose "sound explanation" as an alternative approach that focuses on providing causally sufficient information for human understanding. The framework is applied to cancer prediction using EHR data, where a feature selection algorithm reduces thousands of features to less than 100 while maintaining high predictive accuracy and aligning with known risk factors, thereby increasing clinician trust in the models.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a feature selection algorithm for neural networks that implements the concept of sound explanation. The approach uses L0 regularization with a BinMask method to identify a subset of causally sufficient features. The neural network architecture consists of two hidden layers with 64 and 20 neurons using tanh activation. The algorithm is applied to binary classification tasks for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) using federated EHR data with millions of patients and thousands of features including diagnosis, medication, lab records, and demographics. The feature selection process aims to reduce the feature space to less than 100 features while maintaining high predictive performance.

## Key Results
- Proved theoretically that no attribution method can satisfy specificity, additivity, completeness, and baseline invariance simultaneously
- Achieved AUC of 0.826 for PDAC prediction and 0.922 for HCC prediction using reduced feature sets
- Reduced thousands of features to less than 100 while maintaining high predictive accuracy
- Selected features aligned well with known risk factors for both cancer types

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The paper's theoretical proof establishes that no attribution-based method can simultaneously satisfy specificity, additivity, completeness, and baseline invariance.
- Mechanism: By demonstrating that these four desirable properties are mutually exclusive for attribution methods, the paper justifies why alternative approaches to explainability are necessary.
- Core assumption: The properties of specificity, additivity, completeness, and baseline invariance are indeed essential for any meaningful attribution method.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We prove that no attribution algorithm satisfies specificity, additivity, completeness, and baseline invariance."
  - [section] Theorem 2.6 provides a formal proof that no interpretation satisfying specificity, additivity, and completeness can also satisfy baseline invariance.
  - [corpus] The paper's claim aligns with the broader discourse on explainability limitations in AI, as evidenced by related works like "False Sense of Security in Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)".
- Break condition: If future research demonstrates that one of these four properties is not actually necessary for a useful attribution method, the proof's premise would be weakened.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sound explanation provides a theoretically sound alternative to attribution-based methods by focusing on causally sufficient information.
- Mechanism: By formalizing the concept of sound explanation, the paper offers a principled approach that guarantees accuracy and reliability in explanations.
- Core assumption: Humans can understand and derive causal relationships from the subset of information provided by sound explanation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A sound explanation entails providing sufficient information to causally explain the predictions made by a system."
  - [section] The paper defines sound explanation formally as a cut in the computational graph that causally determines the system's output.
  - [corpus] This approach is supported by the broader literature on causal methods in machine learning, as seen in works like "Causality Is Key to Understand and Balance Multiple Goals in Trustworthy ML and Foundation Models".
- Break condition: If the presented subset of information fails to capture all causally relevant factors, the sound explanation would be incomplete.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The feature selection algorithm for cancer prediction provides a practical implementation of sound explanation that increases clinician trust.
- Mechanism: By reducing thousands of features to less than 100 while maintaining high predictive accuracy, the algorithm creates an interpretable model that aligns with known risk factors.
- Core assumption: Clinicians' trust in the model is significantly influenced by the interpretability and alignment with known medical knowledge.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The selected features align well with known risk factors, increasing clinician trust in the models."

  - [corpus] This approach is consistent with the broader trend of developing interpretable models for healthcare applications, as seen in works like "fCrit: A Visual Explanation System for Furniture Design Creative Support".
- Break condition: If the selected features fail to capture all relevant medical factors or if clinicians do not find the explanation satisfactory, the trust-building mechanism would fail.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but several implicit questions emerge from the analysis:

1. How can the tradeoff between explainability and prediction accuracy be formally quantified and optimized in sound explanation approaches?
   - Basis in paper: The authors note that after achieving soundness, one can trade off between explainability and prediction accuracy, but do not provide a formal framework for this tradeoff.
   - Why unresolved: The paper presents sound explanation as a conceptual framework but does not develop mathematical tools to optimize the explainability-accuracy tradeoff.
   - What evidence would resolve it: A formal mathematical framework that quantifies the tradeoff between explainability and accuracy in sound explanations, along with optimization algorithms that balance these competing objectives.

2. What are the conditions under which feature selection as a sound explanation approach performs optimally compared to other interpretation methods?
   - Basis in paper: The authors demonstrate success with feature selection on EHR data but do not analyze when this approach is most effective.
   - Why unresolved: The paper presents a case study but does not provide theoretical analysis of when feature selection outperforms other interpretation methods.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis across diverse datasets and model types showing the conditions (data characteristics, model complexity, domain requirements) where feature selection as sound explanation is superior to attribution-based methods.

3. How can the faithfulness of sound explanations be formally evaluated, particularly when explanations may fail to match claimed semantics?
   - Basis in paper: The authors acknowledge that a sound explanation may fail to match the claimed semantics but do not propose methods to evaluate faithfulness.
   - Why unresolved: The paper establishes the concept of sound explanation but does not address how to verify that the explanation accurately represents the model's reasoning.
   - What evidence would resolve it: A formal framework for evaluating the faithfulness of sound explanations, including metrics and experimental validation showing the relationship between explanation faithfulness and model interpretability.
