# Exploring the Limits of Model-Targeted Indiscriminate Data Poisoning Attacks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.03592
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03592
- Reference count: 40
- This work introduces the notion of model poisonability to study the limits of indiscriminate data poisoning attacks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the concept of model poisonability to characterize when a target model can be achieved through data poisoning attacks. The authors derive a computable threshold τ that determines whether a model is poisonable based on the poisoning ratio εd. They show that a phase transition phenomenon exists: attacks can only succeed when εd exceeds τ. Building on parameter corruption attacks and refining the Gradient Canceling attack, the work provides new insights into data poisoning limits and significantly improves baselines across multiple datasets and models.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a framework for understanding the limits of indiscriminate data poisoning attacks through the concept of model poisonability. The key method is the Gradient Canceling (GC) attack, which solves a convex optimization problem to find a poisoning distribution that minimizes the gradient of the target model on the mixed dataset. The attack uses projected gradient descent to optimize the poisoning distribution, with the theoretical foundation provided by the model poisonability framework. Experiments are conducted on multiple datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, TinyImageNet) and models (Logistic Regression, Neural Networks, CNNs, ResNets) to validate the theoretical findings and compare against existing baselines.

## Key Results
- A computable threshold τ characterizes when a target model becomes poisonable based on poisoning ratio εd
- Phase transition phenomenon exists: attacks can only achieve certain target parameters when εd exceeds τ
- Gradient Canceling attack significantly improves indiscriminate poisoning baselines across multiple datasets and models
- Existing defenses are ineffective against strong poisoning attacks when εd > τ

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Data poisoning attacks succeed only when the poisoning ratio εd exceeds a computable threshold τ.
- Mechanism: The threshold τ is derived from the inner product ⟨w, g(µ)⟩ between the target model w and the gradient g(µ) of the clean training data. For logistic regression and margin classifiers, τ is proportional to this inner product divided by a constant related to the loss function's properties.
- Core assumption: The gradient of the target model over the mixed distribution χ must vanish for the model to be achievable through retraining.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We derive an easily computable threshold to establish and quantify a surprising phase transition phenomenon among popular ML models: data poisoning attacks can achieve certain target parameters only when the poisoning ratio exceeds our threshold."
  - [section 3.2] "Thus, we verify more easily that data poisoning succeeds on least-square regression for any εd > 0. In Remark 2 of Appendix A we discuss the connection between our result and the breakdown point in robust statistics."
- Break condition: If the poisoning distribution Γ is restricted to a subset of all distributions (e.g., due to attacker constraints), the threshold τ may increase, making poisoning harder or impossible.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The Gradient Canceling (GC) attack refines the KKT attack by directly targeting the vanishing of the gradient over the mixed distribution.
- Mechanism: GC solves a convex optimization problem to find a poisoning distribution ν such that the weighted sum of gradients from the clean and poisoned data approximately cancels out. This is achieved by minimizing the squared norm of the combined gradient.
- Core assumption: The poisoning distribution can be parameterized as a uniform distribution over a finite set of points, allowing gradient-based optimization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4] "We simply find a poisoning distribution ν ∈ Γ so that g(χ) = (1 − λ)g(µ) + λg(ν) ≈ 0"
  - [section 5] "We apply (projected) gradient descent to solve (33), where the gradient with respect to the j-th poisoning data zj is: ∂/∂zj = 1/n ∇z∇wℓ(zj; w) · [g(µ) + εdg(ˆν)]"
- Break condition: If the loss function is highly nonconvex or the constraint set Γ is too restrictive, the optimization problem may become intractable or the solution may not exist.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The effectiveness of data poisoning is determined by the interplay between the loss function, the clean training data distribution, and the target model's properties.
- Mechanism: The model poisonability framework quantifies this interplay by characterizing when a target model w can be achieved through poisoning, based on the gradient conditions over the mixed distribution.
- Core assumption: The loss function is smooth enough for gradients to exist and be computable, and the target model is within the model class being trained.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "We say a target model w is (ℓ, µ, Γ, λ)-poisonable if there exists some poisoning distribution ν ∈ Γ such that g(χ; w) = (1 − λ)g(µ; w) + λg(ν; w) = 0"
  - [section 3.2] "We can now state our fundamental problem of interest: Definition 1 (Model Poisonability). We say a target model w is (ℓ, µ, Γ, λ)-poisonable if there exists some poisoning distribution ν ∈ Γ such that g(χ; w) = (1 − λ)g(µ; w) + λg(ν; w) = 0"
- Break condition: If the loss function is non-differentiable or the model class does not include the target model, the framework does not apply.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Model poisonability framework -> Gradient Canceling attack algorithm -> Experimental validation
- Critical path: Compute gradient of clean data → Initialize poisoned dataset → Iteratively update poisoned data using gradient descent → Project updates to admissible set → Evaluate attack effectiveness
- Design tradeoffs: Choice of loss function and model architecture affects ease of poisoning. Convex losses are easier to poison than margin-based losses. Simpler models are more vulnerable than complex neural networks.
- Failure signatures: If εd < τ threshold, attack fails to achieve target model (non-vanishing gradient). If optimization problem is ill-conditioned or constraint set Γ is too restrictive, attack may fail or produce suboptimal results.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between the threshold τ and the complexity of the model or dataset?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors derive τ as a function of the model parameters and data distribution, but they do not explore how this threshold scales with model complexity or dataset dimensionality.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on empirical validation rather than theoretical analysis of τ's behavior under varying model or dataset characteristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical bounds or empirical studies showing how τ changes with model depth, dataset size, or feature dimensionality.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the results extend to data poisoning attacks in online or federated learning settings?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on centralized training, but many real-world ML systems operate in decentralized or federated contexts.
- Why unresolved: The model poisonability framework assumes access to the full training distribution, which is not always feasible in distributed settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical or theoretical studies of poisoning attacks in online or federated learning scenarios, with comparisons to centralized training.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Are there inherent limitations to the Gradient Canceling attack when applied to non-linear models or datasets with complex decision boundaries?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors test the Gradient Canceling attack on various models and datasets, but they do not explore its limitations in highly non-linear or complex scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The attack's effectiveness may degrade in scenarios where the gradient-based optimization struggles to find suitable poisoning points.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments or theoretical analysis showing the attack's performance on highly non-linear models or datasets with intricate decision boundaries.

## Limitations
- The theoretical framework relies on assumptions about loss function convexity and gradient availability, which may not hold for highly non-convex deep neural networks
- The GradPC method for generating target models is cited but specific implementation details are not provided, potentially affecting reproducibility
- The assumption of unrestricted optimization over distribution Γ may not hold in practice, limiting the applicability of threshold τ

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The derivation of threshold τ and its role in characterizing model poisonability is well-supported by both theoretical proofs and empirical evidence across multiple datasets and models.
- **Medium confidence**: The Gradient Canceling attack's effectiveness is demonstrated empirically, but the optimization's convergence properties and sensitivity to hyperparameters warrant further investigation.
- **Low confidence**: The paper's claims about limitations of existing defenses against indiscriminate poisoning attacks are based on a limited set of defenses and may not generalize to all defense mechanisms.
