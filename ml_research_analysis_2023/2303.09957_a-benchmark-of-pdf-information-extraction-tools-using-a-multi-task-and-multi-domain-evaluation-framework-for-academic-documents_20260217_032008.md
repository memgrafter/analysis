# A Benchmark of PDF Information Extraction Tools using a Multi-Task and Multi-Domain Evaluation Framework for Academic Documents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.09957
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09957
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: GROBID achieves the best results for metadata and reference extraction, followed by CERMINE and Science Parse. Adobe Extract performs best for table extraction. However, the performance of all tools is much lower for table extraction than for other content elements. All tools struggle to extract lists, footers, and equations.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive benchmark of ten open-source PDF information extraction tools using a multi-task and multi-domain evaluation framework. The study evaluates tools across 11 content elements including metadata, references, tables, paragraphs, sections, captions, equations, lists, and footers using 500K pages from arXiv research papers. The evaluation reveals significant performance variations across different content elements and tools, with GROBID showing the best overall performance for metadata and reference extraction, while Adobe Extract excels at table extraction. The study highlights the ongoing challenges in PDF information extraction, particularly for tables, lists, footers, and equations.

## Method Summary
The study builds upon the DocBank dataset containing 500K pages from arXiv research papers with annotations for 12 diverse content elements. Ten open-source tools are evaluated including Adobe Extract, Apache Tika, Camelot, CERMINE, GROBID, PdfAct, PyMuPDF, RefExtract, ScienceParse, and Tabula. The evaluation framework parses ground-truth and tool outputs into separate and collated token data frames, computes Levenshtein ratio similarity matrices, and calculates Precision, Recall, F1, and Accuracy scores for each content element and tool combination. The framework supports both strict token-level and lenient collated token-level evaluation approaches.

## Key Results
- GROBID achieves the best metadata and reference extraction results with F1 scores of 0.984 and 0.928 respectively.
- Adobe Extract performs best for table extraction with an F1 score of 0.738.
- All tools struggle significantly with lists, footers, and equations, with most achieving F1 scores below 0.2 for these elements.
- The performance gap between content elements is substantial, with metadata extraction achieving much higher scores than table extraction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Large, multi-domain datasets enable better generalization of PDF extraction tools.
- Mechanism: The DocBank dataset used in the benchmark contains 500K pages from arXiv papers spanning multiple fields (Physics, Mathematics, Computer Science, etc.), allowing tools to learn diverse layouts and content structures rather than overfitting to a single domain.
- Core assumption: Extraction performance improves when training or evaluation data covers a wide range of document types and domains.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our framework builds upon DocBank, a multi-domain dataset of 1.5M annotated content elements extracted from 500K pages of research papers on arXiv."
  - [section] "Most datasets exclusively offer labels for document metadata, references, or both. Only the DocBank dataset by Li et al. [31] offers annotations for 12 diverse content elements..."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct comparison experiments between single-domain and multi-domain datasets in the paper.
- Break condition: If a tool is specifically designed for a narrow domain, multi-domain datasets may not improve its performance and could even hurt it.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Evaluation frameworks that support multiple extraction tasks provide more comprehensive tool assessment.
- Mechanism: The benchmark framework evaluates tools across 11 content elements (metadata, references, tables, paragraphs, sections, captions, equations, lists, footers) rather than just 1-2 elements, revealing strengths and weaknesses across the full spectrum of PDF content.
- Core assumption: A tool's performance on one content element does not predict its performance on others; comprehensive evaluation is necessary.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We provide a large and diverse evaluation framework that supports more extraction tasks than most related datasets."
  - [section] "The tools' results differ greatly for different content elements. Certainly, no tool performs best for all elements..."
  - [corpus] Weak - the paper doesn't show quantitative evidence that multi-task frameworks lead to better tool selection decisions.
- Break condition: If users only care about extracting one specific element, a multi-task framework may add unnecessary complexity.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Combining multiple extraction approaches yields better overall performance than any single approach.
- Mechanism: The paper shows that GROBID (which uses cascaded sequence labeling models) outperforms other tools on multiple tasks, suggesting that sophisticated multi-model approaches are more effective than simple rule-based or single-model approaches.
- Core assumption: Complex PDF structures require multiple specialized models working in sequence to handle different content types effectively.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "GROBID achieves the best metadata and reference extraction results, followed by CERMINE and Science Parse."
  - [section] "For the general extraction task, GROBID outperforms other tools due to its segmentation model, which detects the main areas of documents based on layout features."
  - [corpus] Moderate - the paper compares different technological approaches but doesn't directly test combined approaches.
- Break condition: If computational resources are limited, the complexity of combined approaches may not be worth the marginal performance gains.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - PDF files → preprocessing → extraction tools → ground truth comparison
  - Evaluation engine: Similarity matrix computation → metric calculation → result aggregation
  - Dataset management: DocBank loading → filtering by content element → annotation parsing
  - Tool integration: Wrapper classes for each extraction tool → standardized output format

- Critical path:
  1. Load and preprocess PDF documents and ground truth annotations
  2. Run extraction tools on PDF documents
  3. Compare tool outputs with ground truth using Levenshtein ratio
  4. Calculate precision, recall, F1, and accuracy metrics
  5. Aggregate results by tool and content element

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Strict vs. lenient evaluation: Separate tokens format (strict) vs. collated tokens format (lenient)
  - Tool coverage vs. depth: Evaluating many tools on fewer elements vs. fewer tools on more elements
  - Dataset size vs. annotation quality: Large weakly-supervised datasets vs. smaller manually-annotated ones

- Failure signatures:
  - Zero F1 scores across all tools for specific content elements (lists, footers, equations)
  - Large performance gaps between tools for the same element
  - Tools failing to process documents with special characters in filenames

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify the evaluation pipeline works by running a single tool (GROBID) on a small subset of documents and checking metric calculations
  2. Compare strict vs. lenient evaluation results on the same dataset to understand the impact of evaluation methodology
  3. Test tool robustness by running all tools on a diverse sample of documents with different layouts and content types

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific architectural modifications could improve the extraction quality for tables, equations, lists, and footers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "All tools struggle to extract lists, footers, and equations" and that "The performance of all tools is much lower for table extraction than for other content elements"
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies these as challenging elements, it does not explore specific architectural changes that could address these difficulties, such as different neural network architectures or pre-processing techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing different architectural modifications (e.g., transformer-based approaches, specialized pre-processing pipelines) for these specific content elements would provide evidence of which modifications are most effective.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of information extraction tools vary across different academic disciplines beyond Physics, Mathematics, and Computer Science?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses arXiv documents covering "multiple domains" but primarily focuses on arXiv papers from Physics, Mathematics, and Computer Science, and notes that "more datasets suitable for information extraction from PDF documents are available but unused thus far."
- Why unresolved: The evaluation is limited to arXiv papers, which are heavily weighted toward STEM fields, leaving the generalizability of the results to other disciplines unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Performance benchmarks of the same tools on datasets from social sciences, humanities, and other academic disciplines would reveal whether the observed performance patterns hold across different document types and writing styles.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal approach for combining multiple information extraction tools to achieve better overall performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "Combining extraction approaches could lead to a one-fits-all extraction tool, which we consider desirable" and references ongoing work on this approach.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies this as a promising direction, it does not evaluate specific combination strategies or quantify the potential performance gains from such approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing different combination strategies (e.g., ensemble methods, hierarchical selection, confidence-based merging) and measuring their impact on extraction quality across various content elements would provide evidence of optimal approaches.

## Limitations
- The evaluation relies on a weakly-supervised dataset that may contain annotation errors affecting tool comparisons.
- The study focuses exclusively on arXiv documents, limiting generalizability to other academic domains.
- The benchmark does not evaluate tools on their ability to handle corrupted or malformed PDFs.

## Confidence
- High confidence: GROBID's superior performance on metadata and reference extraction is well-supported by experimental results.
- Medium confidence: Conclusions about table extraction difficulty are supported but may be influenced by DocBank's specific table detection algorithms.
- Low confidence: Claims about the superiority of multi-domain datasets over single-domain datasets lack direct experimental validation.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct ablation studies using single-domain subsets of DocBank to quantify the actual benefit of multi-domain training data for each tool.
2. Implement and evaluate combined approaches that route content elements to different specialized tools (e.g., using GROBID for metadata and CERMINE for tables) to test the hypothesis that tool combination improves overall performance.
3. Test tool robustness on intentionally corrupted or poorly formatted PDFs to assess real-world applicability beyond clean arXiv documents.
