# PEOPL: Characterizing Privately Encoded Open Datasets with Public Labels

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.00047
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00047
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: PEOPL achieves competitive prediction accuracy to raw-sample baselines and improved privacy over linear baselines while enabling collaborative learning across institutions

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of enabling organizations to share data for machine learning model training without unintended information leakage. The proposed method, Privately Encoded Open Datasets with Public Labels (PEOPL), uses randomly constructed transforms to encode sensitive data while publishing raw labels for ML training. The core contribution is a theoretical framework that quantifies privacy and utility using information-theoretic scores, motivating the use of random deep neural networks as encoders. The approach demonstrates that multiple institutions can collaborate using independent random encoders to achieve improved ML model performance while maintaining strong privacy guarantees.

## Method Summary
PEOPL employs randomly selected encoders from families of neural networks (CNNs for images, RNNs for text) to transform sensitive data before sharing. The method characterizes privacy using Shannon entropy metrics and utility using prediction accuracy on encoded data. The theoretical framework shows that composing families of random encoders can only preserve or increase privacy while maintaining utility. Data owners sample encoders from these families, apply them to their sensitive data, and publish the encoded data with raw labels. ML developers can then train classifiers on this encoded data, while adversaries face the challenge of identifying the specific encoder used from the published information.

## Key Results
- PEOPL achieves competitive prediction accuracy compared to raw-sample baselines across MIMIC-CXR, BBCNews, and SpamBase datasets
- The method demonstrates improved privacy over linear baseline encoders while maintaining utility
- Collaborative learning with independent random encoders from multiple institutions leads to improved ML model performance
- Theoretical results show that encoder composition preserves or increases privacy scores

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Random encoder composition increases privacy score while maintaining utility
- Mechanism: Theorem 2 shows that composing families of encoders F'∘F can only preserve or increase the privacy score compared to individual families. The composition creates a richer family of encoding functions that an adversary must distinguish between, increasing uncertainty about the true encoder.
- Core assumption: Each individual family F and F' has a well-defined probability distribution for selecting encoders, and the composition creates independent random choices
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section] with Theorem 2, corpus analysis showing related work on federated learning

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Random neural networks as encoders provide better privacy-utility tradeoff than linear baselines
- Mechanism: The paper implements random convolutional neural networks for images and random recurrent neural networks for text. These non-linear transforms create complex feature mappings that are harder to reverse-engineer while still preserving enough information for accurate classification
- Core assumption: The random initialization of neural networks creates sufficiently diverse and unpredictable transformations
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section] describing implementation, weak corpus evidence but related work exists

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Collaborative learning with independent random encoders improves utility without compromising privacy
- Mechanism: Proposition 3 shows that combining encoded data from multiple institutions with independent random encoders provides lower bound utility equal to individual encoding, and can theoretically increase utility if the classifiers for different institutions contain useful information for each other
- Core assumption: The independent random encoders from different institutions create complementary feature representations that can be jointly learned
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section] with Proposition 3, moderate corpus evidence on federated learning

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Information-theoretic privacy metrics (Shannon entropy, mutual information)
  - Why needed here: The paper uses Shannon entropy to quantify both privacy (uncertainty about the encoder) and utility (uncertainty about the labeling function). Understanding these metrics is crucial for evaluating the privacy-utility tradeoff
  - Quick check question: How does the privacy score Sprivacy(TA) = H[TA|OTA(XA),Ke] relate to the average uncertainty an adversary has about the true encoder?

- Concept: Randomization and probability distributions in privacy
  - Why needed here: The core mechanism relies on randomly selecting encoders from families of functions. Understanding how randomization provides privacy guarantees is essential for the scheme design
  - Quick check question: Why does composing two random families of encoders (F∘F') provide at least as much privacy as either individual family?

- Concept: Adversarial attack models and computational privacy
  - Why needed here: The paper evaluates privacy by considering optimal and sub-optimal attacks on the encoder. Understanding these attack models is crucial for assessing the scheme's practical security
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between the optimal attack (using the actual distribution P(T)) and the sub-optimal attack (using a mismatched distribution Q(T))?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data owner -> Random encoder TA from family F -> Encoded data OTA(XA) -> ML developer -> Trained classifier -> Adversary (attempts to identify TA using OTA(XA) and public dataset P)

- Critical path:
  1. Data owner samples encoder TA from family F
  2. Data owner encodes data: OTA(XA) = {(TA(x), L(x))}x∈XA
  3. Data owner publishes OTA(XA) with labels
  4. ML developer trains classifier on OTA(XA)
  5. Adversary attempts to identify TA using OTA(XA) and P

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Privacy vs utility: More complex encoders increase privacy but may reduce utility
  - Randomness vs determinism: Random encoders provide better privacy but make evaluation harder
  - Linear vs non-linear: Linear encoders are simpler but less private; non-linear provide better privacy but require more computation
  - Single vs multiple data owners: Multiple owners improve utility but increase coordination complexity

- Failure signatures:
  - Privacy failure: Adversary successfully identifies TA with high confidence (privacy score approaches 0)
  - Utility failure: Classifier trained on encoded data performs significantly worse than raw data baseline
  - Attack success: Validation MMD between TA(XA) and T_sub-opt(P) is low, indicating attacker approximated the encoder well
  - Communication failure: Encoder selection or data publication process breaks down between data owners and ML developer

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement linear encoder baseline and evaluate privacy-utility tradeoff on MIMIC-CXR dataset
  2. Implement random CNN encoder and compare privacy-utility performance against linear baseline
  3. Test collaborative learning with two data owners using independent random encoders and measure utility improvement

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the privacy guarantees of PEOPL be formalized and proven, or is it inherently limited to empirical evaluations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "our empirical results do not guarantee privacy against all possible attackers and PEOPL should not be used in real-world sensitive settings today."
- Why unresolved: The paper relies on information-theoretic scores and empirical comparisons against specific attacks, but does not provide formal privacy guarantees. This leaves open the question of whether the scheme can be rigorously proven to protect against all adversaries.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal proof showing that PEOPL achieves a certain level of privacy (e.g., differential privacy or another well-defined metric) against all possible adversaries, or a demonstration that such a proof is impossible.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of PEOPL scale with the size and complexity of the datasets, and are there practical limitations on the number of data-owners that can collaborate effectively?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates improved utility with combined encoded data from multiple data-owners, but does not explore scalability limits or performance degradation with larger datasets or more participants.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows benefits in a small-scale setting, it does not address how the scheme performs as the number of data-owners or the size of the datasets increases, which is crucial for real-world applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results showing the performance of PEOPL on larger datasets with more data-owners, and an analysis of the computational and communication costs as the system scales.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the specific conditions under which the assumption H[TA | X A, OTA(XA), Ke] = 0 holds, and how robust is the scheme when this assumption is violated?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "if the realization of encoder can be identified given un-ordered sets of original data and encoded data, the privacy score of the encoding scheme is equivalent to the average uncertainty of Eve about Alice's data."
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that this condition holds for their specific encoding scheme, but does not explore the robustness of the scheme when this condition is not met, such as when there is overlap between the public and private data.
- What evidence would resolve it: An analysis of the privacy guarantees of PEOPL when the assumption H[TA | X A, OTA(XA), Ke] = 0 is violated, and a demonstration of the scheme's performance in such scenarios.

## Limitations

- The paper does not provide formal privacy guarantees, relying instead on information-theoretic metrics and empirical evaluations against specific attack models
- The exact architecture of random CNN and RNN encoders is not specified, making faithful reproduction challenging
- Scalability to large-scale datasets and multiple collaborating institutions is not thoroughly evaluated

## Confidence

**High Confidence** - The theoretical foundations regarding information-theoretic privacy metrics and their application to encoder selection are well-established and clearly presented. The core claim that random encoder composition preserves or improves privacy is mathematically sound.

**Medium Confidence** - The empirical results showing competitive prediction accuracy with improved privacy over linear baselines are promising, but the evaluation is limited to specific datasets and attack models. The generalizability to other domains and more sophisticated attacks requires further validation.

**Low Confidence** - The claim about collaborative learning with independent random encoders improving utility is supported by theoretical propositions but lacks extensive empirical validation across diverse scenarios and dataset combinations.
