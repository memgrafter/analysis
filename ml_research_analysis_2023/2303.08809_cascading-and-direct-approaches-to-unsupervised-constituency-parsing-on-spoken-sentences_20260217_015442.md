# Cascading and Direct Approaches to Unsupervised Constituency Parsing on Spoken Sentences

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08809
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08809
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 0
- This paper presents the first study on unsupervised constituency parsing of spoken sentences using only raw speech and unpaired textual data.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces two approaches for unsupervised constituency parsing of spoken sentences: a cascading approach that combines unsupervised ASR with an unsupervised parser, and a direct approach that parses continuous speech representations without intermediate text. The study evaluates these methods on English and Korean datasets, demonstrating that training a parser on clean unpaired text and applying it to ASR transcripts outperforms training directly on ASR transcripts. The direct approach successfully learns language-specific branching direction without explicit inductive bias.

## Method Summary
The study compares two approaches for unsupervised constituency parsing of spoken sentences. The cascading approach uses an unsupervised ASR model (wav2vec-U) to generate transcripts, which are then parsed by an unsupervised parser (DIORA) trained on unpaired text. The direct approach segments speech into word-level units, aggregates self-supervised speech representations within these segments, and feeds the resulting embeddings directly to the parser. Both approaches use the DIORA framework with reconstruction loss for training, and evaluate performance using F1 scores with alignment between predicted and ground truth parse trees.

## Key Results
- Training a parser on clean unpaired text and applying it to ASR transcripts outperforms training the parser directly on ASR transcripts
- Accurate word segmentation alone may be sufficient for accurate parsing of spoken sentences
- The direct approach learns correct head-directionality (right-branching for English, left-branching for Korean) without explicit inductive bias

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Training an unsupervised parser on unpaired text and applying it directly to ASR transcripts outperforms training the parser on ASR transcripts.
- **Mechanism:** The unsupervised parser learns syntactic patterns from clean, error-free text. When applied to ASR transcripts, it leverages this learned structure to parse despite transcription errors. Training on ASR transcripts, which contain fewer word types and more out-of-vocabulary errors, degrades parser performance.
- **Core assumption:** The parser's syntactic generalization from clean text transfers effectively to ASR transcripts with errors.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "training a parser separately on unpaired text and applying it on ASR transcripts for inference produces better results for unsupervised parsing"
  - [section]: "by comparing rows (B)/(C) and rows (D)/(E), we see that training our parser on unpaired oracle text is consistently better than training on ASR transcripts"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence found in corpus; the mechanism is primarily derived from paper experiments.
- **Break condition:** If ASR error rates are extremely low (near-perfect transcription), the gap between training on text vs. ASR may diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Accurate word segmentation is sufficient for parsing spoken sentences accurately.
- **Mechanism:** When word boundaries are correctly identified, the parser can operate on continuous speech embeddings representing word segments. The syntactic structure can be inferred from these segment embeddings without needing intermediate text representation.
- **Core assumption:** Word-level segmentation accuracy directly correlates with parsing accuracy when using continuous speech embeddings.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "accurate segmentation alone may be sufficient to parse spoken sentences accurately"
  - [section]: "when provided with ground truth segmentation during testing...the parser trained on fixed length segments is able to achieve a performance similar to the parser trained on ground truth"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence found in corpus; the mechanism is primarily derived from paper experiments.
- **Break condition:** If segmentation errors are systematic (e.g., consistently merging content words with function words), parsing accuracy may still degrade.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The direct parsing approach can learn language-specific branching direction (head-initial vs. head-final) without explicit inductive bias.
- **Mechanism:** The parser trained on continuous speech embeddings of word segments naturally converges to producing parse trees that match the syntactic properties of the language (right-branching for English, left-branching for Korean) based on patterns in the input data.
- **Core assumption:** The input speech patterns contain sufficient information about syntactic structure to induce language-specific branching without explicit architectural bias.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "our direct approach may learn head-directionality correctly for both head-initial and head-final languages without any explicit inductive bias"
  - [section]: "we observe that our models tend to converge to producing right-branching trees on SpokenCOCO...we empirically verify this claim by conducting experiments on Korean"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence found in corpus; the mechanism is primarily derived from paper experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the language has mixed branching patterns or the speech corpus is too small to capture sufficient syntactic diversity, the model may fail to learn correct branching direction.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** XLSR-53 feature extractor -> Segmentation module -> Segment embedding generator -> DIORA parser -> Evaluation module
- **Critical path:** Speech → Segmentation → Segment Embeddings → Parser → Parse Tree
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Segmentation accuracy vs. parsing accuracy: More accurate segmentation improves parsing but may require more complex segmentation models
  - Fixed-length segmentation vs. learned segmentation: Fixed-length is simpler but less accurate; learned segmentation requires additional model complexity
  - Cascading vs. direct approach: Cascading leverages existing ASR but introduces error propagation; direct approach is end-to-end but requires accurate segmentation
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low parsing F1 scores despite high segmentation accuracy may indicate the parser isn't learning syntactic patterns effectively
  - High parsing F1 scores with poor segmentation accuracy may indicate the parser is overfitting to segment boundaries rather than learning syntax
  - Systematic branching errors (e.g., always right-branching for a head-final language) may indicate insufficient language diversity in training data
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Compare parsing performance using oracle segmentation vs. fixed-length segmentation on the same speech corpus to establish the segmentation accuracy threshold
  2. Train the parser on unpaired text and evaluate on ASR transcripts vs. training directly on ASR transcripts to confirm the cascading advantage
  3. Test the parser on a language with different branching direction (e.g., Korean vs. English) to verify language-specific syntactic learning

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does unsupervised spoken constituency parsing performance compare across different language families with varying syntactic structures?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that English (head-initial) and Korean (head-final) produce different branching patterns, but only compares these two languages. The authors suggest exploring whether their approach works across diverse languages.
- Why unresolved: The study only examined English and Korean, which represent different head-directionality types but share some syntactic similarities as both are subject-object-verb languages. Testing on languages from different families (e.g., Arabic, Japanese, Swahili) would provide more comprehensive insights.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing parsing performance across multiple language families with varying word orders, morphological complexity, and prosodic features would reveal whether the approach generalizes beyond head-initial and head-final languages.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relative contribution of acoustic-prosodic features versus lexical information to unsupervised spoken parsing accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that prosodic features have been shown beneficial for parsing in supervised settings, and their direct approach uses speech representations, but doesn't isolate the contribution of prosody versus lexical content.
- Why unresolved: The direct approach uses self-supervised speech representations that may encode both lexical and prosodic information, but the paper doesn't analyze which component drives performance improvements. The cascading approach also uses ASR transcripts without prosodic cues.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments that separately evaluate systems using only prosodic features, only lexical information, and combined features would quantify their individual and joint contributions to parsing accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can joint optimization of word segmentation and parsing improve performance compared to the two-stage approach used in the direct method?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state "For future work, we expect to extend our system to end-to-end training to jointly optimize word segmentation and parsing."
- Why unresolved: The current direct approach uses fixed segmentation (either 0.5-second intervals or ASR-derived boundaries) before parsing, treating these as separate problems rather than optimizing them jointly.
- What evidence would resolve it: An end-to-end model that simultaneously learns segmentation and parsing through a unified objective function, compared against the two-stage baseline, would demonstrate whether joint optimization provides performance gains.

## Limitations

- The study only evaluates on read speech (SpokenCOCO and Zeroth-Korean), not spontaneous conversational speech with disfluencies
- The cascading approach relies on wav2vec-U for unsupervised ASR, which may not generalize to domains with different acoustic characteristics
- The direct approach assumes word segmentation accuracy alone determines parsing quality, potentially oversimplifying the relationship between segmentation and syntactic parsing

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The finding that training an unsupervised parser on clean unpaired text and applying it to ASR transcripts outperforms training the parser directly on ASR transcripts.

**Medium Confidence:** The claim that accurate segmentation alone is sufficient for parsing spoken sentences accurately.

**Medium Confidence:** The assertion that the direct approach learns correct head-directionality without explicit inductive bias.

## Next Checks

1. **Domain Transfer Validation:** Test both cascading and direct approaches on spontaneous conversational speech (e.g., Switchboard or Fisher corpora) to assess robustness to disfluencies and natural speech patterns.

2. **Segmentation Error Analysis:** Conduct a systematic analysis of how different types of segmentation errors (insertion, deletion, boundary shifts) specifically impact parsing performance in the direct approach, moving beyond aggregate accuracy metrics.

3. **Multi-Language Generalization:** Evaluate the direct approach on a typologically diverse set of languages (e.g., Japanese, Turkish, Hindi) to verify that head-directionality learning generalizes beyond the head-initial/head-final binary demonstrated in English and Korean.
