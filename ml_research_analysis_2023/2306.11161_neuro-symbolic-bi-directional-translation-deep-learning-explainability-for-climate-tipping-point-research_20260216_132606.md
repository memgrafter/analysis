# Neuro-Symbolic Bi-Directional Translation -- Deep Learning Explainability for Climate Tipping Point Research

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.11161
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11161
- Reference count: 7
- One-line primary result: Near-perfect QTQ/QTP translation accuracy and 61.97% normalized Levenshtein distance for PTQ in AMOC collapse detection

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces NS-QAPT, a neuro-symbolic bidirectional translation method that converts natural language questions into executable programs and vice versa, aimed at improving explainability in deep learning climate simulations. Applied to AMOC collapse detection, NS-QAPT links climate scientists with deep learning models via a domain-specific language and executable programs. The model architecture combines question-to-question autoencoding with question-to-program and program-to-question translation, trained jointly using cross-entropy and length-difference losses. Evaluated on a custom AMOC dataset, the method achieves near-perfect translation from questions to programs, and 61.97% normalized Levenshtein distance from programs to questions, indicating strong bidirectional interpretability potential.

## Method Summary
NS-QAPT is a triangular model architecture combining a question-to-question auto-encoder with question-to-program and program-to-question translation modules, all sharing the same latent space and token embeddings. It is trained jointly on a custom AMOC dataset using cross-entropy loss for sequence prediction and a length-difference term to control output length. The system aims to improve explainability of deep learning models in climate science by providing a bridge between natural language queries and executable simulation programs.

## Key Results
- QTQ translation accuracy: near-perfect
- QTP translation accuracy: near-perfect
- PTQ translation accuracy: 61.97% normalized Levenshtein distance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: NS-QAPT achieves high bidirectional translation accuracy by sharing latent space embeddings and using joint training with cross-entropy and length-difference losses.
- Mechanism: The model encodes both natural language questions and executable programs into a shared latent space via bidirectional GRUs, then decodes back to either modality. Joint optimization aligns the two representations, while the length-difference term encourages similar sequence lengths, reducing trivial over-generation.
- Core assumption: Questions and programs are semantically aligned and can be mapped into a shared embedding space without loss of meaning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "model architecture combines question-to-question autoencoding with question-to-program and program-to-question translation, trained jointly using cross-entropy and length-difference losses."
  - [section] "All parts share the same latent space and token embedding, and are optimized jointly during training."
- Break condition: If semantic alignment is poor (e.g., due to ambiguity in natural language or lack of program diversity), the shared latent space cannot capture all nuances, degrading translation quality.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The triangular architecture (QTQ auto-encoder + QTP + PTQ) stabilizes learning by providing reconstruction supervision and bidirectional feedback.
- Mechanism: The QTQ auto-encoder ensures the question encoder produces stable embeddings, while QTP and PTQ decoders learn conditional generation in opposite directions. The shared embedding space forces consistency across all three paths, and the loss function balances reconstruction and translation fidelity.
- Core assumption: Bidirectional reconstruction (QTQ and PTQ) provides sufficient supervision to guide the shared encoder-decoder training toward coherent cross-modal mappings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The bidirectional question-to-program translation is accomplished via a triangular shaped system of model architectures as seen in Figure 2."
  - [section] "All encoders and decoders share a latent space."
- Break condition: If one path (e.g., PTQ) dominates the loss, the shared encoder may overfit to that direction, harming performance on the other.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Domain-specific language (DSL) design and program templates reduce ambiguity and improve translation fidelity.
- Mechanism: The DSL constrains questions to specific forms with placeholders, and each form maps to a unique program structure. This reduces semantic ambiguity, making it easier for the model to learn one-to-one mappings. Templates also enable systematic dataset generation with controlled variations.
- Core assumption: The DSL is expressive enough to capture the domain's questions while remaining restrictive enough to avoid ambiguity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We generated a custom set of AMOC-collapse questions and their corresponding programs to further evaluate our model. Our approach is to define question 'forms' in which words and numerical values may be inserted to create valid questions answerable by a set of implemented programs."
  - [section] "Each question has a discrete set of parameters from which question, program pairs may be generated."
- Break condition: If the DSL is too restrictive or missing important linguistic variations, the model will fail to generalize to real-world natural language inputs.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Cross-entropy loss in sequence-to-sequence models
  - Why needed here: Measures token-level prediction accuracy for both question and program sequences, enabling gradient-based optimization of the translation mappings.
  - Quick check question: In NS-QAPT, why is cross-entropy applied separately to QTQ, QTP, and PTQ outputs?
- Concept: Bidirectional GRUs for sequence encoding
  - Why needed here: Captures context from both directions, improving representation of variable-length questions and programs before decoding.
  - Quick check question: How does sharing the same latent space between question and program encoders help bidirectional translation?
- Concept: Normalized Levenshtein distance for evaluation
  - Why needed here: Provides a symmetric, scale-invariant measure of translation quality, useful for comparing performance across different sequence lengths and modalities.
  - Quick check question: Why might normalized Levenshtein distance not reflect semantic correctness?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Token embedding layer (size 512) -> Learned positional embedding (size 128) -> Question encoder: 2-layer bidirectional GRU (hidden=512) -> Program encoder: 2-layer bidirectional GRU (hidden=512) -> Shared latent space (size 512) -> Question decoder: 1-layer GRU (hidden=1024) -> Linear(512) -> LeakyReLU -> Linear(253) -> Program decoder: same as question decoder

- Critical path:
  1. Input question/program -> token embedding + positional embedding
  2. Shared encoder -> latent representation
  3. Shared decoder -> predicted sequence
  4. Loss computation -> gradient update

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Shared latent space vs. separate spaces: sharing enforces consistency but may limit expressiveness if modalities differ greatly.
  - Length-difference term: helps control sequence length but may conflict with semantic accuracy.
  - Token-based VALUE placeholders vs. numeric embedding: simpler to implement but less precise for continuous values.

- Failure signatures:
  - High QTQ accuracy but low PTQ accuracy: decoder overfits to reconstruction, fails to generalize to generation.
  - High QTP accuracy but low PTQ accuracy: asymmetry in training data or decoder capacity.
  - All metrics moderate: latent space not discriminative enough; consider deeper encoders or attention.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train NS-QAPT on CLEVR dataset only (validation of baseline architecture).
  2. Train on balanced AMOC dataset with equal question types (check for bias in program diversity).
  3. Train on unbalanced dataset (many variants of one question form) to observe impact on PTQ performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the PTQ translation accuracy be improved for questions that generate a small number of unique programs, such as questions 8-10 in the AMOC dataset?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that PTQ performance drops for questions 8-10, which constitute over 99% of the dataset, due to a lack of variety in the program set.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the issue but does not propose or test solutions to increase program variety or improve PTQ accuracy for these questions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing methods to increase program variety, such as introducing more diverse question forms or using data augmentation techniques, and measuring their impact on PTQ accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the semantic correctness of NS-QAPT's translations compare to its syntactic correctness as measured by normalized Levenshtein distance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that normalized Levenshtein distance does not capture correctness of meaning between predictions and ground truth, and provides an example of a semantically incorrect translation with high Levenshtein distance.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not evaluate or compare the semantic correctness of translations to their syntactic correctness.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study comparing human evaluations of semantic correctness to normalized Levenshtein distance scores for a sample of NS-QAPT translations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can NS-QAPT be extended to handle one-to-many translations from programs to questions, where a single program can correspond to multiple valid questions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that future work will explore semantic methods to support one-to-many translations from programs to question.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not propose or test any methods for handling one-to-many translations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing different approaches for generating multiple valid questions from a single program, such as using semantic similarity metrics or reinforcement learning, and evaluating their effectiveness.

## Limitations
- Limited program diversity in the AMOC dataset leads to potential overfitting and poor PTQ performance for questions mapping to similar programs.
- Normalized Levenshtein distance may not fully capture semantic correctness, potentially masking meaningful errors.
- Exact question forms and program templates used for dataset generation are not fully specified, limiting reproducibility.

## Confidence

- Medium confidence in the DSL-based approach to reducing ambiguity (Mechanism 3)
- Medium confidence in the translation performance metrics, given the dataset limitations
