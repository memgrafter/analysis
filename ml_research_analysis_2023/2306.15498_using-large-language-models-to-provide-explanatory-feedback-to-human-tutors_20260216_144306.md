# Using Large Language Models to Provide Explanatory Feedback to Human Tutors

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.15498
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15498
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: F1 scores of 0.811 (effort-based) and 0.350 (outcome-based) praise classification

## Executive Summary
This work-in-progress paper presents a method to provide real-time explanatory feedback to novice tutors using large language models (LLMs) within an online lesson on effective praise. The system employs a binary classification approach to label tutor responses as effective (effort-based) or ineffective (outcome-based) praise, achieving F1 scores of 0.811 and 0.350 respectively. Additionally, named entity recognition (NER) is used to identify relevant components of tutor responses, enabling the generation of templated feedback. The results demonstrate the potential of LLMs for enhancing automatic short answer grading and providing timely, specific feedback to novice tutors.

## Method Summary
The study uses a BERT-based model for binary classification of tutor responses into effort-based or outcome-based praise categories, with a 70:10:20 train-validation-test split. The same BERT architecture is applied to NER tasks using BIO-tagging to identify praise-related text segments. These tags are then mapped to predefined feedback templates that explain why one praise type is more effective than the other. The system processes tutor input through classifier → NER → template → feedback pipeline.

## Key Results
- BERT model achieved F1 score of 0.811 for effort-based praise classification
- BERT model achieved F1 score of 0.350 for outcome-based praise classification
- NER successfully identified and tagged praise segments for template-based feedback generation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Named Entity Recognition (NER) can identify specific praise types (effort vs. outcome) within tutor responses, enabling generation of targeted explanatory feedback.
- Mechanism: The NER model tags segments of tutor text using BIO-tagging, which are then mapped to templates that explain why one praise type is more effective than the other.
- Core assumption: Tutor responses contain identifiable, non-overlapping text segments corresponding to distinct praise types that can be consistently tagged.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Using NER, segments of tutor responses can be systematically identified aligning with the appropriate NEs." [section] "Case 1 showed that the model could accurately identify the location of the praise in the text (i.e., the text highlighted in blue) and predict the accurate entity type (i.e., Effort)."
- Break condition: If tutor responses mix praise types in ways that prevent clear segment isolation, or if new praise expressions fall outside the training set, NER accuracy degrades.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: BERT-based binary classification can accurately distinguish effort-based from outcome-based praise in tutor responses.
- Mechanism: The model is trained on labeled tutor responses and learns to classify text as containing effort-based praise (high F1=0.811) or outcome-based praise (lower F1=0.350).
- Core assumption: The textual features of effort-based and outcome-based praise are distinct enough for BERT to learn reliable decision boundaries.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "The BERT model could effectively tag Effort, which could further help the provision of corrective feedback to inform the novice tutors on providing effort-based praise." [abstract] "This work-in-progress demonstrates considerable accuracy in binary classification for corrective feedback of effective, or effort-based (F1 score = 0.811), and ineffective, or outcome-based (F1 score = 0.350), praise responses."
- Break condition: If the dataset lacks sufficient outcome-based praise examples, classification performance drops sharply, as seen with the high variance in outcome F1.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Templated feedback using NER-identified entities improves tutor learning by providing specific, contextual explanations.
- Mechanism: Once NER tags are extracted, they are inserted into predefined feedback templates to give immediate, explanatory feedback during lesson interaction.
- Core assumption: Tutors benefit more from feedback that references their exact wording rather than generic advice.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "By tagging pieces of information, aligning with tutoring approaches for responding to the given scenarios, NER becomes a suitable method for generating templated feedback to tutors." [corpus] Weak: No direct citation of learning gains from templated feedback in this corpus; inferred from feedback literature.
- Break condition: If templates are too rigid or do not cover the variety of tutor responses, feedback may feel irrelevant or unhelpful.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input text → Text preprocessor → BERT classifier → BERT NER → Template engine → Explanatory feedback
- Critical path: Response → Classifier → NER → Template → Feedback
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using BERT for both classification and NER increases model size but simplifies deployment.
  - Templates offer interpretability but limit feedback flexibility.
  - Low-resource NER data may force reliance on data augmentation or AUC maximization techniques.
- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in classification F1 scores → possible class imbalance or insufficient training data.
  - NER returns "O" for all tokens → model not trained or domain mismatch.
  - Feedback templates produce nonsensical output → NER tagging errors or template logic bugs.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test BERT classifier on a balanced subset of effort/outcome responses to confirm F1 scores match reported values.
  2. Run NER on a small hand-annotated test set to verify BIO-tagging accuracy and identify common failure modes.
  3. Validate template engine by feeding it NER outputs from step 2 and checking if generated feedback is coherent and contextually appropriate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal threshold for determining when to provide hedged feedback responses versus confident feedback in the NER-based explanatory feedback system?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that when model confidence is below a certain threshold, hedged responses should be provided, but does not specify what that threshold should be.
- Why unresolved: Determining the optimal confidence threshold requires empirical testing with actual users to balance between providing helpful feedback and maintaining system credibility.
- What evidence would resolve it: User studies comparing different confidence thresholds and their effects on tutor learning outcomes and system trust.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the F1 score evaluation metric be modified to better account for partial correctness and true negative predictions in NER tasks for educational feedback?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that traditional F1 score doesn't fully account for partial correctness and true negative predictions, which are important for educational feedback.
- Why unresolved: The standard F1 score treats all incorrect predictions equally, but in educational contexts, some errors (like partial matches) may still provide useful feedback.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of alternative evaluation metrics that incorporate partial credit and true negative detection.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What data augmentation techniques are most effective for improving NER model performance on low-resource educational datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies data augmentation as a key future direction but doesn't specify which techniques would work best.
- Why unresolved: Different augmentation methods (oversampling, synonym replacement, ChatGPT-generated instances) may have varying effectiveness depending on the domain and task.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies testing multiple augmentation approaches on the same educational dataset.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can AUC maximization approaches be effectively applied to handle imbalanced class distributions in educational NER tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions AUC maximization as a potential solution but doesn't explore its application to this specific context.
- Why unresolved: The high class imbalance (76.5% O-tag) in the dataset presents unique challenges that haven't been addressed with existing AUC maximization techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation and evaluation of AUC maximization methods specifically adapted for educational NER tasks.

## Limitations
- Small dataset size (129 responses) may not generalize well across diverse tutoring scenarios
- Substantial performance gap between effort-based (F1=0.811) and outcome-based praise classification (F1=0.350) suggests potential class imbalance
- Templated feedback approach may lack flexibility to handle nuanced or mixed praise expressions in real-world tutoring contexts

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: BERT's effectiveness for effort-based praise classification (F1=0.811) is well-supported by the reported metrics and literature precedent.
- **Medium confidence**: NER's ability to identify praise segments is demonstrated but limited by the small dataset and potential domain specificity.
- **Low confidence**: The claim that templated feedback improves tutor learning is inferred from feedback literature rather than directly measured in this study.
