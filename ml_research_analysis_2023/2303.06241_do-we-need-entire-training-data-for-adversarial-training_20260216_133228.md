# Do we need entire training data for adversarial training?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.06241
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06241
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves 3.52x speedup on MNIST and 1.98x on CIFAR-10 with comparable robust accuracy by training only on adversarially-prone subset

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a method to significantly reduce adversarial training time by filtering out only the most adversarially-prone samples from the training data. The authors use simple perturbations to identify samples near the decision boundary, then perform adversarial training exclusively on this subset while maintaining vanilla training on the full dataset. The approach achieves substantial speedups (3.52x on MNIST, 1.98x on CIFAR-10) with minimal loss in robust accuracy, and even demonstrates 1.2x speedup on ImageNet with Free adversarial training.

## Method Summary
The method works by first performing a simple adversarial attack on all training examples to identify adversarially-prone samples - those whose predictions change under small perturbations. These prone samples are then used exclusively for adversarial training, while vanilla training continues on the full dataset in a 2:1 ratio (vanilla:adversarial). Interval analysis is used to efficiently estimate prediction ranges without full attack computation, and the combined training approach preserves robustness while dramatically reducing computation time.

## Key Results
- Achieves 3.52x training speedup on MNIST with comparable robust accuracy
- Achieves 1.98x speedup on CIFAR-10 with minimal robust accuracy drop
- Achieves 1.2x speedup on ImageNet using Free adversarial training with marginal accuracy loss
- Identifies 30-40% of training samples as adversarially-prone across datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Adversarially-prone samples contain most "hard examples" needed for robust training
- Core assumption: Samples resisting simple perturbations are already robust and don't need adversarial augmentation
- Evidence: [abstract] "We perform a simple adversarial attack on all training examples to filter this subset"
- Break condition: If decision boundary shifts significantly during training, prone subset becomes stale

### Mechanism 2
- Combining adversarial training on subset with vanilla training on full dataset preserves efficiency and robustness
- Core assumption: Vanilla:adversarial ratio can be tuned without sacrificing robustness
- Evidence: [section] "A ratio of 2 gives the best tradeoff between robust accuracy and training time"
- Break condition: Skewed ratios cause loss of robustness or efficiency gains

### Mechanism 3
- Interval-based prediction range analysis approximates adversarial susceptibility without full attack computation
- Core assumption: Output range extremes can be captured with random samples within input interval
- Evidence: [section] "We use the concept of interval analysis... to find a prediction range for each training sample"
- Break condition: Highly non-linear activations make interval bounds too loose

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data loader → Interval perturbation module → Model forward pass → Range analysis → Subset filter → Training loop (vanilla/adversarial mix)
- Critical path:
  1. Load batch
  2. Generate perturbations for interval bounds
  3. Forward pass to compute prediction ranges
  4. Mark samples as prone if class prediction changes
  5. Alternate between vanilla and adversarial updates
- Design tradeoffs:
  - More perturbation types → better prone subset but slower filtering
  - Higher vanilla:adversarial ratio → faster training but lower robustness
  - Interval size → coarser bounds but faster computation
- Failure signatures:
  - Robust accuracy drops sharply → prone subset too small or stale
  - Training time not reduced → filtering overhead dominates
  - Model diverges → adversarial perturbations too strong relative to interval size
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run filtering on MNIST with interval length 60, verify subset size ~30-40%
  2. Train with 2:1 vanilla:adversarial ratio, measure speedup and accuracy drop
  3. Compare full adversarial training vs filtered approach on CIFAR-10, record time and robust accuracy

## Limitations
- Effectiveness depends heavily on interval length parameter, not extensively validated across datasets
- 2:1 vanilla:adversarial ratio empirically determined but may not generalize to all architectures
- Interval analysis assumes monotonicity in certain layers, which may not hold for all activation functions

## Confidence

- High confidence: Speedup claims on MNIST and CIFAR-10 well-supported with specific numbers (3.52x and 1.98x)
- Medium confidence: ImageNet results show marginal drops but speedup less impressive (1.2x)
- Low confidence: Claim that prone samples "contain most hard examples" lacks rigorous justification
