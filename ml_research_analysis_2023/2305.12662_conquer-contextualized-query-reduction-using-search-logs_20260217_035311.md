# ConQueR: Contextualized Query Reduction using Search Logs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12662
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12662
- Reference count: 38
- Key outcome: Achieves up to 8.45% gains in exact match scores and 5.54% in accuracy over best competing model on real-world search logs

## Executive Summary
ConQueR introduces a contextualized query reduction model that addresses the linguistic chasm in ad-hoc retrieval by removing extraneous terms while preserving user intent. The model combines two complementary approaches: core term extraction that identifies essential terms at the term level using contextual embeddings, and sub-query selection that evaluates semantic coherence between original and candidate sub-queries at the sequence level. These methods are aggregated using a weighted sum and optimized through robust training with truncated loss to handle noisy search logs.

## Method Summary
ConQueR uses a pre-trained language model (ELECTRA-base) with two distinct branches: one for core term extraction at the term level and another for sub-query selection at the sequence level. The core term extraction branch scores each term's importance using contextual embeddings, while the sub-query selection branch uses a cross-encoder to evaluate coherence between the original query and candidate sub-queries. The model is trained on real-world search logs using truncated loss to handle noise, with the two approaches aggregated via weighted summation and optimized using greedy search for final reduction selection.

## Key Results
- Achieves up to 8.45% gains in exact match scores over best competing model
- Improves accuracy by 5.54% on real-world search logs
- User study shows 35% of users find ConQueR's reductions more appropriate than other methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Core term extraction removes extraneous terms at the term level using contextual embeddings from a PLM
- Mechanism: Each term in the query is scored independently using the hidden representation from a transformer encoder, and terms below a threshold are removed
- Core assumption: Contextual embeddings from the transformer encoder can accurately distinguish between essential and extraneous terms for a given query
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "One extracts core terms from an original query at the term level, and the other determines whether a sub-query is a suitable reduction for the original query at the sequence level."
  - [section 2.1] "We evaluate whether each query term is important or not at a term level. That is, each hidden vector h_i is projected into term importance score ŷ_i."
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct experimental comparison with term-level baselines; inference relies on reported F1 gains over GRU

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sub-query selection evaluates semantic coherence between original query and candidate sub-query at the sequence level using a cross-encoder
- Mechanism: The transformer encoder processes the original query and a candidate sub-query as a pair, producing a single coherence score that indicates whether the sub-query sufficiently captures the original intent
- Core assumption: The cross-encoder can learn the semantic equivalence between full query and reduced query pairs from search logs
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The other determines whether a sub-query is a suitable reduction for the original query at the sequence level."
  - [section 2.2] "It determines whether the given sub-query is suitably reducing the original query. The coherence of two queries is evaluated by the cross-encoder mechanism."
  - [corpus] Weak: No ablation showing sequence-level coherence alone is sufficient; reported gains rely on aggregation with term-level method

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Aggregating term-level and sequence-level scores exploits complementary strengths, yielding better reductions than either method alone
- Mechanism: The core term extraction probability score is averaged across terms in the candidate sub-query and combined with the cross-encoder score via a weighted sum, then greedy search selects the best sub-query
- Core assumption: The two methods capture orthogonal aspects of query reduction (local term importance vs. global semantic coherence), so their combination improves overall accuracy
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Since they operate at different levels of granularity and complement each other, they are finally aggregated in an ensemble manner."
  - [section 2.3] "Since the two methods capture contextual information at multifaceted levels, i.e., term and sequence levels, they complement each other."
  - [corpus] Strong: ConQueRagg outperforms ConQueRcore and ConQueRsub individually by 8.45% EM gain, and user study shows 35% positive response ratio

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs) and contextual embeddings
  - Why needed here: PLMs provide rich, context-aware representations that enable both term-level importance scoring and sequence-level coherence evaluation
  - Quick check question: Why is a PLM preferred over a static embedding model for query reduction?

- Concept: Cross-encoder architecture for sequence pair scoring
  - Why needed here: The cross-encoder allows all-to-all interactions between terms of the original query and candidate sub-query, capturing semantic equivalence better than independent encodings
  - Quick check question: How does the cross-encoder differ from a bi-encoder in handling query-sub-query pairs?

- Concept: Negative log-likelihood and truncated loss for robust training
  - Why needed here: Search logs contain noise; truncated loss removes high-loss (likely noisy) samples during training to improve generalization
  - Quick check question: What is the purpose of truncating large-loss samples in the training set?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Tokenization -> Core term extraction branch -> Sub-query selection branch -> Aggregation layer -> Greedy search
- Critical path:
  1. Tokenize original query and candidate sub-query
  2. Pass through respective PLM branches
  3. Generate scores from both branches
  4. Aggregate scores and select best sub-query
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Term-level vs. sequence-level granularity: term-level is precise but may break phrase structure; sequence-level preserves coherence but may under-reduce
  - Full enumeration vs. greedy search: enumeration is exact but exponential; greedy is efficient but may miss optimal reductions
  - Fixed α vs. learned aggregation: fixed is simpler and stable; learned could adapt but risks overfitting
- Failure signatures:
  - Low recall: aggregation overweights sequence-level coherence and fails to remove enough terms
  - Low precision: aggregation overweights term-level scores and removes contextually necessary terms
  - Slow inference: greedy search still too expensive; consider beam search or limiting candidate generation
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation: Train ConQueR with α=0 (only core term extraction) and α=1 (only sub-query selection) to confirm complementary gains
  2. Robustness test: Compare training with and without truncated loss on noisy vs. clean subsets of logs
  3. Scalability check: Measure inference latency for greedy search vs. full enumeration on queries of varying length

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does ConQueR perform when evaluated on non-Korean query logs or cross-lingual query reduction tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The current evaluation uses only Korean queries from a commercial search engine
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore cross-lingual performance or generalization to other languages
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluation on multilingual query logs showing whether the model transfers to other languages or requires language-specific adaptation

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the computational efficiency trade-off between ConQueR's greedy search and exhaustive enumeration of all possible sub-queries?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using greedy search instead of exhaustive enumeration due to complexity concerns
- Why unresolved: No quantitative comparison of search efficiency or quality trade-offs between the two approaches is provided
- What evidence would resolve it: Runtime comparisons and accuracy measurements comparing greedy search vs. exact search on subsets of the data

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does ConQueR handle queries where the optimal reduction requires preserving multi-word expressions or phrases that should not be split?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions challenges with terms that "only make sense when they exist together" but doesn't test specialized phrase handling
- Why unresolved: No specific evaluation or ablation study on multi-word expression preservation
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments with queries containing known multi-word expressions to test whether the model preserves them appropriately

## Limitations

- Limited to Korean queries from a single commercial search engine, raising questions about cross-lingual and cross-domain transferability
- Exact method for generating negative samples in sub-query selection is underspecified, which may affect reproducibility
- Aggregation weight α is tuned rather than learned, potentially limiting adaptability to different query distributions

## Confidence

**High confidence:** The core mechanisms of term-level core extraction and sequence-level sub-query selection are well-specified and logically sound. The empirical results showing 8.45% EM improvement and 35% user study positive response provide strong evidence for the approach's effectiveness.

**Medium confidence:** The truncated loss training strategy's effectiveness is supported by reported results, but the sensitivity to hyperparameters (𝜖𝑚𝑎𝑥, 𝜖𝑁, 𝛾) and the exact implementation details for negative sampling in sub-query selection remain unclear. These factors could significantly impact real-world performance.

**Low confidence:** Claims about the complementary nature of the two approaches are supported by ablation results, but the paper doesn't explore whether learned aggregation weights or alternative ensembling methods might yield better performance. The analysis of failure modes is limited to qualitative examples rather than systematic error analysis.
