# Analysis and Evaluation of Explainable Artificial Intelligence on Suicide Risk Assessment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.06052
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06052
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: ML models with data augmentation achieve 95.23% accuracy and 0.95 AUC in suicide risk prediction

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the effectiveness of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques in predicting suicide risks and identifying dominant causes. Using data augmentation and machine learning models, including Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost, the study achieves a prediction accuracy of 95.23% and an Area Under Curve (AUC) of 0.95. SHAP and correlation analysis identify anger problems, depression, and social isolation as leading predictors of suicide risk. The findings demonstrate the potential of ML and XAI frameworks to assist psychiatrists in understanding complex behaviors and enhancing clinical decision-making.

## Method Summary
The study uses a suicide risk dataset with 22 variables and approximately 1K records, augmented to 50,000 samples using Scikit-learn data augmentation. Seven machine learning models (Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, Logistic Regression, linear SVC, and Perceptron) are trained and evaluated. SHAP values are computed for feature importance analysis. Performance metrics include accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC.

## Key Results
- Achieved 95.23% prediction accuracy and 0.95 AUC using augmented data and tree-based models
- SHAP analysis identifies anger problems, depression, and social isolation as top suicide risk predictors
- Random Forest and XGBoost outperform other models, with RF achieving AUC of 0.947

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Data augmentation with Scikit-learn enables robust prediction despite small original dataset size
- Mechanism: Augmented dataset increases training samples from ~1K to 50K while preserving feature distributions, reducing overfitting
- Core assumption: Synthetic data preserves statistical properties matching original data
- Evidence anchors: Table 3 shows Scikit-learn augmentation preserves original data statistics; abstract reports 95.23% accuracy

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: SHAP values provide reliable feature importance rankings for clinical interpretation
- Mechanism: SHAP decomposes predictions into additive feature contributions, revealing key mental health factors
- Core assumption: SHAP's Shapley value framework fairly distributes credit among features
- Evidence anchors: SHAP identifies anger problems, depression, social isolation as leading predictors; psychiatric hospitalization and occupation also crucial

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Tree-based models perform well due to natural suitability for mental health variable relationships
- Mechanism: Tree models capture nonlinear relationships and interactions without explicit feature engineering
- Core assumption: Relationship between predictors and suicide risk is sufficiently complex for tree models
- Evidence anchors: Experimental results show DT, RF, and XGBoost achieve best results; RF achieves AUC of 0.947

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data pipeline → augmentation → model training → SHAP analysis → clinical interface
  - Key components: Scikit-learn augmenter, XGBoost classifier, SHAP explainer, visualization tools

- Critical path:
  1. Load and preprocess original dataset
  2. Apply Scikit-learn augmentation to reach 50K samples
  3. Train DT, RF, and XGBoost models with cross-validation
  4. Generate SHAP values for feature importance
  5. Visualize results for clinical review

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Augmentation size vs. distribution fidelity
  - Model complexity vs. interpretability needs
  - SHAP computation time vs. real-time application requirements

- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in cross-validation → overfitting or insufficient augmentation
  - SHAP values close to zero for all features → model not learning meaningful patterns
  - Performance gap between training and test sets → data leakage or augmentation artifacts

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train models on original 1K dataset without augmentation to establish baseline performance
  2. Compare CTGAN vs Scikit-learn augmentation by measuring feature distribution preservation
  3. Test SHAP stability by computing values on multiple bootstrap samples of the augmented data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal sample size for training ML models on suicide risk prediction datasets?
- Basis: Paper discusses using 50K samples as compromise but doesn't determine optimal size
- Why unresolved: No experiments conducted with varying sample sizes to identify optimal training size
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with different sample sizes comparing ML model performance

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different ML models vary when using different feature selection methods?
- Basis: Paper uses SHAP and correlation analysis but doesn't compare model performance across feature selection methods
- Why unresolved: Focuses on overall performance rather than method-specific impacts of feature selection
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different feature selection methods across ML models

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can ML model explainability be further improved for clinical decision-making?
- Basis: Discusses SHAP for interpretability but doesn't propose comprehensive framework for clinical settings
- Why unresolved: Highlights importance without providing specific implementation framework
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of comprehensive XAI framework tailored for clinical suicide risk assessment

## Limitations
- Reliance on data augmentation introduces uncertainty about preservation of complex mental health variable relationships
- SHAP explanations may not fully capture nonlinear interactions in high-risk clinical scenarios
- No clinical validation with real-world patient outcomes or psychiatrist review

## Confidence
- Prediction performance (95.23% accuracy, 0.95 AUC): High - well-supported by standard ML evaluation metrics
- Feature importance rankings via SHAP: Medium - reasonable but requires clinical validation
- Clinical utility claims: Low - theoretical framework not tested with actual psychiatrists or patients
