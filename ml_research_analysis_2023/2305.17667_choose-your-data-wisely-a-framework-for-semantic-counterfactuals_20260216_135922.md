# Choose your Data Wisely: A Framework for Semantic Counterfactuals

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.17667
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17667
- **Reference count**: 7
- **Primary result**: Semantic counterfactual explanations generated via knowledge graphs achieve 34.93% and 23.65% preference rates over baseline methods on CUB dataset for VGG-16 and ResNet-50 classifiers respectively

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel framework for generating counterfactual explanations by leveraging knowledge graphs to provide semantically meaningful edits. The core idea is to use structured semantic knowledge from knowledge bases to produce explanations in terms of high-level concepts and relationships rather than low-level features. The framework guarantees valid, feasible, minimal, and actionable counterfactuals by computing semantic edit distances on knowledge graphs. Experiments include a human study comparing semantic counterfactuals with state-of-the-art methods on the CUB dataset, achieving 34.93% and 23.65% preference rates over baseline methods for VGG-16 and ResNet-50 classifiers respectively, along with 62.67% and 50.42% "can't tell" responses. Additional experiments demonstrate the framework's utility in explaining classifiers for scene classification (Places dataset) and COVID-19 diagnosis using cough audio, successfully uncovering classifier biases related to demographic features. The approach is domain-agnostic and can utilize automatically generated semantic descriptions via scene graph generators when domain expertise is unavailable.

## Method Summary
The framework transforms the problem of finding counterfactual explanations from a feature-space optimization problem into a graph edit distance problem on knowledge graphs. It represents exemplars as nodes in a knowledge graph and computes minimal edit paths between them, ensuring that counterfactuals are semantically meaningful and preserve the validity of the knowledge base. The method involves encoding datasets into Description Logic knowledge bases, computing set-based edit distances using bipartite graph matching with TBox-derived costs, and generating local and global counterfactual explanations. The approach is domain-agnostic and can work with automatically generated semantic descriptions when domain expertise is unavailable.

## Key Results
- Human preference study shows semantic counterfactuals achieve 34.93% and 23.65% preference rates over baseline methods for VGG-16 and ResNet-50 on CUB dataset
- Framework successfully uncovers classifier biases in COVID-19 diagnosis system, revealing demographic-related biases in predictions
- Semantic explanations provide global insights by accumulating concept importance statistics across multiple exemplars and target classes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The framework guarantees valid, feasible, minimal, and actionable counterfactual explanations by computing semantic edit distances on knowledge graphs.
- Mechanism: The framework transforms the problem of finding counterfactual explanations from a feature-space optimization problem into a graph edit distance problem on knowledge graphs. By representing exemplars as nodes in a knowledge graph and computing minimal edit paths between them, the framework ensures that counterfactuals are semantically meaningful and preserve the validity of the knowledge base.
- Core assumption: The knowledge graph accurately represents the semantic relationships relevant to the classification task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The framework guarantees valid, feasible, minimal, and actionable counterfactuals by computing semantic edit distances on knowledge graphs."
  - [section]: "Given an explanation dataset ⟨EN → D , ⟨A, T ⟩⟩, the first step for computing counterfactual explanations is to determine the edit operations on the ABox that transform the description of every exemplar to every other exemplar"
- Break condition: If the knowledge graph does not capture relevant semantic relationships or contains incorrect information, the counterfactual explanations may be misleading or invalid.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Semantic counterfactual explanations are more understandable, informative, and trustworthy than low-level feature explanations.
- Mechanism: By representing explanations in terms of high-level concepts and relationships from knowledge graphs rather than low-level features like pixels or sound frequencies, the framework provides explanations that are more intuitive for human users to understand. This transparency makes it easier to identify potential biases in the classifier.
- Core assumption: Humans can more easily understand explanations expressed in terms of high-level semantic concepts rather than low-level features.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The framework guarantees optimal flip-rate, minimality, feasibility, and actionability. Furthermore, we argue that by utilizing knowledge graphs, the explanations become more understandable, informative, and trustworthy"
  - [section]: "Explanations based on low-level features, such as pixel brightness or sound frequency, have not proven to be particularly helpful or trustworthy to users"
- Break condition: If users are not familiar with the semantic concepts used in the knowledge graph, the explanations may still be difficult to understand.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The framework can uncover classifier biases by comparing semantic edits needed for different class transitions.
- Mechanism: By computing global counterfactual explanations across multiple exemplars, the framework can identify which semantic concepts are most frequently added or removed when transitioning between classes. This reveals the semantic features the classifier uses to make decisions, exposing potential biases.
- Core assumption: Classifier biases manifest as systematic differences in the semantic edits needed for different class transitions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "To provide more information to the end user, we can accumulate counterfactual explanations for multiple exemplars and the desired class and provide statistics about what changes tend to flip the prediction of the classifier"
  - [section]: "One could gather all exemplars that are classified as WildAnimal, along with their counterfactual explanations for target class DomesticAnimal and compute how important the presence (or absence) of a concept or a role is for distinguishing between the two classes"
- Break condition: If the classifier does not rely on the semantic features represented in the knowledge graph, the framework may fail to identify biases or may identify irrelevant ones.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Description Logics and Knowledge Bases
  - Why needed here: The framework uses Description Logics to represent and reason about the knowledge base, which is essential for computing semantic edit distances.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between an ABox and a TBox in Description Logics?

- Concept: Graph Edit Distance
  - Why needed here: Computing the minimal set of edits between exemplars requires solving a graph edit distance problem, which is fundamental to the framework's approach.
  - Quick check question: Why is computing the exact graph edit distance NP-hard, and what are the implications for the framework?

- Concept: Semantic Similarity Measures
  - Why needed here: The framework uses semantic similarity measures based on the knowledge graph to assign costs to different edit operations.
  - Quick check question: How does the framework determine the cost of replacing one concept with another in the knowledge graph?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Knowledge Base Construction -> Exemplar Mapping -> Edit Distance Computation -> Explanation Generation -> Bias Detection
- Critical path: Knowledge Base Construction → Exemplar Mapping → Edit Distance Computation → Explanation Generation → Bias Detection
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Expressiveness vs. Efficiency: More expressive knowledge representations allow for more nuanced explanations but increase computational complexity
  - Manual Curation vs. Automatic Extraction: Manually curated knowledge bases are more accurate but require more resources than automatically extracted ones
  - Local vs. Global Explanations: Local explanations are more specific but may miss broader patterns that global explanations can reveal
- Failure signatures:
  - Invalid explanations: If the knowledge base contains incorrect information, the framework may generate explanations that are not actually valid counterfactuals
  - Non-minimal explanations: If the edit distance computation is not optimal, the framework may generate explanations that are not minimal
  - Biased explanations: If the knowledge base or exemplars are biased, the framework may generate explanations that reinforce those biases rather than revealing them
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement the framework on a simple dataset (e.g., MNIST) with a manually constructed knowledge base to verify basic functionality
  2. Compare the framework's explanations with baseline methods (e.g., feature importance methods) on a standard dataset (e.g., CUB) to evaluate explainability
  3. Apply the framework to a real-world dataset (e.g., COCO) to demonstrate its ability to uncover classifier biases in practical scenarios

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do edit costs affect the quality and interpretability of semantic counterfactual explanations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that edit costs can be manually assigned by users, particularly for edits that may not be feasible in the real world (e.g., assigning infinite cost to age reversal).
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis or experimental evaluation of how different edit cost assignments impact the resulting explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying edit costs and measuring their effects on explanation quality metrics (validity, feasibility, minimality, actionability) and user study outcomes.

### Open Question 2
- What is the optimal way to compute semantic similarities between concepts and roles beyond simple graph distance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that using graph distance in the TBox "is not necessarily the optimal way to compute semantic similarities of concepts and roles" and references other measures from literature.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a straightforward graph distance approach without exploring or comparing alternative semantic similarity measures from the literature.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using different semantic similarity measures (e.g., information content-based, feature-based, or hybrid approaches) and evaluating their impact on explanation quality and user comprehension.

### Open Question 3
- How does the quality of automatically generated semantic descriptions (e.g., from scene graph generators) affect the usefulness of counterfactual explanations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates using scene graph generators to extract semantic descriptions when domain expertise is unavailable, but does not evaluate the impact of the quality of these generated descriptions.
- Why unresolved: The experiments use scene graph generators without assessing how errors or limitations in the generated semantic descriptions propagate to the final explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of explanations generated from manually curated versus automatically generated semantic descriptions, measuring differences in explanation accuracy, user comprehension, and bias detection capability.

## Limitations
- Framework's effectiveness is directly tied to the quality and completeness of the underlying knowledge base
- Scalability of graph edit distance computation to very large explanation datasets remains unclear
- Substantial "can't tell" responses (62.67% and 50.42%) in human studies suggest semantic explanations may not be clearly superior in all cases

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The framework's ability to generate semantically meaningful counterfactuals through knowledge graph edit distances
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that semantic counterfactuals are more understandable than low-level features, given the substantial "can't tell" responses in human studies
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of automatically generated scene graphs when domain expertise is unavailable, as this was only briefly demonstrated
