# SingSong: Generating musical accompaniments from singing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.12662
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12662
- Reference count: 13
- Generates instrumental accompaniments from singing vocals with 66% preference over retrieval baselines

## Executive Summary
SingSong is a system that generates instrumental music to accompany input vocal audio by adapting the AudioLM framework for conditional audio-to-audio generation. The method leverages source separation to create aligned pairs of vocals and instrumentals, then uses a three-stage cascade of Transformer models to generate discrete acoustic codes for the accompaniment given the vocal input. To address the challenge of generalizing from source-separated vocals to real isolated vocals, the authors propose adding noise to vocal inputs and using only semantic codes as conditioning. In pairwise comparisons, SingSong-generated instrumentals were preferred 66% of the time over a strong retrieval baseline.

## Method Summary
The method uses source separation to create aligned vocal-instrumental pairs from a large corpus of music, then adapts AudioLM to generate instrumentals conditioned on vocals. AudioLM is a cascade of three Transformer models that generate increasingly high-rate discrete codes: semantic codes, coarse acoustic codes, and fine acoustic codes. The adaptation involves replacing the unconditional decoder with a conditional model that takes vocal features as input. Two featurization strategies improve generalization: adding white noise to vocal inputs to mask source separation artifacts, and using only semantic codes (dropping coarse acoustic codes) as conditioning. The model is trained on 1 million source-separated audio tracks and evaluated on isolated vocals from MUSDB18.

## Key Results
- SingSong instrumentals were preferred 66% of the time over a strong retrieval baseline in pairwise comparisons
- Adding noise and using only semantic codes independently improved generalization from source-separated to isolated vocals
- FAD (Fréchet Audio Distance) on isolated vocals (FADs) was significantly better than on source-separated vocals (FADi), indicating successful generalization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adding noise to vocal inputs during training helps the model generalize from source-separated vocals to isolated vocals.
- Mechanism: The noise masks the barely-audible instrumental artifacts present in source-separated vocals, preventing the model from learning to reconstruct the original instrumental from these artifacts.
- Core assumption: The instrumental artifacts in source-separated vocals are weaker than the actual vocals but stronger than added white noise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "To improve generalization, we propose two featurization strategies for the input vocals: (1) adding noise to vocal inputs to conceal artifacts"
  - [section 3.4]: "We propose to add white noise to vocal inputs during both training and inference. The primary purpose of adding noise is to conceal barely-audible artifacts of the original instrumental that remain in source-separated vocals"
- Break condition: If the noise amplitude is too high relative to the vocals, the model may not learn meaningful relationships between vocals and instrumentals.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using only semantic codes for vocal inputs (S-SA) improves generalization compared to using both semantic and acoustic codes.
- Mechanism: Semantic codes capture higher-level musical information about the vocals, while acoustic codes may contain information about the instrumental artifacts present in source-separated vocals. Removing acoustic codes reduces the model's reliance on these artifacts.
- Core assumption: Semantic codes are sufficient to capture the necessary information about vocals for accompaniment generation, while acoustic codes primarily contain artifact-related information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.5]: "We experiment with several different audio featurization configurations for both the input vocals and the target instrumentals... We also experiment with dropping coarse acoustic codes from the input conditioning (S-SA)"
  - [section 4.5]: "Both the Noisy and the S-SA configurations independently seem to improve generalization... Together, both featurizations essentially eliminate the generalization gap"
- Break condition: If semantic codes alone do not capture sufficient information about the vocals' musical characteristics, the model may generate less musically coherent accompaniments.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The cascade of three models in AudioLM, adapted for conditional generation, enables effective generation of instrumentals given vocals.
- Mechanism: The first model generates semantic codes for the instrumental given vocal features, the second generates coarse acoustic codes given the semantic codes and vocal features, and the third generates fine acoustic codes given the coarse codes. This hierarchical approach allows the model to capture both high-level musical structure and fine-grained audio details.
- Core assumption: The independence assumption Fine(y) ⊥ ⊥ Sem(y), Feats(x) | Coarse(y) holds, meaning that fine acoustic codes can be reconstructed from coarse codes alone without additional information from semantic codes or vocal features.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3]: "We adopt a three-stage factorization reminiscent of that of AudioLM... with an analogous independence assumption"
  - [section 3.2]: "AudioLM is a cascade of three models that generate increasingly high-rate codes... Each model is an autoregressive, decoder-only Transformer"
- Break condition: If the independence assumption does not hold, the model may generate instrumentals that are not well-aligned with the vocal input.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Source separation (MDXNet) -> Discrete code extraction (SoundStream, w2v-BERT) -> AudioLM cascade (3 Transformer models) -> Audio decoding

- Critical path:
  1. Apply source separation to music tracks to obtain vocal-instrumental pairs
  2. Extract discrete codes from vocals and instrumentals using the codec and semantic encoder
  3. Train the adapted AudioLM model on the source-separated data
  4. At inference, extract codes from input vocals, generate codes for the instrumental, and decode to audio

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using discrete codes instead of waveforms reduces computational cost but may limit audio fidelity
  - Adding noise to inputs improves generalization but may introduce some distortion
  - Using only semantic codes for conditioning reduces artifact reliance but may lose some acoustic information

- Failure signatures:
  - Model outputs silence or mostly silence when given isolated vocals
  - Model outputs instrumentals that closely resemble the original instrumental from source-separated vocals
  - Generated instrumentals lack temporal or harmonic alignment with the input vocals

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a baseline model using the default AudioLM featurization (SA-SA) without noise on a small subset of the data to verify the training pipeline works
  2. Train models with different featurization strategies (e.g., adding noise, using only semantic codes) on the full dataset to identify which improves generalization
  3. Scale up the best-performing model to a larger size and evaluate its performance on isolated vocals to assess the impact of scaling

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but it mentions that the current sampling rate of the AudioLM acoustic codec (16kHz) is below conventional sampling rates for high-fidelity music, and the authors hope to increase the sampling rate in future work.

## Limitations
- The model's performance relies heavily on the quality of source separation and pre-trained encoders, making it unclear how much is the proposed approach versus its dependencies
- The 66% preference rate, while statistically significant, represents a modest improvement that may not translate to real-world deployment scenarios
- The evaluation primarily measures fidelity to source material rather than creative quality or musical appropriateness

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The technical approach of adapting AudioLM for conditional generation and the use of source separation to create training data are well-established and reproducible. The hierarchical code generation framework is clearly specified.

- **Medium Confidence**: The featurization strategies (adding noise, using only semantic codes) are empirically justified through ablation studies, but the exact mechanisms by which they improve generalization remain somewhat speculative. The 66% preference rate is statistically significant but represents a modest margin.

- **Low Confidence**: Claims about the model's ability to generate musically coherent accompaniments in real-world settings are not well-supported. The evaluation primarily measures fidelity to source material rather than creative quality or musical appropriateness.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-dataset evaluation**: Test the model on a held-out dataset of isolated vocals from different sources than MUSDB18 to verify that the generalization improvements from featurization strategies transfer beyond the evaluation set.

2. **Ablation on featurization components**: Systematically vary noise amplitude and examine the relationship between noise level, artifact masking, and generation quality to determine optimal trade-offs rather than using fixed configurations.

3. **Long-form generation analysis**: Generate accompaniments for full songs (rather than 10-second clips) and evaluate temporal coherence, structural development, and consistency across sections to assess practical deployment readiness.
