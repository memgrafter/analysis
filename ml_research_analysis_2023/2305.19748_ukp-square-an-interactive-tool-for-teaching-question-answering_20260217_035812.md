# UKP-SQuARE: An Interactive Tool for Teaching Question Answering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19748
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19748
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: UKP-SQuARE improves student comprehension of QA formats by 91% and reduces estimated assignment time by 75%

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces UKP-SQuARE, an interactive platform designed to teach question answering (QA) concepts in NLP courses. The platform enables students to run, compare, and analyze various QA models with integrated tools for explainability, adversarial attacks, and graph visualizations. Using a flipped classroom approach, students interact with UKP-SQuARE during class sessions to gain hands-on experience with different QA formats, information retrieval methods, and trustworthy AI concepts. The platform was evaluated in a postgraduate NLP course through student surveys, demonstrating significant improvements in comprehension and engagement.

## Method Summary
The method involves adopting UKP-SQuARE in an NLP course divided into three modules: basic QA concepts, trustworthy QA, and multi-agent systems. Students complete pre-class readings and documentation, then participate in interactive in-class sessions using the platform for demonstrations and analysis. Homework assignments require students to train, deploy, and analyze their own QA models using UKP-SQuARE. Student feedback is collected through surveys to evaluate platform effectiveness and learning outcomes.

## Key Results
- 91% of students reported better comprehension of different QA formats
- Majority found the platform easy to use
- Students estimated assignment time would increase from 2-5 hours to over 8 hours without UKP-SQuARE
- Positive feedback suggests enhanced student engagement and learning outcomes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Interactive model comparison and analysis reduces cognitive load in understanding QA architectures.
- Mechanism: By allowing students to directly compare different QA models (e.g., SpanBERT vs. CommonsenseQA) in real-time, the platform externalizes architectural differences, making them more tangible and easier to grasp than abstract descriptions alone.
- Core assumption: Students can better understand architectural differences when they can observe model behavior side-by-side with theoretical explanations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "students can run, compare, and analyze various QA models from different perspectives"
  - [section 3.1.1] "students can get first-hand experience by interacting with multiple models on our platform"
- Break condition: If the interface becomes too complex or overwhelming, students may experience increased cognitive load rather than reduction.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Real-time experimentation with information retrieval methods clarifies the semantic vs. lexical retrieval distinction.
- Mechanism: Students can actively compare BM25 (lexical) and DPR (semantic) retrieval by observing their different outputs for the same query, making the abstract concept of semantic similarity concrete through direct observation.
- Core assumption: Students will recognize the difference between keyword matching and semantic understanding when they see contrasting retrieval results.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1.2] "students can easily understand that DPR retrieves semantically similar passages while BM25 only retrieves passages that contain the query tokens"
- Break condition: If students lack prerequisite knowledge about semantic similarity or if retrieval results are too similar to distinguish.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Immediate feedback from explainability and adversarial attack tools accelerates learning of model behavior and limitations.
- Mechanism: Students can immediately see how models make decisions (via saliency maps) and where they fail (via adversarial attacks), creating a feedback loop that reinforces understanding of model strengths and weaknesses.
- Core assumption: Students learn more effectively when they can observe both correct model reasoning and failure cases in real-time.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2.1] "students can learn how to explain the output of a QA model based on saliency maps"
  - [section 3.2.3] "students can attack real models with examples by tuning different parameters"
- Break condition: If the tools produce misleading visualizations or if students misinterpret the feedback without proper guidance.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Question Answering Formats (Extractive, Abstractive, Multiple-Choice)
  - Why needed here: Understanding different QA formats is prerequisite to using the platform effectively and comparing models
  - Quick check question: Can you explain the difference between extractive and abstractive QA, and give an example of when each would be used?

- Concept: Information Retrieval Fundamentals (Lexical vs. Semantic)
  - Why needed here: Students need to understand retrieval methods to interpret why different models retrieve different contexts
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between BM25 and DPR retrieval, and how would this affect the answers generated by QA models?

- Concept: Model Interpretability and Adversarial Robustness
  - Why needed here: To meaningfully use explainability and adversarial attack tools, students must understand what these methods reveal about model behavior
  - Quick check question: How does a saliency map help explain a model's decision, and what does an adversarial attack reveal about model robustness?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Frontend interface → Model selection → Retrieval method application → Answer generation → Explainability/adversarial analysis → Visualization tools
- Critical path: User query → Model selection → Retrieval method application → Answer generation → Explainability/adversarial analysis → Visualization
- Design tradeoffs: The platform prioritizes ease of use over fine-grained control, which may limit advanced experimentation but lowers the barrier for students new to QA.
- Failure signatures: Common issues include model loading failures, visualization rendering problems, or incorrect retrieval results. These often manifest as empty responses or error messages in the interface.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare SpanBERT and CommonsenseQA models on the same question to observe format differences
  2. Test BM25 vs. DPR retrieval on a question where semantic understanding is crucial
  3. Apply a simple adversarial attack (e.g., input reduction) to observe model robustness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How effective is UKP-SQuARE for teaching QA concepts compared to traditional methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper evaluates the effectiveness of UKP-SQuARE through student surveys, with 91% reporting better comprehension of different QA formats.
- Why unresolved: While the survey results are positive, they are based on a small sample size of seven students. Further studies with larger and more diverse student populations are needed to confirm the effectiveness of UKP-SQuARE.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting larger-scale studies with control groups using traditional teaching methods to compare learning outcomes and student satisfaction.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the long-term impacts of using UKP-SQuARE on students' understanding and application of QA techniques?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that students estimated they would spend significantly more time on assignments without UKP-SQuARE, suggesting its efficiency. However, it does not address long-term retention or application of knowledge.
- Why unresolved: The study only evaluates immediate feedback and perceived usefulness. Longitudinal studies tracking students' performance in advanced courses or professional settings are needed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Follow-up studies assessing students' performance in subsequent NLP courses or their ability to apply QA techniques in real-world projects after using UKP-SQuARE.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can UKP-SQuARE be adapted to teach other NLP topics beyond QA?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention plans to extend UKP-SQuARE to support prompting large language models and suggest potential applications for other NLP topics like word embeddings and machine translation.
- Why unresolved: While the authors propose extending the platform, they do not provide specific methodologies or evidence of effectiveness for these new applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating curricula for other NLP topics using UKP-SQuARE, with assessments of student learning outcomes and engagement.

## Limitations

- Limited empirical evidence for the specific effectiveness of UKP-SQuARE compared to traditional teaching methods - the study relies on self-reported student satisfaction rather than objective learning outcome measures
- Potential selection bias in the student sample, as the course was likely taken by motivated students already interested in NLP
- Unclear generalizability to different educational contexts (undergraduate vs. postgraduate, different cultural settings, varying levels of prior knowledge)

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the platform's usability and student satisfaction based on the survey results showing 91% comprehension improvement and positive feedback
- **Medium confidence** in the learning mechanism claims, as the paper describes how the platform enables learning but provides limited evidence that these mechanisms actually improve understanding
- **Low confidence** in claims about time efficiency (8+ hours without the platform) without baseline measurements of how long similar assignments took before UKP-SQuARE implementation

## Next Checks

1. Conduct controlled experiments comparing UKP-SQuARE with traditional QA teaching methods using pre/post tests to measure actual knowledge gain
2. Test the platform across multiple educational contexts (different course levels, institutions, and cultural settings) to assess generalizability
3. Implement A/B testing where some students use only certain features (e.g., explainability tools but not model comparison) to isolate which components drive the most learning benefit
