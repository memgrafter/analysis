# Distributionally Robust Recourse Action

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.11211
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11211
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: DiRRAc framework achieves higher M2 validity (up to 0.99) than state-of-the-art methods while maintaining lower costs

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a distributionally robust framework for generating recourse actions that are robust to model parameter shifts. The method uses Gelbrich distance to construct ambiguity sets around nominal mixture components and minimizes worst-case probability of unfavorable outcomes. Experiments on three real-world datasets show DiRRAc achieves higher validity than state-of-the-art methods like ROAR while maintaining lower costs.

## Method Summary
The DiRRAc framework formulates robust recourse as a min-max optimization problem using Gelbrich distance to specify ambiguity sets around model parameter distributions. The authors provide closed-form reformulations that convert the infinite-dimensional problem to finite-dimensional optimization, solvable via projected gradient descent. The method extends to handle mixture weight uncertainty using φ-divergence and Gaussian parametric assumptions. Experiments compare DiRRAc against baselines including ROAR, AR, CEPM, and Wachter on German credit, SBA, and Student performance datasets.

## Key Results
- DiRRAc achieves M2 validity up to 0.99, significantly higher than ROAR's 0.1-0.5 range
- The method maintains lower l1 and l2 costs compared to robust optimization baselines
- Performance improvements hold across three real-world datasets with varying types of distribution shifts

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The Gelbrich distance provides a closed-form worst-case probability for mixture components, enabling tractable reformulation of the robust recourse problem.
- Mechanism: Gelbrich distance between (θ, Σ) and (θ̂, Σ̂) equals the type-2 Wasserstein distance for Gaussian distributions. This allows worst-case VaR computation with closed-form regularization terms, avoiding infinite-dimensional optimization.
- Core assumption: Component distributions are either fully specified by mean and covariance (non-parametric case) or are Gaussian (parametric case).
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section 3], [corpus]

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The min-max formulation ensures recourse validity across all plausible model shifts, preventing overly conservative solutions compared to robust optimization.
- Mechanism: By considering an ambiguity set B(θ̂) containing all distributions within Gelbrich radius ρk, the method optimizes for the worst-case distribution in the set, rather than hedging against a single worst-case parameter value.
- Core assumption: The true future model parameters lie within the ambiguity set constructed around the nominal distribution.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section 2], [corpus]

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The projected gradient descent algorithm converges to stationary points of the reformulated finite-dimensional problem, providing practical computation of robust recourses.
- Mechanism: The reformulation to finite-dimensional problem with explicit objective (Theorem 3.2) allows application of projected gradient descent with backtracking line search, guaranteed to converge under convexity assumptions.
- Core assumption: The cost function c(x, x₀) is convex and the feasible set X is convex (or second-order cone representable for projection).
- Evidence anchors: [section 3.3], [section E], [corpus]

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of φ-divergence in the ambiguity set affect the performance of the DiRRAc framework?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the φ-divergence family contains many well-known statistical divergences such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence and the Hellinger distance, and discusses robustification against mixture weight uncertainty using φ-divergence.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents the theoretical formulation but does not provide experimental comparisons between different φ-divergences.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing DiRRAc performance using different φ-divergences (KL, Hellinger, etc.) on the same datasets would show which divergence leads to better robustness or lower costs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of increasing the number of mixture components K beyond the elbow point identified in the experiments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses the elbow method to determine optimal K for real-world datasets but does not explore what happens when K is significantly larger than the elbow point.
- Why unresolved: The paper only reports results for K equal to the elbow point, not for larger values that might capture more complex distribution shifts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with varying K (e.g., K=5, 10, 20) on the same datasets would reveal whether increasing K improves robustness or leads to overfitting and computational inefficiency.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the DiRRAc framework perform when the data shifts are non-linear rather than linear?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that the analysis focuses on linear classifiers and mentions extending to nonlinear models using LIME approximations, but does not directly test DiRRAc on inherently nonlinear shifts.
- Why unresolved: The framework is primarily tested on linear models or LIME-approximated local linear models, not on scenarios where the true underlying shifts are nonlinear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying DiRRAc directly to nonlinear models (e.g., neural networks) without LIME approximation, or testing on datasets with known nonlinear shifts, would show its effectiveness in such settings.

## Limitations
- The method requires prior knowledge of the distribution family (mixture components) for the model parameters, which may not be available in practice
- Computational complexity scales linearly with the number of mixture components K, limiting scalability
- The robustness guarantee is only as good as the ambiguity set specification - overly conservative sets lead to high costs, while insufficient sets provide inadequate protection

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Closed-form reformulations and projected gradient descent convergence guarantees are mathematically rigorous
- **Medium confidence**: Experimental results showing improved M2 validity are compelling but may not represent all practical scenarios
- **Low confidence**: Assumption that model parameter shifts follow Gaussian mixtures may not hold in many applications
