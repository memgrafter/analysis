# Language models are weak learners

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.14101
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14101
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: LLMs can function as weak learners in boosting for tabular data classification

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that large language models (LLMs) can serve as weak learners in a boosting framework for tabular data classification. By converting tabular records into natural language descriptions and using LLM summarization to generate weak learner hypotheses, the proposed Summary Boosting method outperforms zero-shot and few-shot prompting approaches. The approach leverages LLM summarization capabilities without requiring fine-tuning, making it a promising alternative for tabular data tasks where LLMs typically struggle. Experiments on 18 UCI and OpenML datasets show competitive performance, especially on small datasets.

## Method Summary
The method converts tabular data into natural language descriptions, then uses hierarchical clustering on GPT embeddings to select representative subsets according to AdaBoost weights. These subsets are summarized by the LLM to create weak learner hypotheses. The process iterates through T boosting rounds, updating sample weights based on classification performance. The final classifier combines all weak learners using weighted voting. Key innovations include using LLM summaries as weak learners and cluster-based stratified sampling to focus on harder examples in each round.

## Key Results
- Summary Boosting outperforms zero-shot and few-shot prompting approaches on 18 benchmark datasets
- The method occasionally outperforms traditional tree-based boosting and fine-tuning methods, particularly on small datasets
- GPT-3 Curie (13B parameters) performs comparably to Davinci (175B parameters), with no consistent improvement from the larger model
- Cluster sampling improves performance by selecting representative sets that generalize better than random sampling

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Language models can function as weak learners in boosting by generating summaries that capture discriminative features of subsets of tabular data.
- Mechanism: The LLM converts tabular records into natural language descriptions, clusters and samples representative examples, summarizes them into concise hypotheses, and uses these summaries as prompts to classify new data. Multiple such weak learners are combined via AdaBoost.
- Core assumption: Summarization of representative examples produces a prompt that encodes sufficient discriminative information to perform better than random guessing on the target task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "by providing (properly sampled according to the distribution of interest) text descriptions of tabular data samples, LLMs can produce a summary of the samples that serves as a template for classification and achieves the aim of acting as a weak learner"
  - [section 3.2] "producing summaries of a collection of examples can serve as a powerful proxy for learning models based upon some number of examples"
- Break condition: If the summary fails to achieve better-than-random performance on the weighted distribution, boosting will not improve over random guessing.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Weighted cluster sampling ensures that the LLM focuses on "harder" examples in each boosting round, improving ensemble performance.
- Mechanism: Hierarchical agglomerative clustering on GPT embeddings of data descriptions groups semantically similar examples; sampling from these clusters according to current AdaBoost weights biases the summary toward underrepresented or misclassified instances.
- Core assumption: Cluster sampling based on language embeddings produces a more representative subset than random sampling, leading to better weak learners.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2] "use weighted stratified sampling using subpopulations defined by clusters of language embeddings of each data description"
  - [section 5] "Cluster sampling improves the performance of the LLM by selecting a representative set of texts that generalize better"
- Break condition: If the clustering fails to separate classes or if the weights do not meaningfully shift focus, sampling will not improve over random selection.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Converting numerical attributes to binned qualitative descriptors ("low", "medium", "high") enables LLMs to reason about tabular data without fine-tuning.
- Mechanism: Numerical features are discretized into percentiles and encoded with natural language quantifiers, making the data more amenable to LLM-based reasoning.
- Core assumption: LLMs can effectively compare and reason about qualitative descriptors but struggle with raw numerical values in zero-shot settings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "we adopt a straightforward approach: we bin all numerical features into percentiles and encode them descriptively as 'low,' 'medium,' and 'high'"
  - [section 5] "binning with quantifiers such as 'low', 'medium', and 'high' was most effective for comparing examples and generating high-quality summaries"
- Break condition: If the discretization loses too much information or if the LLM cannot map descriptors to actual values, performance will degrade compared to raw numerical models.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Boosting (AdaBoost)
  - Why needed here: The paper uses AdaBoost to combine multiple LLM-generated weak learners into a strong classifier, following the theoretical guarantee that weak learners suffice for strong learning.
  - Quick check question: What is the minimum error rate a weak learner must achieve to be useful in AdaBoost?
    - Answer: Better than random guessing (error < 0.5 for binary classification, < 1/K for K classes).

- Concept: Prompt-based few-shot learning
  - Why needed here: The LLM is used without fine-tuning; instead, it is prompted with data descriptions and summaries to perform classification, leveraging in-context learning.
  - Quick check question: How does providing labeled examples in the prompt help the LLM classify new instances?
    - Answer: It conditions the model on the task structure and label space, enabling it to generalize to unseen data via in-context learning.

- Concept: Clustering for stratified sampling
  - Why needed here: To select a representative subset of examples that fit within the LLM's context window while preserving class balance and focusing on hard examples.
  - Quick check question: Why use hierarchical agglomerative clustering on language embeddings instead of random sampling?
    - Answer: To ensure semantic diversity and coverage of the example space, improving the quality of the summary as a weak learner.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Data conversion -> Cluster sampling -> Summary generation -> Inference -> Boosting loop

- Critical path:
  1. Convert training data to descriptions
  2. Cluster and sample weighted subset
  3. Generate summary candidate (repeat until error < threshold)
  4. Evaluate on validation set, record hypothesis and weight
  5. Update sample weights, repeat for T rounds
  6. Combine hypotheses via weighted vote

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Prompt engineering vs. learned prompts: Hand-crafted prompts are flexible and gradient-free but require manual tuning; learned prompts (e.g., soft prompts) could automate but need gradients.
  - Cluster sampling vs. random sampling: Clustering improves representativeness but adds embedding and clustering overhead; random sampling is simpler but may miss hard examples.
  - Discretization granularity: Finer bins preserve more info but may confuse the LLM; coarser bins simplify reasoning but lose detail.

- Failure signatures:
  - Summaries that are too generic ("this is a customer") → high training error, no improvement over random
  - Validation error plateaus early → weak learners not improving, likely due to poor sampling or summarization
  - Performance worse than KNN baseline → LLM not leveraging prior knowledge effectively, possibly due to poor data conversion or prompt design

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Zero-shot classification on a simple dataset (e.g., iris) to confirm LLMs lack prior knowledge for tabular tasks
  2. Few-shot classification with 15 examples to measure improvement from in-context learning
  3. Summary method with cluster sampling on a small dataset to validate weak learner generation before boosting

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different LLM sizes (parameter counts) affect the quality of weak learners generated through summarization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares GPT-3 Curie (13B parameters) vs Davinci (175B parameters) and finds no consistent improvement with the larger model, with the authors noting "Surprisingly, we find that the larger model (davinci) does not consistently improve upon the smaller model."
- Why unresolved: The experiments only compare two specific models from the same family. The relationship between model size and weak learner quality across different model architectures and families remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying model size across different LLM families (GPT, Claude, PaLM) while keeping other factors constant, measuring weak learner quality through validation error rates.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal context length for few-shot learning vs summary-based approaches on tabular data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that "Few-shot performance reaches an optimal size around the medium context length and degrades with more examples. In contrast, Summary improves with more data."
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests a few context sizes and doesn't provide a systematic study of the optimal context length for each approach or how this optimal length varies with dataset characteristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying context length systematically across datasets with different numbers of examples, features, and classes to identify the optimal context length for each approach.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the quality of weak learners scale with dataset size when using LLM-based summarization?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that "LLMs have a remarkable amount of general prior knowledge about the worlds compared to methods like XGboost" and that "Summary Boosting performs very well when the size of the dataset is very small," but also acknowledges limitations when datasets contain many continuous attributes.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide systematic analysis of how weak learner quality changes as dataset size increases from very small to large, particularly for datasets with different proportions of continuous vs categorical features.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments tracking weak learner quality metrics across datasets of varying sizes (10 to 10,000 examples) with controlled variations in feature types and class distributions.

## Limitations

- The method relies heavily on hand-crafted prompts and binning strategies, making it sensitive to prompt engineering choices that aren't systematically explored
- Computational cost of generating summaries and performing hierarchical clustering for each boosting round is not discussed, raising scalability concerns
- Performance degrades on datasets with many continuous attributes, limiting applicability to certain tabular data types

## Confidence

- High confidence: The core experimental finding that Summary Boosting outperforms zero-shot and few-shot prompting approaches on the tested datasets
- Medium confidence: The claim that Summary Boosting can occasionally outperform traditional tree-based boosting and fine-tuning methods, given the limited number of comparisons and lack of statistical significance testing
- Medium confidence: The assertion that the method is particularly effective for small datasets, as this is based on a subset of the experiments without comprehensive ablation studies

## Next Checks

1. **Prompt sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary the binning strategy (e.g., different numbers of bins, alternative labeling schemes) and prompt templates to quantify the impact on performance and identify the most critical design choices.

2. **Scalability assessment**: Measure the computational cost (time and API calls) of the Summary Boosting method as a function of dataset size and number of boosting rounds, and compare against traditional boosting methods.

3. **Statistical significance testing**: Perform paired t-tests or bootstrap confidence intervals on the classification errors across datasets to determine whether the improvements of Summary Boosting over baselines are statistically significant.
