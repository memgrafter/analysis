# Understanding and Unifying Fourteen Attribution Methods with Taylor Interactions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.01506
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01506
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Attribution scores generated by fourteen methods can be reformulated as weighted sums of independent and interaction effects.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a unified mathematical framework using Taylor interactions to explain the core mechanisms of fourteen attribution methods for deep neural networks. The key insight is that all these methods can be reformulated as specific ways of allocating Taylor expansion terms (independent and interaction effects) to input variables. Based on this unified perspective, the paper proposes three principles for evaluating the faithfulness of attribution methods, showing that methods satisfying these principles typically perform better on standard evaluation metrics like infidelity and bounding box accuracy.

## Method Summary
The paper formulates fourteen attribution methods within a unified mathematical framework based on Taylor interactions. It proves that attribution scores can be decomposed into independent effects of individual input variables and interaction effects between variables, with the essential difference among methods being how they allocate these effects through different weights. The framework represents each method as a specific re-allocation of Taylor expansion terms to input variables. Based on this unified perspective, three principles for fair allocation of effects are proposed to evaluate attribution method faithfulness: low approximation error, avoiding allocation to unrelated variables, and complete allocation of all effects.

## Key Results
- Attribution scores from all fourteen methods can be mathematically reformulated as weighted sums of Taylor independent effects and interaction effects
- The essential differences among methods lie in their specific allocation weights for these effects
- Methods satisfying the proposed three principles of fair allocation typically show better performance on infidelity and bounding box accuracy metrics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Taylor interaction effect provides a unified mathematical framework explaining how attribution methods decompose DNN outputs into independent and interaction effects
- Mechanism: Represents each attribution method as a specific re-allocation of Taylor expansion terms (independent effects φ(κ) and interaction effects I(κ)) to input variables
- Core assumption: DNN output can be accurately approximated by Taylor expansion at a baseline point, and attribution scores can be expressed as weighted sums of these expansion terms
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] proves attribution scores can be reformulated as weighted sums of two types of effects; [section] Theorem 1 shows Harsanyi dividend equivalence to interaction effects
- Break condition: If Taylor expansion approximation error becomes significant, or baseline point selection introduces systematic bias

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Essential difference between attribution methods lies in how they allocate Taylor effects to input variables
- Mechanism: Each method uses different weights wi,j and wi,S to distribute independent effects ψ(j) and interaction effects J(S) to attribution score of variable i
- Core assumption: Allocation weights can be determined by method's design principles and don't violate proposed faithfulness principles
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] states essential difference lies in weights of allocating different effects; [section] Theorem 9 shows Shapley value reformulation
- Break condition: If allocation weights violate proposed principles or cannot be consistently determined

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Three proposed principles provide rigorous evaluation framework for attribution methods
- Mechanism: Principles assess whether methods have low approximation error, avoid allocating effects to unrelated variables, and completely allocate all effects
- Core assumption: Methods satisfying all three principles are more likely to faithfully represent true contribution of input variables
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] proposes three principles to evaluate faithfulness; [section] outlines low approximation error, avoiding unrelated allocation, and complete allocation principles
- Break condition: If method performs well on other metrics but violates proposed principles, suggesting principles may be too restrictive

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: DNN f + input sample x + baseline point b -> Taylor expansion decomposition -> Attribution methods as weight-based effect allocation -> Three principles evaluation -> Attribution scores ai

- Critical path: 1) Choose DNN and input sample, 2) Select baseline point for Taylor expansion, 3) Compute Taylor expansion terms up to desired order, 4) Classify terms as independent or interaction effects, 5) Apply attribution method to allocate effects to input variables, 6) Evaluate method using proposed principles

- Design tradeoffs: Higher-order Taylor expansions capture more complex interactions but increase computational cost; baseline point selection affects approximation error and may introduce bias; fine-grained effect allocation provides more detailed explanations but may be harder to interpret

- Failure signatures: Large approximation error (ϵK) indicates Taylor expansion is insufficient to explain DNN output; violations of three principles suggest method is not faithfully attributing effects; inconsistent results across different baseline points or DNN architectures

- First 3 experiments: 1) Verify Taylor expansion decomposition on simple polynomial function where true effects are known, 2) Apply unified framework to two attribution methods (e.g., Gradient×Input and Shapley value) and compare their effect allocations, 3) Test three principles on set of attribution methods and evaluate their performance on standard metrics (infidelity, bounding box accuracy)

## Limitations
- Computational complexity prevents full implementation of higher-order Taylor interactions, with only first and second-order effects considered
- Framework evaluation is limited to specific datasets (MNIST and ImageNet) without exploring generalization across different model architectures or problem domains
- Approximation error for truncated Taylor expansions is not fully characterized, potentially limiting applicability to complex DNNs

## Confidence

**Major Uncertainties:**
The paper's theoretical framework is mathematically rigorous but computationally challenging for higher-order Taylor interactions. The paper acknowledges that enumerating all Taylor interaction effects is impossible, and only first and second-order effects are implemented. The approximation error (ϵK) for these truncated expansions is not fully characterized, which could limit the framework's applicability to complex DNNs. Additionally, the paper's evaluation focuses on specific datasets (MNIST and ImageNet) without exploring whether the findings generalize across different model architectures or problem domains.

**Confidence Labels:**
- **High Confidence:** The mathematical proofs connecting Taylor interactions to attribution methods are sound and well-established in game theory literature. The three proposed principles for fair allocation are logically consistent.
- **Medium Confidence:** The experimental validation showing that methods satisfying proposed principles perform better on infidelity and bounding box metrics, though sample size and diversity of models tested could be expanded.
- **Low Confidence:** The claim that Taylor interactions provide the "fundamental mechanism" for all fourteen attribution methods, as the paper acknowledges computational limitations prevent full implementation of higher-order effects.
