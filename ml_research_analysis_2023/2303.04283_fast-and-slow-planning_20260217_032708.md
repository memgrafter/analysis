# Fast and Slow Planning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04283
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04283
- Reference count: 9
- Key outcome: Dual-process architecture improves classical and multi-agent epistemic planning by combining fast and slow solvers with metacognitive selection

## Executive Summary
This paper presents Plan-SOFAI, a dual-process architecture for planning inspired by Kahneman's "Thinking Fast and Slow" cognitive theory. The system combines fast solvers (case-based selection and Plansformer LLM) with slow solvers (Fast Downward and EFP 2.0) coordinated by a metacognitive module that decides which solver to use. Experimental results show the architecture solves more planning instances than state-of-the-art planners alone, with improved average solving times and higher solution correctness in both classical and epistemic domains.

## Method Summary
Plan-SOFAI implements a dual-system architecture where fast solvers (System 1) handle simple problems quickly while slow solvers (System 2) tackle complex cases. The metacognitive module evaluates fast solver confidence and estimated slow solver costs to make routing decisions. Two fast solvers are employed: a case-based plan selector using Jaccard/Levenshtein distance to retrieve precomputed solutions, and Plansformer, an LLM that generates plans with confidence scores. For slow solving, Fast Downward handles classical planning while EFP 2.0 addresses epistemic planning challenges.

## Key Results
- Plan-SOFAI solves more planning instances than state-of-the-art planners alone
- The system achieves improved average solving times across benchmark domains
- Higher solution correctness rates are demonstrated in both classical and epistemic planning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The dual-system architecture improves generality by routing simple problems to fast solvers and complex problems to slow solvers.
- Mechanism: The metacognitive module evaluates the confidence of the fast solver and the estimated cost of using the slow solver to decide whether to accept the fast solution or switch.
- Core assumption: The fast solver's confidence is a reliable proxy for solution quality.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Experimental results show that the proposed system solves more planning instances than state-of-the-art planners alone, with improved average solving times and higher solution correctness"
  - [section]: "MC-1 takes this decision considering the confidence, among other few factors, of the S1 solver"
- Break condition: The confidence estimate is unreliable or the metacognitive module's thresholds are poorly tuned, causing overuse of either fast or slow solvers.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Experience-based fast solvers reduce solving time for similar problems by reusing precomputed solutions.
- Mechanism: Fast solvers maintain a case library and retrieve the closest matching precomputed plan based on similarity metrics (Jaccard or Levenshtein distance).
- Core assumption: Past problems are similar enough to current ones that a close match yields a useful solution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "S1 can be used to determine which of the plans already generated by past experiences is the one that 'fits the best' the current problem"
  - [section]: "The first approach makes use of pre-computed plans; that is, S1 can be used to determine which of the plans already generated by past experiences is the one that 'fits the best' the current problem"
- Break condition: The problem space has low similarity between instances, making precomputed plans rarely applicable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLM-based fast solvers (Plansformer) generate valid plans for classical planning with high confidence.
- Mechanism: Plansformer maps planning problems to sequential prompts and generates plans with associated confidence scores.
- Core assumption: The LLM has learned sufficient syntactic and semantic patterns from training data to generate valid plans.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Plansformer is capable of generating valid plans ~90% of the time, along with the confidence score for the generated plan"
  - [section]: "The other type of S1 takes the domain and problem description of a planning instance and maps it to a sequential prompt given as input to Plansformer"
- Break condition: The LLM's token limit or training data coverage prevents handling of complex or novel problem types.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input Layer -> Fast Solver 1 (Case-based) OR Fast Solver 2 (Plansformer) -> Metacognitive Module (MC-1/MC-2) -> Decision -> Fast Solver output OR Slow Solver -> Output

- Critical path: Input → Fast Solver → Metacognitive Module → Decision → Fast Solver output OR Slow Solver → Output

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fast solver accuracy vs. speed: Case-based methods are fast but may find suboptimal solutions
  - Memory usage: Case library grows with experience but requires storage and similarity computation
  - LLM dependency: Plansformer provides high accuracy but has token limits and requires fine-tuning

- Failure signatures:
  - High rate of slow solver invocations: Fast solver confidence estimates are too conservative
  - Low solution quality: Case library lacks relevant examples or LLM generates invalid plans
  - High memory usage: Case library grows too large without pruning strategy

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare Jaccard vs. Levenshtein distance for case-based plan selection on classical planning benchmarks
  2. Evaluate Plansformer's plan generation accuracy across different problem sizes and domains
  3. Test metacognitive module thresholds (T1, T2, T3) to optimize the fast/slow solver balance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the internal parameters of the Plan-SOFAI architecture be automatically tuned to optimize the balance between fast (S1) and slow (S2) solver usage?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the parametric nature of the architecture and mentions that the balance between S1 and S2 usage needs to be preserved, but it also notes that finding the best trade-off for limiting S1 usage is not easy and leaves this as an open issue.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a method for automatically adjusting parameters like acceptable correctness, time thresholds, and risk aversion to optimize performance across different planning domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study showing an adaptive parameter tuning mechanism that improves the architecture's performance across various planning problems would resolve this question.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can Plansformer be extended to handle epistemic planning problems despite the token length limitation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that Plansformer currently cannot handle epistemic planning problems due to its 512-token input limit and leaves this as a future work item.
- Why unresolved: The current token length restriction prevents Plansformer from processing the more complex epistemic planning problems, and no solution has been proposed yet.
- What evidence would resolve it: A successful implementation of Plansformer that can process epistemic planning problems within the token constraints or a demonstration of how to extend the token limit would resolve this question.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of incorporating multiple S2 solvers in epistemic planning, and how does it compare to using a single S2 solver?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions ongoing work on a version of the architecture that allows for multiple S2 solvers in epistemic planning, specifically EFP 2.0 and RP-MEP, but does not provide experimental results.
- Why unresolved: The potential benefits or drawbacks of using multiple S2 solvers in epistemic planning have not been explored or quantified in the paper.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing the performance of Plan-SOFAI with multiple S2 solvers versus a single S2 solver in epistemic planning would provide the necessary evidence.

## Limitations

- The sensitivity of metacognitive thresholds (T1, T2, T3) to problem characteristics is not explored
- The case library's scalability and pruning strategy are not addressed, potentially limiting practical deployment
- The LLM-based fast solver's ability to handle complex or novel problem types beyond its training data is uncertain

## Confidence

- High confidence: The dual-system architecture design and its theoretical foundation in Kahneman's dual-process theory
- Medium confidence: The experimental results showing improved performance, as they are based on specific benchmark domains and may not generalize
- Low confidence: The LLM-based fast solver's ability to handle complex or novel problem types beyond its training data
