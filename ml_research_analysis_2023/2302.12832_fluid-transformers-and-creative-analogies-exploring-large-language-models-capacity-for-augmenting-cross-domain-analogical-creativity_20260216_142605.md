# Fluid Transformers and Creative Analogies: Exploring Large Language Models' Capacity for Augmenting Cross-Domain Analogical Creativity

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12832
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12832
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: LLM-generated cross-domain analogies were frequently helpful (median 4/5 rating) in creative problem reformulation tasks, with 70% being unique and potentially useful, but showed an upper bound of 25% potentially harmful outputs

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically explored the potential of Large Language Models (LLMs) to augment cross-domain analogical reasoning in creative tasks. The authors used carefully crafted prompts to generate 480 cross-domain analogies across six design problems, and conducted three studies to evaluate their usefulness and potential risks. Study 1 found that one-shot prompt learning yielded 70% unique and potentially useful cross-domain analogies. Study 2, involving a creative problem reformulation task with 110 participants, showed that LLM-generated analogies were frequently helpful (median 4/5 helpfulness rating), led to observable changes in problem formulations (~80% of cases), and had no correlation with a priori usefulness judgments. Study 3 revealed an upper bound of 25% potentially harmful outputs, with most being potentially upsetting rather than biased or toxic.

## Method Summary
The authors generated cross-domain analogies using GPT-3 through zero-shot and one-shot prompting approaches, creating 480 analogies across six design problems. They conducted three empirical studies: Study 1 evaluated analogy quality using Amazon Mechanical Turk raters who assessed semantic similarity, uniqueness, and potential usefulness; Study 2 involved 110 participants in a creative problem reformulation task where they received LLM-generated analogies and reformulated their design problems, with outcomes rated by independent raters; Study 3 had three researchers screen all analogies for potentially harmful content using a conservative rubric. The studies used various metrics including semantic similarity scores, uniqueness counts, helpfulness ratings, and harmfulness assessments.

## Key Results
- One-shot prompt learning achieved 70% unique and potentially useful cross-domain analogies
- LLM-generated analogies received median 4/5 helpfulness rating in creative problem reformulation tasks
- Approximately 80% of analogies led to observable changes in problem formulations
- No correlation found between a priori usefulness judgments and actual in-use helpfulness
- Upper bound of 25% potentially harmful outputs identified, mostly potentially upsetting rather than biased or toxic

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: One-shot prompt-based learning yields cross-domain analogies that are both semantically distant and potentially useful for creative problem reformulation
- Mechanism: Providing a single example in the prompt helps GPT-3 learn the structure of cross-domain analogies (matching stakeholder/context differences with goal/obstacle similarities) without overfitting to specific examples
- Core assumption: The model can generalize from one example to understand the abstract concept of cross-domain analogy
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we were able to construct one- or few-shot prompts that yielded cross-domain analogies where 70% of outputs were both unique and judged to be potentially useful"
  - [section] "Study 1: One-shot outputs were judged to be consistently more potentially useful (~80% compared to ~40%) and dissimilar (<0.5 compared to >0.7) from the input problems compared to zero-shot outputs"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the model cannot generalize from a single example, or if the example provided is too specific and causes overfitting

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LLM-generated analogies can be effectively used by humans in creative problem reformulation tasks
- Mechanism: The analogies provide new perspectives that help users reformulate problems by adding new considerations or shifting problem perspectives
- Core assumption: Humans can process and integrate LLM-generated analogies into their problem-solving process even if the analogies are not perfect matches
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "LLM-generated cross-domain analogies were frequently judged as helpful in the context of a problem reformulation task (median 4 out of 5 helpfulness rating), and frequently (~80% of cases) led to observable changes in problem formulations"
  - [section] "We observed a statistically significant positive correlation between reformulation responses coded as having an addition and helpfulness ratings for the corresponding problem-analogy pair"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains related work on LLM analogical reasoning but not direct evidence for this specific usage mechanism
- Break condition: If users cannot understand or integrate the analogies into their problem-solving process, or if the analogies are too dissimilar to be useful

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: There is little correlation between a priori judgments of potential usefulness and actual in-use helpfulness of analogies
- Mechanism: The usefulness of analogies depends on the specific context and user's cognitive state during the creative task, which cannot be fully predicted beforehand
- Core assumption: The creative process is highly contextual and dynamic, making pre-judgment of usefulness unreliable
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "there was no correlation between a priori usefulness judgments in Study 1 and the in-use helpfulness ratings and reformulation behaviors in Study 2"
  - [section] "We observed no statistically significant correlation between a priori usable ratings and helpfulness ratings: point-biserial = -0.0266, p = 0.6939"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains related work on LLM evaluation but not specific evidence for this mechanism
- Break condition: If the creative task context is highly predictable or if user cognitive states are highly consistent across different tasks

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Design problems → GPT-3 model with prompts → Analogy generation → Human evaluation (MTurk raters, study participants, researchers) → Analysis
- Critical path: Problem formulation → Prompt generation → Analogy generation → Human evaluation → User task with analogies → Analysis of usefulness and harmfulness
- Design tradeoffs: Zero-shot vs. few-shot learning (simplicity vs. performance), automated vs. human evaluation (scalability vs. depth), prompt complexity vs. model performance
- Failure signatures: Low uniqueness rate (indicates overfitting), low helpfulness ratings (indicates poor analogy quality or mismatch with task), high harmful content rate (indicates safety issues)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test different numbers of examples in few-shot learning to find optimal balance between performance and prompt size
  2. Implement a hybrid approach combining LLM generation with structured knowledge bases to improve depth of analogies
  3. Develop an iterative prompt refinement system that incorporates user reformulation data to improve subsequent analogy generation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can LLMs be effectively integrated into iterative design processes to support creative problem reformulation beyond the static, one-shot prompt-based learning paradigm explored in this study?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss the limitations of their static prompt approach and suggest exploring more iterative methods, such as using user reformulations as subsequent prompts or providing more personalized support.
- Why unresolved: The study only examined static prompt-based learning. The potential benefits and challenges of iterative LLM-assisted analogical reasoning remain unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the effectiveness of iterative vs. static LLM-generated analogies in creative problem reformulation tasks, including measures of insightfulness, novelty, and usefulness of generated ideas.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the positionality of LLM output screeners (e.g., cultural background, demographics) influence the identification of potentially harmful or toxic content in LLM-generated analogies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge their own positionality as US-based researchers and note that different positionalities may lead to different conclusions about the harmfulness of LLM outputs. They also mention the limitations of automated harm detection tools.
- Why unresolved: The study used a small, diverse group of screeners, but the impact of their individual positionality on the results is not fully explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study with a larger, more diverse group of screeners from various cultural backgrounds, comparing their ratings of potentially harmful content in LLM-generated analogies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can LLM-generated analogies be used not only to inspire creative problem reformulation but also to facilitate communication and explanation of design concepts to others, particularly those from different domains?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention the potential for LLMs to assist with explaining unfamiliar concepts using analogies, citing examples from scientific communication. They also discuss the possibility of using LLM-generated analogies to connect design concepts to others' domain knowledge.
- Why unresolved: The study focused on using LLM-generated analogies for individual problem reformulation. The potential for using them in collaborative or explanatory contexts is not explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies examining the effectiveness of LLM-generated analogies in facilitating communication and understanding of design concepts between individuals from different domains, compared to traditional explanation methods.

## Limitations
- Controlled experimental setting may not fully capture real-world creative problem-solving complexity
- Relatively modest sample sizes in user studies (110 participants) limit generalizability
- Focus on specific design problems restricts applicability to other creative domains

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The finding that one-shot prompting achieves 70% unique and potentially useful analogies is well-supported by Study 1's systematic evaluation with multiple raters
- **Medium confidence**: The helpfulness ratings (median 4/5) and reformulation impacts (~80% leading to changes) are based on a single user study and may be influenced by novelty effects
- **Medium confidence**: The upper bound of 25% potentially harmful outputs is concerning but based on conservative interpretation of potentially upsetting content

## Next Checks
1. **Ecological validity test**: Conduct a longitudinal field study with professional designers/creatives using LLM-generated analogies over multiple weeks, comparing outcomes against traditional analogical reasoning methods

2. **Generalization assessment**: Test the prompting approach across 20+ diverse problem domains beyond design problems to establish robustness and identify domain-specific failure patterns

3. **Bias and safety expansion**: Implement and evaluate enhanced filtering mechanisms specifically targeting harmful analogies, measuring whether safety interventions reduce useful outputs and quantifying the tradeoff curve
