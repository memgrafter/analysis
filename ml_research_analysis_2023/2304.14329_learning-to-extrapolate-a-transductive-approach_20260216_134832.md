# Learning to Extrapolate: A Transductive Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.14329
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14329
- **Reference count**: 40
- **One-line primary result**: Proposes a transductive approach that reparameterizes OOS extrapolation as within-support combinatorial generalization, demonstrating strong empirical results across regression, grasping, and sequential decision-making tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the fundamental challenge of machine learning models failing to generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) test data, specifically when the test data's support lies outside the training data's support (OOS). The authors propose a transductive approach that converts the OOS extrapolation problem into a within-support combinatorial generalization problem by comparing OOS test points to anchor points in the training data. The method represents the difference between test points and anchor points, then models the prediction as a bilinear function of this difference and the anchor point. The approach leverages low-rank structure in the data and demonstrates strong empirical results on various tasks, outperforming standard inductive methods and other baselines in OOS settings.

## Method Summary
The method reparameterizes the prediction function from h(x) to h(∆x, x'), where ∆x = x - x' is the difference between a test point and an anchor point from the training set. This converts the OOS problem into an OOC (out-of-combination) problem where both ∆x and x' are individually in-support, though their combination is not. The transductive predictor is modeled as a bilinear function h(∆x, x') = ⟨f(∆x), g(x')⟩, where f and g map their inputs into vector spaces of the same dimension. This bilinear form implicitly enforces a low-rank constraint that, under certain coverage conditions, allows accurate predictions for novel combinations of (∆x, x'). The method assumes bounded combinatorial density ratio between training and test distributions.

## Key Results
- Outperforms standard inductive methods and other baselines in OOS settings across regression, grasping, and sequential decision-making tasks
- Demonstrates strong empirical results particularly when ground-truth function exhibits certain symmetries or when training data provides sufficient coverage of combinatorial space
- Successfully extrapolates to periodic patterns, rotation angles, and spatial goals beyond training distribution support

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Transduction converts OOS extrapolation into in-support combinatorial generalization by representing test points relative to anchor points from training data.
- **Mechanism**: The method reparameterizes the prediction function from h(x) to h(∆x, x'), where ∆x = x - x' is the difference between a test point and an anchor point from the training set. This converts the OOS problem into an OOC problem where both ∆x and x' are individually in-support.
- **Core assumption**: The input space has group structure with well-defined addition and subtraction operators, and there exists an anchor point x' such that ∆x = x - x' is close to a difference observed in the training data.
- **Break condition**: If no suitable anchor point exists such that ∆x is within the training distribution's coverage, or if the input space lacks the required group structure for meaningful subtraction.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Low-rank bilinear structure enables extrapolation in the OOC regime by capturing the relationship between differences and anchor points.
- **Mechanism**: The transductive predictor is modeled as a bilinear function h(∆x, x') = ⟨f(∆x), g(x')⟩, where f and g map their inputs into vector spaces of the same dimension. This bilinear form implicitly enforces a low-rank constraint.
- **Core assumption**: The ground-truth function has a low-rank bilinear structure, meaning h⋆(x) = ⟨f⋆(∆x), g⋆(x')⟩ for some low-dimensional embeddings f⋆ and g⋆.
- **Break condition**: If the ground-truth function does not have low-rank structure, or if the training data does not provide sufficient coverage of the combinatorial space of differences and anchor points.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The bounded combinatorial density ratio assumption ensures that the training distribution covers the marginal distributions of differences and anchor points sufficiently to enable extrapolation.
- **Mechanism**: Assumption 3.1 requires that the test distribution has a κ-bounded combinatorial density ratio with respect to the training distribution. This means that while the joint distribution of (∆x, x') at test time may be novel, the marginal distributions are individually covered by the training data.
- **Core assumption**: There exist distributions over differences and anchor points such that the test distribution can be expressed as a combination of these marginals, and the training distribution covers all but the bottom-right block of this combinatorial matrix.
- **Break condition**: If the test distribution's marginals are not sufficiently covered by the training distribution, or if the coverage is too sparse to satisfy the bounded density ratio condition.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Group structure in input space
  - **Why needed here**: The method requires well-defined addition and subtraction operators to compute differences ∆x = x - x', which is fundamental to the transduction approach.
  - **Quick check question**: Can you identify whether your input space has a group structure? For example, can you meaningfully subtract two points in your domain and get a difference that has semantic meaning?

- **Concept**: Low-rank matrix factorization and completion
  - **Why needed here**: The bilinear representation h(∆x, x') = ⟨f(∆x), g(x')⟩ is mathematically equivalent to a low-rank matrix factorization, and the extrapolation guarantee relies on results from matrix completion theory.
  - **Quick check question**: If you were to represent your problem as a matrix where rows are differences and columns are anchor points, would this matrix have low rank? Can you estimate the rank from your data?

- **Concept**: Transductive learning vs. inductive learning
  - **Why needed here**: The method fundamentally changes the learning paradigm from making predictions based solely on learned parameters (inductive) to making predictions based on relationships to training examples (transductive).
  - **Quick check question**: In your current approach, do predictions for new data points depend on the specific training examples, or only on learned parameters? How would your system change if you had to explicitly compare new inputs to training data?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Input preprocessing -> Compute ∆x = x - x' -> Bilinear embedding networks f(∆x) and g(x') -> Dot product layer -> Anchor selection module -> Loss function
- **Critical path**: 1. During training: Sample pairs (x_i, x_j) from training data, compute ∆x_ij = x_i - x_j, predict y_i using h(∆x_ij, x_j) 2. During testing: For test point x_test, find anchor x' with ∆x = x_test - x' close to training differences, predict using h(∆x, x') 3. Model optimization: Train bilinear embeddings to minimize prediction error on transduced training pairs
- **Design tradeoffs**: Embedding dimension vs. expressiveness (higher dimensions allow more complex relationships but may overfit), Anchor selection radius ρ (larger radius increases coverage but may include less relevant differences), Pair sampling strategy (uniform sampling vs. weighted sampling based on similarity or learned weights), Computational cost (O(n²) training pairs vs. O(n) for standard methods)
- **Failure signatures**: Poor performance when test points require differences far outside training distribution coverage, Degradation when ground-truth function lacks low-rank structure, Instability when anchor selection radius is too large or too small, Overfitting when embedding dimension is too high relative to data size
- **First 3 experiments**: 1. Synthetic periodic function: Train on [20, 40], test on [10, 20] ∪ [40, 50] to verify extrapolation to periodic patterns 2. Grasp point prediction: Train on object orientations [0, 1.2π], test on [1.2π, 2π] to verify rotation extrapolation 3. Goal-reaching: Train on goals in one quadrant, test on goals in opposite quadrant to verify spatial extrapolation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can the proposed transductive approach be extended to handle scenarios where the test distribution is not simply an out-of-support shift but also involves a different density ratio compared to the training distribution?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper mentions that the approach focuses on out-of-support (OOS) scenarios where the test distribution's support is outside the training distribution's support. It does not explicitly address scenarios where the density ratio between the training and test distributions is also different.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper's theoretical analysis and empirical results primarily focus on OOS settings. Extending the approach to handle varying density ratios would require additional theoretical development and empirical validation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Theoretical analysis showing how the transductive approach can be adapted to handle varying density ratios, along with empirical results demonstrating improved performance in such scenarios compared to existing methods.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What are the limitations of the bilinear embedding approach in capturing complex relationships between input features and target variables, especially in high-dimensional spaces?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper assumes a low-rank structure in the data for the bilinear embeddings to be effective. However, it does not explicitly discuss the limitations of this approach in capturing complex, high-dimensional relationships that may not exhibit low-rank structure.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper's empirical results demonstrate the effectiveness of the bilinear approach in various domains, but it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of its limitations in handling complex, high-dimensional data.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical studies comparing the performance of the bilinear approach with other methods on high-dimensional datasets with varying levels of complexity, and theoretical analysis of the conditions under which the bilinear approach may fail to capture complex relationships.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can the proposed transductive approach be adapted to handle sequential decision-making tasks with continuous action spaces, such as robotic control problems?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of the transductive approach in goal-conditioned imitation learning tasks with discrete action spaces. However, it does not explicitly address the challenges of applying the approach to continuous action spaces.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper's empirical results focus on discrete action spaces, and the theoretical analysis does not provide insights into adapting the approach to continuous action spaces.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical results demonstrating the effectiveness of the transductive approach in continuous action space tasks, along with theoretical analysis of the modifications required to handle continuous action spaces.

## Limitations
- Requires input space with well-defined group structure and meaningful subtraction operations
- Depends critically on training data providing sufficient coverage of combinatorial space of differences and anchor points
- O(n²) computational complexity during training poses practical challenges for large datasets

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The mechanism of converting OOS problems to OOC combinatorial generalization through transductive reparameterization

**Medium confidence**: The effectiveness of low-rank bilinear structure for extrapolation, as this depends on specific data properties

**Medium confidence**: The bounded combinatorial density ratio assumption enabling theoretical guarantees, which may be difficult to verify in practice

## Next Checks

1. **Structure verification test**: Systematically evaluate the method on domains with varying degrees of group structure (e.g., translations, rotations, periodic functions) to quantify how the presence/absence of structure affects performance.

2. **Coverage sensitivity analysis**: Design experiments that vary the coverage of training data in the combinatorial space of differences and anchor points, measuring the relationship between coverage density and extrapolation accuracy.

3. **Low-rank assumption validation**: Apply singular value decomposition to the matrix of training outputs to empirically estimate the rank, then correlate this with the method's performance across different datasets to verify the low-rank assumption's practical importance.
