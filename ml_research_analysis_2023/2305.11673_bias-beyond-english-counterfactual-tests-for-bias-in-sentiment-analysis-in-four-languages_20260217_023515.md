# Bias Beyond English: Counterfactual Tests for Bias in Sentiment Analysis in Four Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11673
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11673
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: This paper develops a multilingual counterfactual evaluation dataset for gender and racial bias in sentiment analysis systems across four languages (Japanese, Chinese, Spanish, and German). The authors create counterfactual sentence pairs that differ only in demographic variables and measure how sentiment analysis models respond to these changes. Their experiments compare baseline SVM models against transformer models with and without pretraining, finding that pretrained models exhibit less bias than baseline models across all languages. The analysis reveals that while pretrained models show many small nuanced changes in bias behavior, baseline models exhibit more extreme shifts in sentiment predictions under counterfactual conditions. The authors release their code and evaluation corpora to support further research on bias in non-English sentiment analysis systems.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a multilingual counterfactual evaluation methodology for detecting gender and racial bias in sentiment analysis systems across four languages: Japanese, Chinese, Spanish, and German. The authors create template-based counterfactual sentence pairs that differ only in demographic variables, enabling causal attribution of sentiment changes to specific bias sources. Their experiments demonstrate that transformer models with pretraining exhibit significantly less bias than baseline SVM models across all languages, with pretrained models showing many small nuanced changes rather than the extreme label flips characteristic of baseline models.

## Method Summary
The methodology employs template-based counterfactual sentence generation to create pairs of sentences that differ only in demographic terms (e.g., changing "Korean person" to "Japanese person"). The authors compare baseline SVM models against transformer models (BERT-base and DistilBERT) with and without pretraining using the multilingual Amazon Reviews Corpus (MARC) for training. Evaluation uses both aggregate metrics (mean and variance of sentiment score differences between counterfactual pairs) and confusion matrices showing predictions for privileged versus minoritized group scores. The approach enables granular analysis of bias patterns by comparing model predictions on counterfactual pairs, revealing whether bias manifests as consistent label flips or more varied behavior.

## Key Results
- Pretrained transformer models exhibit less bias than baseline SVM models across all four languages (Japanese, Chinese, Spanish, German)
- Baseline models show consistent extreme shifts in sentiment predictions (e.g., neutral to very negative) under counterfactual conditions
- Pretrained models demonstrate many small nuanced changes in bias behavior rather than extreme shifts
- The methodology enables granular analysis of bias patterns through confusion matrix visualization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Counterfactual evaluation isolates the causal effect of demographic variables on model predictions by controlling for all other factors. The evaluation creates pairs of sentences that differ only in a single demographic term (e.g., changing "Korean person" to "Japanese person"). By comparing model predictions on these pairs, researchers can attribute any sentiment changes specifically to demographic bias rather than other confounding factors. The template-based sentence generation process successfully creates natural-sounding sentences that differ only in the targeted demographic variable, addressing the potential confound of unnatural text.

### Mechanism 2
Pretrained transformer models reduce bias compared to baseline SVM models by learning richer, more nuanced representations from larger corpora. Pretrained models like BERT-base learn contextualized embeddings from massive text corpora, which may capture more subtle language patterns and reduce reliance on spurious correlations that lead to bias. The fine-tuning process then adapts these representations to the sentiment analysis task. However, if the pretraining corpus itself contains significant demographic biases, the model may simply transfer those biases rather than eliminating them.

### Mechanism 3
Counterfactual evaluation enables granular analysis of bias patterns that aggregate metrics cannot capture. By creating confusion matrices comparing predictions on counterfactual pairs, researchers can see specific patterns like consistent label flips (e.g., neutral to very negative) that would be averaged out in aggregate bias metrics. This reveals whether bias manifests as extreme shifts or many small changes. The confusion matrix visualization provides meaningful insights into bias patterns that can guide mitigation strategies, though this analysis method may become less useful if the bias patterns are too complex or numerous to be meaningfully visualized.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Counterfactual evaluation
  - Why needed here: This is the core methodology for isolating demographic bias in sentiment analysis models. Without it, researchers cannot determine whether sentiment changes are due to demographic factors or other confounds.
  - Quick check question: If a model gives different sentiment scores to "The conversation with that Korean person was irritating" versus "The conversation with that Japanese person was irritating," what can we conclude about the model's bias?

- Concept: Pretraining and fine-tuning
  - Why needed here: Understanding how pretraining affects bias requires knowledge of how language models are trained and adapted to specific tasks. This explains why pretrained models might show different bias patterns than baseline models.
  - Quick check question: Why might a model pretrained on general web text show less bias than one trained only on sentiment analysis data?

- Concept: Template-based sentence generation
  - Why needed here: The evaluation relies on creating natural-sounding counterfactual sentence pairs. Understanding this process is crucial for interpreting the results and potentially extending the methodology to other languages or bias types.
  - Quick check question: What challenges arise when translating sentiment analysis templates to languages with different grammatical structures?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Template-based sentence generation -> Model prediction -> Confusion matrix creation -> Aggregate bias calculation -> Analysis of bias patterns
- Critical path: Sentence generation → Model prediction → Confusion matrix creation → Aggregate bias calculation → Analysis of bias patterns
- Design tradeoffs: Using templates ensures controlled experiments but may limit naturalness; using pretrained models reduces bias but increases computational cost; counterfactual evaluation provides causal insights but requires careful template design
- Failure signatures: Unnatural sentences in evaluation data, extreme bias in baseline models but not pretrained models, or confusion matrices showing no discernible patterns despite significant aggregate bias
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the counterfactual evaluation on a pretrained model and a baseline model using a small subset of the dataset to verify the methodology works as expected
  2. Compare confusion matrices between models to identify specific bias patterns before calculating aggregate metrics
  3. Test the sensitivity of results to different template variations to ensure the evaluation is robust to template design choices

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Do transformer-based models with pretraining consistently reduce bias across all tested languages, or are there language-specific factors that influence this effect? The paper shows that pretrained models exhibit less bias than baseline models across all languages, but notes that the patterns of bias reduction vary between languages and model types, suggesting underlying linguistic or cultural factors may influence how pretraining affects bias. Comparative analysis of bias reduction patterns across different language families, controlled experiments varying pretraining corpus composition, or analysis of how linguistic features interact with bias reduction mechanisms would resolve this question.

### Open Question 2
How does the relationship between sentiment analysis bias and training data size vary across different languages and demographic groups? The paper contrasts two training approaches but doesn't investigate whether increasing task-specific training data alone would reduce bias, or how this relationship differs across languages and demographic groups. Experiments varying training data sizes for both baseline and pretrained models across all languages, analysis of how bias reduction scales with data volume, and comparison of efficiency between pretraining and task-specific data augmentation would address this question.

### Open Question 3
What specific linguistic features in different languages make certain types of bias more prevalent or more difficult to detect? The paper notes that Chinese requires "no special handling" due to lack of gender agreement or inflectional morphology, while German requires handling gender agreement and noun declension, and Spanish requires handling idiomatic verb usage. Comparative analysis of bias patterns across languages with different morphological features, experiments testing whether certain linguistic constructions amplify or mask bias, and investigation of how template design choices interact with language-specific features to affect bias detection would resolve this question.

## Limitations
- The evaluation methodology relies on template-based counterfactual generation, which may not fully capture the complexity of natural language bias
- The study focuses on a limited set of demographic variables (gender and racial/immigrant bias), potentially missing other important bias dimensions
- The naturalness of generated sentences across different languages remains uncertain, particularly for languages with complex grammatical structures

## Confidence
- High confidence in the finding that pretrained models exhibit less bias than baseline models, supported by consistent results across all four languages and multiple evaluation metrics
- Medium confidence in the specific bias patterns identified through confusion matrix analysis, as these depend on the quality and representativeness of the template-based evaluation data
- Medium confidence in the generalizability of results to other languages and bias types, given the limited language coverage and focus on only two bias dimensions

## Next Checks
1. **Naturalness validation**: Conduct human evaluation studies to assess whether the counterfactual sentence pairs sound natural to native speakers and whether they successfully isolate the targeted demographic variables without introducing confounding factors
2. **Cross-linguistic robustness**: Extend the evaluation methodology to additional languages and bias types (e.g., religious, socioeconomic) to test whether the observed patterns hold across broader linguistic and demographic contexts
3. **Bias pattern verification**: Compare the confusion matrix-based bias analysis with alternative evaluation approaches, such as human-annotated bias assessments or adversarial testing, to validate that the observed patterns accurately reflect real-world bias manifestations
