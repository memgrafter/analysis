# Matching Exemplar as Next Sentence Prediction (MeNSP): Zero-shot Prompt Learning for Automatic Scoring in Science Education

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.08771
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08771
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Zero-shot approach reformulates scoring as NSP task using exemplars as prompts, achieving Kappa scores from 0.30 to 0.57 and F1 scores from 0.54 to 0.81 across three science argumentation tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MeNSP, a zero-shot approach for automatic scoring of student-written responses in science education. The method reformulates scoring as a next sentence prediction task using exemplars as prompts, eliminating the need for labeled training data. Experiments on three argumentation tasks show promising results, with Cohen's Kappa scores ranging from 0.30 to 0.57 and F1 scores from 0.54 to 0.81. The few-shot setting further improves performance on easier tasks, with Kappa increasing from 0.30 to 0.38 and F1 from 0.54 to 0.59. The study demonstrates the potential of prompt learning in reducing the cost of model training for educational assessments.

## Method Summary
MeNSP reformulates automatic scoring as a next sentence prediction task by concatenating student responses with exemplar responses at each grading level and using the NSP head of a pre-trained language model to determine semantic matching. The approach includes a zero-score filter that computes cosine similarity between response and exemplar embeddings to identify invalid responses. For few-shot learning, the method either randomly samples real student responses or constructs augmented responses similar to exemplars to provide additional context for the NSP scoring process.

## Key Results
- Zero-shot scoring achieves Cohen's Kappa scores of 0.30-0.57 and F1 scores of 0.54-0.81 across three science argumentation tasks
- Few-shot learning improves performance on easier tasks (G4), with Kappa increasing from 0.30 to 0.38 and F1 from 0.54 to 0.59
- The zero-score filtering mechanism effectively identifies invalid responses by comparing cosine similarity against a threshold computed from exemplar pairs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Reformulating scoring as Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) bypasses the need for fine-tuning by leveraging pre-trained NSP capabilities of PLMs.
- Mechanism: The model concatenates a student response with exemplar responses at each grading level and uses the NSP head to determine if the pair belongs to the same context, with the highest-scoring pair indicating the grade.
- Core assumption: The NSP task learned during pre-training is sufficiently general to capture semantic similarity between student responses and exemplar responses across diverse science argumentation contexts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "aligning the scoring procedure as the next sentence prediction task using prompts can skip the costly fine-tuning stage"
  - [section] "we propose reforming the scoring procedure as the Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) task [5], which requires PLMs to determine whether the given two texts come from the same context"
- Break condition: If the NSP head's learned representations are too domain-specific or if the exemplars are not sufficiently representative of the grading rubric, the matching performance will degrade significantly.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Zero-score identification via cosine distance between response and exemplar embeddings prevents misclassification of low-quality responses.
- Mechanism: Before applying NSP, the model computes cosine similarity between the student response embedding and the exemplar for grade 2; if below a threshold (average of cos(0,2) and cos(1,2)), it assigns a zero score directly.
- Core assumption: Zero-point responses are semantically distant from high-quality exemplars, and the threshold computed from exemplar pairs captures this separation reliably.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Since the zero-point response is very different from the perfect response, the distance between the vectors of them should be far away"
  - [section] "If the cosine similarity between them is smaller than a threshold, we directly grade them with zero point"
- Break condition: If the threshold is too high, valid responses may be incorrectly classified as zero; if too low, invalid responses may slip through.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Diversity in few-shot samples improves model performance more than manual exemplar quality.
- Mechanism: Random sampling from real student responses provides varied linguistic patterns and error types, enabling the model to generalize better than when trained on manually crafted responses that mimic exemplars.
- Core assumption: Real student responses contain natural variability that better represents the target distribution than augmented examples designed to fit rubrics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "we observe an improvement of Kappa on G5 from 0.59 to 0.61 with random seed 55301... a possible speculation of the successful improvement with these random seeds is that the sample responses selected for training are diverse enough"
  - [section] "the random strategy extracts samples from the real student responses, the manual strategy provides augmented student responses similar to the exemplars"
- Break condition: If the dataset lacks sufficient diversity even in random samples, or if the few-shot setting is too constrained, diversity may not translate to improved generalization.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs) and their adaptation via prompting
  - Why needed here: MeNSP relies on the NSP head of a PLM to perform scoring without fine-tuning, so understanding how PLMs encode semantic relationships is essential.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between MLM and NSP tasks in BERT, and why is NSP more suitable for exemplar matching in this context?

- Concept: Zero-shot and few-shot learning paradigms
  - Why needed here: The study explicitly compares zero-shot (no training samples) and few-shot (1-3 samples per class) performance, so understanding how these paradigms affect model behavior is critical.
  - Quick check question: In what ways does few-shot learning improve performance over zero-shot, and under what conditions might it fail to help?

- Concept: Scoring rubrics and exemplar-based assessment design
  - Why needed here: The approach requires constructing exemplars at each grading level from rubrics, so familiarity with rubric decomposition and exemplar generation is necessary.
  - Quick check question: How would you systematically convert a multi-level rubric into exemplar responses that can be used for automated scoring?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Student response -> Zero-score filter -> NSP scorer -> Grade output
- Critical path: Student response → zero-score filter → (if not zero) → NSP scorer → grade output
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using NSP instead of MLM avoids template complexity but requires exemplars at each level.
  - Zero-score filtering adds a preprocessing step that may reject valid responses if threshold is misestimated.
  - Few-shot tuning improves performance on simpler tasks but may overfit or underperform on complex ones.
- Failure signatures:
  - All responses scored as zero → threshold too high or embeddings poorly aligned.
  - Random grade assignment → NSP head not capturing semantic similarity or exemplars not representative.
  - Inconsistent grading across runs → randomness in few-shot selection not controlled or seed management issues.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate zero-score filter: Run cosine similarity on a held-out set of known zero and non-zero responses and plot ROC curve to tune threshold.
  2. Baseline NSP matching: Test NSP scoring with perfect exemplars only (no zero filter) to isolate NSP performance.
  3. Few-shot ablation: Compare performance using random vs. manual samples with controlled diversity metrics (e.g., lexical overlap, semantic distance).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the upper limit of complexity for science argumentation tasks that MeNSP can effectively score without significant performance degradation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that MeNSP's performance improves on lower-complexity items (G4) but does not improve on higher-complexity items (G5, G6) with few-shot training.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests three items with different complexity levels. A broader range of tasks would be needed to establish performance boundaries.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing MeNSP on a diverse set of science argumentation tasks spanning multiple complexity levels, measuring performance thresholds where Kappa scores fall below acceptable benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific characteristics of sample responses (beyond diversity) most strongly influence MeNSP's few-shot learning performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests diversity may be a factor but does not systematically analyze other characteristics like length, vocabulary range, or argumentation structure.
- Why unresolved: The paper only compares random versus manually constructed samples without deeper analysis of sample features.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying sample characteristics (length, vocabulary diversity, argument structure complexity) while measuring their impact on MeNSP performance across different tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does MeNSP's zero-shot performance compare to traditional fine-tuned models when limited labeled data is available?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates MeNSP's zero-shot capability but does not directly compare it to traditional fine-tuned models trained on equivalent amounts of data.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on MeNSP's advantages but doesn't benchmark against traditional approaches under comparable data constraints.
- What evidence would resolve it: Head-to-head comparison of MeNSP zero-shot performance versus traditional fine-tuning on identical datasets with varying amounts of labeled data (1-shot, 3-shot, 10-shot scenarios).

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the optimal number of exemplars needed per scoring level for MeNSP to achieve maximum performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses one exemplar per scoring level but doesn't explore whether multiple exemplars per level would improve performance.
- Why unresolved: The study uses a single exemplar per level without investigating whether multiple exemplars provide better coverage of scoring rubric interpretations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing MeNSP with varying numbers of exemplars per scoring level (1, 3, 5, 10) to determine performance saturation points and optimal exemplar-to-level ratios.

## Limitations
- Limited generalizability beyond science education argumentation tasks to other subjects and response types
- Exemplar construction process lacks detailed guidelines, affecting reproducibility
- Zero-score filtering threshold stability across different datasets and domains is unexplored

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The zero-shot scoring mechanism using NSP matching with exemplars is technically sound and the experimental results are reproducible.
- Medium Confidence: The effectiveness of the zero-score filtering mechanism and the few-shot learning improvements.
- Low Confidence: The generalizability of MeNSP to other educational domains and assessment types.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-domain validation: Test MeNSP on student responses from different educational domains (e.g., mathematics, humanities, language learning) to assess generalizability.
2. Exemplar quality analysis: Systematically vary the quality and diversity of exemplars at each grading level to determine sensitivity to exemplar construction quality.
3. Threshold stability assessment: Evaluate the robustness of the zero-score filtering threshold by testing it across multiple datasets and educational contexts.
