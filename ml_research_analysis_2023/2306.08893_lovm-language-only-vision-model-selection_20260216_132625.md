# LOVM: Language-Only Vision Model Selection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08893
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08893
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: LOVM introduces a text-only approach for selecting vision-language models, achieving top-5 recall of 0.539 and mean absolute error of 0.145 for performance prediction, outperforming ImageNet baselines by ~15% and ~36% respectively.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LOVM, a new task and benchmark for selecting vision-language models (VLMs) based only on text descriptions of downstream tasks, without requiring access to the actual data. The authors propose using large language models to generate synthetic text datasets and then compute various text-derived scores (e.g., text classification accuracy, Fisher criterion, Silhouette score, Class Dispersion, and Synonym Consistency) to predict VLM performance. They evaluate 35 VLMs on 23 diverse datasets and show that their text-based baselines outperform a simple ImageNet benchmark baseline for both model ranking and performance prediction.

## Method Summary
The LOVM approach uses LLMs (GPT-3.5) to generate synthetic text datasets based on task descriptions, then extracts text embeddings using VLM text encoders. Various granularity scores are computed from these embeddings (Fisher criterion, Silhouette score, Class Dispersion, Synonym Consistency) to quantify inter-class and intra-class similarity. These scores, along with classification accuracy on the synthetic dataset, are used as features in linear regression models to predict VLM performance on the target vision tasks. The method is evaluated on 35 VLMs across 23 datasets, comparing performance prediction accuracy and model ranking capability against baselines.

## Key Results
- LOVM achieves top-5 recall of 0.539 and mean absolute error of 0.145 for performance prediction
- Text-based methods outperform ImageNet baseline by ~15% (recall) and ~36% (MAE)
- Classification accuracy and Fisher criterion are the most effective features for LOVM tasks
- Different VLMs show varying effectiveness on different tasks, with no single model dominating across all datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Text embeddings can serve as proxies for image embeddings in VLMs due to cross-modality transferability.
- Mechanism: The "modality gap" between text and image embeddings in VLMs is orthogonal to the decision boundaries, enabling transferable performance prediction.
- Core assumption: The modality gap is consistent across different VLMs and datasets, and can be approximated as a constant orthogonal shift.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Liang et al. [2022] has shown that there exists a modality gap between text and image embeddings generated from VLMs, the geometry of this modality gap permits cross-modality transferability."
  - [section]: "Recent studies have demonstrated that text embeddings from VLMs can be used as a proxy for their corresponding image embeddings in various downstream tasks... This phenomenon allows text to serve as a proxy to corresponding images and vice versa."
- Break condition: If the modality gap varies significantly across VLMs or datasets, or if the gap is not orthogonal to decision boundaries, the transferability breaks down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Text-based granularity scores (Fisher criterion, Silhouette score, Class Dispersion, Synonym Consistency) correlate with VLM performance on vision tasks.
- Mechanism: These scores quantify inter-class and intra-class similarity in the embedding space, which reflects the VLM's ability to separate and cluster classes in the target task.
- Core assumption: The embedding space geometry (inter-class and intra-class distances) is predictive of downstream task performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Fisher criterion measures the degree of similarity of the classes or the extent of their separation... Silhouette score is a well-established metric used to quantify the tightness of the same-class samples to the separation of different-class samples... Class Dispersion score quantifies the degree of same-class tightness or data cone radius."
  - [section]: "we can identify a clear trend of Fisher criterion and Silhouette score improving with model size, while Class Dispersion score and Synonym Consistency score degrade with model size."
- Break condition: If the embedding space geometry does not correlate with task performance, or if the scores do not capture relevant aspects of the task difficulty, the prediction fails.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLM-generated text datasets can approximate real image data for VLM evaluation.
- Mechanism: LLMs can generate realistic captions for images containing target classes, and these captions can be used to extract text embeddings that approximate the image embeddings.
- Core assumption: LLM-generated captions are sufficiently diverse and representative of real images to capture the relevant visual features.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "In our study, we employ GPT-3.5, tasked to produce two distinct text-based datasets, each corresponding to a given vision task."
  - [section]: "To generate the captions dataset, Dcap, we prompt an LLM to generate realistic - but confusing - captions for images containing the user-provided classes in the user-provided domain."
- Break condition: If LLM-generated captions are not diverse or representative enough, or if they introduce bias, the approximation breaks down.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: LLM text generation (GPT-3.5) -> VLM text encoder (from open-clip library) -> Text dataset generation (captions and synonyms) -> Granularity score computation (Fisher, Silhouette, Class Dispersion, Synonym Consistency) -> Linear regression model for performance prediction
- Critical path: Text generation → Text embedding extraction → Granularity score computation → Linear regression → Performance prediction
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using text instead of images reduces data requirements but may introduce approximation errors
  - LLM-generated text may not be as diverse or representative as real images
  - Granularity scores may not capture all aspects of task difficulty
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor correlation between predicted and actual performance
  - High variance in predictions across different VLMs or datasets
  - Inability to capture domain shifts or distribution changes
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Generate text datasets for a simple vision task (e.g., MNIST) and compare predicted vs. actual VLM performance
  2. Vary the noise level in text embeddings and observe its effect on performance prediction accuracy
  3. Compare the effectiveness of different granularity scores in predicting VLM performance on a diverse set of tasks

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can text-based methods be improved to better capture distribution shifts between datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] from discussion of domain shifts in ImageNet variants and limitations of text-only methods
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that current text-only methods struggle to capture dataset difficulty when there are distribution shifts, and suggests that incorporating unlabeled test images may be necessary
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of hybrid methods that combine text-based features with minimal image-based evaluations to better predict performance across shifted distributions

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number and type of features/metrics for LOVM methods to achieve better model ranking and performance prediction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] from ablation experiments showing that using more than ~3 features seldom improves performance
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that different combinations of features have varying effectiveness, but does not identify an optimal set or explore more sophisticated models that might utilize more features
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of various feature combinations and machine learning models to identify the most effective approach for LOVM tasks

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do LOVM methods perform when applied to fine-tuned or linear probed models rather than zero-shot VLMs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] from limitations section stating that LOVM methods cannot make differential predictions without access to fine-tuning data
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses exclusively on zero-shot tasks due to LOVM's design, but acknowledges that some correlation exists between pre and post fine-tuning performance
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluation of LOVM methods on a dataset of fine-tuned models to quantify the correlation between LOVM predictions and actual fine-tuned performance

## Limitations

- The modality-gap transferability assumption lacks direct validation across all 35 VLMs tested
- LLM-generated captions may not fully capture the visual complexity of real images, particularly for fine-grained tasks
- The study focuses on relatively coarse classification tasks, leaving questions about LOVM's effectiveness on segmentation, detection, or more complex vision tasks

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The LOVM benchmark methodology and evaluation protocol are clearly specified and reproducible
- **Medium confidence**: The correlation between text-derived scores and VLM performance holds for the tested datasets, but may not generalize to all vision tasks
- **Medium confidence**: LLM-generated text can approximate image data for coarse-grained classification, but effectiveness for fine-grained tasks remains untested
