# Reckoning with the Disagreement Problem: Explanation Consensus as a Training Objective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.13299
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13299
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Training neural networks with PEAR loss improves consensus among post-hoc feature attribution explainers while maintaining accuracy above linear baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of improving consensus among post-hoc feature attribution explainers for neural networks. The authors introduce PEAR (Post hoc Explainer Agreement Regularization) loss, which measures correlation between explanations from different explainers and adds this as a regularization term alongside standard accuracy loss. Experiments on three tabular datasets demonstrate that training with PEAR improves agreement metrics across multiple explainers while maintaining competitive accuracy, and that these improvements generalize to unseen data and explainers not used in the loss term.

## Method Summary
The PEAR method trains neural networks by adding a consensus loss term that measures the correlation between feature attribution scores from two explainers (Grad and Integrated Gradients). This loss combines Pearson and Spearman correlations to capture both raw score alignment and rank alignment. The total loss is a weighted combination of the standard cross-entropy task loss and the PEAR consensus loss, with a hyperparameter λ controlling the trade-off. The approach is evaluated on three tabular datasets using MLPs, measuring improvements in agreement metrics while maintaining accuracy above linear baselines.

## Key Results
- PEAR loss significantly improves consensus metrics (Pairwise Rank Agreement, Rank Correlation) across all three datasets while maintaining accuracy above linear baselines.
- Improvements generalize to explainers not used in the PEAR loss term (e.g., LIME and SHAP show improved agreement when model trained with Grad-IntGrad consensus).
- PEAR prevents models from placing high importance on random "junk" features, unlike baseline models which rank junk features highly.
- The consensus improvements persist on unseen test data and do not require retraining explainers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Local Linearity
- Claim: PEAR loss increases agreement between feature attribution scores by making the model's decision surface more locally linear.
- Mechanism: PEAR loss encourages Grad and IntGrad to agree, which happens when the gradient at a point equals the average gradient along a path to that point. This requires the model to behave more linearly locally.
- Core assumption: Local linearity correlates with better agreement between gradient-based explainers.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions PEAR improves consensus; section states Grad-IntGrad alignment requires local linearity.
- Break condition: If the model finds a non-linear way to make Grad and IntGrad agree (e.g., by producing uninformative explanations), the linearity benefit may not materialize.

### Mechanism 2: Generalization to Unseen Explainers
- Claim: PEAR loss generalizes to improve agreement between explainers not used in the loss term.
- Mechanism: By encouraging consensus between Grad and IntGrad, the model learns to produce smoother, more consistent decision boundaries that benefit other explainers like LIME and SHAP.
- Core assumption: Improved local linearity and smoother decision surfaces benefit multiple types of explainers.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract notes improved consensus between explainers other than those used in the loss term.
- Break condition: If the model learns to produce consensus only for the specific explainers in the loss but not others, generalization fails.

### Mechanism 3: Dual Correlation Terms
- Claim: The dual correlation terms (Pearson and Spearman) in PEAR loss capture different aspects of agreement needed for various metrics.
- Mechanism: Pearson correlation measures raw score alignment while Spearman measures rank alignment. Since different agreement metrics emphasize different aspects, combining both ensures improvement across all metrics.
- Core assumption: Different agreement metrics require different types of correlation to be optimized.
- Evidence anchors: Section states a mixture of Pearson and Spearman is appropriate since metrics involve both ranking and non-ranking.
- Break condition: If one correlation type dominates the other in the convex combination, some agreement metrics may not improve.

  - Why needed here: Understanding how to balance accuracy with explanation consensus is central to the paper's contribution.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data pipeline → Model (MLP) → Loss computation (task + consensus) → Optimizer (AdamW) → Updated model
- Critical path: 1) Forward pass through model to get predictions 2) Compute task loss (cross-entropy) 3) Generate explanations from two explainers (Grad and IntGrad) 4) Compute PEAR loss (weighted sum of Pearson and Spearman correlations) 5) Backpropagate combined loss 6) Update model weights
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using Grad and IntGrad vs other explainer pairs: Grad and IntGrad are differentiable and their agreement implies local linearity
  - Weighting between Pearson and Spearman (μ parameter): Different datasets may benefit from different weightings
  - λ parameter: Controls the accuracy-consensus trade-off; too high sacrifices accuracy, too low provides no consensus benefit
- Failure signatures:
  - Explanations become uninformative (all features get near-zero scores)
  - Model accuracy drops significantly below linear baseline
  - Consensus improves only for explainers in loss but not others
  - Explanations place high importance on random "junk" features
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train MLP on Bank Marketing with λ=0.5, μ=0.75 and compare LIME-SHAP agreement to baseline
  2. Test junk feature experiment: Add random features and measure if PEAR prevents them from being ranked highly
  3. Linear fit experiment: Measure how linear the logit surface becomes as λ increases using three-point plane interpolation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of explainer pair in the PEAR loss affect the agreement of other explainers at test time?
- Basis in paper: The paper states "Our loss is defined for any pair and perhaps another choice would better suit specific applications" and notes that they only explored training with Grad and IntGrad.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only evaluated one pair of explainers (Grad and IntGrad) in the loss term, leaving open whether different pairs would generalize differently to unseen explainers.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training PEAR with different explainer pairs (e.g., LIME-SHAP, SmoothGrad-IntGrad) and measuring how this affects agreement between all other explainer pairs at test time.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does training with PEAR actually improve human interpretability and trust in model explanations?
- Basis in paper: The authors state "Whether our proposed loss is useful in practice is not completely answered simply by showing accuracy and agreement" and note that human perspective studies are needed.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides quantitative agreement metrics, not human-centered evaluation of whether practitioners find explanations more trustworthy or interpretable when models are trained with PEAR.
- What evidence would resolve it: User studies where practitioners compare model explanations with and without PEAR training, measuring their confidence in explanations and ability to debug models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Is the improved consensus achieved by PEAR due to increased linearity of the model, or are other factors involved?
- Basis in paper: The paper shows that increasing weight decay also increases linearity but does not improve consensus, leading them to reject the hypothesis that "linearity implies more explainer consistency."
- Why unresolved: While PEAR increases both linearity and consensus, the exact mechanism connecting these remains unclear - it could involve linearity but also other aspects of how gradients change during training.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments isolating specific aspects of PEAR's effect on gradients (e.g., gradient smoothness, local linearity at individual points vs average path gradients) and testing whether these correlate more strongly with consensus than overall linearity.

## Limitations

- Scope of improvement: The method shows promise on three tabular datasets but the magnitude of improvement varies significantly across datasets.
- Mechanistic understanding: The causal mechanism linking PEAR to improved consensus through local linearity is not rigorously validated.
- Generalizability concerns: The approach focuses on Grad and Integrated Gradients, leaving open questions about optimal explainer pairs and transferability to other model types.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The experimental methodology for measuring agreement metrics is sound and the implementation of the PEAR loss function is clearly specified.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that PEAR maintains accuracy above linear baselines is supported by the data, though the cost-benefit trade-off varies by dataset.
- **Medium confidence**: The observation that PEAR generalizes to improve agreement with explainers not used in the loss term is demonstrated but could benefit from more systematic analysis.
- **Low confidence**: The proposed mechanism that local linearity is the primary driver of improved consensus lacks direct empirical validation beyond correlation.

## Next Checks

1. **Mechanistic validation**: Conduct controlled experiments varying model depth and non-linearity to test whether deeper, more non-linear models show proportionally larger consensus improvements with PEAR, directly testing the local linearity hypothesis.

2. **Explainer pair sensitivity**: Systematically test PEAR with different explainer pairs (e.g., LIME-SHAP, Grad-SHAP) to determine whether Grad-Integrated Gradients is optimal or if other pairs yield better consensus across diverse datasets.

3. **Generalization stress test**: Evaluate PEAR-trained models on out-of-distribution data and on datasets with known feature correlation structures to assess whether consensus improvements transfer beyond the training distribution and whether they prevent explanations from focusing on spurious correlations.
