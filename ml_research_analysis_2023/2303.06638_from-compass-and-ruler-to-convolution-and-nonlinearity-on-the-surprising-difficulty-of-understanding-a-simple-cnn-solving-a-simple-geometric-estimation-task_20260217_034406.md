# From Compass and Ruler to Convolution and Nonlinearity: On the Surprising Difficulty of Understanding a Simple CNN Solving a Simple Geometric Estimation Task

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.06638
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06638
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Simple CNNs struggle to learn radius estimation from both positive and negative polarities due to ReLU's limited expressiveness

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates a fundamental question about neural network understanding by studying a simple geometric estimation task: estimating the radius of a centred pulse in 1D or disk in 2D images using a minimal CNN architecture. The authors theoretically analyze and design network weights, revealing that ReLU nonlinearity's limited expressiveness prevents learning the radius concept when both positive and negative polarities are present in the data. The study demonstrates that invariance to intensity differences and the shape of pointwise nonlinearities are crucial factors, showing that increasing the number of flat regions in nonlinearities provides more flexibility for learning complex concepts in a single layer.

## Method Summary
The authors study a CNN with one convolution layer (single channel, small support) and ReLU activation, followed by a fully connected layer for radius estimation. They generate synthetic 1D pulse data and 2D disk images with random radius, foreground/background intensities, and optional Gaussian blur and noise. The networks are trained using gradient descent (ADAM or SGD) to minimize mean squared error with L2 regularization. Theoretical analysis derives exact weights for the 1D case, while empirical experiments validate the theoretical predictions and explore the impact of different polarities and nonlinearity shapes.

## Key Results
- Networks trained on positive-only polarity data achieve good radius estimation (RMSE ~0.6 pixels)
- Networks trained on both polarities fail to learn the radius concept (RMSE ~2.6 pixels)
- Exact convolution filter, bias, and fully connected layer weights can be derived for 1D case with positive-only data
- ReLU's limited expressiveness prevents simultaneous thresholding of both upward and downward peaks
- Increasing flat regions in nonlinearities provides more flexibility for learning complex concepts in single layer

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: ReLU nonlinearity's limited expressiveness prevents learning radius concept with both polarities
- Mechanism: ReLU only thresholds at zero, so it cannot simultaneously suppress both upward and downward peaks from the convolution output. When both polarities are present, the activated signal still depends on intensity differences in a non-invariant way.
- Core assumption: The convolution filter is a local derivative operator producing peaks at pulse edges
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ReLU nonlinearity's limited expressiveness makes it difficult to learn the radius concept for both polarities of data"
  - [section 2.3] "the ReLU nonlinearity not being flexible enough" and "cannot accurately learn the radius concept"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct mention of ReLU limitations in neighboring papers
- Break condition: Adding more convolution layers or using a nonlinearity with multiple flat regions

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Increasing number of flat regions in nonlinearities provides more flexibility for learning complex concepts
- Mechanism: Nonlinearities with multiple plateaus (like sigmoid) can threshold both above and below, creating invariant signals that don't depend on intensity differences. This allows proper radius estimation regardless of polarity.
- Core assumption: The data is bounded and can be rescaled to utilize all flat regions
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "increasing the number of flat regions in nonlinearities provides more flexibility for learning complex concepts in a single layer"
  - [section 2.3] "Using the universal approximation theorem... requires to switch the local convolution to a wide and multi-channel fully connected layer" but "we change the nonlinearity"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct discussion of flat regions in neighboring papers
- Break condition: When data is unbounded or when multiple layers are not available

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Invariance to intensity differences is essential for learning the radius concept
- Mechanism: The network must produce activated signals that are independent of the foreground and background intensities. This requires either proper thresholding to create constant-height features or multi-layer architectures that can sequentially threshold both peaks.
- Core assumption: The radius estimation should depend only on the spatial extent of the pulse, not its intensity contrast
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Two fundamental concepts of neural networks arise: the importance of invariance and of the shape of pointwise nonlinearities"
  - [section 2.3] "The goal is to integrate the weighted activated signal... and obtain the radius up to a final additive constant" and "make the estimator independent from f-b"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct discussion of invariance in neighboring papers
- Break condition: When the network architecture is changed to allow multiple thresholds or when data has no intensity variations

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input -> Convolution -> ReLU Activation -> Weighted Integration -> Final Bias Addition -> Output
- Critical path: Convolution → Activation → Weighted integration → Final bias addition
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single layer vs. deeper networks: Deeper networks can achieve invariance but lose interpretability
  - ReLU vs. sigmoid: ReLU is computationally efficient but less expressive; sigmoid is more flexible but slower
  - Small convolution support vs. larger: Smaller supports are more interpretable but may miss information
- Failure signatures:
  - RMSE much higher for networks trained on both polarities vs. positive-only
  - Estimations cluster around average radius regardless of input
  - Intermediate representations show intensity-dependent features
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train on positive-only polarity data and verify RMSE ~0.6 pixels
  2. Train on both polarities and verify RMSE ~2.6 pixels (failure case)
  3. Replace ReLU with sigmoid and verify improved performance on both polarities

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does increasing the number of flat regions or plateaux in pointwise nonlinearities affect the learning of complex concepts in a single layer of a neural network?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that increasing the number of flat regions in nonlinearities provides more flexibility for learning complex concepts in a single layer, and suggests that choosing a nonlinearity with many more plateaux, such as a sine nonlinearity, would not be better altogether than the simplistic ReLU for neural networks in general.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how different nonlinearities with varying numbers of plateaux affect the learning capabilities of neural networks.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study comparing the performance of neural networks using different nonlinearities with varying numbers of plateaux on a range of tasks and datasets.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the exact mechanism by which the multi-channel architecture helps to remove intensity dependence in the estimation of the radius of a centred disk in images?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the use of multi-channel architectures to help handle both polarities in the dataset, but the analysis seems complex and the exact mechanism by which the fully connected final layer mixes all channels together to allow complex compensation mechanisms to remove intensity dependence is not fully understood.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how the multi-channel architecture works in this context, and the compensation mechanisms are not explicitly explained.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the weight maps and intermediate representations of the multi-channel networks, along with a mathematical explanation of how the compensation mechanisms work to remove intensity dependence.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of convolution filter (e.g., unbiased derivative filter vs. higher order local filters) affect the ability of a neural network to accurately estimate the radius of a centred pulse in one-dimensional signals?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the choice of convolution filter in the context of the one-dimensional pulse function exercise and shows that using a higher order local filter leads to the network being unable to accurately estimate the radius in all cases.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison of different convolution filters and their impact on the performance of the network.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study comparing the performance of neural networks using different convolution filters (e.g., unbiased derivative filters, higher order local filters, and other types of filters) on a range of one-dimensional pulse estimation tasks.

## Limitations

- Theoretical analysis is limited to specific parameter regimes and idealized conditions
- Practical implications for real-world applications are unclear due to highly simplified task
- ReLU expressiveness limitations may not generalize to deeper networks with more layers
- Focus on synthetic data may not capture complexity and variability of real-world signals

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The empirical results showing RMSE differences between positive-only and both polarities training (0.6 vs 2.6 pixels) are directly verifiable from the described experiments.
- Medium Confidence: The theoretical derivation of exact weights for the 1D case assumes specific conditions that may not hold in practice. The universal approximation theorem argument for why ReLU fails is conceptually sound but the practical implications depend on implementation details.
- Low Confidence: The claim that increasing flat regions in nonlinearities provides more flexibility is supported by limited evidence and lacks comprehensive ablation studies across different nonlinearity shapes.
