# Best-of-Three-Worlds Linear Bandit Algorithm with Variance-Adaptive Regret Bounds

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12370
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12370
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Best-of-three-worlds algorithm with O(√T log T) adversarial, O((log T)/Δ_min + √C log T/Δ_min) corrupted stochastic, and O(σ² log T/Δ_min) stochastic regret bounds

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a linear bandit algorithm that adaptively achieves optimal regret bounds across adversarial, stochastic, and corrupted stochastic environments. The key innovation is a "scaled-up sampling" technique that increases the variance of chosen actions, improving the quality of loss estimators. The algorithm combines this with optimistic online learning to achieve data-dependent regret bounds in adversarial settings and variance-adaptive bounds in stochastic settings. The main result is a best-of-three-worlds algorithm with regret bounds of O(√T log T) in adversarial environments, O((log T)/Δ_min + √C log T/Δ_min) in corrupted stochastic environments, and O(σ² log T/Δ_min) in stochastic environments with small variance.

## Method Summary
The algorithm is based on the SCRiBLe framework, using self-concordant barriers and Dikin ellipsoids for action selection. It employs scaled-up sampling to reduce estimator variance, optimistic predictions to improve tracking in adversarial settings, and a self-bounding technique for environment detection. The method maintains an iterate xt that is updated using online projected gradient descent, with actions sampled from a scaled-up version of the Dikin ellipsoid around xt. The learning rate βt is adaptive, balancing stability and penalty terms based on observed loss gradients.

## Key Results
- Achieves O(√T log T) regret in adversarial environments
- Achieves O((log T)/Δ_min + √C log T/Δ_min) regret in corrupted stochastic environments
- Achieves O(σ² log T/Δ_min) regret in stochastic environments with small variance
- Unifies high-level adaptability across environment types with low-level adaptability to specific characteristics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Scaled-up Sampling Variance Reduction
- Claim: The scaled-up sampling technique reduces the variance of the loss estimator by increasing the variance of chosen actions.
- Mechanism: By constructing a scaled-up set W' of the Dikin ellipsoid W₁(xₜ) with a reference point zₜ, the algorithm can either sample from W' with probability α⁻¹ₜ or choose zₜ with probability 1-α⁻¹ₜ. This increases the variance of at by a factor of αₜ, which in turn decreases the variance of the loss-vector estimator by a factor of α⁻¹ₜ.
- Core assumption: The reference point zₜ can be chosen to maximize αₜ while ensuring the scaled-up set remains within the convex hull of the action set.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "By incorporating into this a new technique we call scaled-up sampling, we obtain high-level adaptability"
  - [section]: "In this paper, in order to improve the variance of the loss estimator, we introduce a new technique that we refer to as scaled-up sampling"
  - [corpus]: Weak - No direct corpus evidence for scaled-up sampling specifically, though related variance-reduction techniques exist in the corpus neighbors
- Break condition: If the reference point zₜ cannot be efficiently computed or if the scaled-up set W' extends beyond the convex hull of the action set.

### Mechanism 2: Optimistic Online Learning with Tracking
- Claim: Optimistic prediction mt improves the regret bounds by incorporating predictions about the next loss vector.
- Mechanism: The algorithm uses online projected gradient descent to track the best linear predictor, updating mt based on the difference between the observed loss and the predicted loss. This allows the regret to depend on (⟨ℓₜ - mt, at⟩)² rather than (⟨ℓₜ, at⟩)².
- Core assumption: The optimistic predictions mt can be efficiently computed and provide useful information about the upcoming loss vectors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "by incorporating the technique of optimistic online learning, we obtain low-level adaptability"
  - [section]: "To achieve low-level adaptability... we employ the framework of optimistic online learning"
  - [corpus]: Moderate - The corpus neighbors include "Best-of-Both-Worlds Algorithms for Linear Contextual Bandits" which likely uses similar optimistic prediction techniques
- Break condition: If the optimistic predictions mt are consistently poor or if the computational overhead of tracking them outweighs the benefits.

### Mechanism 3: Self-Bounding Technique for Environment Detection
- Claim: The self-bounding technique allows the algorithm to automatically detect and adapt to the environment type (adversarial, stochastic, or corrupted stochastic).
- Mechanism: The algorithm first shows it achieves a regret bound of O(d√ϑ∑Tₜ₌₁α⁻¹ₜ log T), then proves α⁻¹ₜ = O(Δ(xₜ)/Δₘᵢₙ) in stochastic environments. Combining these yields RT = O(d²ϑΔ⁻¹ₘᵢₙ log T) in stochastic environments.
- Core assumption: The relationship between αₜ and the round-wise regret Δ(xₜ) holds across different environment types.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The proposed algorithm has data-dependent regret bounds that depend on all of the cumulative loss for the optimal action, the total quadratic variation, and the path-length of the loss vector sequence"
  - [section]: "In proving the high-level adaptability, we use the self-bounding technique"
  - [corpus]: Weak - No direct corpus evidence for self-bounding technique, though related adaptive detection methods exist
- Break condition: If the environment characteristics change too rapidly for the self-bounding technique to track, or if the relationship between αₜ and Δ(xₜ) breaks down.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Action selection: Scaled-up sampling from Dikin ellipsoid or reference point
  - Loss estimation: Unbiased estimator ˆℓₜ using scaled-up sampling variance reduction
  - Optimistic prediction: Online projected gradient descent tracking linear experts
  - Learning rate: Adaptive βₜ balancing stability and penalty terms
  - Environment detection: Self-bounding technique combining high-level and low-level adaptability

- Critical path:
  1. Compute xₜ using FTRL with optimistic prediction
  2. Determine scaled-up sampling parameters (E', αₜ, zₜ)
  3. Select action at and observe feedback
  4. Compute unbiased loss estimator ˆℓₜ
  5. Update optimistic prediction mt and learning rate βₜ

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computational complexity vs. regret performance: Computing optimal zₜ for scaled-up sampling is expensive but improves regret bounds
  - High-level vs. low-level adaptability: Balancing general environment detection with specific characteristic exploitation
  - Optimistic predictions vs. stability: Aggressive predictions may improve bounds but increase variance

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance in stochastic environments: May indicate issues with scaled-up sampling or variance reduction
  - High regret in adversarial settings: Could suggest problems with optimistic prediction tracking
  - Computational bottlenecks: Likely related to finding optimal zₜ or solving FTRL subproblems

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare scaled-up sampling vs. uniform sampling from Dikin ellipsoid on synthetic data with known variance characteristics
  2. Test optimistic prediction tracking performance against non-optimistic baseline in environments with predictable loss patterns
  3. Evaluate environment detection accuracy using the self-bounding technique across mixed adversarial-stochastic scenarios

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the computational complexity of finding the optimal reference point zt that maximizes the scaling factor αt in each round?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that finding such a zt is computationally expensive, requiring at least Ω(|A|) time, which can be exponential in the dimensionality d.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide any efficient algorithm for computing this optimal zt, only noting that naive methods are computationally infeasible for large action sets.
- What evidence would resolve it: A polynomial-time algorithm that can find zt satisfying the scaling constraint (11) in each round would resolve this question.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the regret bounds be improved for stochastic environments when the asymptotic lower bound parameter c* is much larger than the variance-dependent term?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that when c* = Ω((dσ²/Δmin + 1)dϑ), their algorithm provides better regret bounds than existing asymptotically optimal algorithms, but when c* is much larger, their bounds may be worse.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide any mechanism to achieve both the asymptotically optimal regret and the variance-adaptive regret simultaneously.
- What evidence would resolve it: An algorithm that achieves O(c* log T + o(log T)) regret while maintaining the variance-adaptive bound would resolve this question.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can high-probability regret bounds be achieved for the proposed algorithm?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper only provides expected regret bounds, while noting that Lee et al. [2021] provide high-probability bounds, and that achieving high-probability bounds would require an extra O(log T) factor.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore whether the techniques used could be modified to provide high-probability guarantees without the additional log T factor.
- What evidence would resolve it: An analysis showing that the algorithm achieves high-probability regret bounds matching the expected bounds (up to constants) would resolve this question.

## Limitations
- The scaled-up sampling technique may face computational challenges in finding optimal reference points efficiently
- The self-bounding technique for environment detection hasn't been tested against rapidly changing or ambiguous environment characteristics
- The optimistic prediction mechanism's benefits depend heavily on the quality of loss vector predictions

## Confidence

- High confidence in the theoretical regret bounds and their derivation
- Medium confidence in the practical implementability of the scaled-up sampling technique
- Low confidence in the self-bounding technique's performance across all possible environment mixtures
