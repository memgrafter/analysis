# Algorithmic Censoring in Dynamic Learning Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09035
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09035
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces algorithmic censoring in dynamic learning systems where models persistently assign negative predictions to subgroups, preventing their entry into training data

## Executive Summary
This work introduces the concept of algorithmic censoring in dynamic learning systems (DLS), where models persistently assign negative predictions to subgroups of points, preventing them from entering the training data. The authors formalize censoring, demonstrate its emergence through mechanisms like sample selection bias and distributional shifts, and propose safeguards like recourse and randomization. Experiments across diverse data generating processes show that censoring is difficult to detect via standard metrics and does not resolve itself without intervention.

## Method Summary
The paper formalizes algorithmic censoring in dynamic learning systems and investigates mechanisms leading to its emergence, including sample selection bias and distributional shifts. The authors propose and evaluate two mitigation strategies: recourse (enabling censored groups to meet model requirements) and randomization (introducing noise to break censoring cycles). Experiments are conducted across diverse data generating processes to test these approaches.

## Key Results
- Censoring emerges through sample selection bias and distributional shifts in dynamic learning systems
- Standard metrics fail to detect censoring, making it a hidden bias problem
- Without intervention, censoring does not resolve itself and persists across training iterations
- Recourse is most effective in causal settings, while a combination of recourse and randomization works best in unknown causal settings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Algorithmic censoring occurs when a model's negative predictions create a feedback loop that prevents certain subgroups from ever entering the training data. This happens because these subgroups are systematically excluded based on the model's current decision boundaries, which are shaped by the biased training data that never included these groups. The mechanism creates a self-reinforcing cycle where the model's predictions about who should be included become a self-fulfilling prophecy, leading to increasingly biased models that fail to learn about the censored populations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Dynamic Learning Systems**: Understanding how models evolve over time with streaming data is crucial because censoring is fundamentally a temporal phenomenon where feedback loops compound over iterations
- **Sample Selection Bias**: Essential for recognizing how initial data collection choices can create persistent exclusion patterns that the model never learns to correct
- **Causal Inference**: Critical for distinguishing between correlation and causation in understanding why certain groups are excluded and how to effectively intervene
- **Distributional Shifts**: Important for identifying how changes in data distributions over time can exacerbate censoring by moving excluded groups further from the decision boundary
- **Feedback Loops in ML Systems**: Necessary for understanding how model predictions can shape future data collection, creating self-reinforcing patterns

## Architecture Onboarding

Component Map:
Dynamic Learning System -> Data Collection -> Model Training -> Prediction -> Feedback Loop -> Data Collection

Critical Path:
1. Initial data collection may contain biases
2. Model training on biased data creates decision boundaries
3. Predictions exclude certain subgroups from future data collection
4. Feedback loop reinforces exclusion
5. Model becomes increasingly biased over time

Design Tradeoffs:
- Recourse requires understanding causal mechanisms but can be highly effective
- Randomization is simpler to implement but may introduce unnecessary noise
- Combined approach balances effectiveness with practical implementation constraints

Failure Signatures:
- Persistent underrepresentation of certain subgroups despite overall system performance
- Metrics appearing stable while underlying fairness degrades
- Model performance improvements that coincide with increasing group disparities

First Experiments:
1. Test recourse effectiveness in controlled causal scenarios with known intervention points
2. Evaluate randomization impact on breaking censoring cycles across different noise levels
3. Compare detection capabilities of standard metrics versus proposed censoring-specific metrics

## Limitations
- Reliance on simulated data experiments may not capture real-world system complexity
- Evaluation focuses on specific data-generating processes, limiting generalizability
- Does not explore novel detection methods beyond standard metrics
- Limited experimental evidence for the recommended combination approach in unknown causal settings
