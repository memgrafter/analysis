# Improving Gradient Computation for Differentiable Physics Simulation with Contacts

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.00092
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00092
- Reference count: 9
- Key outcome: TOI-Velocity method successfully learns optimal control sequences that match analytical solutions in problems where existing differentiable simulation methods fail.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of inaccurate gradient computation in differentiable rigid-body simulation with contacts, particularly when contact normal directions are not fixed. The authors propose TOI-Velocity (Time-of-Impact-Velocity), a method that leverages continuous collision detection to improve gradient calculation. By adjusting post-collision velocities using collision velocities and directions computed at the time of impact rather than penetration velocities, the method eliminates discontinuities that cause optimization failures. The approach is demonstrated on two optimal control problems where existing methods fail to converge to analytical solutions, showing that TOI-Velocity enables successful learning of optimal control sequences.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes TOI-Velocity to improve gradient computation in differentiable physics simulation with contacts. The method uses continuous collision detection to compute the time of impact (TOI) and leverages collision velocity and direction at impact time to calculate post-collision velocities, rather than using penetration velocity and direction. This approach addresses discontinuities in contact normal directions that occur when contacts happen at different time steps across optimization iterations. The method is combined with TOI-Position, which adjusts post-collision positions using TOI, ensuring both position and velocity gradients are continuous. The authors demonstrate their approach on two optimal control problems involving collisions between rigid bodies, showing that TOI-Velocity enables convergence to analytical optimal solutions where existing methods fail.

## Key Results
- TOI-Velocity successfully learns optimal control sequences matching analytical solutions in two test problems where existing differentiable simulation methods fail
- The method demonstrates improved gradient computation by eliminating discontinuities in contact normal directions across optimization iterations
- Ablation studies confirm that both TOI-Position and TOI-Velocity components are necessary for achieving analytical optimal loss values

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Discontinuity in contact normal direction across optimization iterations causes gradient computation errors in differentiable physics simulation.
- Mechanism: When contact occurs at different time steps across optimization iterations, the penetration direction (contact normal) can change discontinuously. This discontinuity propagates to post-collision velocities, causing gradients to jump abruptly between iterations.
- Core assumption: The contact normal direction is not fixed during optimization, which is a common situation when the contacts are between two moving objects.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We find that existing differentiable simulation methods provide inaccurate gradients when the contact normal direction is not fixed - a general situation when the contacts are between two moving objects."
  - [section 4.1] "We find that the increase of loss appears when the time step at which the collision happens changes over optimization iterations."
- Break condition: This mechanism breaks when contacts occur with fixed normal directions (e.g., static walls or fixed surfaces) or when the simulation time step is sufficiently small that collisions happen at the same time step across iterations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: TOI-Velocity improves gradient computation by using collision velocity and direction instead of penetration velocity and direction.
- Mechanism: TOI-Velocity calculates the time of impact (TOI) and uses the velocity and position at collision time (rather than penetration time) to determine post-collision velocities. This ensures that the collision direction and velocity are continuous across optimization iterations.
- Core assumption: The continuous collision detection can accurately estimate the time of impact and the corresponding collision velocity and direction.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "We propose to improve gradient computation by continuous collision detection and leverage the time-of-impact (TOI) to calculate the post-collision velocities."
  - [section 4.2] "We propose to use collision velocity ¯vn+1 and collision direction ¯n to solve for post-collision velocities vn+1."
- Break condition: This mechanism breaks if the continuous collision detection is inaccurate or if the TOI calculation introduces numerical instability.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: TOI-Position alone is insufficient; both TOI-Position and TOI-Velocity are needed for accurate gradient computation.
- Mechanism: TOI-Position adjusts the post-collision position using TOI, but this doesn't address the discontinuity in post-collision velocities. TOI-Velocity complements TOI-Position by adjusting the post-collision velocities, ensuring both position and velocity gradients are continuous.
- Core assumption: Adjusting only position without adjusting velocity leads to gradient discontinuities, as evidenced by the ablation study.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.4] "We find that only by applying both TOI-Position and TOI-Velocity can we end up with a loss value close to the analytical optimal loss in both examples."
  - [section 4.2] "However, we argue that adjusting the position only is not enough since the post-collision velocity is also incorrectly estimated."
- Break condition: This mechanism breaks if the TOI-Position component is not needed for the specific simulation setup or if the simulation already handles position discontinuities effectively.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Differentiable Physics Simulator -> Continuous Collision Detection Module -> TOI-Velocity Implementation -> Gradient Computation Module -> Optimization Loop

- Critical path:
  1. Simulate physics forward using symplectic Euler integration
  2. Detect collisions using continuous collision detection
  3. Compute TOI and collision velocity/direction
  4. Update post-collision velocities using TOI-Velocity
  5. Compute gradients through the differentiable simulator
  6. Update control inputs using gradient descent

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs. computational cost: More accurate CCD and TOI calculations may increase computational cost.
  - Integration scheme: Symplectic Euler is used for simplicity, but other schemes might offer different tradeoffs.
  - Contact model: Velocity impulse methods are used, but compliant models or position-based dynamics might be alternatives.

- Failure signatures:
  - Loss increasing over iterations: Indicates gradient discontinuities, possibly due to incorrect contact normal directions.
  - Unstable learning curves: Suggests issues with the CCD or TOI calculations.
  - Control sequences not matching analytical solutions: Points to persistent gradient errors.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement TOI-Velocity on a simple two-ball collision problem and verify that the loss converges to the analytical optimal value.
  2. Compare learning curves with and without TOI-Velocity on a more complex multiple-collision problem.
  3. Perform an ablation study to confirm that both TOI-Position and TOI-Velocity are necessary for accurate gradient computation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does TOI-Velocity perform in scenarios with complex contact geometries beyond simple sphere collisions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on two-ball collisions and mentions the technique "applies to collisions among multiple objects with a general class of shapes," but does not demonstrate this generalization.
- Why unresolved: The experimental validation is limited to simple sphere-sphere collisions, leaving performance on complex geometries untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments demonstrating TOI-Velocity's effectiveness on contacts involving non-spherical shapes (e.g., boxes, ellipsoids) or higher-dimensional contact manifolds.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the computational overhead of TOI-Velocity compared to existing methods in large-scale simulations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows TOI-Velocity works for small-scale optimal control problems but does not report timing comparisons or scalability analysis.
- Why unresolved: No runtime benchmarks or scaling studies are provided to quantify the trade-off between improved gradient accuracy and computational cost.
- What evidence would resolve it: Profiling results comparing wall-clock time and memory usage of TOI-Velocity versus baseline methods across varying problem sizes and contact frequencies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does TOI-Velocity handle numerical instability in scenarios with rapidly changing contact conditions or near-collision events?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions "TOI-Position computes TOI as the time spent after the penetration appears" but does not discuss robustness to numerical noise or ill-conditioning in contact detection.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not analyze the sensitivity of TOI estimation to numerical errors or explore stabilization techniques for challenging contact configurations.
- What evidence would resolve it: A stability analysis showing TOI-Velocity's behavior under perturbed initial conditions, including convergence properties and failure modes in numerically sensitive scenarios.

## Limitations
- Performance with complex contact geometries beyond simple sphere collisions has not been evaluated
- Computational overhead of TOI-Velocity compared to existing methods is not quantified
- Limited evaluation scope to frictionless elastic collisions without friction or plastic deformation

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core claim that existing differentiable simulation methods produce inaccurate gradients when contact normal directions change discontinuously across optimization iterations. This is well-supported by empirical evidence showing loss increases when collision timing shifts.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that TOI-Velocity successfully addresses these gradient discontinuities and enables convergence to analytical optimal solutions. While demonstrated on two problems, the limited scope and lack of comparison to alternative gradient correction methods reduces confidence.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that both TOI-Position and TOI-Velocity components are necessary for accurate gradient computation. The ablation study provides supporting evidence, but the analysis is limited to the specific examples tested.
