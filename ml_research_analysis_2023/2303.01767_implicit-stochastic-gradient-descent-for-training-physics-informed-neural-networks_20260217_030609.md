# Implicit Stochastic Gradient Descent for Training Physics-informed Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.01767
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01767
- Reference count: 40
- This paper proposes using implicit stochastic gradient descent (ISGD) to train physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) for improved stability, especially on stiff problems with multi-scale solutions.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of training physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) for stiff problems involving multi-scale solutions. The authors propose using implicit stochastic gradient descent (ISGD) as an alternative to standard optimizers like SGD and Adam. ISGD is shown to be more stable during training, particularly when dealing with high-frequency or multi-scale functions where traditional methods often fail. The paper provides both theoretical analysis and empirical validation demonstrating ISGD's effectiveness for PINN training.

## Method Summary
The method implements PINNs with a loss function combining supervised data-based loss (boundary/initial conditions) and unsupervised physics-based loss (PDE residuals). ISGD updates are defined implicitly by θn+1 = θn - α·∇L(θn+1), implemented using an inner optimization loop (Adam or L-BFGS) to solve the proximal subproblem. The training procedure uses a hybrid approach: K0 iterations of ISGD with learning rate α=0.5, followed by Adam fine-tuning with learning rate η=0.0005 for K2 iterations. The method is tested on various PINN problems including 1D Poisson equation with smooth and multi-scale solutions, singularly perturbed ODEs, and 2D Poisson and Helmholtz equations.

## Key Results
- ISGD trains PINNs more stably than SGD and Adam, especially for stiff problems with multi-scale solutions
- For two-layer networks with large hidden nodes, ISGD converges to a global minimum for quadratic loss with linear convergence rate
- The hybrid ISGD→Adam pipeline maintains comparable computational cost to Adam while improving stability

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Implicit updates in IGD/ISGD eliminate the learning rate constraint that arises from stiffness in the gradient flow dynamics.
- Mechanism: By solving the implicit update equation θn+1 = θn - α · ∇L(θn+1), the method ensures the loss always decreases regardless of the magnitude of the Hessian eigenvalues, unlike explicit GD which requires α ≤ 2/λmax to remain stable.
- Core assumption: The loss function is smooth enough for the implicit update to be well-defined and computable via an inner optimization loop.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we heuristically analyze how ISGD overcome stiffness in the gradient flow dynamics of PINNs"
  - [section] "The loss decay rate D = max{ 1/(1+αK1)2, 1/(1+αK2)2 } satisfies D < 1 automatically for all learning rates α > 0"
  - [corpus] Weak – no direct neighbor papers discuss the theoretical stability mechanism of implicit methods for PINNs.
- Break condition: If the inner optimization loop fails to converge (e.g., L-BFGS or Adam get stuck), the implicit update cannot be computed, breaking the method.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: ISGD converges globally for two-layer networks with large hidden nodes under quadratic loss.
- Mechanism: The analysis shows that the Gram matrix H(n) remains strictly positive definite with high probability, which drives the loss to decrease at a linear rate: L(n) ≤ (1/(1 + αλmin(H∞)/2))ⁿ L(0).
- Core assumption: The hidden layer width m is sufficiently large relative to the number of training points N and the smallest eigenvalue of the limiting Gram matrix λmin(H∞).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We theoretically prove that for two-layer fully connected neural networks with large hidden nodes, randomly initialized ISGD converges to a globally optimal solution for the quadratic loss function."
  - [section] Theorem 1 statement with conditions on m and α.
  - [corpus] Weak – neighbor papers focus on convergence for wide two-layer PINNs but do not detail the implicit method's role.
- Break condition: If the network is not wide enough or the activation function violates the smoothness/lipschitz assumptions, the Gram matrix may lose positive definiteness and convergence can fail.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Switching from ISGD to Adam/L-BFGS after a few implicit steps combines stability with fast local convergence.
- Mechanism: The implicit phase handles the stiff early training landscape, moving weights closer to a good basin; the subsequent explicit optimizer then refines quickly because the initial point is already near a good local minimum.
- Core assumption: The initial ISGD steps provide a sufficiently good initialization for the following Adam/L-BFGS phase.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We used practical L-BFGS and Adam optimizer to deal with the implicit updates in IGD/ISGD, which is effective in practice."
  - [section] Algorithm 1 description of the hybrid "ISGD,Adam" optimizer.
  - [corpus] None directly – this hybrid scheme is not discussed in neighbors.
- Break condition: If the implicit phase is too short or the learning rate too small, the subsequent explicit optimizer may still face stiffness; if too long, training becomes computationally expensive.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Stiff ordinary differential equations and implicit numerical schemes.
  - Why needed here: The gradient flow of PINNs becomes stiff when approximating multi-scale solutions; understanding implicit methods explains why ISGD remains stable where explicit GD diverges.
  - Quick check question: What condition on the learning rate ensures stability for explicit GD in the presence of a stiff gradient flow?

- Concept: Positive definiteness of the Gram matrix in neural network training.
  - Why needed here: The convergence proof for ISGD relies on the Gram matrix being strictly positive definite; this property guarantees that the loss decreases monotonically.
  - Quick check question: Under what conditions on the activation function and data distribution is the Gram matrix H∞ positive definite?

- Concept: Over-parameterization and its role in global convergence.
  - Why needed here: The theoretical result for ISGD assumes a large number of hidden nodes; this over-parameterization ensures the network can fit the data and the optimization landscape is benign.
  - Quick check question: How does increasing the hidden layer width affect the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix and the convergence rate?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input coordinates -> Fully connected layers (tanh activation) -> Output layer -> Loss function (data + physics terms) -> ISGD/Adam optimizer -> Updated weights

- Critical path:
  1. Sample training and collocation points
  2. Forward pass to compute predictions and residuals
  3. Compute loss and gradients
  4. Perform ISGD step via inner optimization
  5. After K0 steps, switch to Adam for fine-tuning
  6. Evaluate on validation set

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Wider networks → better Gram matrix conditioning but higher compute
  - Larger learning rate in ISGD → faster implicit steps but risk of inner loop divergence
  - More collocation points → better PDE enforcement but slower training
  - Hybrid optimizer → stable early training, fast later convergence, but more code complexity

- Failure signatures:
  - Inner loop (L-BFGS/Adam) fails to converge → ISGD step cannot be computed
  - Loss increases after Adam phase → poor initialization from ISGD phase
  - Overfitting to collocation points → insufficient data points or too many network parameters

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate the 1D Poisson example with smooth vs. multi-scale solutions; verify ISGD trains both while Adam fails on multi-scale
  2. Train a two-layer network on a synthetic quadratic loss; measure linear convergence rate and compare to theoretical bound
  3. Apply the hybrid ISGD→Adam pipeline to the MNIST classification task; compare test accuracy across learning rates and batch sizes

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the ISGD method maintain its theoretical convergence guarantees when applied to PINNs with multi-scale solutions, where the Gram matrix may not be strictly positive definite?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that for PINN loss, "the Gram matrix H∞ may not guarantee strict positivity" but empirical results show ISGD remains stable and effective.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical analysis proving ISGD convergence for two-layer networks with quadratic loss relies on the Gram matrix being strictly positive definite, but this property may not hold for PINN training with multi-scale solutions.
- What evidence would resolve it: A proof extending the convergence guarantees to cases where the Gram matrix is only semi-positive definite, or empirical evidence showing ISGD consistently converges even when the Gram matrix loses strict positive definiteness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the precise relationship between the stiffness of the gradient flow dynamics (as measured by the spectral gap of the Hessian) and the performance gap between ISGD and traditional optimizers like Adam and SGD?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates ISGD's superiority on stiff problems but doesn't quantify how the performance gap scales with the degree of stiffness.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows ISGD works better on stiff problems, it doesn't provide a quantitative measure of how the performance advantage grows with increasing stiffness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the spectral gap of the Hessian across multiple problem instances, measuring the performance difference between ISGD and other optimizers as a function of stiffness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the computational efficiency of ISGD compare to other optimizers when training very deep PINNs (e.g., networks with more than 5 layers or 200+ hidden units per layer)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "the method is stable for the learning rate and batch size" and compares computational cost to Adam, but doesn't explore scalability to very deep networks.
- Why unresolved: The current analysis focuses on relatively shallow networks, and the computational complexity of the inner optimization in ISGD may scale differently with network depth compared to other optimizers.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmark studies comparing wall-clock time and memory usage of ISGD versus Adam/SGD for training PINNs with varying depths (e.g., 5, 10, 20 layers) on the same hardware.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis is limited to two-layer networks with quadratic loss, which may not directly translate to deeper or non-quadratic settings
- Practical implementation relies on inner optimizers (L-BFGS/Adam) that are not guaranteed to converge
- Empirical validation doesn't extensively test limits of ISGD's applicability to very high-frequency solutions or highly nonlinear PDEs

## Confidence
- **High confidence** in the mechanism that implicit updates eliminate the learning rate constraint for stability, as this follows directly from the properties of implicit numerical methods
- **Medium confidence** in the global convergence proof for two-layer networks, as it depends on specific assumptions about network width and activation functions that may not hold in all cases
- **Medium confidence** in the hybrid ISGD→Adam strategy, as while the intuition is sound, the optimal switching point and inner optimizer settings are problem-dependent
