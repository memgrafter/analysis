# Multi-lingual and Multi-cultural Figurative Language Understanding

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16171
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16171
- Reference count: 14
- Key outcome: Multilingual figurative language dataset covering seven diverse languages; models show high cross-lingual and concept shift gaps, with modest improvements from few-shot examples.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the MABL dataset, a multilingual collection of 6,366 figurative language expressions across seven diverse languages. The authors find that figurative expressions are highly culturally-specific, with minimal overlap across languages, and that all target languages perform significantly worse than English in zero-shot settings. Cross-lingual transfer is a major factor in performance degradation, with concept shift playing a larger role due to culturally-specific examples. Adding few-shot examples helps lower-resource languages more, but performance gains are modest overall.

## Method Summary
The MABL dataset was collected by native speakers in seven languages (Hindi, Indonesian, Javanese, Kannada, Sundanese, Swahili, Yoruba) using a template-based approach to generate culturally-relevant figurative language examples. Multilingual models (XLM-R-large, XLM-R-base, mBERT-base) were fine-tuned on English data from Liu et al. (2022) and evaluated zero-shot on target languages. Few-shot experiments added 2-50 examples per target language to the English training data. Hyperparameters were tuned via grid search, and models were trained on A6000 GPUs.

## Key Results
- All target languages show significant performance degradation compared to English in zero-shot settings.
- Concept shift (culturally-specific expressions) is a larger factor than cross-lingual transfer in performance gaps.
- Adding few-shot examples improves performance, especially for lower-resource languages, but gains are modest.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Cross-lingual transfer is a major factor in figurative language understanding performance.
- Mechanism: Models trained on English data struggle to transfer to other languages due to linguistic and cultural differences.
- Core assumption: Figurative language relies heavily on cultural context and shared knowledge that varies across languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "All languages exhibit a significant deficiency compared to English, with variations in performance reflecting the availability of pre-training and fine-tuning data."
  - [section] "We observe that concept shift seems to play a larger role due to culturally specific examples."
- Break condition: If models could be trained on diverse, culturally-relevant data from multiple languages, the cross-lingual transfer gap might be reduced.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Concept shift is a significant contributor to performance degradation in cross-lingual figurative language understanding.
- Mechanism: Figurative expressions are highly culturally-specific, making it difficult for models trained on one language/culture to understand expressions from another.
- Core assumption: Figurative language relies on culturally-specific concepts and objects that may not have direct equivalents in other languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our dataset reveals that each language relies on cultural and regional concepts for figurative expressions, with the highest overlap between languages originating from the same region."
  - [section] "We find considerable variation in figurative language use across languages, particularly in the unique objects that people invoke in their comparisons."
- Break condition: If cultural concepts and objects could be mapped across languages, the concept shift gap might be reduced.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Adding few-shot examples in the target language can improve performance, especially for lower-resource languages.
- Mechanism: Providing models with a small number of culturally-relevant examples helps them adapt to the specific figurative language patterns of that language.
- Core assumption: Models can learn from a limited number of examples if they are representative of the target language's figurative language use.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We find collecting some cultural examples could disproportionately help low-resource languages."
  - [section] "Augmenting with few examples usually does not help much... However, there are a few exceptions to this trend."
- Break condition: If the few-shot examples are not representative of the target language's figurative language use, or if the model is already well-adapted to the language, the performance gain might be minimal.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Data collection -> Model training -> Evaluation -> Analysis

- Critical path:
  Collect culturally-relevant data in multiple languages -> Fine-tune multilingual models on English data -> Evaluate model performance on target languages -> Analyze cross-lingual and concept shift gaps

- Design tradeoffs:
  Using machine translation to approximate concept shift vs. human translation; focusing on high-resource languages vs. including lower-resource languages; using a template-based approach vs. allowing more diverse sentence structures

- Failure signatures:
  High cross-lingual gap: Model struggles to transfer from English to target language; High concept shift gap: Model struggles with culturally-specific figurative expressions; Low performance on lower-resource languages: Model not well-adapted to less-represented languages

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune a multilingual model on English data and evaluate on all target languages (zero-shot)
  2. Machine-translate test sets to English and re-evaluate to approximate concept shift gap
  3. Add few-shot examples in the target language and re-evaluate to measure improvement

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal strategy for selecting source languages to maximize cross-lingual transfer performance for figurative language understanding?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that English may not be the best source language and that machine-translating training sets could help, but does not explore this avenue.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on zero-shot and few-shot transfer from English due to training data availability constraints, leaving the question of optimal source language selection unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing transfer performance from different source languages (e.g., multilingual vs. monolingual fine-tuning, or using machine-translated data) would provide evidence for optimal strategies.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we better distinguish between common sense knowledge and cultural knowledge in figurative language datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that annotators, being familiar with their own cultures, may have attributed culturally specific facts as common sense, making it difficult to disentangle common sense and culture.
- Why unresolved: The current annotation process does not clearly separate common sense from cultural knowledge, potentially conflating the two.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing more precise annotation guidelines or using a different annotation approach (e.g., involving annotators from diverse cultural backgrounds) could help better distinguish between common sense and cultural knowledge.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of using more complex and naturalistic language in figurative language datasets on model performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge that the dataset may not have the most diverse syntax, as many examples follow the template "<X> is like <Y>", and suggest that extension to more complex and naturalistic language could be included in future work.
- Why unresolved: The current dataset uses simpler examples, and the impact of using more complex language on model performance is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Creating a dataset with more diverse and naturalistic language, and evaluating model performance on it, would provide insights into the impact of language complexity on figurative language understanding.

## Limitations

- Dataset covers only seven languages, limiting generalizability.
- Template-based approach may not capture the full diversity of real-world figurative language.
- Machine translation used to approximate concept shift is acknowledged as imperfect.
- Analysis does not account for intra-language variation (dialects, registers).

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The claim that figurative language understanding performance degrades significantly in cross-lingual settings is well-supported by consistent accuracy drops across all evaluated languages and models. The observation that culturally specific concepts drive this degradation is also strongly evidenced by the dataset analysis.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that adding few-shot examples can help lower-resource languages is supported, but the effect size is modest and inconsistent across languages. The claim that concept shift is a larger factor than cross-lingual transfer is based on machine-translated approximations, which introduces uncertainty.

**Low Confidence**: The conclusion that no figurative expressions are shared across languages is overstated; the analysis only examined top-5 objects, and more nuanced overlap (e.g., similar metaphors with different cultural referents) may exist.

## Next Checks

1. Expand language coverage and expression diversity: Replicate the study with a larger set of languages and allow for more diverse, non-template-based figurative expressions to test the robustness of the cross-lingual and concept shift findings.

2. Use human translation for concept shift estimation: Replace machine translation with human-translated test sets to obtain a more accurate measure of the concept shift gap and validate the approximation used in the current study.

3. Analyze fine-grained overlap and cultural equivalence: Conduct a deeper qualitative and quantitative analysis of figurative expressions across languages to identify potential cultural equivalents or shared metaphors that may not be captured by object-level overlap.
