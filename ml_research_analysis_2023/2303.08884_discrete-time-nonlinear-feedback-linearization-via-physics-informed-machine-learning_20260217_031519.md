# Discrete-Time Nonlinear Feedback Linearization via Physics-Informed Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08884
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08884
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Greedy-wise physics-informed machine learning achieves superior numerical accuracy for discrete-time nonlinear feedback linearization, particularly near singularities

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel physics-informed machine learning (PIML) approach for feedback linearization of discrete-time nonlinear dynamical systems. The method learns the nonlinear transformation law in a single step while ensuring stability through pole placement. To address the challenge of steep gradients and singularities in the transformation law, the authors employ a greedy-wise training procedure that progressively expands the training domain from an initial subset.

The proposed method demonstrates significant improvements over traditional power-series expansion methods and standard PIML approaches. On a benchmark problem with an analytically known solution, the greedy-wise approach reduces approximation errors from order 100 to order 10^-3 in regions near singularities. This advancement is particularly relevant for control problems involving functional differential equations where transformation laws commonly exhibit steep gradients or singularities.

## Method Summary
The method combines physics-informed machine learning with a greedy-wise training strategy for discrete-time nonlinear feedback linearization. The approach first identifies the nonlinear transformation law that maps the original system to a linear form using PIML, with stability ensured through pole placement. To handle the challenges of steep gradients and singularities in the transformation law, the training procedure starts with a small subset of the domain and progressively expands it. This incremental approach allows the model to learn the transformation law more effectively in difficult regions. The method is evaluated on a benchmark problem with an analytical solution, comparing performance against traditional power-series expansion methods and standard PIML trained on the entire domain.

## Key Results
- Greedy-wise training approach outperforms standard PIML trained on the entire domain
- Error reduction from order 100 to order 10^-3 in challenging regions near singularities
- Superior numerical approximation accuracy compared to traditional power-series expansion methods
- Single-step learning of nonlinear transformation law while ensuring stability through pole placement

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by leveraging physics-informed constraints during the learning process, which guide the neural network to satisfy the fundamental laws of the dynamical system. The greedy-wise training strategy is particularly effective because it allows the model to first learn in easier regions of the domain before gradually tackling more difficult areas with steep gradients or singularities. This progressive approach prevents the model from being overwhelmed by challenging regions from the start, which can lead to poor convergence or suboptimal solutions. The stability guarantee through pole placement ensures that the learned transformation law maintains system stability, a critical requirement for control applications.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Physical System -> Transformation Law Neural Network -> Linearized System -> Controller -> Original System

**Critical Path**: The most critical path is from the Transformation Law Neural Network through to the controlled Original System, as errors in the learned transformation directly impact control performance and stability.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The greedy-wise training approach trades off computational efficiency for improved accuracy in difficult regions. While it may require more iterations than full-domain training, it achieves better results in areas with steep gradients or singularities.

**Failure Signatures**: The method may struggle with very high-dimensional systems or when the underlying physics is not well-represented by the chosen neural network architecture. Additionally, if the initial subset chosen for training is not representative of the full domain, the progressive expansion may lead to suboptimal results.
