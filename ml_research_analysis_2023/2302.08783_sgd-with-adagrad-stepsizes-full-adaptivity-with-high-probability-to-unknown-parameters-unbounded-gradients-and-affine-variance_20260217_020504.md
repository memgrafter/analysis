# SGD with AdaGrad Stepsizes: Full Adaptivity with High Probability to Unknown Parameters, Unbounded Gradients and Affine Variance

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.08783
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08783
- Reference count: 40
- This paper provides high-probability analysis of AdaSGD without requiring knowledge of problem parameters or imposing global Lipschitz conditions

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first high-probability convergence analysis of Stochastic Gradient Descent with AdaGrad stepsizes (AdaSGD) for smooth optimization problems. The key innovation is achieving full adaptivity to unknown problem parameters while maintaining convergence guarantees without global Lipschitz assumptions or bounded gradient conditions. The analysis handles both convex and non-convex settings with affine variance noise models, showing that AdaSGD automatically interpolates between optimal rates for high-noise and low-noise regimes without parameter tuning.

## Method Summary
The method implements AdaSGD updates where the stepsize at iteration t is computed as ηt = η / √(γ² + ∑s=1t ∥gs∥²), with η and γ as hyperparameters and gs being stochastic gradients. The algorithm uses a decorrelation technique to break dependencies between stepsizes and gradients, enabling martingale concentration inequalities to establish high-probability bounds. The analysis is built around the affine variance noise model where ∥g(w) - ∇f(w)∥² ≤ σ₀² + σ₁²∥∇f(w)∥², which generalizes bounded variance assumptions.

## Key Results
- AdaSGD achieves convergence rates of O(1/T + σ₀/√T) in both high-noise and low-noise regimes without parameter tuning
- The method decorrelates stepsizes to handle dependencies and uses martingale concentration inequalities for high-probability bounds
- In the non-convex case, the analysis shows f(wt) - f* ≤ O(1) for all t with high probability
- In the convex case, the analysis shows convergence of f(1/T ∑wt) - f* to O(1/T), with diameter bounds on wt - w* for all t

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: AdaSGD achieves automatic interpolation between high-noise and low-noise regimes without parameter tuning.
- Mechanism: The adaptive stepsize ηt = η / √(γ² + ∑t gs²) scales inversely with the accumulated gradient norm, automatically increasing step size when gradients are small (low noise) and decreasing it when gradients are large (high noise).
- Core assumption: The a priori unknown noise parameters σ0 and σ1 follow the affine variance model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "automatically interpolating between rates without parameter tuning"
  - [section 1.1]: "our bounds maintain a remarkable property of the non-adaptive bounds for smooth SGD: as can be seen from the bounds above, they exhibit optimal rates (in the context of non-accelerated gradient methods) in both the high-noise and low-noise regimes"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mention of adaptive interpolation
- Break condition: If the affine variance assumption fails or if gradients have pathological correlations that prevent proper adaptation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The decorrelation technique breaks the dependency between stepsizes and gradients, enabling high-probability analysis without global Lipschitz assumptions.
- Mechanism: By replacing ηs with a decorrelated proxy stepsize based on ∥∇f(ws)∥² instead of ∥gs∥², the analysis separates the randomness in the gradient norm from the randomness in the gradient direction.
- Core assumption: The noise magnitude scales with ∥∇f(w)∥² according to the affine variance model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 1.2]: "To break the correlation between ηs and gs in the second term above, Kavis et al. (2021) replace ηs with ηs−1"
  - [section 3.1]: "To avoid this limiting assumption, we use a different 'decorrelated' proxy for the stepsize, defined by replacing ∥gt∥² with (1 + σ₁²)∥∇f(wt)∥² + σ₀²"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mention of decorrelation technique
- Break condition: If the noise model doesn't satisfy the affine variance structure or if the gradient norm becomes unbounded.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Martingale concentration inequalities handle the adaptive stepsize dependency while maintaining high-probability bounds.
- Mechanism: The analysis uses Bernstein-type martingale concentration (Lemma 5) to bound cumulative martingale terms, with careful inductive arguments that leverage the smoothness property ∥∇f(w)∥² ≤ 2β(f(w) - f⋆).
- Core assumption: The martingale differences satisfy sub-Gaussian-like tail bounds.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 1.2]: "we appeal to a Bernstein-type martingale concentration inequality (specifically, we use one due to Li and Orabona 2020)"
  - [section 3.3]: "Lemma 5. Assume that Z1, Z2, ..., ZT is a martingale difference sequence..."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mention of martingale concentration for AdaSGD
- Break condition: If the martingale differences have heavier tails than assumed or if the inductive bound on ∥∇f(w)∥² fails.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Gradient oracle (g(w)) providing noisy gradient estimates
  - AdaGrad-style accumulator tracking ∑∥gs∥²
  - Adaptive stepsize computation ηt = η / √(γ² + accumulator)
  - Decorrelated proxy stepsize for analysis
  - Martingale tracking for high-probability bounds

- Critical path:
  1. Query gradient oracle at current iterate wt
  2. Update accumulator with ∥gt∥²
  3. Compute adaptive stepsize ηt
  4. Update iterate: wt+1 = wt - ηtgt
  5. Track martingale terms for convergence analysis

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Adaptive stepsizes provide automatic tuning but complicate theoretical analysis
  - Decorrelation introduces approximation error but enables cleaner bounds
  - High-probability analysis requires stronger concentration tools than in-expectation analysis

- Failure signatures:
  - Divergence: If accumulated gradient norm grows too quickly, stepsizes become too small
  - Slow convergence: If initial γ is too large relative to problem scale
  - High variance in convergence: If martingale concentration bounds are loose

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test on a simple convex quadratic with known smoothness to verify automatic adaptation to noise level
  2. Compare convergence on a problem with affine variance noise versus bounded variance to demonstrate the advantage of the noise model
  3. Vary the initial γ parameter across orders of magnitude to verify robustness and identify sensitivity

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does AdaSGD maintain its convergence rates when applied to non-smooth objectives?
- Basis in paper: The analysis explicitly assumes β-smoothness throughout the convergence proofs
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses entirely on smooth optimization without exploring non-smooth extensions
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical or theoretical analysis showing AdaSGD convergence on non-smooth problems

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the polynomial dependence on 1/γ in the convex case be eliminated?
- Basis in paper: The authors note polynomial dependence on 1/γ in Theorem 2 and provide a lower bound example
- Why unresolved: The authors show this dependence is necessary but don't explore whether it can be reduced
- What evidence would resolve it: Either a refined analysis removing the polynomial dependence or a tighter lower bound

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does AdaSGD perform with different noise models beyond affine variance?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses specifically on affine variance noise and provides a reduction to sub-Gaussian
- Why unresolved: The analysis is specialized to affine variance without exploring other noise distributions
- What evidence would resolve it: Convergence analysis for other noise models like heavy-tailed or heteroscedastic noise

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the optimal choice of γ for AdaSGD in practice?
- Basis in paper: The authors note the algorithm is "fairly robust to the choice of γ" but don't provide concrete guidance
- Why unresolved: The analysis shows γ matters but doesn't characterize its optimal value
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical study identifying optimal γ across problem classes or a theoretical characterization of the optimal γ range

## Limitations
- The affine variance assumption, while more general than bounded variance, may not capture all practical noise structures
- The decorrelation technique introduces approximation error that could affect tight bounds in practice
- The high-probability guarantees require careful parameter selection for the concentration inequalities, which may be sensitive to problem structure

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The convergence rates O(1/T + σ₀/√T) for both convex and non-convex cases are well-established through the martingale concentration framework
- **Medium confidence**: The automatic interpolation between noise regimes is theoretically sound but may be sensitive to initial parameter choices in practice
- **Medium confidence**: The decorrelation technique effectively breaks dependencies for analysis, though practical impact on convergence speed requires empirical validation
