# Textless Speech-to-Music Retrieval Using Emotion Similarity

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.10539
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10539
- Reference count: 0
- The paper proposes a cross-domain retrieval framework using emotion similarity regularization to improve speech-to-music retrieval performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of textless speech-to-music retrieval by proposing a cross-domain framework that leverages emotion similarity. The method employs pre-trained encoders for audio and text representations, combined with a triplet loss function enhanced by emotion similarity regularization. The approach aims to bridge the gap between speech and music domains in a joint embedding space, enabling retrieval based on emotional content rather than semantic meaning. Experiments on three speech datasets (IEMOCAP, RAVDESS, Hi, KIA) and a music mood dataset demonstrate improved retrieval performance, with MRR scores reaching 0.83 and NDCG@5 up to 0.92 for the fusion modality.

## Method Summary
The proposed method uses pre-trained encoders (Wav2vec 2.0 for speech, DistilBERT for text, Music Tagging Transformer for music) with projection MLPs to map inputs into a joint embedding space. A triplet loss with emotion similarity regularization is employed to train the model, where the regularization helps capture the continuous distribution of emotions between speech and music. The approach uses valence-arousal mapping to handle mismatched dataset taxonomies between speech and music domains. During retrieval, speech inputs are encoded and used to find the most emotionally similar music tracks in the learned embedding space.

## Key Results
- MRR scores up to 0.83 for the fusion modality
- NDCG@5 scores up to 0.92 for the fusion modality
- Improved retrieval performance compared to baseline methods
- Emotion similarity regularization helps capture continuous emotion distributions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by creating a shared embedding space where speech and music representations are positioned based on their emotional similarity rather than semantic content. The emotion similarity regularization term in the triplet loss function encourages the model to learn representations that preserve emotional relationships across domains. By mapping emotion labels to valence-arousal space, the approach handles the mismatched taxonomies between speech and music datasets. The pre-trained encoders provide robust feature representations that capture domain-specific characteristics, while the projection MLPs learn to map these features into the shared emotional space.

- **Cross-domain retrieval**: Why needed - to enable finding music that matches the emotional content of speech input; Quick check - verify embedding space supports meaningful distance calculations between speech and music representations
- **Pre-trained encoder fine-tuning**: Why needed - to leverage existing feature representations while adapting to specific retrieval task; Quick check - monitor training stability and overfitting on small datasets

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Speech Audio -> Wav2vec 2.0 -> MLP -> Joint Embedding Space <- Music Tagging Transformer <- Music Audio

**Critical Path**: Input speech → Wav2vec 2.0 → MLP projection → Joint embedding → Nearest neighbor search → Retrieved music

**Design Tradeoffs**: The use of pre-trained encoders provides strong initialization but limits flexibility; emotion similarity regularization adds complexity but improves cross-domain alignment; valence-arousal mapping handles taxonomy mismatch but may lose fine-grained emotion distinctions.

**Failure Signatures**: Poor retrieval performance may indicate mismatched emotion label mappings, inadequate regularization, or overfitting on small datasets. Embedding space collapse could suggest learning rate issues or triplet loss configuration problems.

**First Experiments**:
1. Train with only triplet loss (no emotion regularization) to establish baseline performance
2. Test different emotion label mappings to valence-arousal space to find optimal configuration
3. Compare retrieval performance using different pre-trained encoder combinations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the proposed emotion similarity regularization term perform compared to other regularization techniques in cross-domain retrieval tasks beyond speech-to-music?
- Basis in paper: The authors state that the emotion similarity regularization helps capture the continuous distribution of emotions, leading to better retrieval results compared to baseline methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the proposed method on speech-to-music retrieval tasks. It is unclear how the method would perform on other cross-domain retrieval tasks such as image-to-text or video-to-music.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on various cross-domain retrieval tasks with different data modalities (e.g., image-to-text, video-to-music) using the proposed emotion similarity regularization term.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of using different pre-trained models for feature extraction on the performance of the speech-to-music retrieval framework?
- Basis in paper: The authors use pre-trained models such as Music Tagging Transformer, Wav2vec 2.0, and DistilBERT for feature extraction. It is not clear how the choice of these models affects the retrieval performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comparative analysis of using different pre-trained models for feature extraction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of the proposed framework using different combinations of pre-trained models for feature extraction.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed method handle cases where the speech and music emotion labels do not have a direct mapping in the valence-arousal space?
- Basis in paper: The authors use valence-arousal mapping to address the mismatched dataset taxonomies between speech and music domains. However, it is not clear how the method handles cases where the emotion labels do not have a direct mapping in the valence-arousal space.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not discuss the handling of cases where the emotion labels do not have a direct mapping in the valence-arousal space.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments or analysis on cases where the speech and music emotion labels do not have a direct mapping in the valence-arousal space, and how the proposed method handles such cases.

## Limitations
- Reliance on pre-trained encoders that may not be optimal for cross-domain emotion retrieval
- Limited evaluation on only three speech datasets and one music dataset
- Lack of human evaluation to verify perceived emotional alignment between speech and retrieved music
- Uncertainty about method generalization to broader applications beyond tested datasets

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Experimental results showing improved retrieval performance compared to baseline methods
- **Medium confidence**: Claims about emotion similarity regularization effectiveness and valence-arousal mapping utility
- **Low confidence**: Generalization claims and conceptual bridging of speech-music domain gaps
