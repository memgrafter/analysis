# Towards hate speech detection in low-resource languages: Comparing ASR to acoustic word embeddings on Wolof and Swahili

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00410
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00410
- Reference count: 0
- One-line primary result: ASR fine-tuning outperforms AWE in controlled settings but AWE is more robust to domain mismatch in real-world hate speech detection

## Executive Summary
This study compares two approaches for hate speech detection in low-resource languages: fine-tuning pre-trained multilingual ASR models and using acoustic word embeddings (AWE) for keyword spotting. The authors evaluate both methods on Wolof and Swahili using radio broadcast data. While ASR achieves higher performance in controlled settings with as little as five minutes of training data, the AWE approach proves more robust when deployed on real-world radio broadcasts with actual hate speech content.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune a pre-trained multilingual XLS-R ASR model on varying amounts of labeled data (5 minutes to 30 hours) for each target language. For the AWE approach, they train a multilingual CAE-RNN model on word pairs from five well-resourced languages, then apply query-by-example search to the target languages using template audio. Both methods are evaluated for keyword spotting performance using precision, recall, and F1-score, with in-domain testing on Common Voice data and out-of-domain testing on actual radio broadcasts.

## Key Results
- ASR fine-tuning achieves up to 98% F1-score with 30 hours of training data in controlled settings
- With just 5 minutes of training data, ASR achieves 59% (Swahili) and 60% (Wolof) F1-scores
- In real-world Swahili radio broadcasts, AWE achieves 45% precision versus 36-52% for ASR systems
- AWE approach shows greater robustness to domain mismatch compared to ASR systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multilingual acoustic word embeddings enable keyword spotting in unseen languages without requiring language-specific labeled data
- Mechanism: The AWE model is trained on multiple well-resourced languages using labelled word pairs, learning a shared embedding space where acoustically similar words across languages map to nearby vectors. When applied to a low-resource target language, the model can map speech segments to this space and compare them using cosine distance
- Core assumption: Acoustic patterns for similar words are sufficiently consistent across languages that a model trained on diverse languages can generalize to an unseen language
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "multilingual AWE model trained on labelled data from well-resourced languages to spot keywords in data in the unseen target language"
  - [section 4] "multilingual AWE model is trained on five different Common Voice languages... We pool the data from all languages and extract 300k positive word pairs which we use to train a multilingual CAE-RNN AWE model"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus evidence only shows related papers on multilingual AWEs but doesn't validate cross-language generalization
- Break condition: If the acoustic-phonetic patterns of the target language differ significantly from the training languages, or if the target language has unique phonetic features not represented in training data

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning pre-trained multilingual ASR models requires minimal labeled data for effective keyword spotting
- Mechanism: XLS-R, a pre-trained multilingual model, has learned cross-lingual speech representations during pre-training on 128 languages. Fine-tuning on just 5 minutes of labeled data adapts these representations to the target language while preserving the cross-lingual knowledge
- Core assumption: The pre-training has captured universal speech features that transfer well to low-resource languages with minimal adaptation
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "an ASR model trained on just five minutes of data outperforms the AWE approach"
  - [section 3] "We use an ASR model from the Wav2vec 2.0 XLS-R family... enabling a pre-trained ASR model to be fine-tuned using much less training data"
  - [section 6.1] "fine-tuning XLS-R on only five minutes of labelled data achieves decent KWS performance, with F1-scores of 59% and 60% for Swahili and Wolof"
- Break condition: If the target language is too phonetically distant from any of the 128 pre-training languages, or if the 5 minutes of data doesn't capture sufficient acoustic diversity

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: AWE-based KWS is more robust to domain mismatch than fine-tuned ASR systems
- Mechanism: The AWE approach relies on template-based matching that doesn't depend on language-specific acoustic models. Since it uses fixed templates and a learned embedding space, it's less sensitive to domain-specific acoustic variations that can degrade ASR performance
- Core assumption: Domain mismatch affects ASR systems more severely than template-based matching approaches because ASR relies on learned acoustic models that are domain-specific
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "in an in-the-wild test on Swahili radio broadcasts with actual hate speech keywords, the AWE model (using one minute of template data) is more robust, giving similar performance to an ASR system trained on 30 hours of labelled data"
  - [section 6.2] "the AWE approach seem to be more robust to domain mismatch compared to training an ASR system on one hour or five minutes of labelled data"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus evidence only shows related papers on domain adaptation but doesn't directly support this specific robustness claim
- Break condition: If the domain mismatch is so severe that the acoustic characteristics of the target domain are completely outside the distribution seen during AWE training

  - Quick check question: How does training on word pairs from multiple languages enable an AWE model to work on an unseen target language?

  - Why needed here: The paper's key finding is that AWE is more robust to domain mismatch, so understanding why domain mismatch affects ASR more requires knowledge of how acoustic models are affected by training data characteristics

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-trained models (XLS-R ASR, CAE-RNN AWE) -> Fine-tuning/training pipeline -> Keyword spotting application -> Evaluation framework -> Human annotation (for real-world test)

- Critical path: Data → Model training (ASR fine-tuning or AWE training) → Keyword spotting application → Evaluation → Human annotation (for real-world test)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - ASR approach: Higher performance in matched conditions but requires labeled data and is sensitive to domain mismatch
  - AWE approach: Lower performance in matched conditions but requires minimal data and is more robust to domain mismatch
  - Training time: AWE training requires multilingual data and takes time, but fine-tuning ASR is faster once the pre-trained model is available

- Failure signatures:
  - ASR failures: Poor keyword detection when domain mismatch occurs, degradation with insufficient training data
  - AWE failures: Poor keyword detection when the target language is phonetically very different from training languages, false positives from similar-sounding words

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train both ASR and AWE systems on in-domain data (same domain for training and testing) to establish baseline performance comparison
  2. Test both systems on out-of-domain data (radio broadcasts) to measure domain robustness
  3. Vary the amount of ASR training data (5min, 1hr, 30hr) to quantify the data-efficiency of the fine-tuning approach

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the performance trade-offs between ASR and AWE-based KWS systems change when using different amounts of labeled training data beyond the five-minute to 30-hour range tested?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that ASR performance improves with more training data, but also mention that even five minutes of data yields high precision (95% for Swahili, 88% for Wolof) in controlled settings
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests ASR systems trained on three specific amounts of data (5 minutes, 1 hour, and 30 hours), leaving a gap in understanding the performance curve for intermediate or larger amounts of data
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing ASR systems with varying amounts of labeled data (e.g., 15 minutes, 2 hours, 10 hours) and comparing their KWS performance to AWE-based systems in both controlled and real-world settings

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the specific factors contributing to the superior robustness of AWE-based KWS systems in out-of-domain settings compared to ASR systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that AWE systems are more robust to domain mismatch, as evidenced by fewer music retrievals and better precision in the in-the-wild Swahili radio broadcast test
- Why unresolved: The paper does not delve into the underlying reasons for this robustness, such as differences in how the systems handle acoustic variability or background noise
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of the acoustic features and segment matches in both ASR and AWE systems, along with experiments varying the domain mismatch (e.g., testing on different types of audio data)

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of AWE-based KWS systems vary with the number and diversity of template queries used?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors use a fixed number of templates (e.g., ten templates per keyword) for the AWE system but do not explore the impact of varying this number or using more diverse templates
- Why unresolved: The study does not investigate how the choice and quantity of template queries affect the system's ability to detect keywords, particularly in challenging real-world scenarios
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing AWE systems with different numbers of templates and varying levels of diversity in the template set, measuring changes in precision and recall across controlled and real-world datasets

## Limitations

- The study uses controlled experiments with predefined keywords and test sets, which may not fully capture the complexity of real-world hate speech detection where context and nuance matter significantly
- The comparison between ASR and AWE approaches uses different evaluation methodologies (automated scoring for in-domain vs human annotation for out-of-domain), making direct performance comparisons potentially biased
- The AWE approach relies on unsupervised segmentation and template selection, which introduces variability that isn't fully characterized in the results

## Confidence

- High confidence: The finding that ASR fine-tuning on 5 minutes of data achieves reasonable performance in controlled conditions is well-supported by the experimental results
- Medium confidence: The claim that AWE is more robust to domain mismatch is supported by the real-world Swahili radio broadcast results, but the evaluation methodology differs from the controlled experiments
- Low confidence: The practical applicability of these approaches for actual hate speech detection is limited by the simplified keyword-based evaluation and lack of contextual understanding

## Next Checks

1. Replication with different hate speech keywords: Test both approaches with alternative keyword sets to verify that the performance differences aren't specific to the particular keywords chosen in this study

2. Ablation study on AWE template selection: Systematically evaluate how different template selection strategies (random, multiple templates, template quality) affect AWE performance to understand the sensitivity to this critical component

3. Cross-lingual transfer validation: Test the AWE approach on a third low-resource language not similar to the training languages to validate whether the cross-lingual generalization claims hold beyond the tested languages
