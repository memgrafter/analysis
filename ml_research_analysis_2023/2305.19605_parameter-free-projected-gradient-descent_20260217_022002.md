# Parameter-free projected gradient descent

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19605
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19605
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Presents FREE ADAGRAD algorithm achieving optimal regret bounds up to logarithmic factors for convex optimization without requiring knowledge of distance to optimum, Lipschitz constant, or time horizon

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a parameter-free projected gradient descent algorithm called FREE ADAGRAD for convex optimization over closed convex sets. The algorithm adaptively tunes the step size using a doubling trick when the distance to initialization exceeds a threshold, eliminating the need for prior knowledge of problem parameters. It achieves optimal regret bounds up to logarithmic factors and extends to stochastic optimization. The algorithm is simple to implement, requiring no line searches, restarts, or extra gradient evaluations compared to classical PGD.

## Method Summary
The method implements a parameter-free AdaGrad variant where the step size is dynamically adjusted based on the distance between the current iterate and the initialization point. When this distance exceeds a threshold proportional to the current step size, the algorithm doubles the step size. This adaptive mechanism ensures progress toward the optimum without requiring knowledge of the Lipschitz constant, distance to optimum, or time horizon. The algorithm handles non-Lipschitz convex functions by adapting to the sum of squared subgradient norms and maintains optimal regret bounds through careful threshold selection.

## Key Results
- Achieves optimal regret bounds up to logarithmic factors without requiring problem parameters
- Handles projection steps and non-Lipschitz convex functions through adaptive step size tuning
- Performs on par or better than existing methods in experiments
- No restarts, reweighing, or additional gradient evaluations compared to classical PGD

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The doubling trick on the step size when the distance to initialization exceeds a threshold ensures adaptivity without prior knowledge of the optimal distance.
- **Mechanism**: Algorithm 1 monitors the distance between the current iterate and the initialization point. When this distance exceeds a threshold proportional to the current step size, the step size is doubled. This allows the algorithm to automatically detect when the initial step size is too small and adaptively increase it until it is sufficiently large to make progress towards the optimum.
- **Core assumption**: The distance from the initialization to the optimum is finite and can be bounded by a function of the initial guess and the observed subgradients.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "The key idea is to use a doubling trick on the step size when the distance to the initialization exceeds a threshold, ensuring adaptivity."
  - [section] "Indeed, we observe that if ∥x+t(kt−1) − x1∥ > 3γkt−1, then it means that γkt−1 < ∥x1 − x∗∥, and our step-size tuning then increases k until the condition ∥x+t(k) − x1∥ ⩽ 3γk is met."
- **Break condition**: If the step size grows too quickly, the algorithm may overshoot the optimum and oscillate, leading to poor convergence. The design ensures that the number of doublings is logarithmic in the ratio of the distance to the optimum and the initial guess.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The choice of the threshold Bt+1(k) = 2γk√k + Γ2t + γ2k/T allows for a tighter control of the number of phases and improves the logarithmic factor in the regret bound.
- **Mechanism**: By setting the threshold to include a term proportional to √k, the algorithm implicitly tracks the distance to the optimum more closely. This prevents unnecessary step size increases and keeps the number of phases within a logarithmic factor of the optimal value. The Γ2t term ensures that the threshold adapts to the cumulative gradient norms.
- **Core assumption**: The distance to the optimum can be tracked using the observed subgradients and the step size history.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section] "To improve the log factor, our second ingredient is to choose a slightly smaller threshold Bt+1, at the price of possibly moderately increasing the number kT of doubling."
  - [section] "Setting Bt+1(k) = 2γk√k + Γ2t + γ2k/T, we get that kT ⩽ k∗ + 0.5 log2(k∗) + 1.25, and instead of log(Dγ0) we have √log(Dγ0) in the regret bound."
- **Break condition**: If the subgradients are not well-behaved or the distance to the optimum is extremely large, the √k term might not provide sufficient control, leading to a larger number of phases than expected.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The algorithm handles non-Lipschitz convex functions by adapting to the sum of squared subgradient norms, achieving optimal regret bounds up to logarithmic factors.
- **Mechanism**: The algorithm does not require a global Lipschitz constant. Instead, it uses the sum of squared subgradients to adapt the step size. This allows it to handle functions that are not globally Lipschitz but still convex. The regret bound depends on the distance to the optimum and the sum of squared subgradients, both of which are estimated adaptively.
- **Core assumption**: The function is convex and has a bounded minimizer, ensuring that the sum of squared subgradients is finite over any finite horizon.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Our algorithm is able to handle projection steps, does not involve restarts, reweighing along the trajectory or additional gradient evaluations compared to the classical PGD. It also fulfills optimal rates of convergence for cumulative regret up to logarithmic factors."
  - [section] "Let us highlight that we do not assume that the subgradients gt are uniformly bounded, that is, we do not require f to be globally Lipschitz."
- **Break condition**: If the function is not convex or does not have a bounded minimizer, the sum of squared subgradients may grow without bound, invalidating the regret bound.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Initialization -> Iteration (subgradient computation, threshold calculation, step size selection) -> Update (projected gradient step) -> Output trajectory

- **Critical path**:
  1. Compute subgradient gt
  2. Update cumulative gradient norm St
  3. Calculate threshold Bt+1(k) for all relevant k
  4. Find the smallest k such that ∥x+t(k) − x1∥ ⩽ Bt+1(k)
  5. Perform the projected gradient step with step size γk
  6. Update the trajectory and continue to the next iteration

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Simplicity vs. adaptivity: The algorithm is simple to implement but requires checking multiple thresholds to find the appropriate step size
  - Logarithmic factors: The regret bounds include logarithmic factors, which are unavoidable in the general non-Lipschitz setting but can be improved in specific cases
  - Parameter-free vs. parameter-dependent: The algorithm is parameter-free, but the initial guess γ0 affects the logarithmic factors in the regret bound

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Slow convergence: If the initial guess γ0 is too small, the algorithm may take many iterations to reach a sufficiently large step size, leading to slow initial progress
  - Oscillations: If the step size grows too quickly, the algorithm may overshoot the optimum and oscillate, especially in ill-conditioned problems
  - Divergence: If the function is not convex or does not have a bounded minimizer, the algorithm may not converge, and the regret bounds may not hold

## Limitations
- Performance depends critically on the initial step size guess γ0, which affects logarithmic factors in the regret bound
- Limited experimental validation across diverse problem classes, restricting confidence in real-world applicability
- Theoretical guarantees rely on convexity and bounded minimizer assumptions that may not hold in practice

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism of using doubling tricks for step size adaptation is well-established and theoretically sound. The regret bounds up to logarithmic factors are rigorously proven.
- **Medium Confidence**: The extension to stochastic optimization and the handling of non-Lipschitz convex functions are theoretically justified but lack comprehensive empirical validation.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that the algorithm performs "on par or better" than existing methods is based on limited experiments and requires further validation.
