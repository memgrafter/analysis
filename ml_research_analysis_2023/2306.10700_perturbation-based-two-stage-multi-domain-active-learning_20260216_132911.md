# Perturbation-Based Two-Stage Multi-Domain Active Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10700
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10700
- Reference count: 23
- Key outcome: Introduces P2S-MDAL, a two-stage perturbation-based active learning method that achieves superior performance across three real-world datasets by selecting cross-domain influential samples

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of high labeling effort in multi-domain learning (MDL) by introducing P2S-MDAL, a two-stage perturbation-based active learning method. The approach combines budget allocation based on domain sample sizes with diversity selection through k-means clustering, followed by a perturbation metric to identify cross-domain influential samples. Experiments on Amazon, COIL, and FDUMTL datasets demonstrate consistent performance improvements over conventional active learning strategies, showcasing the method's ability to leverage shared information while reducing labeling effort.

## Method Summary
P2S-MDAL operates in two stages: first, it allocates budgets to domains proportionally to their sample sizes and establishes diversity regions within each domain using k-means clustering on gradient embeddings; second, it evaluates cross-domain influence by applying Gaussian perturbations to samples and computing KL divergence between original and perturbed outputs. The method selects the most influential samples from each region for labeling, iteratively retraining the ASP-MTL model with newly annotated data until the budget is exhausted.

## Key Results
- P2S-MDAL consistently outperforms conventional active learning strategies across three diverse datasets
- The perturbation-based evaluation effectively identifies cross-domain influential samples
- Budget allocation based on domain sample size ensures fair representation across domains
- The two-stage approach successfully combines diversity and cross-domain influence for sample selection

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Perturbation-based cross-domain influence evaluation identifies samples that are most informative to the shared feature extractor across domains.
- Mechanism: Samples that are more informative to the shared feature extractor are more vulnerable to perturbations, leading to larger KL divergence between original and perturbed outputs.
- Core assumption: The shared feature extractor learns domain-invariant representations, and perturbations expose the model's uncertainty about informative samples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A perturbation metric has been introduced to evaluate the robustness of the shared feature extractor of the model, facilitating the identification of potentially cross-domain influential samples."
  - [section] "The intuition of our method is that if a sample is informative to the shared feature extractor, it will be informative to all domains... if a sample is more informative to the shared feature extractor, it will be more vulnerable to perturbations"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Budget allocation based on domain sample size ensures fair in-domain comparison during selection.
- Mechanism: Domains with more samples receive proportionally larger budgets, preventing smaller domains from being underrepresented in the selection process.
- Core assumption: Domain sample size is a reasonable proxy for domain importance or influence on the overall model performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Here we take the total number of samples as an influence estimate. Let 𝑛𝑘 denote the number of samples in domain 𝑘, and 𝐵 denote the total budget. The budget allocation is calculated as follows: 𝐵𝑘 = 𝑛𝑘Í𝐾𝑖=1 𝑛𝑖 × 𝐵"
  - [section] "To avoid the incomparability between sample evaluation scores from different domains, we constrain the score-based selection within each domain."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-stage selection (budget allocation + region establishment + perturbation evaluation) ensures both diversity and cross-domain influence.
- Mechanism: First stage allocates budgets and creates diverse regions within each domain, then second stage uses perturbation metrics to select most cross-domain influential samples within each region.
- Core assumption: Combining diversity (through k-means clustering) with cross-domain influence (through perturbation) yields better sample selection than either approach alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "P2S-MDAL involves allocating budgets for domains and establishing regions for diversity selection, which are further used to select the most cross-domain influential samples in each region."
  - [section] "Next, to ensure the diversity of the sample selection, the selection space is divided in each domain with the corresponding allocated budgets. We employ the 𝑘-Means algorithm to divide the selection space into 𝐵𝑘 regions for the 𝑘-th domain."
  - [section] "The underlying intuition is that if a sample is more informative to the shared extractor, it will be more vulnerable to perturbations"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Domain budget allocator (proportional to domain size) -> Region establisher (k-means clustering on gradients) -> Perturbation evaluator (Gaussian perturbations + KL divergence) -> Sample selector (chooses highest-scoring sample from each region)

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize model on small labeled set
  2. Calculate domain budgets based on sample sizes
  3. For each domain: cluster samples into regions using gradients
  4. For each region: perturb samples and calculate KL divergence scores
  5. Select top-scoring sample from each region for labeling
  6. Retrain model with newly labeled samples
  7. Repeat until budget exhausted

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using gradient embeddings for clustering vs. raw features: gradients are more discriminative but computationally heavier
  - Number of perturbation samples: more samples give better estimates but increase computation time
  - Region count vs. budget: too few regions wastes budget, too many creates computational burden

- Failure signatures:
  - If all selected samples come from one or two regions, budget allocation or clustering may be incorrect
  - If selected samples show low cross-domain influence, perturbation metric may not be working
  - If performance doesn't improve over iterations, either the model isn't learning or the selection isn't effective

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run on single domain with known easy/hard samples to verify perturbation metric correlates with uncertainty
  2. Test budget allocation by running with equal budgets vs. proportional budgets to see impact on smaller domains
  3. Compare performance with and without region establishment to verify diversity component adds value

## Limitations

- The effectiveness of the perturbation metric depends on the shared feature extractor successfully learning domain-invariant representations, which may not hold in all scenarios
- The budget allocation strategy assumes domain size is a reasonable proxy for domain importance, which may not be true when domains have inherently different labeling difficulties
- The method requires careful tuning of hyperparameters including perturbation amplitude, number of samples, and region count

## Confidence

- **Medium**: The perturbation-based evaluation mechanism (Mechanism 1) - while theoretically sound, depends on untested assumptions about the relationship between perturbation vulnerability and cross-domain influence
- **Medium**: The budget allocation strategy (Mechanism 2) - reasonable but potentially oversimplified approach to domain balancing
- **High**: The two-stage framework design (Mechanism 3) - the combination of diversity and influence evaluation is well-established in active learning literature
