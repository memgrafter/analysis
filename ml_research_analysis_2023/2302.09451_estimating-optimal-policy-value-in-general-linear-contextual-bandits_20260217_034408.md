# Estimating Optimal Policy Value in General Linear Contextual Bandits

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.09451
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09451
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Achieves sublinear sample complexity of O(√d) for estimating optimal policy value V* in contextual bandits using polynomial approximation and moment estimation.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of estimating the optimal policy value in contextual bandits without learning the optimal policy itself. The authors develop two novel approaches: a moment-based estimator using polynomial approximation of the max function, and a Gaussian process upper bound estimator. These methods enable efficient model selection and treatment effect testing by providing fast estimates of V* with sublinear sample complexity in the feature dimension.

## Method Summary
The authors propose two main algorithms: (1) a moment-based estimator that approximates the max function using polynomials and estimates moments independently, achieving O(√d) sample complexity under known covariance assumptions, and (2) a Gaussian process upper bound estimator that constructs a Gaussian process majorizing the reward process and estimates its expected maximum using Monte Carlo sampling. The methods are applied to improve model selection regret and treatment effect testing efficiency.

## Key Results
- Achieves sublinear sample complexity of O(√d) for V* estimation using polynomial approximation of max function
- Develops efficient Gaussian process upper bound estimator requiring O(√d/ε²) samples
- Improves model selection regret from O(K^(1/3)) to O(√log K)
- Reduces treatment effect testing gap threshold from O(√d/n) to O(√d/p + d^(1/4)/√n)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Sublinear O(√d) sample complexity is achievable for V* estimation using polynomial approximation of the max function
- Mechanism: Reduce V* estimation to estimating moments of a polynomial approximation of the max function. Use independent unbiased estimators for each moment, then combine via median-of-means to achieve sublinear scaling in dimension
- Core assumption: The covariance matrices are known and well-conditioned (Assumption 1), and moments up to degree t are known or computable
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "under stronger assumptions, we give an algorithm and analysis proving that O(√d) sublinear estimation of V* is indeed information-theoretically possible"
  - [section]: "We achieve this by estimating individual α-moments between the {⟨θ, φ(X, a)⟩}_a random variables using Equation (2)"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct mention of polynomial approximation for bandits
- Break condition: When Assumption 1 is violated (ill-conditioned covariances) or moments beyond second order are unknown/uncomputable

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Estimating a Gaussian process upper bound on V* can be done efficiently with O(√d/ε²) samples
- Mechanism: Construct a Gaussian process that majorizes the reward process and estimate its expected maximum using increment estimators. The Gaussian approximation enables fast statistical estimation
- Core assumption: The process has increments that are sub-Gaussian (Assumption 4) and the covariance structure allows for good Gaussian approximation
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We then present a more practical, computationally efficient algorithm that estimates a problem-dependent upper bound on V*"
  - [section]: "Our main insight is to majorize the general stochastic process using a Gaussian process {Za}a∈[K] ~ N(0, Λ)"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct mention of Gaussian process majorization for bandits
- Break condition: When the reward process has heavy-tailed increments that violate sub-Gaussianity or when the Gaussian approximation becomes poor (e.g., extreme non-Gaussian distributions)

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fast V* estimation enables improved model selection and treatment effect testing
- Mechanism: Use V* estimation to test whether simpler model classes achieve the same optimal value, and to test whether additional treatments provide meaningful improvement
- Core assumption: The gap between optimal values can be bounded by V* values of derived problems, which can be estimated efficiently
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We use this upper bound and the estimator to obtain novel and improved guarantees for several applications in bandit model selection and testing for treatment effects"
  - [section]: "We show that it can be used to develop provably efficient tests for treatment effects"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct mention of model selection applications
- Break condition: When the gap between model classes is too small to detect with available samples, or when the assumptions about the feature structure don't hold

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Is the exponential dependence on 1/ε in the sample complexity of Corollary 3.5 truly unimprovable in general, or can it be reduced to polynomial dependence while maintaining the sublinear d^(1/2) scaling?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors conjecture this exponential dependence is unimprovable but cannot prove it, suggesting it might be shown by "matching moments."
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge this as an important open problem but have not been able to verify the conjecture. Proving or disproving this would require novel techniques beyond current moment-matching approaches
- What evidence would resolve it: A proof showing that any polynomial approximation achieving the required error bounds must have exponential dependence on 1/ε, or a construction of a polynomial approximation with polynomial dependence on 1/ε that still achieves the sublinear d^(1/2) sample complexity

### Open Question 2
- Question: How much does the sample complexity degrade when the covariance matrices are unknown and must be estimated from data, particularly in high-dimensional settings where d is large?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention this extension in Appendix F but note that the analysis is more involved and the sample complexity depends on the estimation error of the covariance matrices
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the case of known covariance matrices and only sketches the extension to unknown covariances. A rigorous analysis of the trade-off between the number of unlabeled samples needed for covariance estimation and the resulting impact on the overall sample complexity is missing
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the sample complexity of the algorithm when covariance matrices are estimated from data, including explicit bounds on the number of unlabeled samples required and the resulting degradation in the overall sample complexity as a function of d and the estimation error

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the results of Section 4 be extended to settings where the feature vectors are not sub-Gaussian but satisfy weaker moment conditions, such as sub-Weibull distributions or heavy-tailed distributions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper relies heavily on sub-Gaussian assumptions for the feature vectors in both the moment-based estimator and the Gaussian process upper bound estimator. The authors mention that the Gaussian process upper bound is tight when the context distribution is Gaussian, suggesting that the results may not extend to non-sub-Gaussian settings
- Why unresolved: The current analysis relies on specific properties of sub-Gaussian distributions, such as the Hanson-Wright inequality and concentration of measure. Extending the results to weaker moment conditions would require developing new techniques that can handle the heavier tails and potential lack of concentration
- What evidence would resolve it: A modified version of the Gaussian process upper bound estimator that achieves sublinear sample complexity in d for feature vectors satisfying weaker moment conditions, along with a rigorous analysis of the resulting sample complexity and error bounds

## Limitations
- The moment-based estimator requires known covariance matrices, which may not be realistic in practice
- The Gaussian process approximation may perform poorly when the reward process has heavy-tailed increments or is far from Gaussian
- The sublinear scaling assumes access to high-quality moment estimators, which may be challenging to achieve in practice
