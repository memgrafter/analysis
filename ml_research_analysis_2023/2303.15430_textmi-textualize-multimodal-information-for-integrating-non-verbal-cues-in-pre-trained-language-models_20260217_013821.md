# TextMI: Textualize Multimodal Information for Integrating Non-verbal Cues in Pre-trained Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15430
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15430
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: TextMI achieves state-of-the-art performance on multimodal sarcasm detection and near-SOTA on sentiment and humor tasks by textualizing non-verbal cues and feeding them to BERT.

## Executive Summary
TextMI proposes a simple yet effective method to integrate visual and acoustic non-verbal cues into pre-trained language models for multimodal behavioral analysis. The approach clusters facial action units and acoustic features from videos, generates textual descriptions of these clusters, and concatenates them with the spoken text. This augmented text is then fed into a pre-trained BERT model for sentiment, humor, and sarcasm detection tasks. TextMI significantly outperforms state-of-the-art models in multimodal sarcasm detection and achieves near state-of-the-art performance in multimodal sentiment and humor detection, demonstrating the effectiveness of this simple approach in integrating non-verbal cues presented in textual form.

## Method Summary
TextMI extracts word-aligned visual (facial action units) and acoustic features from videos using OpenFace and OpenSmile. These features are clustered using K-means, and dominant features per cluster are identified and described textually. The spoken text is then concatenated with the visual-text and acoustic-text descriptions to form the BERT input. The model is fine-tuned end-to-end on the target task using standard BERT training procedures. This approach simplifies multimodal fusion by leveraging BERT's pre-trained capacity to interpret textualized non-verbal cues, avoiding the need for complex multimodal encoders and fusion layers.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art performance on multimodal sarcasm detection task
- Achieves near state-of-the-art performance on multimodal sentiment and humor detection tasks
- Significantly reduces model complexity compared to joint modeling of multiple modalities

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pre-trained BERT can understand multimodal behavioral cues when non-verbal information is represented as textual descriptions.
- Mechanism: TextMI converts visual (facial action units) and acoustic (pitch, loudness, jitter, shimmer) features into text clusters, concatenates them with spoken text, and feeds them into BERT. BERT's attention mechanism integrates these text representations across modalities.
- Core assumption: BERT's self-attention and contextual embeddings can interpret non-verbal cues encoded as text with the same effectiveness as traditional multimodal fusion models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] states TextMI achieves superior or near-SOTA performance by textualizing non-verbal cues and feeding them to BERT.
  - [section] explains how facial action units and acoustic features are clustered, described textually, and combined with speech for BERT input.
  - [corpus] shows related work on integrating non-verbal cues in language models, supporting the general feasibility of the approach.
- Break condition: If the textual description of non-verbal features is too abstract or loses critical timing/relational information, BERT may fail to capture subtle behavioral nuances.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: K-means clustering of unimodal features enables grouping of co-occurring non-verbal signals that humans naturally express together.
- Mechanism: Extracted action unit and acoustic feature vectors are clustered using K-means, dominant features per cluster are identified, and textual descriptions of those dominant features become the non-verbal text representation.
- Core assumption: K-means clustering with silhouette score effectively groups semantically related non-verbal expressions that humans perceive as a single behavioral cue.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] details using K-means on action unit and acoustic feature vectors, selecting clusters by silhouette score, and describing dominant features as text.
  - [abstract] confirms that these clusters are textualized and combined with speech for model input.
  - [corpus] includes related papers on multimodal fusion, indicating clustering is a known approach for grouping multimodal signals.
- Break condition: If clusters are too granular or too broad, the textual description may miss important distinctions or become too vague for BERT to differentiate.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Simplifying multimodal fusion to text concatenation reduces model complexity while maintaining or improving performance.
- Mechanism: Instead of separate encoders and complex fusion layers, TextMI feeds all modalities as a single extended text input to BERT, leveraging BERT's pre-trained capacity.
- Core assumption: BERT's large-scale pre-training provides sufficient capacity to learn multimodal interactions from concatenated textual descriptions without separate modality encoders.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] emphasizes TextMI significantly reduces model complexity compared to joint modeling of multiple modalities.
  - [section] contrasts TextMI's single BERT encoder with complex multimodal architectures using multiple transformers and fusion layers.
  - [corpus] lists related multimodal fusion methods, showing the complexity reduction claim is plausible.
- Break condition: If BERT's capacity is insufficient to model complex cross-modal dependencies from text alone, performance may degrade compared to specialized multimodal encoders.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Feature extraction: OpenFace (visual/AUs), OpenSmile (acoustic)
  - Clustering: K-means on extracted feature vectors
  - Textualization: Generate descriptions of dominant features per cluster
  - Input construction: Concatenate spoken text with visual-text and acoustic-text
  - Model: Pre-trained BERT fine-tuned on multimodal tasks

- Critical path:
  1. Extract word-aligned visual and acoustic features from videos
  2. Cluster features and generate textual descriptions
  3. Concatenate descriptions with spoken text
  4. Feed to BERT and fine-tune for target task

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Simplicity vs. potential information loss: BERT-only approach is simpler but may lose some cross-modal interactions that complex fusion captures.
  - Interpretability vs. performance: Textual descriptions make model decisions interpretable but may abstract away fine-grained details.
  - Clustering granularity: Too few clusters lose nuance; too many may overfit or create redundancy.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor clustering silhouette scores → noisy or uninformative non-verbal text
  - BERT fine-tuning diverges or overfits → input representation may be too complex or too simple
  - Performance gap vs. complex multimodal models → may indicate loss of subtle cross-modal dependencies

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test clustering quality: Run K-means on extracted features with different K values and evaluate silhouette scores; verify generated text descriptions match expected expressions.
  2. Ablation study: Compare BERT fine-tuning with text-only vs. text+visual-text vs. text+acoustic-text to confirm each modality's contribution.
  3. Interpretability check: Use integrated gradients to visualize token importance for a sample prediction; verify non-verbal text tokens are being attended to.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of TextMI scale with increasing dataset size, and at what point might complex multimodal models outperform it?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests TextMI is particularly effective for low-resource settings and tasks with limited training data, implying performance may change with larger datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on low-resource settings and doesn't provide experiments or analysis of performance scaling with larger datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing TextMI's performance on datasets of varying sizes, particularly when moving from low-resource to high-resource scenarios, would clarify its performance scaling and when complex models might become more advantageous.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would an end-to-end model trained from representative and unbiased data for textualizing acoustic and visual features perform compared to TextMI?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that TextMI depends on existing tools like Openface and Opensmile, which may introduce errors and biases. They suggest exploring an end-to-end model trained from representative and unbiased data as future work.
- Why unresolved: The paper proposes this as future work but doesn't implement or test such a model, leaving the performance comparison open.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating an end-to-end model that directly converts acoustic and visual features to text, then comparing its performance to TextMI on the same tasks, would resolve this question.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of clustering algorithm (e.g., K-means vs. Gaussian Mixture Models) affect the performance and interpretability of TextMI?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors chose K-means clustering because it provides hard labels for clusters, making it easier to convert to words. They mention that other techniques like Gaussian Mixture Models give probabilities, making text conversion difficult, but don't explore the performance implications of this choice.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't compare the performance of TextMI using different clustering algorithms or explore the trade-offs between interpretability and potential information loss.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing TextMI's performance using different clustering algorithms (e.g., K-means, GMM, hierarchical clustering) would reveal the impact of this choice on both performance and interpretability.

## Limitations
- The approach relies heavily on the quality of clustering and textual descriptions of non-verbal features, which may not capture all nuances of facial expressions and acoustic signals.
- Performance is demonstrated primarily on datasets with relatively clear non-verbal cues, and effectiveness on more subtle or ambiguous cases remains untested.
- The method's performance scaling with increasing dataset size and when it might be outperformed by complex multimodal models is unknown.

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The mechanism that BERT can process concatenated textual descriptions of non-verbal cues, given the strong empirical results and reasonable alignment with BERT's known capabilities.

**Medium confidence**: The clustering approach using K-means and silhouette scores for grouping non-verbal features, as the method is standard but effectiveness depends on feature quality and clustering parameters.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that this approach significantly reduces model complexity while maintaining performance, though this is supported by comparison to more complex multimodal architectures.
