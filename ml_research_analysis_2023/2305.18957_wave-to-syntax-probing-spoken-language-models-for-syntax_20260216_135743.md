# Wave to Syntax: Probing spoken language models for syntax

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2305.18957
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18957
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Key outcome:** Spoken language models encode syntactic information most prominently in middle layers, with larger models capturing more explicit syntax, though generally weaker than text-based models.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates syntactic information encoding in deep models of spoken language using TreeDepth and TreeKernel probes across several self-supervised and visually-grounded models. The research reveals that syntax is most prominently captured in middle transformer layers, with larger models encoding more explicit syntactic structure. However, the encoding is generally weaker than in text-based models, and much of the captured syntactic structure appears to be encoded in lexical rather than purely syntactic form. The findings provide important insights into how speech models represent linguistic structure and highlight differences between self-supervised and visually-grounded training objectives.

## Method Summary
The study employs two probing techniques - TreeDepth (predicting constituency tree depth) and TreeKernel (measuring similarity between model embeddings and syntactic trees via representational similarity analysis) - on LibriSpeech and SpokenCOCO datasets. Ridge regression models are trained for TreeDepth probe and RSA regress models for TreeKernel probe across transformer layers of various speech models including wav2vec2, HuBERT, and FaST-VGS variants, with BERT serving as a text model reference. R² scores are evaluated per layer and compared against Bag-of-Words and word count baselines.

## Key Results
- Syntax is captured most prominently in middle layers of speech models across all model types
- Larger models (wav2vec2-large) encode more explicit syntax than smaller variants
- Self-supervised training captures more syntax in later layers than visually-grounded training
- Syntactic encoding in speech models is generally weaker than in text-based models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Syntax is encoded most prominently in the middle layers of speech models
- Mechanism: The middle layers serve as a "syntactic workspace" where acoustic features are abstracted into hierarchical linguistic representations, before being refined into task-specific outputs in later layers
- Core assumption: The model's training objectives create pressure to encode useful syntactic information at intermediate stages of processing
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We show that syntax is captured most prominently in the middle layers of the networks"
  - [section]: Figures 1 and 2 show clear peaks in syntactic probe scores at middle layers across multiple models
  - [corpus]: Weak corpus support - only 1 of 8 related papers mentions middle-layer probing
- Break condition: If the model's training objective doesn't require hierarchical structure (e.g., pure reconstruction), middle layers may not develop strong syntactic encoding

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Self-supervised training captures more syntax than visually-grounded training in later layers
- Mechanism: Self-supervised objectives like masked prediction require maintaining syntactic dependencies across masked regions, while visual grounding focuses more on semantic correspondence
- Core assumption: The masked language modeling objective creates stronger pressure for syntax preservation than image-text matching
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "self-supervised and combined objectives lead to less syntactic encoding in the final model layers"
  - [abstract]: "self-supervised and combined objectives lead to less syntactic encoding in the final model layers, while the visually-supervised objective does not have this effect"
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus support for this specific claim about self-vs-visual supervision effects
- Break condition: If visual grounding requires complex syntactic structures to describe images accurately, the advantage of self-supervised methods may diminish

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Larger models encode more explicit syntax
- Mechanism: Increased model capacity allows dedicating separate representational space to abstract syntactic information rather than collapsing it with lexical features
- Core assumption: Parameter count directly correlates with representational capacity for abstract linguistic structures
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "more explicitly within models with more parameters"
  - [section]: "the larger hidden size and deeper model architecture prove useful in encoding extra information" - wav2vec2-large-ft outperforms smaller variants
  - [corpus]: Weak corpus support - no related papers explicitly discuss parameter size effects on syntax encoding
- Break condition: If increased capacity leads to better lexical representations that subsume syntactic information, larger models might not show better abstract syntax encoding

## Limitations

- Relatively weak syntactic encoding compared to text-based models suggests speech models may prioritize acoustic-phonetic information over hierarchical structure
- Corpus support for key claims is notably weak, with limited related papers addressing middle-layer probing effects or parameter size impacts
- Study focuses exclusively on English datasets, limiting generalizability to other languages and linguistic typologies

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The observation that syntax is captured most prominently in middle layers is well-supported by consistent probe scores across multiple models and visualization in Figures 1 and 2.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that larger models encode more explicit syntax has moderate support from the wav2vec2-large-ft vs. base comparisons, but lacks strong corpus validation.

**Low confidence**: The broader claim that "spoken language models encode less syntax than text models" is based on a single BERT comparison without accounting for modality differences or training objectives.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-linguistic validation**: Apply the same probing methodology to multilingual speech models (e.g., XLS-R) and typologically diverse languages to determine whether the observed middle-layer syntax encoding is universal or English-specific.

2. **Control for lexical confounds**: Design an ablation study where lexical information is explicitly controlled (e.g., using delexicalized representations or controlled vocabulary) to isolate pure syntactic encoding from lexical-syntactic entanglement.

3. **Task-based validation**: Test whether the syntactically informative middle layers actually contribute to downstream syntactic tasks (e.g., syntactic parsing or grammaticality judgments) rather than just showing probe correlations, to validate that the encoded information is functionally useful.
