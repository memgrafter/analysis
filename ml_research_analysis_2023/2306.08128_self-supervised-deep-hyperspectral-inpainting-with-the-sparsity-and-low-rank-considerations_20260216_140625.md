# Self-supervised Deep Hyperspectral Inpainting with the Sparsity and Low-Rank Considerations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08128
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08128
- Reference count: 40
- The LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm achieves MPSNR of 42.488 and MSSIM of 0.9487 on a real hyperspectral dataset

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of hyperspectral image inpainting by proposing two self-supervised algorithms that combine sparsity and low-rank constraints without requiring external training data. The LRS-PnP and LRS-PnP-DIP algorithms leverage the intrinsic structure of hyperspectral images by exploiting spectral and spatial correlations through dictionary-based sparse coding and low-rank minimization. The LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm incorporates a deep image prior that learns the low-rank structure directly from the noisy image, achieving state-of-the-art performance with MPSNR of 42.488 and MSSIM of 0.9487 on real hyperspectral data.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a two-stage approach for hyperspectral inpainting that learns a dictionary from the noisy incomplete image and then applies an iterative optimization algorithm. The LRS-PnP algorithm uses alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) to solve for sparse representations and low-rank spatial correlations, while LRS-PnP-DIP replaces the low-rank step with a deep image prior network. Both algorithms operate in a self-supervised manner, requiring no external training data. The dictionary learning step extracts spectral signatures from the available pixels, and the optimization alternates between sparse coding using a plug-and-play denoiser and either singular value thresholding or deep image prior for low-rank structure learning.

## Key Results
- LRS-PnP-DIP achieves MPSNR of 42.488 and MSSIM of 0.9487 on real hyperspectral dataset
- Outperforms existing traditional methods like BM4D and NLTV
- Demonstrates competitive performance against supervised deep learning methods without requiring training data
- Shows stable convergence under mild assumptions with theoretical guarantees

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The proposed algorithms use sparsity and low-rank constraints to exploit the intrinsic structure of hyperspectral images, enabling accurate inpainting without external training data.
- Mechanism: The algorithm combines a sparse representation of spectral signatures with a low-rank assumption on spatial correlations. The sparse coding step decomposes the image into a linear combination of dictionary atoms, while the low-rank step captures global spatial patterns. These constraints regularize the ill-posed inpainting problem, ensuring that the solution is both spectrally and spatially coherent.
- Core assumption: Hyperspectral images exhibit high correlation between spectral channels and spatial pixels, allowing them to be well-approximated by sparse and low-rank representations.
- Evidence anchors: The authors introduce two novel self-supervised Hyperspectral Images (HSI) inpainting algorithms: Low Rank and Sparsity Constraint Plug-and-Play (LRS-PnP), and its extension LRS-PnP-DIP, which features the strong learning capability, but is still free of external training data.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The Plug-and-Play (PnP) framework allows the integration of powerful denoisers and deep image priors into the inpainting process, improving reconstruction quality.
- Mechanism: The PnP framework replaces the proximal operators in the ADMM algorithm with off-the-shelf denoisers (e.g., BM3D, NLM) and deep image priors. These denoisers act as implicit regularizers, promoting sparsity in the sparse coding step and low-rank structure in the low-rank step. The deep image prior (DIP) further enhances the low-rank constraint by learning the low-rank structure directly from the noisy image.
- Core assumption: The denoisers and deep image priors used in the PnP framework are effective at promoting the desired sparsity and low-rank structures in the hyperspectral image.
- Evidence anchors: The LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm features the strong learning capability, but is still free of external training data.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The stability analysis provides theoretical guarantees for the convergence of the LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm, ensuring that it will converge to a critical point under mild assumptions.
- Mechanism: The authors prove that the LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm converges to a critical point by showing that a Lyapunov function, which measures the distance to the optimal solution, is non-increasing. The proof relies on the assumptions that the denoiser is θ-averaged and the DIP is L-Lipschitz bounded.
- Core assumption: The denoiser used in the sparse coding step is θ-averaged and the DIP function is L-Lipschitz bounded with L ≤ 1.
- Evidence anchors: We conduct the stability analysis under some mild assumptions which guarantees the algorithm to converge.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm converge for denoisers that do not satisfy the θ-averaged property?
- Basis in paper: The paper states that the θ-averaged property is a weaker assumption compared to the non-expansive denoiser, and that the convergence of PnP methods using such denoisers can still be empirically verified.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides a theoretical convergence proof for the LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm under the assumption that the denoiser satisfies the θ-averaged property.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting empirical experiments with denoisers that do not satisfy the θ-averaged property and comparing the convergence behavior with the theoretical predictions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of the dictionary size and the number of spectral bands affect the performance of the LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that the dictionary Φ was trained with a size of 1296x2000 and the test HSI image consisted of 128 spectral bands.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a systematic study on how the dictionary size and the number of spectral bands affect the performance of the algorithm.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different dictionary sizes and varying the number of spectral bands to assess the impact on the inpainting performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the LRS-PnP-DIP algorithm perform on hyperspectral images with different noise levels and types of corruption?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that Gaussian noise with a fixed σ value of 0.12 was introduced to the cropped HSI images and that the mask M was applied to all spectral bands in the given region.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the performance of the algorithm on a single noise level and a specific type of corruption.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different noise levels and types of corruption to assess the robustness of the algorithm under various conditions.

## Limitations

- The stability analysis relies on strong assumptions about the denoiser being θ-averaged and the DIP being L-Lipschitz bounded, which may not hold for all denoisers
- The dictionary learning step is performed from noisy/incomplete data, which may introduce artifacts that propagate through the algorithm
- Performance evaluation is limited to a single hyperspectral dataset, limiting generalizability claims
