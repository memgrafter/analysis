# Distilling Semantic Concept Embeddings from Contrastively Fine-Tuned Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09785
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09785
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Contrastive fine-tuning substantially improves concept embedding quality for semantic tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of learning high-quality static concept embeddings from pre-trained language models. The key insight is that contextualised word vectors can be fine-tuned to capture semantic properties rather than just contextual information. Two contrastive learning strategies are proposed: an unsupervised approach using neighbourhood structure of word vectors, and a distantly supervised approach using ConceptNet. The methods substantially outperform existing approaches on word classification, clustering, and ontology completion tasks, with the ConceptNet-based strategy achieving the best performance.

## Method Summary
The method involves fine-tuning BERT using contrastive learning to create static concept embeddings that capture semantic properties. Sentences mentioning target concepts are collected from Wikipedia, with concepts masked to obtain mention vectors that encode what sentences reveal about concepts rather than prior knowledge. Two strategies generate positive training pairs: (1) unsupervised neighbourhood structure analysis using compatibility degrees between masked vectors, and (2) distant supervision from ConceptNet property triples. The fine-tuned model then produces improved concept embeddings through aggregation of mention vectors.

## Key Results
- ConFT (ConceptNet-based fine-tuning) achieves F1 scores of 0.836 on word classification and 0.776 on ontology completion
- Both unsupervised and ConceptNet-based strategies outperform existing methods across all benchmarks
- Fine-tuning (ConFT) consistently outperforms the projection method (ConProj) on all tasks
- Filtering idiosyncratic vectors improves performance on clustering and ontology completion tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Fine-tuning with contrastive loss moves contextualised vectors closer when sentences express similar semantic properties. By sampling positive pairs where two sentences reveal the same property about their target concepts, the model learns to map these pairs to nearby vectors through a supervised contrastive loss.

### Mechanism 2
Masking the target concept before contextualisation forces the vector to encode only what the sentence reveals about that concept. By replacing the target word with [MASK], the contextualised representation reflects only the properties expressed in the surrounding sentence, not the model's prior knowledge.

### Mechanism 3
Using distant supervision from ConceptNet provides higher-quality positive pairs than unsupervised neighbourhood matching. ConceptNet provides explicit property-concept triples, allowing sentences containing both the concept and property to be identified as high-confidence positive pairs for contrastive training.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Contrastive learning with supervised loss
  - Why needed here: To learn fine-grained property similarity rather than just sentence similarity
  - Quick check question: What loss function would you use if you wanted embeddings of sentences expressing the same property to be close, but embeddings of sentences with different properties to be far apart?

- Concept: Neighbourhood structure as a proxy for property similarity
  - Why needed here: To generate weakly supervised positive pairs without manual annotation
  - Quick check question: If two masked vectors have neighbours from the same set of concepts, what does that suggest about the properties their sentences express?

- Concept: Masking for decontextualised property extraction
  - Why needed here: To isolate sentence-level information about a concept from prior knowledge encoded in the LM
  - Quick check question: What changes in the contextualised representation of "bank" when you mask it in "river bank" vs "savings bank"?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Wikipedia sentences -> Mask target concepts -> BERT encoder -> Contrastive module (positive/negative mining) -> Fine-tuned LM -> Aggregated concept embeddings -> Filtered vectors

- Critical path:
  1. Sample sentences for each concept
  2. Mask target concept, generate mention vectors
  3. Mine positive pairs (neighbourhood-based or ConceptNet-based)
  4. Fine-tune LM with contrastive loss
  5. Aggregate mention vectors into concept embeddings
  6. Apply filtering to remove idiosyncratic vectors

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Neighbourhood-based vs ConceptNet-based positive mining: unsupervised vs higher precision but domain-dependent
  - Masking vs no masking: isolates sentence context vs retains prior knowledge
  - Linear projection vs full fine-tuning: faster but less expressive vs more computationally expensive
  - Aggregation strategy: simple averaging vs CNN pooling vs filtering

- Failure signatures:
  - Embeddings cluster by syntactic role rather than semantic property
  - Performance plateaus quickly during contrastive training
  - Nearest neighbours in embedding space are topically related but not taxonomically close
  - High anisotropy persists in fine-tuned embeddings

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare masking vs no masking on a small concept classification task
  2. Test neighbourhood-based positive mining vs random pairing on embedding quality
  3. Evaluate linear projection vs full fine-tuning on downstream F1 scores

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does fine-tuning the language model (ConFT) outperform the projection method (ConProj) on tasks beyond word classification and ontology completion? The authors note that ConFT outperforms ConProj but only evaluate on word classification, clustering, and ontology completion tasks.

### Open Question 2
How sensitive are the proposed methods to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of neighbors (k) for the compatibility degree or the threshold (θ) for positive example selection? The authors mention specific hyperparameter values used in their experiments but do not provide a sensitivity analysis.

### Open Question 3
Can the proposed methods be extended to learn concept embeddings for languages other than English? The authors use English Wikipedia and ConceptNet as data sources, suggesting that the methods are currently limited to English.

## Limitations

- Weak supervision quality: Both strategies rely on automatic methods for generating positive training pairs, which may conflate topical similarity with property similarity
- Generalization across domains: Evaluation focuses on general domain concepts, with unclear performance on specialized or multilingual contexts
- Computational cost: Method requires collecting hundreds of sentences per concept and fine-tuning large language models, which may be prohibitive for large-scale vocabularies

## Confidence

- High confidence in the core methodology of using masked vectors to extract sentence-level properties and the general effectiveness of fine-tuning for improving static embeddings
- Medium confidence in the specific mechanisms for generating positive training pairs and the superiority of the ConceptNet-based approach
- Low confidence in the scalability and practical applicability of the approach for large concept vocabularies

## Next Checks

1. Conduct human evaluation study to assess quality of positive pairs generated by both neighbourhood-based and ConceptNet-based strategies, sampling 100 pairs from each method

2. Systematically add noise to positive pair mining by randomly replacing some positive pairs with negative pairs, then measure performance degradation to quantify robustness

3. Evaluate the method on a specialized domain dataset (e.g., medical concepts from UMLS) where ConceptNet coverage is limited to assess cross-domain generalization capabilities
