# Counterfactuals of Counterfactuals: a back-translation-inspired approach to analyse counterfactual editors

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17055
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17055
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: Introduces inconsistency metric to evaluate counterfactual editors via iterative self-feedback

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a back-translation inspired methodology to evaluate counterfactual explanations for NLP models. The authors introduce a new metric called "inconsistency" to measure how well counterfactual editors can generate minimal edits by feeding their outputs back into the system iteratively. The methodology is applied to three different counterfactual editors (MiCE, Polyjuice, and TextFooler) on two datasets (IMDb and Newsgroups). The key findings are: (1) The inconsistency metric effectively captures the editors' ability to produce minimal edits, with TextFooler being the most consistent, followed by MiCE and Polyjuice. (2) The feedback approach reveals patterns in editors' behavior, such as Polyjuice's tendency to significantly reduce text length in early steps and MiCE's higher inconsistency for even-numbered steps. (3) The flip rate and fluency of the editors are also analyzed, showing that MiCE has a perfect flip rate initially but decreases over iterations, while Polyjuice's fluency deteriorates with more feedback steps.

## Method Summary
The methodology employs a back-translation inspired approach where counterfactual editors are evaluated through iterative self-feedback. Starting with original text samples, editors generate counterfactual explanations which are then fed back into the same editor for multiple iterations. At each step, minimality is measured using Levenshtein distance between consecutive outputs, flip rate measures classifier prediction changes, and perplexity scores assess fluency. The inconsistency metric (inc@n) is calculated as the Levenshtein distance between step n and n-1 outputs, normalized by the number of tokens. This process is repeated for 10 feedback steps across three editors (MiCE, Polyjuice, TextFooler) on IMDb and Newsgroups datasets.

## Key Results
- Inconsistency metric effectively captures editors' ability to produce minimal edits: TextFooler (most consistent) < MiCE < Polyjuice (least consistent)
- MiCE shows perfect initial flip rate that decreases over iterations, while Polyjuice's fluency deteriorates with more feedback steps
- Polyjuice significantly reduces text length in early feedback steps, while MiCE exhibits higher inconsistency at even-numbered steps

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Back-translation-style iteration exposes hidden inconsistencies in counterfactual explanations by using the explainer's own output as a ground truth proxy
- Mechanism: Each feedback step applies the explainer to its previous output, then compares the Levenshtein distance between consecutive steps. An increase signals suboptimal edits because a minimal edit should not be further from the previous edit than the previous was from the original
- Core assumption: Minimal edits are reversible and symmetric; hence if step A→B is minimal, then B→A should also be minimal
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we can expand on the idea of evaluation by comparison, and compare a counterfactual system's performance against itself."
  - [section]: "Given a ground truth explanation, it is possible to calculate the optimal minimality to be obtained, but without it, it is only possible to compare minimality values across different edits/editors."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no neighbor papers directly address this iterative self-evaluation method
- Break condition: If edits are not reversible (e.g., lossy transformations), the proxy assumption fails

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Flip rate decay over feedback steps reveals distribution mismatches between training and edited samples
- Mechanism: Editors start with high flip rates on the test set, but as edits accumulate, the text drifts from the training distribution, reducing classifier agreement. Monitoring flip rate@n shows this degradation
- Core assumption: The classifier's decision boundary is sensitive to distribution drift; edited samples eventually fall into regions where the classifier is less certain or wrong
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "MiCE has a perfect flip rate initially but decreases over iterations."
  - [section]: "We can measure the flip rate after n loops of feedback to get more detailed information about the ability of the editor to consistently change the predicted class after several perturbations of the sentence."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - neighbor papers focus on robustness and counterfactual explanations but do not discuss flip rate decay in iterative feedback
- Break condition: If the classifier is trained to be invariant to the types of edits made, flip rate will remain stable

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Language model perplexity evolution indicates fluency and out-of-distribution shifts
- Mechanism: Two perplexity measures are computed: one on a general-domain LM (base-ppl) and one on a domain-fine-tuned LM (fine-ppl). Rising base-ppl suggests reduced fluency; divergent trends between base and fine indicate distributional shift
- Core assumption: Perplexity is a reliable proxy for fluency; domain-specific fine-tuning captures the test distribution
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The token-level perplexity of a large language model is a frequently used proxy to the fluency estimation."
  - [section]: "We employ and compare both PPL over MD (PPLD) and PPL over ML (PPLL) in our experiments."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no neighbor papers directly test this dual-ppl approach for counterfactual analysis
- Break condition: If the language model is not sensitive to the type of perturbations (e.g., synonym replacements), perplexity changes may be muted

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Levenshtein distance
  - Why needed here: It quantifies edit cost between original and counterfactual text, forming the basis of minimality and inconsistency metrics
  - Quick check question: If original text is "cat" and edited text is "bat", what is the Levenshtein distance? (Answer: 1)

- Concept: Perplexity as fluency proxy
  - Why needed here: It measures how surprising a language model finds the text; higher perplexity signals less fluent or out-of-distribution language
  - Quick check question: If a LM assigns probability 0.1 to each of 3 tokens in a sentence, what is the sentence's perplexity? (Answer: 10)

- Concept: Flip rate calculation
  - Why needed here: It indicates whether an editor successfully changes classifier predictions, a core goal of counterfactual generation
  - Quick check question: In a test set of 100 samples, if 85 have changed labels after editing, what is the flip rate? (Answer: 0.85)

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Predictor model (classifier) -> Counterfactual editor (MiCE/Polyjuice/TextFooler) -> Language models (base GPT-2, fine-tuned GPT-2)

- Critical path:
  1. Load dataset and predictor
  2. For each sample, apply editor to generate counterfactual
  3. Measure minimality, flip rate, perplexity
  4. Feed counterfactual back into editor for n steps
  5. Aggregate and compare metrics across steps

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a single predictor (MiCE's) ensures consistency but may bias results toward its vulnerabilities
  - Fixed step count (10) simplifies comparison but may miss late-stage behavior; early stopping criteria could save compute
  - Selecting lowest-minimality successful edit per step reduces noise but ignores multiple viable counterfactuals

- Failure signatures:
  - Inconsistent inc@n with no clear trend may indicate random edit patterns or adversarial noise
  - Flip rate increasing with steps could signal the editor is moving samples toward a different, more stable class region
  - Perplexity spikes on fine-ppl but not base-ppl suggest distributional drift rather than fluency loss

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run a single sample through all editors for 3 feedback steps, log minimality and perplexity at each step to verify the pipeline
  2. Compare inc@n@1 values across editors on a small subset to confirm expected ordering (TextFooler < MiCE < Polyjuice)
  3. Plot flip rate@1 vs flip rate@9 for all editors to observe decay patterns and confirm the distribution drift hypothesis

## Limitations
- The inconsistency metric relies on Levenshtein distance as a proxy for minimality, which may not capture semantic equivalence
- The evaluation depends heavily on a single predictor model (MiCE's classifier), potentially biasing results toward that model's specific vulnerabilities
- The 10-step feedback limit is arbitrary and may miss long-term behavioral patterns or convergence behaviors of different editors

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims:**
- The inconsistency metric effectively distinguishes between editors in terms of their ability to produce minimal edits (TextFooler > MiCE > Polyjuice)
- Flip rate decay patterns are consistently observed across editors, confirming distribution drift effects
- The general methodology of using self-feedback to expose editor behavior is sound and reproducible

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- The specific numerical values of inconsistency metrics are comparable across datasets
- The observed differences in step-wise behavior (e.g., Polyjuice's length reduction) are editor-specific rather than artifacts
- Perplexity trends reliably indicate fluency changes across feedback steps

**Low Confidence Claims:**
- The exact ranking of editors' performance in real-world applications
- Whether 10 feedback steps represents sufficient exploration of editor behavior
- The generalizability of findings to other datasets beyond IMDb and Newsgroups

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-predictor validation**: Repeat the evaluation using 3-5 different classifier architectures (BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT) to verify that inconsistency patterns are editor-dependent rather than predictor-specific.

2. **Human evaluation correlation**: Conduct a small-scale human study (20-30 participants) comparing automated inconsistency scores against human judgments of edit quality and minimality to validate the Levenshtein-based metric.

3. **Extended feedback analysis**: Run the feedback loop for 25-50 steps on a smaller sample set to identify whether editors reach stable states, show periodic behavior, or exhibit divergent patterns beyond the initial 10 steps.
