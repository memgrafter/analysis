# Phone and speaker spatial organization in self-supervised speech representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.14055
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14055
- Reference count: 0
- One-line primary result: The authors analyze spatial organization of phone and speaker information in self-supervised speech representations using two model-free methods: representation similarity analysis (CKA) with acoustic features and multivariate Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests to measure clustering.

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes how phone and speaker information is spatially organized in self-supervised speech representations using model-free methods. The authors use representation similarity analysis (CKA) to compare neural representations with acoustic features, and multivariate Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests to measure clustering by phone and speaker classes. They find that representations encode these two types of information differently depending on the pretraining task, with phone information typically peaking in middle layers while speaker information varies by model. Models using similar pretraining objectives show similar patterns, and there is an inverse relationship between speaker and phone clustering within the same representation. The analysis is validated by correlating clustering metrics with downstream task performance.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze spatial organization in self-supervised speech representations using two model-free methods. First, they compute linear CKA similarity between neural representations and acoustic features (F0, F1, F2, spectral centroid) to identify which layers preserve phone information. Second, they use multivariate Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests to measure how well representations cluster by phone and speaker classes across layers, quantifying this with AvgU statistics. They analyze five self-supervised models (Mockingjay, DeCoAR2, HuBERT, WavLM, wav2vec 2.0, data2vec) on L2Arctic and TIMIT datasets, computing these metrics across all layers for both speaker and phone clustering tasks. The validity of their analysis is supported by correlating clustering metrics with downstream task performance on the SUPERB benchmark.

## Key Results
- Different pretraining objectives create distinct spatial organization patterns for phone and speaker information in the same representation space
- Phone information peaks in middle layers (layers 2-4) across most models, while speaker information distribution varies by model
- There is an inverse relationship between speaker and phone clustering within the same representation - representations that cluster speakers well tend to disperse phone information, and vice versa
- Models with similar pretraining objectives (e.g., HuBERT and WavLM using quantized MFCCs) show similar spatial organization patterns
- Correlation between clustering metrics and downstream task performance supports the validity of the analysis (0.84 correlation with phone recognition, 0.75 with speaker identification)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The model-free analysis using CKA and WMW tests can detect meaningful spatial organization patterns in speech representations without relying on downstream models. By comparing representation similarity (CKA) between neural layers and acoustic features, and measuring clustering via multivariate WMW statistics, the paper identifies where phone vs speaker information is optimally represented in different model architectures. Core assumption: Spatial organization patterns detected by these model-free methods correlate with downstream task performance. Evidence: Correlation of 0.84 (p = 0.018) with phone recognition performance and 0.75 (p = 0.05) with speaker identification performance. Break condition: If downstream performance does not correlate with CKA/WMW metrics, the spatial organization findings may not translate to practical utility.

### Mechanism 2
Different self-supervised pretraining objectives create distinct spatial organizations for phone and speaker information within the same representation space. Models with similar pretraining targets (e.g., quantized MFCCs vs internal targets) show similar patterns in how they organize phone vs speaker information across layers, as measured by both CKA similarity and WMW clustering. Core assumption: The pretraining objective directly shapes the learned representation geometry, independent of specific architecture details. Evidence: HuBERT and WavLM behave similarly due to both using quantized MFCCs as targets. Break condition: If models with different objectives but similar architectures show identical spatial patterns, the pretraining objective may not be the primary driver.

### Mechanism 3
There exists a trade-off relationship where representations that cluster speakers well tend to disperse phone information, and vice versa. The spatial organization creates clusters for one class (speaker or phone) at the expense of the other, measured by the inverse relationship between AvgU values for each class within the same representation. Core assumption: A single representation cannot simultaneously optimize clustering for both speaker and phone classes due to the conflicting nature of these tasks. Evidence: Clear trade-off observed between speaker and phone AvgU values for the same representation. Break condition: If a representation can achieve high AvgU values for both speakers and phones simultaneously, the inverse relationship assumption may be incorrect.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Representation Similarity Analysis (RSA) and Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)
  - Why needed here: CKA measures geometric similarity between different representations, allowing comparison of neural representations with acoustic features to understand what information is preserved.
  - Quick check question: What does a CKA value of 1 versus 0 indicate about two representations?

- Concept: Multivariate statistical testing for clustering analysis
  - Why needed here: The multivariate Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test measures how well points from the same class cluster together in representation space without requiring labels or downstream models.
  - Quick check question: How does the multivariate WMW statistic differ from its univariate counterpart?

- Concept: Self-supervised learning objectives and their impact on learned representations
  - Why needed here: Understanding how different pretraining tasks (masked prediction, contrastive loss, etc.) shape the spatial organization of information in neural representations.
  - Quick check question: Why might models predicting quantized MFCCs show different spatial organization than models predicting internal representations?

- Failure signatures: Low or inconsistent CKA values across layers may indicate poor correspondence between neural representations and acoustic features.   3. Compare CKA and WMW results between two models with different pretraining objectives (e.g., HuBERT vs data2vec) to observe objective-dependent patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: Does the spatial organization of speaker and phone information vary systematically across languages, or is it consistent across different linguistic structures?
  - Basis in paper: The paper uses L2Arctic (non-native English) and TIMIT (native English) datasets and notes that "the differences between L2Arctic and TIMIT... were due to inaccuracies in the features rather than a degradation in the representations for non-native speakers."
  - Why unresolved: The analysis only compares English datasets. The paper does not explore cross-linguistic variation in how speaker and phone information are organized in self-supervised representations.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing spatial organization patterns using datasets from languages with different phonological systems (e.g., tonal languages, languages with vowel harmony, languages with contrastive length) while maintaining consistent analytical methods.

- Question: What is the causal relationship between pretraining objectives and the observed peaks in phone clustering (e.g., layers 2-4 for HuBERT and WavLM) - are these peaks a byproduct of architectural choices or fundamental to the learning task?
  - Basis in paper: "For F1 and F2, we can see that the similarity value is larger... HuBERT, WavLM, wav2vec2.0, and DeCoAR2 have peaks of similarity between the layers 2 to 4. These layers appear to preserve the phone information best."
  - Why unresolved: The paper observes correlation between pretraining objectives and spatial organization but does not establish causation or determine whether these patterns are architectural artifacts versus task-dependent.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies varying pretraining objectives while holding architecture constant, or training identical architectures with different objectives to isolate task effects from architectural ones.

- Question: How do the spatial organization patterns change when evaluating representations in cross-modal or multilingual downstream tasks compared to the same-language, same-modality tasks used in SUPERB?
  - Basis in paper: The paper correlates their AvgU metrics with SUPERB benchmark performance, noting "correlation of 0.84 (p = 0.018) with the performance on the phone recognition task" but SUPERB focuses on same-language speech tasks.
  - Why unresolved: The analysis only considers downstream performance on speech-only tasks within the same language, leaving unexplored how these spatial organization patterns might generalize to multilingual or cross-modal applications.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Testing representations on multilingual speech recognition, speech-to-text translation, or speech-to-image retrieval tasks while measuring how spatial organization patterns predict cross-modal/cross-linguistic performance.

## Limitations

- The analysis relies on model-free methods (CKA and WMW tests) whose correlation with downstream performance, while statistically significant, may not fully capture the practical utility of spatial organization patterns.
- The acoustic features used (F0, F1, F2, spectral centroid) represent a limited subset of phonetic information, potentially missing important aspects of phone representations.
- The inverse relationship between speaker and phone clustering, while observed, may depend on the specific datasets and models analyzed.

## Confidence

- High confidence: The observation that different pretraining objectives create distinct spatial organization patterns is well-supported by both CKA similarity results and downstream performance correlations.
- Medium confidence: The inverse relationship between speaker and phone clustering within the same representation requires further validation across more diverse models and datasets.
- Medium confidence: The correlation between model-free metrics and downstream performance supports the analysis validity, though the strength of this relationship may vary with different evaluation protocols.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-dataset validation**: Repeat the CKA and WMW analysis on additional speech datasets (e.g., LibriSpeech, Common Voice) to verify that observed spatial organization patterns generalize beyond L2Arctic and TIMIT.

2. **Feature ablation study**: Systematically remove each acoustic feature (F0, F1, F2, spectral centroid) from the CKA analysis to determine which features contribute most to observed peaks in middle layers, and whether additional phonetic features would strengthen the analysis.

3. **Temporal stability analysis**: Track how CKA similarity and WMW clustering values evolve during pretraining for select models to determine whether spatial organization patterns are emergent properties that develop over training time.
