# Sociocultural knowledge is needed for selection of shots in hate speech detection tasks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.01890
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01890
- Reference count: 17
- This paper introduces HATELEXICON, a lexicon of slurs and targets of hate speech for Brazil, Germany, India, and Kenya, to aid training and interpretability of models.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces HATELEXICON, a lexicon of slurs and targets of hate speech for Brazil, Germany, India, and Kenya, to aid training and interpretability of models. The authors show how their lexicon can be used to interpret model predictions, demonstrating that models developed to classify extreme speech rely heavily on target words when making predictions. Further, they propose a method to aid shot selection for training in low-resource settings via HATELEXICON. In few-shot learning, the selection of shots is of paramount importance to model performance. The authors simulate a few-shot setting for German and Hindi, using HASOC data for training and the Multilingual HateCheck (MHC) as a benchmark. They show that selecting shots based on their lexicon leads to models performing better on MHC than models trained on shots sampled randomly.

## Method Summary
The authors introduce HATELEXICON, a lexicon of slurs and target group terms for Brazil, Germany, India, and Kenya. They use this lexicon to select training examples for few-shot hate speech detection models trained on HASOC German and Hindi datasets using SetFit (a prompt-free approach) with SentenceBERT embeddings. They evaluate performance on the Multilingual HateCheck benchmark and analyze model interpretability by examining reliance on slurs and target group terms.

## Key Results
- Lexicon-based shot selection improves few-shot hate speech detection performance compared to random sampling
- Models trained with lexicon-selected shots show better performance on the Multilingual HateCheck benchmark
- Analysis reveals that hate speech detection models heavily rely on target group names when making predictions, potentially introducing bias

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Lexicon-based shot selection improves few-shot hate speech detection by increasing the density of informative and unambiguous training examples.
- Mechanism: The HATELEXICON contains culturally specific slurs and target group terms that are strong indicators of hate speech. By sampling training shots that include these terms, the training data is enriched with examples that are more likely to be clear-cut cases of hate speech, reducing noise and ambiguity.
- Core assumption: Examples containing slurs and target group terms are more informative and less ambiguous than randomly selected examples, leading to better model learning in few-shot settings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "using our lexicon to select shots containing more sociocultural information leads to better few-shot performance."
  - [section]: "We hypothesize that this is due an increased quality of training data. In few-shot settings, the importance of each training example is magnified and thus noise can potentially affect performance disproportionately to larger-resource settings."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; no direct corpus study comparing example ambiguity or informativeness between lexicon-based and random sampling.
- Break condition: If the lexicon contains terms that are context-dependent or used in non-hateful ways, or if the random sampling accidentally captures highly informative examples, the performance advantage could diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Incorporating sociocultural knowledge into model development helps mitigate bias against target groups.
- Mechanism: Models trained on hate speech data may over-associate target group names with hate speech due to their frequent co-occurrence in training data. By using HATELEXICON to analyze and interpret model predictions, researchers can identify and address this bias.
- Core assumption: Target group names are not inherently hateful but appear frequently in hate speech data, leading to potential bias in model predictions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "The over-reliance on target group names may lead to further marginalization of targets of hate speech, with models flagging as hateful innocuous text mentioning these terms. With HATELEXICON, this erroneous behavior is unveiled."
  - [section]: "In all settings, target group names (such as religious groups) and slurs contribute prominently to model predictions."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence showing the co-occurrence frequency of target group names in hate speech vs. non-hate speech contexts.
- Break condition: If target group names are rarely used in non-hateful contexts in the examined languages, or if the lexicon does not accurately capture the nuances of term usage, the bias mitigation may be ineffective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: HATELEXICON enables the creation of functional tests to evaluate hate speech detection models across different languages and cultural contexts.
- Mechanism: By providing a standardized set of slurs and target group terms for different countries, HATELEXICON allows researchers to test whether models can correctly identify hate speech that uses these specific terms, ensuring the model's effectiveness across diverse sociocultural settings.
- Core assumption: The presence of specific slurs and target group terms is a reliable indicator of hate speech across different cultural contexts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We introduce HateLexicon, a lexicon of slurs and targets of hate speech for the countries of Brazil, Germany, India and Kenya, to aid training and interpretability of models."
  - [section]: "We propose the use of HateLexicon as a tool to interpret model predictions."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence showing the effectiveness of HATELEXICON terms as functional tests for hate speech detection models.
- Break condition: If the lexicon terms are not representative of the actual hate speech used in online contexts, or if the terms have different connotations in different cultural contexts, the functional tests may not accurately evaluate model performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: HATELEXICON -> Shot selection module -> Few-shot learning model -> Interpretability module -> Evaluation module
- Critical path:
  1. Curate HATELEXICON with annotators familiar with each country's sociocultural context.
  2. Select training shots using HATELEXICON terms.
  3. Train a few-shot hate speech detection model on the selected shots.
  4. Evaluate model performance on a benchmark dataset.
  5. Use HATELEXICON to interpret model predictions and identify potential biases.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Exhaustiveness vs. manageability of HATELEXICON: A more comprehensive lexicon may capture more hate speech but could also include more ambiguous terms.
  - Shot selection strategy: Prioritizing examples with HATELEXICON terms may improve model performance but could also introduce bias if the terms are not representative of all hate speech.
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor model performance on the benchmark dataset despite using HATELEXICON for shot selection.
  - Model predictions heavily relying on target group names, indicating potential bias.
  - HATELEXICON terms not appearing frequently in the training or evaluation data.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare model performance using randomly selected shots vs. shots selected with HATELEXICON for a single language (e.g., German).
  2. Analyze model predictions on examples containing HATELEXICON terms to identify potential biases.
  3. Test model performance on a dataset that includes hate speech targeting groups not covered by HATELEXICON to assess the lexicon's comprehensiveness.

- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on a specific model architecture and does not explore how other architectures might interact with HATELEXICON.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different model architectures (e.g., mBERT, other transformer-based models) using HATELEXICON in few-shot learning settings.

- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the potential of HATELEXICON for interpretability but does not fully explore its impact on mitigating bias.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting in-depth interpretability analyses using HATELEXICON to identify and mitigate bias in hate speech detection models, with a focus on specific target groups.

## Limitations
- The lexicon construction process lacks details about inter-annotator agreement and validation against actual hate speech data
- The interpretability claims are based on observational analysis rather than controlled experiments
- Results are limited to a specific number of training examples and model architecture, limiting generalizability

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The technical methodology for implementing few-shot learning with SetFit and the basic evaluation framework using MHC are well-established and correctly implemented. The paper clearly demonstrates that lexicon-based shot selection can improve model performance in the specific experimental setup described.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that HATELEXICON enables better interpretability of model predictions is supported by the analysis presented, but the evidence could be strengthened with more rigorous testing. The connection between lexicon-based shot selection and improved performance is plausible given the mechanism described, but alternative explanations have not been fully explored.

**Low Confidence**: The assertion that models "heavily rely" on target group names when making predictions is based on observational analysis rather than controlled experiments. The claim that HATELEXICON terms are the most informative training examples for few-shot learning assumes that these terms capture the most critical aspects of hate speech, but this has not been empirically validated against other potential indicators.

## Next Checks

1. **Lexicon Coverage Validation**: Conduct a frequency analysis comparing HATELEXICON terms against actual hate speech corpora for each country to verify that the selected terms represent the most common and impactful hate speech indicators. This would involve collecting additional hate speech data and measuring term distribution and co-occurrence patterns.

2. **Controlled Interpretability Experiment**: Design an experiment where target group names are systematically replaced with neutral synonyms in both hate speech and non-hate speech examples, then measure how this affects model predictions. This would help determine whether the observed reliance on target group names represents true bias or legitimate signal.

3. **Shot Selection Strategy Comparison**: Implement and compare multiple shot selection strategies including TF-IDF-based sampling, clustering-based diversity sampling, and human-expert selection against the HATELEXICON approach. This would help determine whether the lexicon method provides unique benefits or if similar performance could be achieved through other approaches.
