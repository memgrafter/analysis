# Three ways to improve feature alignment for open vocabulary detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.13518
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13518
- Authors:

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of zero-shot open vocabulary object detection, where the goal is to detect objects based on textual queries, including classes not seen during training. The authors propose three methods to improve vision-text feature alignment, which is crucial for zero-shot performance. First, they introduce efficient text augmentation to prevent overfitting to a small number of training classes. Second, they modify the detector architecture with trainable gated shortcuts to preserve and promote alignment. Finally, they employ a self-training approach using pseudo-labels from image-text data to further improve detection on unseen classes. Their final method achieves state-of-the-art performance on the LVIS-R benchmark, setting a new record for mAP-all and demonstrating competitive results for mAP-rare, as well as superior transfer to COCO and Objects365 datasets.

## Method Summary
The authors propose three improvements to vision-text feature alignment for open vocabulary detection. First, they use efficient text augmentation by generating 64 variants of class embeddings using dropout templates, preventing overfitting to a small number of training classes. Second, they modify the detector architecture with trainable gated shortcuts in both the feature pyramid network and detection head, preserving and promoting alignment between vision and text features. Third, they employ a self-training approach using pseudo-labels from image-text data to improve detection performance on unseen classes. Their final method combines all three improvements, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the LVIS-R benchmark.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art performance on LVIS-R benchmark with mAP-all of 21.3 and mAP-rare of 10.8
- Demonstrates superior transfer learning to COCO and Objects365 datasets
- Sets a new record for mAP-all on LVIS-R, outperforming previous methods by a significant margin

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Training the language model with a small learning rate improves mAP-all but underperforms on unseen classes due to catastrophic forgetting.
- Mechanism: Small learning rate preserves knowledge about unseen classes during training on a small number of seen classes, but this slow adaptation limits performance on seen classes.
- Core assumption: Language models contain comprehensive textual representations during pretraining that should be preserved.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Many works [24, 42, 46] choose to freeze the pre-trained language model, while others, observe this yielding bad performance [26, 31, 43] and choose to train it, but with a small learning rate to prevent 'catastrophic forgetting'."
  - [section] "Training the LM with the same learning rate as the vision model is unstable and results in poor performance. Using the OWL [31] strategy of training the LM with a very small learning rate yields good performance on all classes. However, when compared with our augmentation approaches, it becomes clear that it is underperforming on unseen classes, as even this small learning rate causes some forgetting, albeit arguably not 'catastrophic'."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found; this mechanism is primarily supported by the paper's own experiments.
- Break condition: If the language model does not contain comprehensive textual representations during pretraining, or if the number of training classes is large enough that catastrophic forgetting is not a concern.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adding trainable gated shortcuts preserves and promotes vision-text feature alignment during detection training.
- Mechanism: At initialization, the gated shortcuts forward final backbone features directly to the detection head, maintaining alignment with text embeddings. Throughout training, the gates allow adaptation while preserving the alignment signal.
- Core assumption: The pretrained vision and text backbones have aligned representations that detection training can leverage.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Secondly, the feature pyramid network and the detection head are modified to include trainable gated shortcuts, which encourages vision-text feature alignment and guarantees it at the start of detection training."
  - [section] "We take most inspiration from the trainable gating of Flamingo [1] where the vision-language model is initialized such that the visual branch is ignored, thus preserving the language model pretraining. FIBER [10] uses the Flamingo-style gating to initialize a joint vision-text encoder with pretrained dual encoders. We instead aim to preserve alignment between visual and language features obtained during pretraining but broken due to the injected detection-specific modules."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found; this mechanism is primarily supported by the paper's own experiments.
- Break condition: If the pretrained vision and text backbones do not have aligned representations, or if the detection training requires significant architectural changes that cannot be accommodated by gated shortcuts.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Self-training with pseudo-labels and batch-negatives improves detection performance on unseen classes.
- Mechanism: A strong open-vocabulary detector is used to pseudo-label additional image-text data, and the resulting pseudo-labels are used to train a new detector. Batch-negatives are crucial for this approach to work well.
- Core assumption: Large amounts of image-text data without bounding box annotations are available and can be effectively pseudo-labeled.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Finally, a self-training approach is used to leverage a larger corpus of image-text pairs thus improving detection performance on classes with no human annotated bounding boxes."
  - [section] "We propose a simple three-stage approach. First, a good open vocabulary detector is trained using the previous two improvements (Sections 3.1 and 3.2), called 2Ways. The detector is then used to pseudo-label an additional dataset that contains only images-text pairs scraped from the internet... Finally, a new, stronger, open vocabulary detector (3Ways) is trained by combining the strongly supervised data (LVIS-R) and the pseudo-labelled dataset, and treating the pseudo-labels as ground truth."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found; this mechanism is primarily supported by the paper's own experiments.
- Break condition: If large amounts of image-text data without bounding box annotations are not available, or if pseudo-labeling is not effective for the target domain.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Image → Vision backbone → FPN → Detector head → Classification features → Text embedding → Final detection scores
- Critical path: Image → Vision backbone → FPN → Detector head → Classification features → Text embedding → Final detection scores
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a frozen language model vs. training it with a small learning rate vs. using text augmentation
  - Modifying the FPN and detector head architecture vs. keeping them standard
  - Using self-training with pseudo-labels vs. relying solely on the original training data
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on unseen classes: likely due to insufficient feature alignment or catastrophic forgetting of the language model
  - Slow training or memory issues: likely due to inefficient text augmentation or large model size
  - Unstable training: likely due to improper initialization of the gated shortcuts or text embedding generation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal number of text embedding variants to use for preventing overfitting without degrading performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper experiments with 1, 8, 64, and 80 text embedding variants, finding that 64 variants performs best while 80 variants performs similarly but with higher memory usage.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests a limited number of variant counts. The optimal number may depend on factors like dataset size, model architecture, and training schedule that were not explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study varying the number of text embedding variants across a wider range (e.g. 16, 32, 128) while keeping other factors constant would reveal the optimal count for different scenarios.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the alignment preserving architecture perform when applied to two-stage detector architectures like Faster R-CNN?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only tests the alignment preserving architecture on single-stage detectors (FCOS and T-Head). Two-stage detectors have a different architecture with region proposal networks that could benefit from alignment preservation.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on single-stage detectors for simplicity and fair comparison with existing work. The benefits of alignment preservation may differ for two-stage architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing the alignment preserving architecture on a two-stage detector like Faster R-CNN and comparing performance to the standard architecture would show if the benefits transfer.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the self-training approach be extended to use more diverse and larger-scale image-text datasets beyond CC12M?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses CC12M for self-training, which contains 12M images. The authors note that using higher resolution images allows fitting more images in the batch and using more batch negatives.
- Why unresolved: CC12M is a relatively small dataset compared to the total available image-text data on the internet. Using more data could further improve performance, but may require more sophisticated filtering and quality control.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training the 3Ways detector using self-training with a much larger and more diverse image-text dataset (e.g. LAION-400M) and comparing performance to the CC12M version would quantify the benefits of scaling up.

## Limitations
- The reliance on large-scale image-text data (CC12M) for self-training may limit applicability in domains where such data is scarce.
- The exact implementation details of the trainable gated shortcuts and specific hyperparameters for the self-training stage could affect reproducibility.
- The benefits of the proposed improvements may be limited to open vocabulary detection tasks and may not generalize to other vision-language tasks.
