# Where's the Point? Self-Supervised Multilingual Punctuation-Agnostic Sentence Segmentation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18893
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18893
- Reference count: 36
- Key outcome: WtP achieves state-of-the-art sentence segmentation performance without punctuation or training data, improving downstream MT BLEU scores by 2.3 points across 14 languages.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces WtP (Where's the Point?), a fully self-supervised sentence segmentation method that operates without punctuation or training data. The key innovation is training a character-level language model to predict whether a character was followed by a newline in the original text, treating newlines as implicit paragraph boundaries. WtP outperforms previous sentence segmentation tools by an average of 6.1% F1 points and can be adapted to specific corpora with as few as 64-256 examples. The method demonstrates significant improvements in downstream machine translation quality, with BLEU scores increasing by 2.3 points on average across 14 languages.

## Method Summary
WtP leverages newlines as implicit paragraph boundaries to train a character-level language model for sentence segmentation. The model learns to predict whether each character was followed by a newline in the original text, effectively learning sentence boundary patterns without explicit punctuation or training data. The approach works by removing newlines from text, training the model to reconstruct them, and using the model's predictions to identify sentence boundaries. Additionally, the authors propose a data-efficient adaptation method that fine-tunes the model on 64-256 examples from a target corpus to improve domain-specific performance.

## Key Results
- WtP outperforms prior sentence segmentation tools by 6.1% F1 points on average
- Achieves state-of-the-art performance across 95 languages
- Improves downstream MT BLEU scores by 2.3 points on average across 14 languages
- Adaptation to specific corpora requires only 64-256 examples

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by exploiting the implicit structure provided by newline characters as paragraph boundaries. By training a character-level language model to predict these boundaries, the system learns to identify natural sentence break points without requiring explicit punctuation. The self-supervised nature eliminates the need for labeled training data, while the character-level approach captures fine-grained linguistic patterns that indicate sentence boundaries.

## Foundational Learning
- Character-level language modeling: Predicts character sequences to capture fine-grained linguistic patterns
  - Why needed: Captures subtle boundary cues invisible to word-level models
  - Quick check: Verify model can predict adjacent characters accurately

- Self-supervised learning: Uses input data itself as supervision signal
  - Why needed: Eliminates dependency on labeled training data
  - Quick check: Confirm model can reconstruct removed newlines

- Implicit boundary detection: Treats newlines as proxies for sentence boundaries
  - Why needed: Leverages existing document structure without requiring punctuation
  - Quick check: Test model on texts with varying newline patterns

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Text -> Newline removal -> Character LM training -> Boundary prediction -> Sentence segmentation

Critical path: Character LM training -> Boundary prediction

Design tradeoffs: Character-level vs word-level modeling, self-supervision vs labeled data, general vs domain-specific adaptation

Failure signatures: Poor performance on texts lacking paragraph structure, reduced accuracy with irregular newline patterns, adaptation sensitivity to small training sets

First experiments:
1. Train character LM on simple text with regular newline patterns
2. Test boundary prediction accuracy on held-out validation set
3. Evaluate adaptation performance with 64 vs 256 training examples

## Limitations
- Reliance on newline characters assumes document structure exists
- May struggle with texts lacking standard paragraph formatting
- Adaptation method requires validation on diverse domain shifts
- Performance may degrade on highly informal text without clear boundaries
