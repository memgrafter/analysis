# Human adaptation to adaptive machines converges to game-theoretic equilibria

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.01124
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01124
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: This study empirically investigates how adaptive machines influence human decision-making by treating the human-machine interaction as a game. The authors designed three experiments using gradient descent and policy optimization algorithms in a two-player quadratic game, measuring whether human actions converged to theoretical game-theoretic equilibria (Nash, Stackelberg, and consistent conjectural variations). The experiments showed that as the machine's adaptation rate or policy strategy changed, human actions shifted predictably toward different equilibria, validating game-theoretic predictions. Notably, one algorithm allowed the machine to steer the interaction to its optimal outcome, effectively controlling human actions while humans remained rational responders. The results demonstrate that game theory can predict and design outcomes in co-adaptive human-machine interactions without requiring inverse optimization to estimate human preferences.

## Executive Summary
This paper empirically demonstrates that human actions in adaptive human-machine interactions converge to theoretical game-theoretic equilibria. Through three experiments using gradient descent and policy optimization algorithms in a two-player quadratic game, the authors show that as machine adaptation parameters change, human behavior shifts predictably toward different equilibria (Nash, Stackelberg, and consistent conjectural variations). The study validates game-theoretic predictions about co-adaptive behavior and demonstrates that machines can steer human actions toward desired outcomes through strategic adaptation.

## Method Summary
The study employed three experiments where human subjects interacted with adaptive machines in a two-player quadratic game. In Experiment 1, the machine adapted its action via gradient descent with varying rates. Experiment 2 used conjectural variations where the machine estimated human policy slopes through controlled perturbations. Experiment 3 employed policy gradient descent to steer human actions toward the machine's optimal outcome. Each experiment used 20 subjects who manually adjusted their actions to minimize displayed costs while the machine adapted according to the specified algorithm. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided t-tests (P ≤ 0.05) comparing observed human actions to theoretical equilibria.

## Key Results
- Human actions shifted from Nash equilibrium to Stackelberg equilibrium as machine adaptation rate increased
- Machine policy perturbations enabled accurate estimation of human policy slopes through conjectural variations
- Policy gradient descent allowed the machine to steer human actions to its global optimum (reverse Stackelberg equilibrium)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Gradient descent adaptation rate in machine actions shifts human behavior from Nash to Stackelberg equilibrium.
- Mechanism: The machine adjusts its action using gradient descent with varying adaptation rates α. As α increases, the machine responds faster to human actions, effectively imposing a constraint on the human's optimization problem. At low α, the machine moves slowly, leading to Nash equilibrium. At high α, the machine moves quickly, approaching the human's action, leading to Stackelberg equilibrium.
- Core assumption: Human acts as rational optimizer of their cost function given machine's action.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our algorithms enable the machine to select the outcome of the co-adaptive interaction from a constellation of game-theoretic equilibria in action and policy spaces."
  - [section]: "In our first experiment (Figure 1), the machine adapted its action within trials using what is arguably the simplest optimization scheme: gradient descent... We found that distributions of median action vectors for the population of n = 20 human subjects in this experiment shifted from the Nash equilibrium (NE) at the slowest adaptation rate to the human-led Stackelberg equilibrium (SE) at the fastest adaptation rate."
  - [corpus]: Weak. No direct matches found in related papers for this specific gradient-descent-based behavioral steering mechanism.
- Break condition: If human behavior deviates from rational optimization of their cost function, or if the cost landscape changes significantly.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Machine policy perturbations enable estimation of human policy slope through conjectural variations.
- Mechanism: The machine plays two trials with slightly different constant terms in its affine policy. By observing the human's responses to these perturbations, the machine estimates the slope of the human's policy using finite differences. This estimated slope becomes the machine's conjecture about how the human will respond to policy changes.
- Core assumption: Human policy is approximately linear over small perturbations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Importantly, the machine learning algorithms work directly from observations of human actions without solving an inverse problem to estimate the human's utility function as in prior work."
  - [section]: "In our second experiment... the machine used the median action vectors from the pair to estimate a conjecture... and the machine's policy was updated to be optimal with respect to this conjecture."
  - [corpus]: Weak. While related papers discuss game-theoretic equilibria, none specifically address this empirical method of estimating human policy slopes through controlled perturbations.
- Break condition: If human policy is highly non-linear, or if human responses are inconsistent or noisy beyond the estimation method's tolerance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Policy gradient descent in machine policy space can steer human actions to machine's global optimum.
- Mechanism: The machine plays two trials with slightly different linear terms in its affine policy. By observing the resulting cost changes, the machine estimates the gradient of its cost with respect to its policy slope parameter. The machine then updates its policy slope in the direction that decreases its cost, effectively steering the human toward the machine's optimal outcome.
- Core assumption: Machine can accurately estimate policy gradients from finite differences, and human responds rationally to machine's policy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "one algorithm can steer the human-machine interaction to the machine's optimum, effectively controlling the human's actions even while the human responds optimally to their perceived cost landscape."
  - [section]: "In our third experiment... Iterating this process shifted the distribution of median action vectors for a population of human subjects... from the human-led Stackelberg equilibrium (SE) toward the machine's global optimum... which can also be regarded as a reverse Stackelberg equilibrium (RSE)."
  - [corpus]: Weak. Related papers discuss policy gradients and game theory but not this specific application of steering human behavior through policy space optimization.
- Break condition: If policy gradient estimates are inaccurate, or if human rational response breaks down under machine's steering.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Human: Manual input device (mouse/touchscreen) -> Cost function display -> Rational optimizer of their cost function
  - Machine: Cost function -> Adaptation algorithm (gradient descent in action space, conjectural variations in policy space, or policy gradient in policy space) -> Action/policy update mechanism
  - Experiment controller: Trial management -> Data collection -> Algorithm parameter configuration

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize trial with random actions
  2. Human observes cost and adjusts action to minimize it
  3. Machine updates action/policy based on adaptation algorithm
  4. Repeat steps 2-3 for trial duration
  5. Record median actions for analysis
  6. Update machine parameters for next trial/iteration

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Adaptation rate vs. convergence stability (faster adaptation may lead to oscillations)
  - Perturbation size in policy estimation (too small: noisy estimates; too large: non-linear effects)
  - Trial duration vs. computational efficiency

- Failure signatures:
  - Human actions not converging to predicted equilibria
  - Machine cost not decreasing as expected
  - Policy gradient estimates becoming unstable or inaccurate
  - Statistical tests showing no significant difference between conditions

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement Experiment 1 with gradient descent in action space, varying α from 0 to 1
  2. Implement Experiment 2 with conjectural variations in policy space, iterating K=10 times
  3. Implement Experiment 3 with policy gradient descent in policy space, iterating K=10 times

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the steering effect observed in Experiment 3 generalize to multi-dimensional action spaces?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "It is important to note that the optimization problems in(11) are coupled. Since both problems must be considered simultaneously, there is no obvious candidate for a 'solution' concept."
- Why unresolved: The experiments were conducted with scalar actions (h ∈ R, m ∈ R). The theoretical framework allows for vector actions, but the empirical validation is limited to one-dimensional cases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the gradient descent in policy space algorithm with vector-valued actions in a similar experimental setup, measuring convergence to the predicted reverse Stackelberg equilibrium.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does human cognitive load affect the consistency of conjectures formed during co-adaptive interactions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "the human’s empirical play is consistent with the theoretically-predicted best-response function for its prescribed cost"
- Why unresolved: The paper assumes rational human behavior but does not measure or account for cognitive load variations across participants. The task load survey shows low to moderate demands, but no correlation analysis with game-theoretic consistency is provided.
- What evidence would resolve it: Correlating NASA-TLX scores with deviations from predicted equilibria across trials and subjects, controlling for adaptation rate and policy complexity.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the long-term dynamics of co-adaptation when the machine employs higher-level reasoning than the human?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] "the machine could then go higher still, theoretically leading to a well-known infinite regress"
- Why unresolved: The experiments were limited to 10-14 minutes. The paper acknowledges computational resource constraints but does not explore the temporal limits of human-machine strategic adaptation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extended-duration experiments with progressive policy complexity, measuring human adaptation rates, strategy shifts, and potential breakdowns in rational response over weeks or months.

## Limitations
- Human rationality assumption may not hold due to cognitive limitations or inconsistent decision-making
- Ecological validity limited by abstract quadratic cost functions and laboratory setting
- Results derived for specific quadratic games may not generalize to non-quadratic or high-dimensional cases

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The experimental design and statistical analysis are sound, with appropriate use of two-sided t-tests and clear demonstration that human actions shifted predictably with machine adaptation parameters. The implementation of gradient descent and policy gradient algorithms is well-specified.

**Medium Confidence**: The convergence to theoretical equilibria is demonstrated empirically, but the underlying assumption of human rationality as rational cost minimizers, while supported by the results, is not independently validated. The interpretation that machines can "control" human actions through adaptation is supported but may overstate the practical implications.

**Low Confidence**: The practical implications for designing adaptive systems are speculative. While the paper shows theoretical predictions hold in controlled experiments, translating these findings to real-world applications requires additional work.
