# Explainable AI is Dead, Long Live Explainable AI! Hypothesis-driven decision support

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12389
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12389
- Reference count: 26
- This paper proposes a new conceptual framework called Evaluative AI that shifts from recommendation-driven to hypothesis-driven decision support

## Executive Summary
This paper critiques the current paradigm of explainable AI (XAI) for decision support, which relies on giving recommendations and explanations. It argues this approach leads to over- or under-reliance on AI systems, as users struggle to calibrate their trust and engage with explanations. Instead, the paper proposes Evaluative AI, a new conceptual framework that shifts from recommendation-driven to hypothesis-driven decision support. In this paradigm, AI tools provide evidence for and against user-generated hypotheses, rather than offering recommendations. This approach aligns better with human cognitive processes, such as abductive reasoning, and gives users greater control over the decision-making process.

## Method Summary
This is a conceptual paper proposing a new framework rather than an empirical study. The paper outlines "Evaluative AI" - a machine-in-the-loop paradigm where AI tools provide evidence for and against user-generated hypotheses rather than recommendations. The framework is built on the Data/Frame model of sensemaking and aims to support abductive reasoning processes. The authors identify the need to identify a medium- or high-stakes decision-making domain, design an interface presenting multiple hypotheses, and implement evidence presentation mechanisms that support users in judging plausibility of different hypotheses.

## Key Results
- The current recommendation-driven XAI paradigm leads to over- or under-reliance on AI systems
- Evaluative AI mitigates trust calibration issues by removing recommendations and providing balanced evidence
- The framework aligns with human abductive reasoning processes and gives users greater control over decision making

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The Evaluative AI framework mitigates over-reliance and under-reliance by removing the recommendation signal that causes automation bias.
- Mechanism: Instead of providing a single recommendation, Evaluative AI presents multiple plausible hypotheses and evidence for/against each. This forces decision makers to actively engage with the evidence rather than passively accept a recommendation.
- Core assumption: Decision makers will actively engage with evidence when given control over which hypotheses to explore.
- Evidence anchors:
  - "evaluative AI tools do not provide recommendations. They either allow the decision maker to determine which options are best or help them to narrow down to a manageable set of options."
  - "Evaluative AI is built on the Data/Frame model of sensemaking [Klein et al., 2007], so supports the decision maker's cognitive process by allowing them to explore hypotheses, rather than providing only the information to justify a machine recommendation."
  - "The ability to assess the strengths and weaknesses of judgements and decisions is key to expert decision making [Klein, 2017, Graber et al., 2012, Lambe et al., 2016]."
- Break condition: If decision makers consistently fail to engage with evidence, the framework loses its advantage over recommendation-driven approaches.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Evaluative AI aligns with human abductive reasoning processes, leading to better decision support.
- Mechanism: The framework matches the human cognitive process of generating hypotheses and judging their plausibility with evidence, rather than forcing users to understand machine reasoning.
- Core assumption: Human decision makers naturally use abductive reasoning and will perform better when tools support this process.
- Evidence anchors:
  - "we argue that this mitigates issues of over- and under-reliance on decision support tools, and better leverages human expertise in decision making."
  - "we argue that the reason this Prudence-like approach is more effective for decision support because it aligns with the cognitive decision-making process that people use when making judgements and decisions [Klein et al., 2007, Hoffman et al., 2022, Peirce, 2009]."
  - "abductive reasoning plays a central role in this sensemaking process."
- Break condition: If the alignment between Evaluative AI and human reasoning processes proves incorrect, the framework may not improve decision making.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Evaluative AI improves trust calibration by providing evidence for and against hypotheses rather than a single recommendation.
- Mechanism: By presenting balanced evidence for multiple hypotheses, users can better assess the strengths and weaknesses of each option, leading to more appropriate trust in the system.
- Core assumption: Balanced presentation of evidence helps users calibrate their trust more effectively than single recommendations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - "This helps with trust calibration because the machine does not give recommendations, as well as over/under-reliance because there is no recommendation to follow."
  - "Good decision makers search for evidence that both supports and refutes a hypothesis."
  - "They assert that this is because participants did not pay attention to explainability information."
- Break condition: If users continue to ignore evidence despite its presentation, trust calibration benefits may not materialize.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Evidence Generator -> Hypothesis Filter -> User Interface -> Decision Logger
- Critical path: User selects hypothesis → System provides evidence → User makes decision → Outcome logged
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Presenting too many hypotheses may overwhelm users
  - Providing too much evidence may increase cognitive load
  - Balancing between automated filtering and user control
- Failure signatures:
  - Users consistently selecting the first presented hypothesis
  - Users ignoring evidence and making decisions based on initial impressions
  - System unable to generate meaningful evidence for certain hypotheses
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare decision accuracy between Evaluative AI and recommendation-driven approaches on a simple diagnostic task
  2. Measure user engagement with evidence presentation using eye-tracking
  3. Test different hypothesis filtering strategies to find optimal balance between choice and cognitive load

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we design evaluative AI systems that provide meaningful support for decision makers without overwhelming them with too much information or cognitive load?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the challenge of designing evaluative AI systems that strike a balance between providing decision makers with the information they need and avoiding cognitive overload.
- Why unresolved: It is difficult to determine the optimal level of information and cognitive load for different decision makers and contexts. Further research is needed to understand how to tailor evaluative AI systems to individual users and decision-making scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental studies comparing different designs of evaluative AI systems, measuring decision maker performance and cognitive load, would provide insights into the optimal balance of information and cognitive load.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can evaluative AI systems be designed to support decision makers in exploring alternative hypotheses and considering a broader range of possibilities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the importance of supporting decision makers in exploring different hypotheses and considering a broader range of possibilities, beyond just the most likely or recommended options.
- Why unresolved: It is challenging to design evaluative AI systems that can effectively generate and present a diverse set of hypotheses and possibilities to decision makers, without overwhelming them or leading to information overload.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research on how decision makers explore and evaluate different hypotheses, combined with user studies of evaluative AI systems, could provide insights into how to design systems that effectively support hypothesis exploration.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can evaluative AI systems be designed to foster trust and collaboration between decision makers and AI systems, without promoting over-reliance or under-reliance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the challenge of designing evaluative AI systems that foster trust and collaboration between decision makers and AI systems, while avoiding the pitfalls of over-reliance or under-reliance on AI recommendations.
- Why unresolved: It is difficult to determine the optimal balance of trust and collaboration between decision makers and AI systems, as it depends on various factors such as the decision-making context, the expertise of the decision maker, and the reliability of the AI system.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies of decision makers using evaluative AI systems, measuring their trust and reliance on AI recommendations, as well as their decision-making performance, would provide insights into how to design systems that foster appropriate trust and collaboration.

## Limitations
- The framework is conceptual and lacks empirical validation through user studies or experiments
- No specific implementation details or technical specifications are provided
- The paper does not address how to handle situations where generating multiple plausible hypotheses is difficult
- The approach may require more cognitive effort from users compared to traditional recommendation systems

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The critique of current recommendation-driven XAI approaches and the identification of over/under-reliance as key problems (supported by corpus citations)
- **Medium confidence**: The alignment of Evaluative AI with abductive reasoning processes (theoretically sound but not empirically validated)
- **Low confidence**: The assumption that users will actively engage with evidence and that this approach will improve decision making (no empirical support provided)
