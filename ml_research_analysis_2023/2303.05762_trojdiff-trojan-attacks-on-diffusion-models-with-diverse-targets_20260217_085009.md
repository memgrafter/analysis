# TrojDiff: Trojan Attacks on Diffusion Models with Diverse Targets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.05762
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05762
- Reference count: 40
- This paper introduces TrojDiff, the first Trojan attack framework for diffusion models, demonstrating that training data manipulations can compromise these models

## Executive Summary
TrojDiff presents the first Trojan attack framework targeting diffusion models, demonstrating that training data manipulations can compromise these generative systems. The method employs a novel Trojan diffusion process that biases noise input with triggers and a Trojan generative process that learns to reverse this biased diffusion. Three attack types are considered: In-D2D (targeting a class within the in-domain distribution), Out-D2D (targeting an out-of-domain class), and D2I (targeting a specific instance). Evaluated on CIFAR-10 and CelebA datasets against DDPM and DDIM models, TrojDiff achieves high attack performance while maintaining benign performance with minimal degradation in FID, precision, and recall.

## Method Summary
TrojDiff introduces a novel Trojan attack framework for diffusion models through two key processes: Trojan diffusion and Trojan generative. The Trojan diffusion process biases the noise input with triggers, while the Trojan generative process learns to reverse this biased diffusion. The framework supports three attack types: In-D2D targeting classes within the in-domain distribution, Out-D2D targeting out-of-domain classes, and D2I targeting specific instances. The attack is implemented by manipulating training data with Trojan triggers and retraining the diffusion model to learn both benign and Trojan behaviors.

## Key Results
- Achieved up to 84.70% attack precision and 96.90% attack success rate in In-D2D attacks
- Maintained benign performance with minimal degradation in FID, precision, and recall metrics
- Successfully demonstrated three distinct attack types (In-D2D, Out-D2D, D2I) on CIFAR-10 and CelebA datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Trojan attack works by manipulating the diffusion process itself through trigger-biased noise injection during training. The Trojan diffusion process creates a path dependency where the model learns to associate specific triggers with target outputs. During inference, when the trigger appears, the model follows this learned biased path to generate Trojan-desired outputs rather than following the standard diffusion reversal. This approach exploits the fundamental training mechanism of diffusion models, making the Trojan behavior inherently integrated into the generation process.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Training Data -> Trigger Injection -> Trojan Diffusion Process -> Trojan Generative Process -> Diffusion Model

**Critical Path**: Trigger injection during training data preparation → Trojan diffusion learning → Trojan generative learning → Integrated model behavior

**Design Tradeoffs**: The framework balances attack effectiveness with benign performance maintenance, requiring careful trigger design and training process control to avoid detection while ensuring the Trojan behavior remains functional.

**Failure Signatures**: Attack failure may manifest as: inability to trigger desired outputs when triggers are present, degradation in benign generation quality, or detection through statistical analysis of model outputs.

## Limitations
- Limited evaluation scope to small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10, CelebA) rather than large-scale, high-resolution image datasets
- Absence of defense mechanism testing against the proposed attacks
- No analysis of attack detectability or backdoor elimination strategies

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The fundamental methodology of Trojan diffusion and Trojan generative processes is technically sound and well-described

**Medium confidence**: Attack effectiveness metrics are reliable for the tested datasets and models, though scalability remains uncertain

**Low confidence**: Long-term persistence of Trojan behavior under fine-tuning and real-world deployment conditions is not evaluated
