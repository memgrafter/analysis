# A roadmap to fair and trustworthy prediction model validation in healthcare

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.03779
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03779
- Reference count: 0
- This paper proposes a roadmap for fair and trustworthy external validation of AI prediction models in healthcare, identifying three validation types and recommending new local data from target populations for reliability assessment.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical challenge of ensuring fair and trustworthy external validation of artificial intelligence prediction models in healthcare settings. The authors develop a comprehensive roadmap that categorizes external validation approaches based on the relationship between validation data and the target population. They argue that using new local data from the target population provides the clearest assessment of model reliability, while validation with data from different populations can lead to ambiguous and potentially misleading results. The roadmap emphasizes the importance of representative sampling, consistent variable definitions, and thorough bias assessment during validation.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a systematic approach to external validation that distinguishes between three types based on population relationships: validation using new data from the target population (Type I), validation using data from similar but different populations (Type II), and validation using data from significantly different populations (Type III). The roadmap emphasizes using new local data from the target population to ensure clear implications for model reliability, while cautioning against interpreting external validation results when population differences are substantial. For cases where population differences are significant, the authors recommend developing new models tailored to local contexts rather than attempting to adapt existing models.

## Key Results
- External validation should use new data from the target population to ensure clear implications for model reliability
- Population differences between validation data and target population can have arbitrary impact on model performance, making validation results ambiguous
- Developing new models for different populations is preferable to adapting existing models when population differences are significant

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: External validation should use new data from the target population to ensure fair and trustworthy model reliability assessment.
- Mechanism: By validating on new data from the same population where the model was developed, performance reflects the model's true generalizability within its intended use context, avoiding confounding by population differences.
- Core assumption: The target population is well-defined and can be sampled representatively for validation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we suggest to externally validate a model using new data from the target population to ensure clear implications of validation performance on model reliability"
  - [section 3] "External validation using new data from the target population directly assesses this, where the validation data should be a representative sample of the target population"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus support for this specific mechanism found; claim based on paper's internal reasoning.
- Break condition: If the target population is poorly defined, heterogeneous, or impossible to sample representatively, this mechanism fails because validation results will be misleading.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: External validation using data from populations with known differences from the target population produces ambiguous and unreliable performance assessments.
- Mechanism: Population differences in characteristics, outcome rates, and predictor definitions confound validation results, making it unclear whether poor performance reflects model inadequacy or data differences.
- Core assumption: Population differences have non-trivial impact on model performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5] "Discrepancies in sample characteristics between external data and target population can have arbitrary impact on model performance and result in mixed findings"
  - [section 4] "finding external data that is 'similar enough' to the target population is non-trivial, and data differences can have unanticipated impact on model performance, making the implications unclear"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus support for this specific mechanism found; claim based on paper's internal reasoning.
- Break condition: If population differences are minimal or well-understood and can be statistically adjusted for, this mechanism's concern about ambiguity is reduced.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Developing new models tailored to new populations is preferable to adapting existing models when population differences are significant.
- Mechanism: Population-specific differences in data recording systems, medical practice, and variable definitions make model adaptation complex and error-prone, while new model development can account for these differences from the start.
- Core assumption: Model adaptation across populations requires addressing fundamental data and practice differences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6] "Time and resources may be better spent by developing new models for the new population...differences in data recording systems and medical practice can lead to misalignments in detailed information that affects the performance of external models"
  - [section 6] "instead of directly applying or adapting an existing model, we propose to build upon the data preparation and model development process established by existing high-quality studies, and develop new models using local data that are tailored to local concerns and needs"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus support for this specific mechanism found; claim based on paper's internal reasoning.
- Break condition: If adaptation can be done systematically with clear guidelines and sufficient data, this mechanism's preference for new development may be unnecessarily resource-intensive.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: The roadmap consists of three validation types (local target population, similar external populations, different external populations) mapped to validation strategies (direct reliability assessment, cautious interpretation, or new model development). Key components include data preparation, model evaluation, performance interpretation, and implementation guidance.
- Critical path: Define target population → Curate representative validation data → Ensure consistent variable definitions → Perform comprehensive performance evaluation → Assess for bias → Make implementation decisions based on results.
- Design tradeoffs: Local validation provides clear reliability assessment but may be resource-intensive; external validation is easier but ambiguous; new model development ensures population fit but requires more effort than adaptation.
- Failure signatures: Poor calibration or discrimination in local validation suggests model inadequacy; inconsistent results across external validations indicate population differences; failure to account for data quality issues leads to misleading performance metrics.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate validation using new local data from the target population to establish baseline reliability.
  2. Test model performance on external data with known minor differences to assess sensitivity to population variation.
  3. Compare adapted model performance against newly developed population-specific model to evaluate adaptation effectiveness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific criteria should be used to determine if external data is "similar enough" to the target population for external validation II?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that finding external data that is "similar enough" to the target population is non-trivial, citing the example of the RACA score validation where European studies had mixed results despite smaller differences in ROSC rates.
- Why unresolved: The paper states that differences in population characteristics can have unanticipated impact on model performance, making the implications unclear, but does not provide specific criteria for determining similarity.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study identifying and validating specific criteria (e.g., demographic thresholds, outcome rate ranges, predictor characteristic thresholds) that consistently predict successful external validation when using data from populations similar to the target population.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between external validation using local data versus developing new models for different populations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose that instead of trying to adapt existing models for different populations, researchers should develop new models using local data tailored to local concerns and needs, but acknowledge this may require more resources.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents a clear preference but does not quantify when external validation is more cost-effective than developing new models, or establish criteria for deciding between these approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the accuracy, resource requirements, and real-world performance of adapted models versus newly developed models across different healthcare contexts and populations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What standardized methods can be developed to assess fairness and potential bias in prediction models during external validation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors recommend assessing potential bias and unfairness in predictions during external validation, referencing recent methodological developments, but do not specify which methods should be standardized.
- Why unresolved: While the paper mentions the importance of fairness assessment and references ongoing work, it does not identify specific fairness metrics or assessment protocols that should be adopted.
- What evidence would resolve it: Validation of fairness assessment methods across multiple prediction model types and healthcare contexts, with evidence for which metrics best predict real-world disparities and which are most practical to implement.

## Limitations
- The roadmap's recommendations are primarily based on theoretical reasoning rather than empirical validation studies
- The paper acknowledges that population differences can have "unanticipated impact" on model performance but provides limited quantitative evidence for the magnitude of these effects
- The proposed preference for developing new models over adapting existing ones may be overly conservative in cases where adaptation frameworks exist

## Confidence
- High confidence: The fundamental principle that external validation should use data representative of the target population to assess model reliability
- Medium confidence: The assertion that population differences create ambiguous validation results, though specific mechanisms and magnitudes are less established
- Medium confidence: The recommendation to develop new models rather than adapt existing ones, as this represents a significant departure from common practice without extensive empirical support
