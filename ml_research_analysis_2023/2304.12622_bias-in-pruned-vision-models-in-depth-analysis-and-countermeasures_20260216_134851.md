# Bias in Pruned Vision Models: In-Depth Analysis and Countermeasures

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.12622
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12622
- Reference count: 40
- This paper investigates the relationship between model pruning and induced bias in computer vision models, showing that pruning to 80-95% sparsity does not significantly increase bias, but higher sparsities do.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how model pruning affects bias in computer vision models, specifically analyzing the relationship between sparsity levels and bias amplification. Using ResNet18 on the CelebA dataset, the authors demonstrate that moderate pruning (80-95% sparsity) maintains bias levels comparable to dense models, while extreme pruning (≥95% sparsity) leads to significant bias amplification. The study reveals that bias amplification correlates with increased prediction uncertainty and label interdependence. Jointly-trained models show less bias than single-attribute models at lower sparsities. The authors propose simple threshold calibration and label override techniques that significantly reduce bias across different architectures and datasets.

## Method Summary
The study employs Global Magnitude Pruning (GMP) to create sparse models at various sparsity levels (80%, 90%, 95%, 98%, 99%, 99.5%) from dense ResNet18, MobileNetV1, and ResNet50 architectures. Models are trained on CelebA (cropped and uncropped), AwA2, and iWildCam datasets for binary attribute prediction tasks. The evaluation framework measures accuracy, AUC, Bias Amplification (BA), Threshold Calibration Bias (TCB), Expected Calibration Error (ECE), uncertainty, and label interdependence. The authors compare jointly-trained models (trained on all attributes simultaneously) versus single-attribute models, and implement bias mitigation techniques including threshold calibration and prediction overrides using dense model predictions or true labels.

## Key Results
- Pruning to 80-95% sparsity does not significantly increase bias, but higher sparsities (≥95%) do
- Jointly-trained models show less bias than single-attribute models at lower sparsities
- Simple threshold calibration and label override techniques significantly reduce bias
- Bias amplification correlates with increased prediction uncertainty and label interdependence
- Findings are validated across architectures (MobileNetV1, ResNet50) and datasets (AwA2, iWildCam)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
At moderate sparsities (80-95%), remaining weights still capture key feature representations, so bias amplification remains low. At very high sparsities (≥95%), the feature space becomes too compressed, leading to higher uncertainty and increased label interdependence, which amplifies bias. Core assumption: remaining weights after pruning still encode essential features. Evidence: [abstract] "pruning to 80-95% sparsity does not significantly increase bias, but higher sparsities do"; [section 3.2] "Uncertainty goes up considerably for almost every attribute, roughly doubling from dense to 99.5%-sparse models"; [section 3.3] "BA presents a slight increase w.r.t. sparsity between 90 and 95%, after which the increase is more pronounced."

### Mechanism 2
Jointly-trained models encourage a more robust feature representation by forcing the model to learn shared features across multiple attributes, reducing overfitting to spurious correlations. Core assumption: a shared feature representation across attributes is more generalizable and less prone to bias. Evidence: [abstract] "Jointly-trained models show less bias than single-attribute models at lower sparsities"; [section 3.1] "single-attribute training shows a much higher BA than joint training"; [section 3.3] "The attributes with highest BA value for dense joint training are Double Chin (Male, 0.053), Wavy Hair (Male, 0.047), Wearing Necktie (Young, 0.046)..."

### Mechanism 3
Adjusting prediction thresholds to better match true label distribution reduces systematic bias, while overriding uncertain predictions with dense model predictions or true labels mitigates category bias by correcting high-risk samples. Core assumption: uncertainty is a good proxy for bias-prone samples. Evidence: [abstract] "Simple threshold calibration and label override techniques significantly reduce bias"; [section 4.1] "we set a single threshold for each attribute so that the predictions are correctly calibrated on the original CelebA validation set"; [section 4.2] "overrides with both human and dense labels substantially decrease the bias amplification of models of all sparsities."

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: CelebA dataset -> ResNet18/MobileNetV1/ResNet50 -> Global Magnitude Pruning -> Sparse models -> Bias metrics evaluation -> Mitigation techniques

- **Critical path**: 1) Load and preprocess CelebA data. 2) Train dense models (jointly and singly) on CelebA attributes. 3) Apply GMP to obtain sparse models at different sparsity levels. 4) Evaluate dense and sparse models using defined metrics. 5) Analyze relationship between sparsity and bias. 6) Implement and evaluate bias mitigation techniques.

- **Design tradeoffs**: Model architecture choice affects results; GMP is primary method but other pruning techniques may yield different results; BA is primary metric but other bias metrics could provide additional insights.

- **Failure signatures**: Accuracy degradation at high sparsity levels (>95%); increased bias amplification at high sparsity levels; poor performance of bias mitigation techniques; inconsistent results across different architectures or datasets.

- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Train dense ResNet18 model on all 40 CelebA attributes and evaluate accuracy, AUC, and bias metrics. 2) Apply GMP to obtain 90% sparse model and evaluate accuracy, AUC, and bias metrics; compare to dense model. 3) Implement threshold calibration on 90% sparse model and evaluate effect on bias amplification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. How do different pruning algorithms (e.g., structured vs. unstructured, global vs. layer-wise) affect bias amplification across various sparsity levels? The paper focuses on GMP and unstructured pruning, leaving open how other pruning methods compare.

2. Can bias amplification be mitigated through architectural changes to the model, such as modifying feature representation or adding regularization? The paper proposes threshold calibration and label overrides but does not explore architectural modifications.

3. How does bias amplification in sparse models generalize to other tasks beyond binary attribute classification, such as object detection or semantic segmentation? The paper validates findings on iWildCam but focuses primarily on binary attribute prediction.

## Limitations

- Analysis primarily conducted on face attribute datasets (CelebA) with binary classification tasks, limiting generalizability to other domains
- Focus on global magnitude pruning; results may differ for structured or more sophisticated pruning methods
- Bias metrics used (particularly BA) may not capture all forms of bias present in real-world applications

## Confidence

- **High**: The relationship between sparsity and accuracy degradation, and the effectiveness of threshold calibration techniques
- **Medium**: The correlation between uncertainty and bias amplification, and the general applicability across different architectures
- **Low**: The complete absence of bias at moderate sparsities in all practical scenarios

## Next Checks

1. Cross-domain validation: Test the pruning-bias relationship on non-face datasets (e.g., medical imaging or satellite imagery) to verify if the 80-95% sparsity threshold for bias stability holds across different application domains.

2. Structured pruning comparison: Implement structured pruning methods (filter-level or channel-level) and compare their bias amplification patterns against unstructured global magnitude pruning to identify if pruning granularity affects bias differently.

3. Dynamic bias monitoring: Develop a framework to monitor bias amplification during the pruning process in real-time, rather than only at final sparsity levels, to identify early warning signals of bias emergence.
