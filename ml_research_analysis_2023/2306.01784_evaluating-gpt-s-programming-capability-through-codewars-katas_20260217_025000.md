# Evaluating GPT's Programming Capability through CodeWars' Katas

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2306.01784
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01784
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: GPT-4 reliably solves Codewars problems up to 4kyu difficulty, but both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 fail on 3kyu and above problems.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 programming capabilities on coding problems of varying difficulty from Codewars. The authors systematically test both models across 24 problems ranging from 8kyu (easiest) to 1kyu (hardest), finding that GPT-4 can reliably solve all problems from 4kyu to 8kyu when given feedback, while both models fail on 3kyu and above. The study reveals fundamental limitations in AI models' ability to validate solutions and engage in creative problem-solving, suggesting that current approaches rely heavily on pattern matching rather than genuine reasoning. The authors propose that enhancing AI with validation and creative thinking capabilities could break the 3kyu barrier and bring models closer to human-level programming ability.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on 24 Codewars katas across 8 difficulty levels, presenting each problem to the models and recording whether solutions pass all test cases on first attempt. For failed problems, they provide feedback with failed test cases and expected results, repeating up to 5 times to observe improvement. The evaluation uses existing Codewars infrastructure and standard API access to both GPT models, with problems implemented in Python.

## Key Results
- GPT-4 reliably solves all Codewars problems from 4kyu to 8kyu difficulty when given feedback
- Both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 fail to solve any problems at 3kyu difficulty and above
- GPT-3.5 shows significantly worse performance, unable to solve 3kyu problems even with feedback
- Models demonstrate pattern matching capabilities but lack validation and creative thinking needed for harder problems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: GPT models solve programming problems by pattern matching from training data, not by genuine problem-solving reasoning.
- **Mechanism**: The models generate code by retrieving and recombining syntactic patterns learned during pre-training on vast code corpora. When faced with problems similar to their training distribution, they produce plausible-looking solutions that often pass simple test cases.
- **Core assumption**: Training data included sufficient examples of the problem types to allow pattern matching.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "GPT-4 is more reliable and can solve all questions from 4kyu to 8kyu when given feedback"
  - [section 3.6] "These are well-known problems, leading the authors to suspect that the GPTs might have encountered similar or identical questions during training"
  - [corpus] Weak - neighboring papers focus on benchmark design but don't directly address the memorization hypothesis
- **Break condition**: When problems require novel algorithmic approaches or creative problem formulation that deviate from training patterns, GPT models fail to produce valid solutions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: GPT models lack iterative validation and debugging capabilities, leading to persistent errors even with feedback.
- **Mechanism**: The models generate solutions in a single pass without internal validation. When given feedback, they attempt modifications but cannot systematically test or verify their changes against the full problem space.
- **Core assumption**: GPT models don't have built-in execution or test harness capabilities to validate generated code.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.4] "GPT-3.5 couldn't provide the correct answer for the second kata. It repeatedly gave wrong answers, even with feedback on failed test cases"
  - [section 4.2] "A notable weakness is their inability to validate solutions, resulting in repeated errors"
  - [corpus] Moderate - related work on code validation exists but isn't cited as evidence for this specific claim
- **Break condition**: When problems require multiple iterations of debugging or when feedback doesn't contain sufficient information for the model to correct its approach.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: GPT models operate on pattern completion rather than genuine creative thinking, limiting their ability to solve complex problems requiring novel approaches.
- **Mechanism**: The models complete patterns based on statistical regularities in training data. They cannot engage in the kind of creative problem formulation humans use when facing novel challenges, instead relying on known solution patterns.
- **Core assumption**: Creative problem-solving involves generating novel solution approaches not present in training data.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2] "the most challenging type of intelligence is 'creative thinking'... there is no fixed list of algorithms to solve a programming problem"
  - [section 4.2] "no AI approach currently emulates this human capability"
  - [corpus] Strong - multiple related papers (e.g., AetherCode, CodeScope) acknowledge this limitation in their benchmarks
- **Break condition**: When problems require entirely novel algorithmic approaches or when multiple solution paradigms need to be combined in creative ways.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Pattern matching vs. reasoning distinction
  - Why needed here: Understanding whether GPT solves problems through genuine reasoning or pattern matching is crucial for interpreting experimental results
  - Quick check question: If GPT generates a solution by recombining code patterns it saw during training, is that reasoning or pattern matching?

- **Concept**: Feedback limitations in language models
  - Why needed here: The paper shows GPT can improve with feedback but only up to a point, revealing fundamental limitations
  - Quick check question: Why does GPT sometimes fail to improve even after multiple rounds of feedback containing failed test cases?

- **Concept**: Problem difficulty scaling in programming
  - Why needed here: The 3kyu boundary reveals how problem complexity scales beyond pattern matching capabilities
  - Quick check question: What makes 3kyu problems fundamentally different from 4kyu problems in terms of the cognitive capabilities required?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Problem statement → GPT code generation → Test case execution → Feedback (if failed) → Model refinement attempt → Final evaluation

- **Critical path**: Problem statement → GPT code generation → Test case execution → Feedback (if failed) → Model refinement attempt → Final evaluation

- **Design tradeoffs**: The evaluation uses existing Codewars infrastructure rather than building custom test harnesses, trading control for standardization. The feedback mechanism is limited to textual descriptions of failures rather than interactive debugging sessions.

- **Failure signatures**: 
  - Pattern matching failure: Model generates syntactically correct but semantically wrong code
  - Validation failure: Model cannot correct persistent bugs despite feedback
  - Creative thinking failure: Model cannot devise novel approaches for complex problems
  - Timeout failures: Model generates inefficient algorithms that don't scale

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Test GPT-3.5 on 8kyu problems with immediate feedback to establish baseline pattern matching capabilities
  2. Test GPT-4 on 7kyu problems without feedback to measure reasoning vs. pattern matching
  3. Test both models on 5kyu problems with structured feedback to measure validation capability limits

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can AI models be enhanced to acquire validation and creative thinking capabilities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose that AI models need validation and creative thinking capabilities to better emulate human problem-solving techniques and break the boundary at 3kyu difficulty level.
- Why unresolved: Current GPT models lack these capabilities, and while some research has been done in this direction, there is no mature approach to emulate creative thinking or integrate validation capabilities into AI models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of AI models that can effectively validate their solutions against comprehensive test cases and generate innovative solutions to complex programming problems.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What objective measures can be developed to evaluate programming problem difficulty?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose that an objective measure of programming problem difficulty is needed, which incorporates both problem difficulty and the time required for solution.
- Why unresolved: Existing difficulty levels (e.g., kyu system) are subjective and based on user reviews, and there is no standardized, objective measure of programming problem complexity.
- What evidence would resolve it: A validated metric that can consistently rank programming problems by difficulty, independent of individual programmer skill levels or subjective opinions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can AI models reach the highest human programming level with enhanced capabilities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest that with the addition of validation and creative thinking capabilities, AI models might be able to break the boundary at 3kyu level and reach the highest human programming level.
- Why unresolved: Current AI models, even with improvements, have not yet demonstrated the ability to solve problems at the highest difficulty levels (1kyu and 2kyu) that experienced human programmers can solve.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful performance of AI models on a wide range of programming problems, including the most difficult ones, comparable to or exceeding the performance of top human programmers.

## Limitations

- The evaluation may be affected by GPT models having seen similar problems during training, potentially conflating memorization with genuine reasoning capability
- The limited number of attempts (5) per problem may not adequately explore the model's problem-solving potential or reveal whether additional attempts would yield solutions
- The paper doesn't control for potential differences in problem formulation or test case complexity across difficulty levels, making it unclear whether failures stem from problem complexity itself or other factors

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The empirical finding that GPT-4 reliably solves 4kyu and below but fails on 3kyu and above. This observation is directly supported by systematic testing across multiple problems and is consistent across different evaluation runs.

**Medium confidence**: The interpretation that these failures indicate fundamental limitations in validation and creative thinking capabilities. While the experimental evidence shows consistent failure patterns, alternative explanations (training data exposure, evaluation methodology limitations) cannot be fully ruled out.

**Low confidence**: The claim that "no AI approach currently emulates this human capability" for creative thinking. This represents a broad generalization about the entire field of AI research that goes beyond the specific experimental scope and lacks systematic comparative analysis with other approaches.

## Next Checks

1. **Training data analysis**: Systematically analyze whether Codewars problems at different difficulty levels appear in common code training datasets using approximate nearest neighbor search on problem descriptions and test cases. This would validate or refute the memorization hypothesis.

2. **Extended iteration study**: Repeat the evaluation with extended feedback loops (20+ attempts) and track solution improvement trajectories. This would clarify whether the 5-attempt limit artificially constrains observed performance and reveal the true learning curve with feedback.

3. **Cross-model capability comparison**: Evaluate GPT models against human programmers at similar skill levels on the same problem set, measuring both success rates and solution quality metrics. This would establish whether the observed failure patterns represent genuine capability gaps or artifacts of the evaluation methodology.
