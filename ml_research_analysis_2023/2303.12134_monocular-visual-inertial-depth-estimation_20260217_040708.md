# Monocular Visual-Inertial Depth Estimation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.12134
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12134

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a monocular visual-inertial depth estimation pipeline that produces dense depth maps with metric scale by combining monocular depth estimation and visual-inertial odometry. The method uses a two-stage alignment process: first, a global scale and shift alignment is performed using sparse metric depth from VIO; then, a learned dense scale map is applied to refine the depth estimates locally. The pipeline is modular and compatible with various monocular depth models. Evaluated on TartanAir and VOID datasets, the method achieves up to 30% reduction in inverse RMSE compared to global alignment alone. It outperforms the state-of-the-art KBNet by over 50% in inverse RMSE at low density (150 sparse points), and demonstrates successful zero-shot transfer from synthetic to real data.

## Method Summary
The method integrates monocular depth estimation with visual-inertial odometry (VIO) to produce dense metric depth maps. It operates in three stages: first, a monocular depth model (DPT-Hybrid, MiDaS-small, etc.) generates unitless depth estimates from RGB images; second, VIO provides sparse metric depth measurements that are used to perform global scale and shift alignment via least-squares fitting; third, a ScaleMapLearner (SML) network learns per-pixel scale factors to refine the globally-aligned depth estimates locally. The SML is trained on synthetic data (TartanAir) and fine-tuned on real data (VOID), using loss functions including L1 loss and multiscale gradient matching. The modular design allows easy integration of improved monocular depth models and VIO systems.

## Key Results
- Up to 30% reduction in inverse RMSE with dense scale alignment vs. global alignment alone
- Outperforms KBNet by over 50% in inverse RMSE at 150 sparse points
- Demonstrates successful zero-shot transfer from synthetic (TartanAir) to real (VOID, NYUv2, VCU-RVI) datasets

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Monocular Depth Estimation -> VIO System -> Global Alignment -> Dense Scale Alignment -> Output Dense Metric Depth

- Critical path:
  1. Input RGB image to monocular depth estimator
  2. Input RGB sequence + IMU to VIO system
  3. Perform global alignment of depth estimates with sparse metric depth
  4. Generate scale map scaffolding
  5. Apply SML to refine depth estimates
  6. Output final dense metric depth map

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Modularity vs. Integration: Modular design allows for easier updates but may introduce data transfer overhead
  - Accuracy vs. Speed: More complex SML architectures may improve accuracy but increase inference time
  - Sparse Density vs. Coverage: Higher density of sparse depth points improves alignment but requires more computational resources

- Failure signatures:
  - Global alignment failure: Large discrepancies between depth estimates and sparse metric depth, visible in error maps
  - SML learning failure: Scale map scaffolding not properly refined, leading to inconsistent local depth adjustments
  - VIO failure: Very few or no sparse depth points, causing global alignment to fail or produce unreliable results

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify global alignment works by comparing depth estimates before and after alignment with sparse metric depth
  2. Test SML learning by training on synthetic data (e.g., TartanAir) and evaluating on validation set
  3. Evaluate zero-shot transfer by testing SML trained on synthetic data on real-world dataset (e.g., VOID)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of the pipeline change when using different monocular depth estimation models beyond those tested (DPT-BEiT-Large, DPT-SwinV2-Large, DPT-Large, DPT-Hybrid, DPT-SwinV2-Tiny, DPT-LeViT, MiDaS-small)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "Our modular pipeline is agnostic to which monocular depth estimation model is used and can therefore benefit from continual improvement in these models" and tests several models, suggesting interest in broader evaluation.
- Why unresolved: Only a limited set of models from the MiDaS/DPT family were tested; the impact of other architectures (e.g., newer transformers, convolutional models) is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of the pipeline with a diverse set of monocular depth estimation models (including those outside the MiDaS/DPT family) on the VOID, TartanAir, and other datasets, comparing iRMSE, iMAE, and iAbsRel.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of varying the number of sparse metric depth points below 50 on the performance of the SML network, and is there a theoretical minimum number of points required for the method to function?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper investigates performance at 150 and 50 sparse points and notes "our observation that our method yields sharper depth maps than KBNet continues to hold across different sparse depth densities," but does not explore densities below 50 or provide a theoretical minimum.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests down to 50 sparse points and does not establish a lower bound or analyze the theoretical requirements for the SML network to function.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing the pipeline with increasingly sparse point clouds (e.g., 25, 10, 5 points) to determine the minimum viable density and the corresponding degradation in performance metrics (iRMSE, iMAE, iAbsRel).

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed method perform in environments with very low texture or repetitive patterns where VIO struggles to track features?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that "In cases of rapid motion, poor lightning, or lack of sufficient texture, VIO will have difficulty tracking landmarks and output very few or even no points," indicating awareness of this limitation but not providing empirical results.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not include experiments in challenging environments (e.g., textureless walls, repetitive patterns) where VIO feature tracking is known to fail, leaving the method's robustness in such scenarios untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical evaluation of the pipeline in synthetic or real-world datasets with known low-texture or repetitive pattern regions, comparing performance against ground truth and baseline methods.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the impact of different scale map scaffolding interpolation methods (e.g., nearest neighbor, cubic interpolation) on the performance of the SML network?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "The region within the convex hull defined by the anchors is filled via linear interpolation of anchor values," suggesting this is a design choice that could be varied.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses linear interpolation for the scale map scaffolding and does not explore the impact of alternative interpolation methods on the SML network's ability to learn dense scale factors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using the same pipeline architecture but with different interpolation methods for the scale map scaffolding (e.g., nearest neighbor, cubic, bilinear) on the VOID and TartanAir datasets, measuring the resulting iRMSE, iMAE, and iAbsRel.

## Limitations

- The method's performance heavily depends on the quality and density of sparse metric depth from VIO, which can fail in low-texture or repetitive pattern environments
- The exact SML network architecture and training hyperparameters are not fully specified, limiting reproducibility
- Computational overhead of the dense alignment stage and its impact on real-time applications is not discussed

## Confidence

- Modular design assumption: High confidence (well-supported by experimental results)
- Global alignment mechanism: Medium confidence (mathematically sound but relies heavily on VIO quality)
- Dense scale alignment claims: Medium confidence (supported by improvements but SML details are sparse)
- Zero-shot transfer capability: Medium confidence (demonstrated but evaluation metrics differ across datasets)
