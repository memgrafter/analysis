# Adversarial Training with Generated Data in High-Dimensional Regression: An Asymptotic Study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.12582
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12582
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Two-stage adversarial training in high-dimensional regression reveals double-descent in ridgeless case, mitigated by L2 regularization; optimal ridge penalty differs between clean and adversarial stages.

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes a two-stage adversarial training framework for high-dimensional linear regression. The method first performs ridge regression on labeled data, then uses the clean model to generate pseudo-labels for adversarial training. Through asymptotic analysis, the authors demonstrate a double-descent phenomenon in ridgeless training that can be controlled with appropriate regularization. A key finding is that the optimal ridge parameter for clean training differs from the one yielding best adversarial robustness in the two-stage framework.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two stages: (1) clean ridge regression on labeled data to obtain initial estimates, and (2) adversarial training using generated data with pseudo-labels derived from the clean model. The analysis assumes high-dimensional asymptotics where both feature dimension d and sample size n grow proportionally. The authors derive closed-form expressions for the asymptotic excess adversarial risk and propose a computationally efficient shortcut cross-validation formula for hyperparameter tuning that leverages results from the clean training stage.

## Key Results
- Double-descent phenomenon observed in ridgeless two-stage adversarial training, mitigated by appropriate L2 regularization
- Optimal ridge penalty for clean training differs from the one yielding best adversarial robustness in two-stage framework
- Proposed shortcut cross-validation formula enables efficient hyperparameter tuning specific to two-stage method

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The two-stage adversarial training mitigates the double-descent phenomenon observed in ridgeless high-dimensional regression by introducing L2 regularization.
- Mechanism: In the first stage, ridge regression with a properly chosen λ shrinks the coefficient estimates, reducing variance and controlling overfitting. In the second stage, the adversarial training uses the clean ridge estimate to generate pseudo-labels, which helps the model learn robust features. The regularization in the first stage changes the bias-variance tradeoff in a way that benefits the subsequent adversarial stage, leading to improved robustness and avoiding the high-variance regime of double descent.
- Core assumption: The data follow the model X ~ N(0, I_d) and Y = X^⊤θ₀ + ε, and the ridge penalty λ is chosen appropriately for the two-stage framework (not necessarily the same as for clean training).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "While a double-descent phenomenon can be observed in ridgeless training, with an appropriate L2 regularization, the two-stage adversarial training achieves a better performance."
  - [section] "It is observed that a proper ridge penalty in the clean training stage benefits the two-stage method."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The shortcut cross-validation formula allows efficient hyperparameter tuning specifically for the two-stage adversarial training by reusing computations from the clean stage.
- Mechanism: The leave-one-out cross-validation for the two-stage method is approximated by leveraging the Kailath variant formula to express the difference between full and leave-one-out estimates in the first stage. The optimal α for the second stage is also approximated as a function of this difference. This avoids recomputing the adversarial training n₁ times, significantly reducing computational cost while maintaining accuracy.
- Core assumption: The approximation errors in the leave-one-out estimates are negligible (bounded by o(1)), and the covariance matrix is identity (Σ = I_d).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We propose adaptations to existing cross validation (CV) methods, improving the time-consuming vanilla CV approach."
  - [section] "Based on the result in Lemma 1, we can use ... to approximate α−j."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: A better clean estimate (in terms of clean testing performance) does not necessarily lead to better adversarial robustness in the two-stage framework.
- Mechanism: The optimal λ for clean ridge regression balances bias and variance for clean prediction. However, in the two-stage adversarial training, the importance of bias and variance in the error decomposition changes. Specifically, the adversarial risk depends on both the estimation error of the clean model and the variance of the pseudo-labeled data. Thus, a λ that minimizes clean risk may not minimize the final adversarial risk, and a larger λ may be preferred when the attack strength ϵ is larger.
- Core assumption: The adversarial risk function R_ϵ(θ, θ₀) has a different bias-variance tradeoff than the clean risk, and the pseudo-response generation amplifies the effect of the bias-variance tradeoff from the clean stage.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Is a better clean estimate (measured by clean testing performance) always preferred in the two-stage method?"
  - [section] "We conjecture that this discrepancy arises from the change in the error decomposition from clean training to two-stage adversarial training."

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Synthetic data generation -> Clean ridge regression (Stage 1) -> Pseudo-label generation -> Adversarial training (Stage 2) -> Risk evaluation
- Critical path:
  1. Generate synthetic data (X, Y)
  2. Run stage 1: clean ridge regression for a grid of λ
  3. For each λ, run stage 2: adversarial training with pseudo-labels
  4. Compute population adversarial risk for each λ
  5. Use shortcut CV to select λ (if applicable)
  6. Compare performance with baselines (clean, vanilla adversarial)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Ridge penalty λ: Larger λ reduces variance but increases bias; must be tuned for the two-stage method, not just clean training.
  - Number of generated samples n₂: The paper assumes n₂ = ∞ for simplicity; in practice, larger n₂ gives better approximation of the population risk but increases computation.
  - Attack strength ϵ: Larger ϵ requires larger λ in the clean stage for best adversarial robustness.

- Failure signatures:
  - Double-descent curve persists: λ is not chosen appropriately for the two-stage method.
  - Shortcut CV selects a suboptimal λ: Approximation errors are too large (e.g., due to strong correlations or small n₁).
  - Adversarial robustness does not improve over clean training: The generated data do not provide useful information, or λ is too large/small.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce the double-descent phenomenon in ridgeless regression (λ → 0) as d/n₁ varies, comparing clean, vanilla adversarial, and two-stage adversarial training.
  2. Sweep λ in clean ridge regression and plot the population adversarial risk for the two-stage method; identify the optimal λ for different attack strengths ϵ.
  3. Implement and validate the shortcut CV formula against brute-force leave-one-out CV for a small n₁; check if the selected λ matches the best λ from experiment 2.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the double-descent phenomenon in adversarial training differ from that in standard regression when varying the data dimension d relative to sample size n?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe a double-descent phenomenon in ridgeless adversarial training, similar to standard regression.
- Why unresolved: The paper only compares the two regimes qualitatively; a formal analysis of the differences in the descent curves is not provided.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical derivation of the learning curves for both cases under identical conditions, showing the shape and location of the peaks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the optimal ridge penalty λ that minimizes adversarial risk always increase monotonically with attack strength ϵ, or are there cases where it decreases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that the optimal λ increases with ϵ in simulations but do not prove this behavior generally.
- Why unresolved: The analysis relies on empirical observation; a rigorous proof is lacking.
- What evidence would resolve it: A mathematical proof or counterexample showing the relationship between λ and ϵ across all possible settings.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the shortcut cross-validation formula perform in scenarios where the labeled and unlabeled data distributions differ significantly?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The formula assumes n2 = ∞ and distribution consistency; real-world data often violate these assumptions.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not test the formula on out-of-distribution data or finite n2.
- What evidence would resolve it: Simulation or real-data experiments comparing the shortcut CV to full CV under distribution shift or finite n2.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the impact of regularization in the second-stage adversarial training on the overall robustness and generalization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that adding regularization in equation (2.2) does not help much, but do not explore why.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not analyze the role of regularization in the adversarial stage or test alternative forms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying regularization strength and type in the second stage, with analysis of bias-variance tradeoffs.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis assumes infinite generated samples (n₂ = ∞) and identity covariance (Σ = I_d), limiting practical applicability
- Shortcut cross-validation relies on approximations that may break down with highly correlated features or small sample sizes
- Analysis focuses on asymptotic regime, with limited characterization of finite-sample transition behavior

## Confidence
- High: The existence of double-descent in ridgeless high-dimensional regression and its mitigation through regularization
- Medium: The derivation of the shortcut cross-validation formula and its effectiveness in practice
- Low: The general applicability of theoretical insights to non-Gaussian data, correlated features, or other model classes
