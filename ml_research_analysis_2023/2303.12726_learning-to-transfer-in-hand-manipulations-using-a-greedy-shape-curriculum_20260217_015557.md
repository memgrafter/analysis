# Learning to Transfer In-Hand Manipulations Using a Greedy Shape Curriculum

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.12726
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12726
- Reference count: 18
- This paper presents a deep reinforcement learning approach for simulating natural in-hand object manipulation using motion capture data.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method for learning and transferring in-hand manipulation skills using a greedy shape curriculum approach. The system trains policies to control hand and object motions by tracking reference trajectories from motion capture data, then adapts these skills to different object shapes. The key innovation is a greedy curriculum search algorithm that automatically discovers effective intermediate shapes for transferring manipulation skills between objects, enabling successful adaptation from simple shapes to complex geometries like teapots, bunnies, and trains.

## Method Summary
The approach uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to train policies that track reference hand and object motions from motion capture data. Policies control hand poses through delta actions while tracking both hand and object trajectories. A greedy shape curriculum algorithm maintains a cache of policies for different shapes, periodically evaluating all policies on all shapes and selecting the highest-scoring shape-policy pair for additional training. The system uses stiff contact parameters and RK4 integration in Mujoco physics simulation to handle contact-rich manipulation tasks.

## Key Results
- Successfully adapts manipulation skills from simple shapes to complex objects including teapots, bunnies, bottles, trains, and elephants
- Achieves high success rates (>80%) on diverse manipulation tasks
- Outperforms naive linear curricula and direct training on target shapes
- Handles dynamic perturbations and shape variations effectively

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The greedy shape curriculum successfully transfers manipulation skills by selecting the most promising (shape, policy) pair for training at each iteration.
- Mechanism: At each iteration, the algorithm evaluates all cached policies on all shapes, then selects the shape-policy pair with the highest goodness score for further training. This allows the algorithm to adaptively focus on the most promising shapes while still exploring other shapes periodically.
- Core assumption: The goodness score effectively predicts which shapes will benefit most from additional training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose a simple greedy curriculum search algorithm that can successfully apply to a range of objects such as a teapot, bunny, bottle, train, and elephant"
  - [section] "We design our criteria as a combination of the rollout duration and tracking accuracy"
- Break condition: If the goodness score becomes an unreliable predictor due to distribution shift or if the algorithm gets stuck exploiting only a few shapes while neglecting others.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using the difference between simulated and reference states as observations improves training stability and convergence.
- Mechanism: By including the difference between simulated and reference poses in the observation space, the policy receives more stable and informative signals about how to adjust its actions to match the reference motion.
- Core assumption: The policy can effectively use these differences to compute appropriate control adjustments.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "using the difference between simulation and reference as observation improves both training speed and quality"
  - [section] "At each time step t, the policy outputs an action at = {∆x, ∆R, ∆q}"
- Break condition: If the reference motion contains errors or noise that would propagate through the difference calculations, potentially destabilizing training.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The combination of stiff contact parameters and RK4 integrator enables stable physics simulation for contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- Mechanism: Stiff contact parameters prevent objects from sinking into surfaces or sliding excessively, while the RK4 integrator provides accurate integration of the equations of motion at high frequency (600Hz).
- Core assumption: The chosen contact parameters and integrator settings are appropriate for the range of manipulation tasks being simulated.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "we choose Mujoco built-in contact parameters that correspond to highly stiff surfaces"
  - [section] "we choose elliptic friction cones and the RK4 integrator, and run the simulation at 600Hz"
- Break condition: If the stiff parameters cause the simulation to become numerically unstable for certain object geometries or contact configurations.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Motion capture data processing -> Physics simulation (Mujoco) -> PPO-based policy learning -> Shape morphing and curriculum management -> Evaluation and visualization

- Critical path:
  1. Load motion capture data and preprocess
  2. Set up physics simulation with appropriate contact parameters
  3. Initialize PPO policy and begin training on source shape
  4. Generate shape morphs between source and target
  5. Implement greedy curriculum search algorithm
  6. Evaluate policies and update shape-policy pairings
  7. Continue training until target shape is successfully manipulated

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Stiffer contact parameters vs. numerical stability
  - Training budget allocation between different shapes
  - Goodness score design (balance between leniency and strictness)
  - Self-collision handling (disabled during training, enabled during refinement)

- Failure signatures:
  - Policies that fail to track object motion despite high reward
  - Numerical instability in physics simulation
  - Curriculum getting stuck on difficult shapes
  - Excessive interpenetration between hand and object

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a policy to track a simple cube manipulation sequence without any shape morphing
  2. Implement and test the shape morphing pipeline on a simple source-target pair (e.g., cube to cylinder)
  3. Evaluate the greedy curriculum search algorithm on a more complex shape transfer (e.g., cube to bunny)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of reward function components and weights affect the quality and naturalness of learned manipulation motions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses reward function design including object position/orientation terms, hand tracking terms, and joint angle terms with specific weight choices (wod=4, wor=4, whd=0.05, whr=0.05, whj=0.1).
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a specific reward function configuration but doesn't systematically explore alternative reward formulations or perform ablation studies on reward weights.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying reward function components and weights, showing their impact on motion quality metrics and success rates.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the greedy shape curriculum approach be extended to discover novel manipulation strategies that differ significantly from the reference motion?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the current approach trades off exploration and exploitation and may be limiting in inventing new ways to interact with objects. It suggests adversarial motion priors as a potential mitigation.
- Why unresolved: The current greedy shape curriculum is fundamentally an exploitation strategy focused on matching reference motions, and the paper doesn't demonstrate ability to discover novel manipulation strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments showing successful learning of manipulation strategies that deviate from reference motions while still achieving task goals.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the theoretical basis for the goodness score metric used in the greedy shape curriculum, and could alternative metrics be more effective?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper describes the goodness score as the product of normalized episode length and sum of hand joint rewards, chosen because it balances tolerance to object deviation with intolerance to failure.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents the metric as empirically effective but doesn't provide theoretical justification for why this particular combination is optimal, nor does it compare against alternative metrics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis of different goodness score formulations and empirical comparison showing their relative effectiveness in guiding curriculum learning.

## Limitations
- The method assumes convex or convex-approximated object geometries, limiting applicability to highly concave objects
- Self-collision is disabled during training but enabled during refinement, creating a potential gap between training and evaluation conditions
- The greedy curriculum search relies heavily on the goodness score heuristic, which is not systematically validated across broader shape distributions

## Confidence
- High confidence: The basic PPO-based training approach with motion capture tracking (proven through multiple successful experiments)
- Medium confidence: The greedy curriculum search algorithm's ability to generalize across diverse object shapes (demonstrated on tested objects but not systematically validated across broader shape distributions)
- Medium confidence: The claim that the method enables "real-time" adaptation (technically demonstrated but practical latency requirements for real-world deployment are not specified)
