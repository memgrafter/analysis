# What's in a Name? Evaluating Assembly-Part Semantic Knowledge in Language Models through User-Provided Names in CAD Files

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14275
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14275
- Reference count: 40
- Pre-trained DistilBERT outperforms task-specific models on CAD semantic tasks using only part names

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can extract meaningful semantic knowledge about part-part and part-whole relationships from natural language names in CAD files. The authors extract and clean a corpus of part, feature, and document names from the ABC dataset, then evaluate DistilBERT on three self-supervised tasks without fine-tuning. They find that pre-trained DistilBERT outperforms several baselines, including TechNet (a model designed for mechanical engineering patents), and that fine-tuning on the CAD text corpus further improves performance. The results suggest that LLMs can leverage general semantic knowledge to understand CAD part relationships, providing motivation for multi-modal approaches that combine text with geometry.

## Method Summary
The authors extract natural language part, feature, and document names from CAD files in the ABC dataset, clean the text by removing default names and normalizing formatting, and create a deduplicated corpus. They evaluate DistilBERT on three self-supervised tasks: predicting if two parts appear in the same assembly, identifying a missing part given the rest of an assembly's parts, and predicting document names from part lists. DistilBERT embeddings are pooled and encoded using a Set Transformer to handle variable-length, unordered part lists. The model is compared against baselines including BOW, FastText, and TechNet embeddings, and fine-tuning is performed using masked language modeling on the generated corpus.

## Key Results
- Pre-trained DistilBERT outperforms TechNet (designed for mechanical patents) on all three self-supervised tasks
- Fine-tuning on the CAD text corpus significantly improves performance across all tasks
- Set Transformer architecture effectively handles variable-length, unordered part lists for encoding part-whole relationships

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pre-trained DistilBERT inherits general semantic knowledge that maps to mechanical engineering concepts.
- Mechanism: The model was pre-trained on large corpora (Wikipedia, BookCorpus) that include technical and domain-specific vocabulary, enabling it to recognize patterns in part-part and part-whole relationships even without CAD-specific fine-tuning.
- Core assumption: General language models trained on diverse text data implicitly encode mechanical engineering concepts that are transferable to CAD text tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "large language models (LLMs) contain useful domain-specific information for working with this data"
  - [section 2.1]: DistilBERT is "initially trained to mimic the outputs of the full-size version of BERT [...] while requiring much less memory"
  - [corpus]: "Average neighbor FMR=0.513" indicates moderate relevance of related work but no strong domain-specific signals
- Break condition: If the CAD text corpus contains highly specialized jargon not present in the pre-training corpora, or if part names follow non-linguistic naming conventions (e.g., opaque part numbers), the model's semantic mapping will fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning on the extracted CAD text corpus improves task performance by adapting embeddings to domain-specific vocabulary and naming conventions.
- Mechanism: The fine-tuning process updates DistilBERT's weights to better align with the distribution of CAD part names, feature names, and document names, capturing relationships unique to the mechanical design domain.
- Core assumption: Domain-specific fine-tuning can meaningfully improve performance even when the pre-trained model already has some semantic knowledge.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we show that fine-tuning on the text data corpus further boosts the performance on all tasks"
  - [section 4.4]: "To generate the corpus we use the following template string: 'An assembly with name {DOCUMENT NAME} contains the following parts: {PART 1}, ..., {PART N}.\\n'"
  - [corpus]: "Average neighbor FMR=0.513" shows moderate domain relevance but insufficient for optimal performance without adaptation
- Break condition: If the fine-tuning corpus is too small or unrepresentative of the broader CAD domain, the improvements will be minimal or overfit to the specific dataset.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Set Transformer architecture handles variable-length part lists and permutation invariance, enabling effective modeling of part-whole relationships.
- Mechanism: The Set Transformer encodes unordered sets of part embeddings into fixed-size document representations, allowing the model to reason about assemblies regardless of part order or count.
- Core assumption: Part-whole relationships can be effectively captured through set-based encoding rather than sequence modeling.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2]: "We have two key considerations to take into account when representing a whole document. First, there can be an arbitrary number of parts, and second, the parts could appear in any order."
  - [section 4.3.2]: "Due to the increased model size in this experiment, we train the set transformer on the full training set"
  - [corpus]: "Average neighbor FMR=0.513" indicates related work is relevant but not strongly domain-specific, suggesting set-based encoding is a practical choice
- Break condition: If part order contains important information not captured by set encoding, or if the set transformer cannot scale to very large part lists, performance will degrade.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Self-supervised learning via contrastive sampling
  - Why needed here: The dataset lacks explicit labels, so positive and negative pairs are generated from the structure of CAD documents to create training signals
  - Quick check question: How are positive and negative pairs created for the "Two Parts" task, and why is class balancing important?
- Concept: Masked language modeling for fine-tuning
  - Why needed here: The pre-trained model needs adaptation to CAD-specific vocabulary and naming conventions without task-specific supervision
  - Quick check question: What template is used to generate the fine-tuning corpus, and how does it expose the model to part-whole relationships?
- Concept: Set-based encoding for unordered data
  - Why needed here: CAD part lists are inherently unordered, requiring models that are invariant to permutation
  - Quick check question: Why is a Set Transformer preferred over a standard transformer for encoding document part lists?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: DistilBERT (pre-trained) → Set Transformer → MLP (for "Two Parts") OR MSE loss (for "Missing Part" and "Document Name")
- Critical path: DistilBERT embeddings → Set Transformer encoding → Task-specific prediction/evaluation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using pre-trained DistilBERT provides strong baseline performance but limits adaptation to CAD-specific patterns
  - Set Transformer handles variable-length inputs but adds computational complexity
  - Classification-based evaluation simplifies comparison but may not reflect real-world use cases
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on "Two Parts" suggests DistilBERT lacks relevant semantic knowledge
  - Degradation on "Missing Part" indicates Set Transformer cannot effectively model part-whole relationships
  - Random guessing on "Document Name" suggests both models lack understanding of assembly semantics
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate DistilBERT performance on "Two Parts" task with default hyperparameters
  2. Train Set Transformer on "Missing Part" task and evaluate on validation set
  3. Fine-tune DistilBERT on generated corpus and re-evaluate all three tasks

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How much do the semantic relationships captured by language models depend on the quality and specificity of the natural language part names provided by designers?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the varying levels of semantic content in part names, from "Clean Semantic" to "Ambiguous," and notes that 77% of parts in documents with at least one non-default name have user-provided names.
  - Why unresolved: The paper does not quantify the impact of name quality on model performance, only providing a qualitative assessment of name categories.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing model performance on datasets with varying levels of name semantic content, or a controlled study where the same parts are named differently to assess impact on model predictions.

- Question: Can the inclusion of geometric data alongside text significantly improve the performance of language models in understanding part-part and part-whole relationships?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper identifies key limitations to using LLMs with text data alone and suggests that multi-modal approaches including geometry are motivated by these limitations.
  - Why unresolved: The paper focuses solely on text data and does not explore the potential benefits of incorporating geometric information.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of model performance on the same tasks using text-only versus text-plus-geometry data, or experiments that incrementally add geometric features to text-based models.

- Question: To what extent can language models generalize learned part-part and part-whole relationships to new, unseen assemblies or domains?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper evaluates models on held-out test sets and notes improvements with fine-tuning, but does not explicitly test generalization to entirely new domains or assembly types.
  - Why unresolved: The experiments focus on performance within the ABC dataset, which may not fully capture the model's ability to generalize beyond its training distribution.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Testing models on datasets from different CAD platforms or industries, or evaluating performance on synthetic assemblies that differ significantly from the training data in terms of part types or naming conventions.

## Limitations
- The paper's findings may not generalize to CAD domains with different naming conventions or part types
- Self-supervised tasks may not fully capture real-world applications where explicit semantic relationships are needed
- Performance gains from fine-tuning may be dataset-specific and not transfer to other CAD corpora

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Pre-trained DistilBERT's baseline performance on all three tasks exceeds random and simple baseline models, indicating it captures non-trivial semantic relationships in part names
- **Medium confidence**: Fine-tuning on the CAD text corpus improves performance across all tasks, though the magnitude of improvement varies and the long-term generalizability remains untested
- **Low confidence**: The paper's claims about DistilBERT's ability to "learn" mechanical engineering concepts are overstated; the model appears to leverage general semantic knowledge rather than domain-specific understanding

## Next Checks
1. Test model performance on CAD datasets from different domains (e.g., aerospace, consumer products) to assess generalizability of semantic relationships learned from the ABC dataset
2. Conduct ablation studies removing domain-specific vocabulary from the pre-training corpora to determine how much of the performance relies on general vs. specialized semantic knowledge
3. Evaluate model performance on a real-world downstream task (e.g., part recommendation, assembly validation) rather than the self-supervised proxy tasks to validate practical utility
