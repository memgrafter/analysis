# SLABERT Talk Pretty One Day: Modeling Second Language Acquisition with BERT

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.19589
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19589
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: Transformer models exhibit both positive and negative cross-linguistic transfer effects mirroring human second language acquisition patterns

## Executive Summary
This work introduces SLABERT, a framework that models sequential language acquisition by training Transformer-based language models on Child-Directed Speech (CDS) in one language (L1) and then fine-tuning on English (L2). The authors construct a novel Multilingual Age-Ordered CHILDES (MAO-CHILDES) dataset from 5 typologically diverse languages and use the TILT transfer learning approach to simulate human-like sequential language acquisition. The study finds that language family distance predicts negative transfer, with greater typological distance from English leading to worse L2 performance. Notably, conversational Adult-Directed Speech (ADS) proves superior to CDS for training language models, and conversational data outperforms scripted speech across grammatical phenomena.

## Method Summary
The study uses BabyBERTa, a RoBERTa-base variant with 8 layers, 8 attention heads, and 256-dimensional hidden states. Models are trained on CDS data from MAO-CHILDES corpus in one language, then fine-tuned on English ADS using TILT-based transfer learning where all parameters except word embeddings are frozen. The approach creates sequential L1→L2 learning that mimics human language acquisition. Models are evaluated on the BLiMP grammar test suite across 67 paradigms and 12 grammatical phenomena, comparing performance across different L1 languages, data types (CDS vs ADS), and speech styles (conversational vs scripted).

## Key Results
- Language family distance predicts negative transfer, with greater typological distance from English correlating with worse L2 performance
- Conversational ADS outperforms scripted ADS on 9 out of 12 grammatical phenomena tested
- Models trained on conversational ADS outperform those trained on CDS, suggesting ADS may be superior training data for LMs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Cross-linguistic transfer in Transformer models mirrors human SLA patterns.
- **Mechanism:** SLABERT models sequential L1 → L2 learning using TILT-based transfer, freezing embeddings after L1 training and fine-tuning on L2 (English). Language family distance predicts negative transfer, with greater typological distance leading to worse L2 performance.
- **Core assumption:** The model's internal representations after L1 training retain structural biases that interfere with L2 learning in proportion to typological distance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "language family distance predicts more negative transfer"
  - [section] "as the typological distance between L1 and L2 increases, the performance of the model decreases"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus statistics don't directly address typological distance effects
- **Break condition:** If typological distance no longer correlates with performance drop after controlling for vocabulary overlap or if L2 performance improves despite increasing distance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Conversational speech data is superior training input for LMs compared to scripted speech data.
- **Mechanism:** Conversational ADS contains interactive alignment patterns, repetition, and dialogic syntax that make it more informative for model training than static scripted data. This is evidenced by higher BLiMP scores on conversational ADS versus scripted ADS.
- **Core assumption:** The interactive and repetitive nature of conversational speech provides better distributional cues for grammatical structure acquisition than scripted text.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "conversational speech data shows greater facilitation for language acquisition than scripted speech data"
  - [section] "conversational data yields better model performance than scripted data"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus statistics don't directly measure conversational versus scripted properties
- **Break condition:** If scripted data performance equals or exceeds conversational data when controlling for token count or diversity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** CDS performs worse than ADS on many grammatical phenomena due to its simplified structure.
- **Mechanism:** CDS's simplified syntax and morphology (short utterances, basic vocabulary) fails to provide adequate exposure to complex grammatical phenomena like long-distance dependencies, NPIs, and advanced morphology, resulting in lower BLiMP scores compared to ADS.
- **Core assumption:** The structural simplicity of CDS limits exposure to the full range of grammatical constructions needed for robust language model training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "CDS performs worse than ADS in several grammatical phenomena (9 out of 12)"
  - [section] "it is likely that such utterances do not make ideal training input for LMs to learn long-distance dependencies"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus statistics don't directly measure syntactic complexity
- **Break condition:** If CDS performance matches or exceeds ADS on all grammatical phenomena when controlling for utterance length or complexity.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Cross-linguistic transfer effects
  - **Why needed here:** Understanding how L1 properties influence L2 learning is central to interpreting SLABERT results and comparing them to human SLA research
  - **Quick check question:** What distinguishes positive transfer from negative transfer in SLA, and how might each manifest in LM performance?

- **Concept:** Typological distance
  - **Why needed here:** The paper uses language family distance as a proxy for structural dissimilarity, which predicts transfer effects in the model
  - **Quick check question:** How is typological distance typically measured, and why would it correlate with transfer difficulty in both humans and LMs?

- **Concept:** Transformer-based transfer learning
  - **Why needed here:** SLABERT uses TILT-based transfer (freeze embeddings after L1 training, fine-tune on L2), which differs from standard pre-training approaches
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between TILT transfer and standard fine-tuning, and why might this better model sequential language acquisition?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** MAO-CHILDES corpus (5 languages) → Tokenizer (BPE, 52k vocab) → BabyBERTa model → TILT transfer (freeze all but embeddings) → BLiMP evaluation
- **Critical path:**
  1. Build MAO-CHILDES corpus for 5 languages
  2. Pre-train BabyBERTa on L1 CDS data
  3. Freeze all parameters except embeddings
  4. Fine-tune on L2 conversational English ADS
  5. Evaluate on BLiMP
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Freezing all but embeddings vs. selective layer freezing (simpler but may lose transfer potential)
  - CDS vs. ADS training data (ecological validity vs. structural complexity)
  - Small model size (computational efficiency vs. capacity for complex phenomena)
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor L2 performance despite good L1 performance → insufficient transfer mechanism
  - High variance across languages → corpus quality issues or typological distance effects
  - Low scores on specific BLiMP phenomena → insufficient exposure to those constructions in training data
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Random baseline: Train model on English ADS with frozen all but embeddings to establish vocabulary-only performance
  2. Within-language comparison: Train BabyBERTa on English CDS vs. English ADS to test CDS vs. ADS effects
  3. Cross-linguistic transfer: Train on one L1 CDS language, transfer to English ADS, measure performance relative to typological distance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the relative importance of the pitch contour, repetitiveness, fluency, and rhythms of CDS versus its simplified vocabulary and grammatical structure in facilitating language acquisition for language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that CDS has distinctive qualities like pitch contours, repetitiveness, fluency, and rhythms, and also typically uses a smaller and simplified vocabulary and syntax. However, the study only uses text-based models and cannot test the effect of these prosodic properties.
- Why unresolved: The current study is limited to text-based language models and cannot incorporate the multimodal aspects of CDS, such as prosody and visual cues like joint attention.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with multimodal language models that can process both text and audio/visual information, comparing models trained on CDS with and without prosodic features.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the superiority of conversational speech data over scripted data for language model training generalize to other languages beyond English?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that conversational English ADS outperforms scripted ADS and CDS in 9 out of 12 grammatical phenomena tested. However, this comparison is only done for English.
- Why unresolved: The study only compares conversational versus scripted data for English. The effects may not generalize to other languages due to differences in language structure, available conversational data, or other factors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replicating the conversational vs. scripted data comparison for multiple languages, particularly those with different typological features from English.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the mechanism behind the negative transfer observed when L1 structures conflict with L2 acquisition in transformer-based language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that models trained on CDS in languages structurally distant from English perform worse on English grammatical tests, suggesting negative transfer. However, the specific mechanisms are not explored.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates the existence of negative transfer, it does not investigate the underlying mechanisms that cause certain L1 structures to interfere with L2 acquisition in the models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed error analysis comparing specific types of errors made by models trained on different L1s, potentially using techniques like attention visualization to identify where cross-linguistic interference occurs in the model architecture.

## Limitations

- **Statistical Power and Generalizability**: The study relies on a relatively small sample of 5 typologically diverse languages, limiting generalizability to other language families and acquisition scenarios.
- **Control Variables**: The study does not fully control for confounding factors like vocabulary overlap, orthographic similarity, and specific grammatical features that could influence transfer effects.
- **Mechanism Validation**: While demonstrating that transfer effects occur, the paper does not provide direct evidence for how specific L1 features interfere with or facilitate L2 acquisition in the models.

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims:**
- Cross-linguistic transfer effects occur in Transformer models when trained sequentially on L1 and L2 data
- Language family distance correlates with negative transfer effects in L2 performance
- Adult-Directed Speech provides better training input for LMs than Child-Directed Speech on most grammatical phenomena

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- Conversational speech data is superior to scripted speech data for LM training
- CDS's simplified structure limits exposure to complex grammatical phenomena
- The TILT transfer learning approach effectively models sequential language acquisition

## Next Checks

1. **Controlled Typological Distance Experiment**: Design a study that systematically controls for vocabulary overlap and specific grammatical features while varying typological distance, to isolate which structural properties drive transfer effects.

2. **Layer-wise Transfer Analysis**: Implement layer-specific freezing during transfer learning to determine which model components are most responsible for transfer effects, providing insight into the mechanism of cross-linguistic influence.

3. **Cross-Modal Validation**: Test whether the transfer patterns observed in the language model generalize to other sequence modeling tasks (e.g., visual reasoning, code generation) to determine if the findings reflect general transfer principles or language-specific phenomena.
