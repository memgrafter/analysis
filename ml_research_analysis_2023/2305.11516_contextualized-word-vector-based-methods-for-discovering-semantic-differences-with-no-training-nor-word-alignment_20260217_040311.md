# Contextualized Word Vector-based Methods for Discovering Semantic Differences with No Training nor Word Alignment

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a method for discovering semantic differences in words between two corpora using the norms of contextualized word vectors, without requiring training or word alignment. The core idea is that the norm of a word's mean contextualized vector indicates the coverage of its meanings in the corpus, with more diverse meanings resulting in smaller norms. The method computes the mean vector and its norm for each word type and uses these to detect semantic differences. The approach is shown to be effective for native vs non-native English corpora and historical corpora, identifying semantic differences in words including infrequent ones. The method is simple, efficient, and does not require assumptions about word correspondences or extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Method Summary
The method involves vectorizing all word instances in both corpora using BERT embeddings, computing mean vectors and their norms for each word type, and calculating coverage scores using the formula: coverage(S,T) = lT(1-lS^2) / lS(1-lT^2). Word types are then sorted by coverage scores in descending order to identify semantic differences, and typical word instances are extracted using representativeness scores. The method is robust for corpus size skew and effective even for infrequent words.

## Key Results
- The method successfully identifies semantic differences in native vs non-native English corpora and historical corpora (ICNALE and COHA data).
- It is effective for infrequent words and robust to corpus size differences.
- The approach is simple, efficient, and does not require assumptions about word correspondences or extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Norms of mean contextualized word vectors indicate semantic coverage
- Mechanism: When a word type has multiple meanings, its contextualized vectors scatter in different directions on the unit hypersphere. The mean vector's norm decreases as the dispersion increases, approaching zero when meanings are perfectly balanced opposites.
- Core assumption: Word vectors follow a von Mises-Fisher distribution on the hypersphere
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "the coverage of meanings is reflected in the norm of its mean word vector"
  - [section]: "we show that the more meanings a word type covers in a corpus, the shorter the norm of their mean word vector gets"
  - [corpus]: The method successfully identifies semantic differences in native vs non-native English corpora and historical corpora (ICNALE and COHA data)
- Break condition: If word vectors don't follow the von Mises-Fisher distribution, the theoretical grounding weakens. Also fails if vector dimensions are too low relative to vocabulary diversity.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Coverage score normalizes for corpus size differences
- Mechanism: The coverage formula c(S,T) = lT(1-lS²)/(lS(1-lT²)) compares relative meaning dispersion between corpora while adjusting for their absolute norms, making comparisons robust to size skew.
- Core assumption: Larger corpora tend to capture more meanings, but this doesn't invalidate the relative comparison
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "They are (i) robust for the skew in corpus size"
  - [section]: "they are effective even for corpus pairs whose sizes are skewed"
  - [corpus]: Works between ICNALE corpora with 10K vs 100K words and COHA with 68M vs 111M words
- Break condition: If one corpus is extremely small with only a few occurrences of a word, the norm may be unstable despite the normalization.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Representativeness score identifies specific word instances with missing meanings
- Mechanism: The score r(x,S,T) compares each word instance's vector to both mean vectors, weighting by the concentration parameters to find instances that align with source corpus meanings but not target corpus meanings.
- Core assumption: Word instances with vectors near the source mean but far from target mean represent the "missing" meaning
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "effective in pinpointing word instances that have a meaning missing in one of the two corpora"
  - [section]: "word instances (or their word vectors) we are looking for now are those that are distant from the mean word vector for the target corpus"
  - [corpus]: Identifies specific examples like "near impossible" meaning "almost" in native corpus but absent in non-native corpus
- Break condition: If the missing meaning is represented by only one instance in the source corpus, the method may miss it due to insufficient signal.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input corpora -> BERT tokenizer -> BERT model -> contextualized vectors -> mean vector calculation -> norm computation -> coverage/representativeness scoring -> sorted output
- Critical path: BERT vectorization -> mean vector calculation -> norm computation -> scoring
- Design tradeoffs: Simple and efficient (no training) vs. potentially missing subtle semantic shifts that require deeper analysis
- Failure signatures:
  - Low precision: Too many false positives in semantic difference detection
  - Low recall: Missing actual semantic differences, especially in infrequent words
  - Instability: Norm calculations vary significantly with small corpus size changes
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run on small synthetic corpora with known semantic differences (e.g., "bank" financial vs river meanings)
  2. Compare results on same corpus split into two parts to establish baseline
  3. Test on corpora with known transcription errors to verify error detection capability

## Limitations
- The theoretical foundation relies heavily on the von Mises-Fisher distribution assumption for word vectors, which while plausible, is not empirically validated in this work.
- The method's effectiveness for infrequent words (with few occurrences) remains somewhat uncertain, as norm calculations may be unstable with limited data.
- The approach assumes that semantic differences manifest as dispersion in vector space, which may not capture all types of semantic shifts (such as gradual meaning evolution or domain-specific usage).

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism linking mean vector norms to semantic coverage is theoretically sound and demonstrated on real corpora (ICNALE, COHA). The coverage score formula and its robustness to corpus size differences are well-established.
- **Medium Confidence**: The method's effectiveness for infrequent words and its ability to pinpoint specific missing meanings in word instances, while demonstrated, could benefit from more extensive validation across diverse corpora and word types.
- **Low Confidence**: The theoretical assumption that word vectors strictly follow von Mises-Fisher distribution on the hypersphere has not been empirically verified, which could affect the reliability of the norm-based approach in edge cases.

## Next Checks

1. **Synthetic Test Suite**: Create controlled synthetic corpora with known semantic differences (e.g., artificially splitting word meanings) to systematically test detection accuracy and precision-recall tradeoffs.
2. **Distribution Validation**: Empirically verify whether contextualized word vectors from BERT actually follow von Mises-Fisher distribution by fitting the model and checking concentration parameters across different word types.
3. **Frequency Analysis**: Conduct a detailed study of method performance across word frequency ranges, particularly focusing on words with 2-10 occurrences to quantify stability and identify failure thresholds.
