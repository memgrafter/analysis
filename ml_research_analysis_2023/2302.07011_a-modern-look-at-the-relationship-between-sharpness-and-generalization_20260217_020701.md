# A Modern Look at the Relationship between Sharpness and Generalization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.07011
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07011
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: Sharpness does not correlate well with generalization in modern deep learning setups, instead correlating with training parameters like learning rate.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the relationship between reparametrization-invariant sharpness measures and generalization in modern deep learning architectures, particularly focusing on transformers. The authors evaluate multiple sharpness definitions across diverse scenarios including training from scratch on ImageNet and CIFAR-10 with transformers and ConvNets, and fine-tuning CLIP and BERT models. Their findings reveal that sharpness does not correlate well with generalization but rather with training parameters like the learning rate, which can be positively or negatively correlated with generalization depending on the setup. Interestingly, they observe a consistent negative correlation between sharpness and out-of-distribution error in multiple cases, suggesting that sharper minima can generalize better.

## Method Summary
The authors study sharpness-generalization relationships using pre-trained models from multiple sources: ViT models trained from scratch on ImageNet-1k, CLIP models fine-tuned on ImageNet, BERT models fine-tuned on MNLI, and ResNet-18/ViT models trained from scratch on CIFAR-10. They compute multiple sharpness definitions including ℓ2/ℓ∞, average/worst-case, adaptive/non-adaptive, with/without logit normalization. Sharpness is evaluated using Auto-PGD for worst-case sharpness (20 iterations) and Monte Carlo sampling for average-case sharpness (100 perturbations). Kendall rank correlation coefficients are computed between sharpness values and test errors across models, with subgroup analysis by training hyperparameters.

## Key Results
- Sharpness does not correlate well with generalization but correlates strongly with learning rate across multiple architectures
- Adaptive sharpness shows inconsistent correlations with generalization, even within restricted settings
- Sharper minima consistently show better out-of-distribution generalization across multiple benchmarks
- The right sharpness measure is highly data-dependent and not well understood for realistic data distributions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adaptive sharpness fails to correlate with generalization because the right measure depends heavily on data distribution and architecture.
- Mechanism: Adaptive sharpness aims for reparametrization invariance by scaling with parameter magnitudes, but this scaling interacts differently with various architectures and data distributions.
- Core assumption: Loss surface geometry relevant for generalization varies across architectures and datasets.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "sharpness does not correlate well with generalization but rather with some training parameters like the learning rate"; [section] "the right sharpness measure is highly data-dependent"
- Break condition: If a sharpness measure shows consistent correlation with generalization across architectures and data distributions.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sharpness captures learning rate more reliably than generalization, especially within subgroups.
- Mechanism: Adaptive sharpness scales with parameter perturbation magnitudes influenced by learning rate, dominating variance within hyperparameter subgroups.
- Core assumption: Learning rate is a dominant factor in determining effective parameter perturbation scale.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "sharpness does not correlate well with generalization but rather with some training parameters like the learning rate"; [section] "significant negative correlation within each subgroup defined by the same values of augment×mixup"
- Break condition: If sharpness correlates with generalization within subgroups after controlling for learning rate.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Worst-case adaptive sharpness correlates negatively with OOD error, suggesting sharper minima generalize better.
- Mechanism: Lower learning rates preserve original features better for OOD tasks, and adaptive sharpness increases with lower learning rates.
- Core assumption: Lower learning rates preserve original model features that are more robust to distribution shifts.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "consistent negative correlation of sharpness with out-of-distribution error implying that sharper minima can generalize better"; [section] "models trained with higher weight decay yield lower test error fairly consistently"
- Break condition: If experiments consistently show positive correlation between sharpness and OOD error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Under what specific conditions, if any, does adaptive sharpness correlate positively with generalization in modern deep learning settings?
- Basis: The paper systematically tests adaptive sharpness across multiple scenarios finding no consistent positive correlation
- Why unresolved: Mixed results with only subgroup correlations in restricted settings
- What would resolve it: Experiments varying architectural choices, dataset sizes, optimization algorithms, and regularization techniques

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the fundamental relationship between sharpness and out-of-distribution generalization?
- Basis: Authors observe consistent negative correlation between sharpness and OOD error
- Why unresolved: Provides empirical observations but no underlying mechanisms or theoretical justification
- What would resolve it: Theoretical analysis and controlled experiments identifying whether this relationship stems from data distribution properties, optimization dynamics, or other factors

### Open Question 3
- Question: How should the right sharpness measure be selected for realistic data distributions?
- Basis: Paper demonstrates the right sharpness measure is highly data-dependent and not well understood
- Why unresolved: Shows different definitions capture different trends but provides no general framework for selection
- What would resolve it: Systematic study mapping data distribution characteristics to appropriate sharpness measures, or adaptive methods that automatically select the right measure

## Limitations
- The study focuses on models achieving near-zero training error, potentially missing phenomena with larger generalization gaps
- Adaptive sharpness measures evaluated may not be the "right" measures for modern architectures
- Interaction between learning rate, sharpness, and generalization across different architectures and data regimes remains incompletely characterized

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Sharpness correlates with training hyperparameters (particularly learning rate) across multiple architectures and datasets
- **Medium confidence**: Sharper minima can generalize better for out-of-distribution tasks, based on consistent negative correlations
- **Low confidence**: The broader conclusion that sharpness fundamentally cannot predict generalization
