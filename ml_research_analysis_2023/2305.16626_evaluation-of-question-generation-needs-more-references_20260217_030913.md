# Evaluation of Question Generation Needs More References

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16626
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16626
- Reference count: 40
- Single-reference question generation evaluation has limitations in capturing question diversity

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the fundamental limitation in question generation evaluation where single-reference metrics like BLEU and ROUGE fail to capture the inherent diversity of possible questions that can be generated from the same context. The authors propose a multi-reference evaluation (MRE) method that uses large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-3 and ChatGPT to generate multiple paraphrased versions of reference questions. The key insight is that by evaluating generated questions against multiple reference variants and taking the maximum similarity score, the evaluation becomes more robust to the natural variability in question phrasing while maintaining computational simplicity.

The method is evaluated on a quiz design dataset using correlation with human scores as the primary metric. Results demonstrate that MRE consistently improves correlation with human judgments across multiple evaluation metrics compared to traditional single-reference evaluation (SRE). The approach is particularly effective when using ChatGPT for paraphrase generation, showing that the quality of the LLM model matters for the success of the multi-reference framework. The paper presents MRE as a simple yet effective enhancement that can be applied to improve existing QG evaluation metrics without requiring fundamental changes to evaluation infrastructure.

## Method Summary
The multi-reference evaluation (MRE) method works by first generating multiple paraphrased versions of the reference question using large language models like GPT-3 or ChatGPT. For each reference question, the LLM generates K different paraphrases that maintain the same semantic meaning but use different wording or structure. When evaluating a generated question, instead of comparing it to just one reference, the system computes similarity scores (BLEU, ROUGE, etc.) between the generated question and all K paraphrased references, then takes the maximum score as the final evaluation metric. This approach captures the idea that there are multiple valid ways to phrase a question while ensuring that at least one reference variant will match the style of the generated question well. The method is computationally efficient since it reuses existing single-reference metrics while only adding the LLM paraphrase generation step.

## Key Results
- MRE improves correlation with human scores across multiple metrics compared to single-reference evaluation
- ChatGPT outperforms GPT-3 in generating paraphrases that enhance MRE effectiveness
- The improvement is consistent across different evaluation metrics including BLEU and ROUGE variants
- The method is simple to implement and can be applied to existing QG evaluation pipelines

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of MRE stems from addressing the fundamental mismatch between evaluation metrics and the generative nature of question generation. Traditional evaluation assumes a one-to-one mapping between reference and generated questions, but in reality, many different questions can be valid answers to the same context. By using multiple paraphrased references, MRE creates a coverage set that better represents the space of acceptable questions. The maximum operator ensures that the evaluation rewards generated questions that match any valid reference variant, reducing the penalty for legitimate stylistic variations. This approach effectively smooths the evaluation landscape and makes it more aligned with human judgment, which naturally considers multiple valid ways to ask the same question.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the impact of different LLM sizes and architectures on the quality and diversity of generated paraphrases for MRE?
- Basis in paper: The paper uses GPT-3 and ChatGPT for paraphrasing, but does not explore the impact of using different LLM sizes or architectures.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on using GPT-3 and ChatGPT, but does not investigate how other LLM models or architectures might affect the quality of paraphrases and, consequently, the effectiveness of MRE.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different LLM sizes and architectures, comparing their performance in generating paraphrases, and analyzing the impact on MRE's correlation with human scores.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of MRE vary across different question types and domains?
- Basis in paper: The paper evaluates MRE on a quiz design dataset, but does not explore its performance on different question types or domains.
- Why unresolved: The effectiveness of MRE might depend on the nature of the questions and the domain in which they are used. It is unclear how MRE would perform on different question types or in other domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating MRE on datasets with diverse question types and domains, comparing its performance across these datasets, and analyzing the factors that influence its effectiveness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can MRE be extended to evaluate other natural language generation tasks beyond question generation?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on applying MRE to question generation, but does not explore its potential for other NLG tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of MRE for question generation, but it is unclear whether the same approach can be applied to other NLG tasks, such as machine translation or text summarization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Investigating the applicability of MRE to other NLG tasks, adapting the method for these tasks, and evaluating its performance in improving the evaluation of generated outputs.

## Limitations

- Evaluation is limited to a single quiz design dataset, raising concerns about generalizability to other QG tasks
- The method relies on LLM-generated paraphrases which may introduce biases toward certain question structures or linguistic patterns
- Computational overhead increases with the number of paraphrases generated, potentially limiting scalability
- The quality of MRE is heavily dependent on the quality of the LLM model used for paraphrase generation

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The core methodology of using multiple references for evaluation is sound and technically feasible. The observation that single-reference evaluation has limitations in capturing question diversity is well-established.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that MRE improves correlation with human scores needs more validation. While results are promising, they are based on a single dataset and specific evaluation metrics.
- **Low confidence**: The paper's assertion that this method can "improve various existing QG evaluation metrics" is premature without broader empirical validation across multiple metrics and datasets.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-domain validation**: Apply MRE to at least three different QG datasets (e.g., SQuAD, MS MARCO, and commonsense QA) to test generalizability across domains with varying question types and answer sources.

2. **Bias analysis**: Systematically analyze whether LLM-generated reference questions introduce systematic biases toward certain question structures, topics, or linguistic patterns compared to human-written references.

3. **Alternative reference generation methods**: Compare MRE performance when using different reference generation approaches (e.g., human-generated paraphrases, template-based paraphrasing, or back-translation) to isolate the contribution of LLM-generated references versus the multi-reference framework itself.
