# A study of conceptual language similarity: comparison and evaluation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.13401
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13401
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: Conceptual similarity achieves 87% accuracy for Indo-European family but is weaker than lexical and typological measures overall

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates conceptual language similarity, a novel approach to measuring linguistic relatedness based on how languages represent basic concepts. The method uses a two-step pipeline to align basic concepts across 1335 languages using the Parallel Bible Corpus. For evaluation, the authors perform binary classification of language families using nearest neighbor prediction with cosine similarity, Hamming distance, and comparison against lexical (ASJP) and typological (URIEL, Grambank) similarity measures. Results show that conceptual similarity achieves good accuracy for some families (e.g., Indo-European at 87%) but is weaker than lexical and typological similarities overall. Analysis reveals that the source language (English) biases results toward Indo-European languages. The authors conclude that while conceptual similarity is weaker than existing measures for family classification, it offers valuable insights for high-level cross-linguistic comparison beyond genealogical boundaries.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a binary classification approach to compare conceptual similarity against lexical and typological measures. The conceptualizer builds language vectors from 83 basic concepts (32 Swadesh + 51 Bible concepts) aligned across 1335 languages using the Parallel Bible Corpus. Classification accuracy is measured using k-nearest neighbors (k=2,4,6,8,10) with cosine similarity and Hamming distance metrics. These results are compared against ASJP lexical distances for 1012 languages and URIEL/Grambank typological features for six major language families (ATLA, AUST, INDO, GUIN, OTOM, SINO).

## Key Results
- Conceptual similarity achieves highest accuracy for Indo-European family (87%) but lower for other families
- Lexical similarity based on ASJP lists outperforms conceptual similarity with a large margin
- Typological features (URIEL, Grambank) show complementary performance to conceptual similarity
- Source language bias toward Indo-European languages is confirmed through analysis of nearest neighbor distributions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Conceptual similarity uses language vectors to measure relatedness by comparing how basic concepts are represented across languages.
- Mechanism: The system builds conceptual vectors for each language based on concept alignments from a parallel Bible corpus, then uses cosine similarity or Hamming distance to compare these vectors.
- Core assumption: Conceptual representations derived from parallel text alignments reflect genuine linguistic conceptualization patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Conceptual language similarity is introduced in our previous work (Liu et al, 2023), in which we propose the Conceptualizer, which uses a two-step pipeline to align basic concepts across 1335 languages with the help of a superparallel dataset: the Parallel Bible Corpus (PBC) (Mayer and Cysouw, 2014)."
  - [section 3] "With a list of 83 concepts in total (32 from the Swadesh-100 list (Swadesh, 2017) and 51 derived from the Bible) and using English as the source language, we implement concept alignment using a directed bipartite graph with two steps: a forward pass and a backward pass."
- Break condition: If the concept alignment pipeline fails to capture meaningful conceptual differences between languages, the similarity measures become meaningless.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Language family classification accuracy using conceptual similarity is lower than using lexical or typological features.
- Mechanism: The system evaluates classification by predicting whether a language's k nearest neighbors belong to the same family, finding that conceptual similarity achieves good results for some families but is weaker overall compared to ASJP lexical distances and URIEL typological features.
- Core assumption: Genealogical relatedness correlates with conceptual similarity in a way that can be captured through nearest-neighbor classification.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Results show that conceptual similarity achieves good accuracy for some families (e.g., Indo-European at 87%) but is weaker than lexical and typological similarities overall."
  - [section 4] "We show that conceptual similarity is weaker than but complementary to similarities based on lexical and typological features."
- Break condition: If the assumption that genealogical proximity should be reflected in conceptual similarity doesn't hold, classification accuracy will remain low regardless of method improvements.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The source language (English) biases conceptual similarity results toward Indo-European languages.
- Mechanism: The conceptualizer uses English as the source language, making associations to English concepts more easily retrieved during the backward pass, which amplifies Indo-European language similarity in the results.
- Core assumption: Using a single source language creates systematic bias in cross-linguistic concept alignment.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Analysis reveals that the source language (English) biases results toward Indo-European languages."
  - [section 4] "INDO has the highest accuracy, which can be explained since the Conceptualizer uses English as its source language, associations to the English (thus INDO) concepts are more easily retrieved during the backward pass."
- Break condition: If using a different source language or multilingual approach reduces the Indo-European bias, this mechanism would be less relevant.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the source language selection impact conceptual similarity results across different language families?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "INDO has the highest accuracy, which can be explained since the Conceptualizer uses English as its source language" and suggest evaluating "whether and how the source language influences the results."
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses English as the source language and does not test alternative source languages to compare results.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing conceptual similarity accuracy using different source languages (e.g., Mandarin, Spanish, Arabic) across the same language families.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific linguistic features contribute to the strong performance of lexical similarity (ASJP) compared to conceptual similarity?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors observe that "lexical similarity based on ASJP lists outperforms conceptual similarity with a large margin" but do not analyze which linguistic features drive this difference.
- Why unresolved: The paper compares overall performance but does not conduct feature-level analysis to identify which linguistic properties (phonological, morphological, lexical) make ASJP more effective.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed feature ablation studies showing which ASJP features (e.g., phoneme inventory, consonant-vowel patterns) most strongly correlate with genealogical classification accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the abstractness level of concepts affect conceptual similarity accuracy across different language families?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "the difference in accuracy is sometimes large between Swadesh and Bible concepts, especially for INDO and OTOM languages, an indication that the abstractness of Bible concepts contributes to variable results."
- Why unresolved: The paper observes variability but does not systematically investigate how concept abstractness levels impact similarity measurements across families.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying concept abstractness levels (concrete to abstract) and measuring their differential impact on conceptual similarity accuracy across multiple language families.

## Limitations

- Source language bias toward Indo-European languages due to English-based concept alignment
- Limited coverage of conceptual domains in Bible-based corpus may not represent all linguistic concepts
- Inconsistent feature coverage across language families in Grambank affects classification accuracy
- Evaluation constrained to six major language families, limiting generalizability
