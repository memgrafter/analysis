# Multivariate Systemic Risk Measures and Computation by Deep Learning Algorithms

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.10183
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10183
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: Deep learning algorithms effectively approximate optimal risk allocations, overall risk measures, and Radon-Nikodym derivatives for multivariate systemic risk measures with 0.92% to 5.80% relative approximation error.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes deep learning algorithms for computing systemic risk measures based on multivariate utility functions. The authors formulate the problem as both a primal minimization problem and its dual representation, then use neural networks to approximate the primal optimizer and the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the dual optimizer. The algorithms are tested on paired exponential utility functions with known explicit formulas and on general cases without explicit solutions, demonstrating good approximation accuracy and convergence properties.

## Method Summary
The method involves using neural networks to solve two related optimization problems: a primal minimization problem that finds optimal cash allocations for financial institutions, and a dual representation that characterizes the systemic risk measure through a pricing measure. Algorithm 1 approximates the primal optimizer by minimizing a loss function that includes variance penalties and acceptance set constraints. Algorithm 2 uses a generator-discriminator setup to estimate the Radon-Nikodym derivative and verify dual constraints. Both algorithms are trained simultaneously using stochastic gradient descent on Monte Carlo samples of risk factors.

## Key Results
- Deep learning algorithms achieve 0.92% to 5.80% relative approximation error in computing systemic risk measures
- The algorithms successfully approximate optimal risk allocations and Radon-Nikodym derivatives for both cases with and without explicit formulas
- Theoretical fairness properties of risk allocations are preserved under neural network approximation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The deep learning algorithms approximate the primal optimizer and dual Radon-Nikodym derivative with bounded error because the neural network parameter space is expressive enough to represent the true optimizers. The primal algorithm minimizes a loss function that includes a penalty for variance of total cash allocation and a penalty for falling outside the acceptance set. The dual algorithm uses a generator-discriminator setup (similar to GANs) where one network estimates the Radon-Nikodym derivative and another generates test scenarios to verify the dual constraints. Both networks are trained simultaneously using stochastic gradient descent. The core assumption is that the true optimizers lie within the function space representable by the neural networks, and the loss function is well-behaved enough for gradient-based optimization to converge to a good approximation.

### Mechanism 2
The fairness properties of the risk allocations are preserved under the neural network approximation because the algorithms are designed to satisfy the same optimality conditions as the theoretical solution. The primal algorithm enforces that the componentwise sums of allocations are deterministic through the variance penalty term. The dual algorithm ensures that the estimated Radon-Nikodym derivative leads to risk allocations that satisfy the full allocation property (sum of individual risk contributions equals total systemic risk). Both algorithms optimize over the same constraint sets as the theoretical problems. The core assumption is that the neural network approximations satisfy the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions of the original optimization problems when trained to convergence.

### Mechanism 3
The algorithm can handle cases where explicit formulas are not available because it learns the solution directly from data rather than relying on analytical expressions. The neural networks learn to map risk factors X to optimal allocations Y through supervised learning on simulated scenarios. The dual network learns the Radon-Nikodym derivative that transforms the physical measure to the optimal pricing measure. Both networks are trained end-to-end using Monte Carlo samples of the risk factors. The core assumption is that the relationship between risk factors and optimal allocations can be learned from sufficient training data, and the dual measure can be represented as a function of the risk factors.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Primal network (ϕ) -> Maps risk factors X to cash allocations Y
  - Dual network (Θ) -> Estimates Radon-Nikodym derivative dQ/dP
  - Dual network (Ψ) -> Generates test scenarios Z for evaluating dual constraints
  - Loss functions: Jprimal for primal problem, Jdual for dual problem
  - Training loop: Simultaneous training of all networks using SGD

- Critical path:
  1. Generate Monte Carlo samples of risk factors X
  2. Forward pass through primal network to get allocations Y
  3. Compute primal loss including variance penalty and acceptance set penalty
  4. Forward pass through dual networks to get dQ/dP and test scenarios Z
  5. Compute dual loss including entropy and constraint penalties
  6. Backpropagate gradients and update all network parameters
  7. Repeat until convergence

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Network architecture: Fully connected vs. specialized architectures for financial data
  - Penalty hyperparameters: Trade-off between constraint satisfaction and optimization accuracy
  - Training data size: Balance between computational cost and approximation quality
  - Learning rates: Different rates for primal and dual networks to ensure stable training

- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in total cash allocation indicates primal network not satisfying deterministic sum constraint
  - Poor approximation of Radon-Nikodym derivative suggests dual network architecture insufficient
  - Instability in training suggests learning rates too high or loss landscape too complex
  - Biased risk allocations suggest systematic error in network representation

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify the algorithm can reproduce known explicit solutions for paired exponential utility with Gaussian risk factors
  2. Test the algorithm on multivariate utility functions without explicit solutions using the same Gaussian risk factors
  3. Validate the algorithm on different risk factor distributions (e.g., beta distribution) while keeping the utility function fixed

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Can the deep learning algorithms be extended to handle systemic risk measures with random allocations that are not necessarily deterministic in their componentwise sums? The paper focuses on random allocations where componentwise sums are (a.s.) deterministic, but mentions the possibility of scenario-dependent allocations. This remains unresolved because the current algorithms are designed for allocations with deterministic componentwise sums, and extending them to handle more general random allocations would require significant modifications to the theoretical framework and algorithmic implementation. Evidence that would resolve it includes developing and testing algorithms for systemic risk measures with random allocations that do not have deterministic componentwise sums, and comparing their performance to the current methods.

### Open Question 2
How does the choice of multivariate utility function impact the fairness properties of the resulting risk allocations? The paper discusses fairness properties of primal optima and associated risk allocations, but does not explore how different multivariate utility functions might affect these properties. This remains unresolved because the fairness properties of risk allocations are influenced by the specific choice of multivariate utility function, and it is not clear how different choices might impact these properties. Evidence that would resolve it includes conducting a comparative analysis of fairness properties for various multivariate utility functions, and identifying which properties are most sensitive to the choice of utility function.

### Open Question 3
Can the deep learning algorithms be adapted to handle systemic risk measures with non-convex feasible allocation sets? The paper assumes convex feasible allocation sets, but does not explore the possibility of non-convex sets. This remains unresolved because non-convex feasible allocation sets may arise in practical applications, and it is not clear how the current algorithms would perform in such cases. Evidence that would resolve it includes developing and testing algorithms for systemic risk measures with non-convex feasible allocation sets, and comparing their performance to the current methods.
