# Detecting Backdoors in Pre-trained Encoders

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.15180
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15180
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: DECREE achieves 99.1% average detection accuracy across 444 encoders with 6-30x efficiency gains

## Executive Summary
DECREE presents the first backdoor detection approach for pre-trained encoders in self-supervised learning (SSL), addressing a critical gap in current security research. Unlike existing backdoor scanners designed for supervised learning, DECREE directly scans encoders without requiring downstream classifiers or input labels. The method exploits a fundamental property of backdoored encoders: they produce highly similar embeddings for inputs stamped with triggers, while clean encoders do not. By inverting triggers to find minimal perturbations that induce similar embeddings and using a normalized trigger size metric (PL1-Norm), DECREE achieves near-perfect detection accuracy while running significantly faster than baseline approaches.

## Method Summary
DECREE uses self-supervised trigger inversion to detect backdoors in pre-trained encoders. The method samples a shadow dataset, optimizes a trigger pattern to maximize cosine similarity of embeddings for trigger-stamped inputs while minimizing trigger size, then computes the PL1-Norm (normalized L1 norm) to distinguish backdoored from clean encoders. The approach requires no labels or downstream classifiers and works by identifying the dense embedding regions created by backdoor attacks. DECREE was evaluated on 444 encoders across three SSL attack paradigms (Image-on-Image, Image-on-Pair, Text-on-Pair) using datasets including CIFAR10, ImageNet, and CLIP, achieving 99.1% average detection accuracy with a PL1-Norm threshold of 0.1.

## Key Results
- Achieves near 100% detection accuracy in most cases, with 99.1% average accuracy across 444 encoders
- Runs 6-30 times faster than baseline approaches while maintaining high accuracy
- Remains effective even with limited or no access to the pre-training dataset
- Successfully detects backdoors across three SSL attack paradigms and multiple datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Backdoored encoders produce highly similar embeddings for inputs stamped with triggers, while clean encoders do not.
- **Mechanism**: The backdoor attack induces the encoder to map trigger-stamped inputs and the attack target to the same region in embedding space, creating a "dense area" where embeddings are highly similar.
- **Core assumption**: The attack target is consistently mapped to the same dense region across different trigger-stamped inputs.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "backdoored encoders produce highly similar embeddings for inputs stamped with triggers, while clean encoders do not"
  - [section]: "In contrast, as the backdoor attack forces the samples with trigger to be close to the attack target, it creates a dense area (shown in Figure 2d) in a backdoored encoder where embeddings share a high similarity (0.9904)"
- **Break condition**: If the attack target is not consistently mapped to the same dense region, or if the trigger induces varied embeddings.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Self-supervised trigger inversion can find a minimal trigger pattern that induces similar embeddings for any inputs stamped with it.
- **Mechanism**: DECREE optimizes a trigger pattern to maximize the cosine similarity of embeddings for trigger-stamped inputs while minimizing the trigger size, effectively searching for the dense area in embedding space.
- **Core assumption**: The dense area in embedding space can be approximated by maximizing the similarity of embeddings for trigger-stamped inputs.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: "DECREE samples a batch of inputs to stabilize the search process. The average of pair-wise similarity within a batch is computed... L is used as the constraint during optimization, assuring that the samples stamped with the optimized trigger are in the dense area in embedding space"
- **Break condition**: If the dense area cannot be approximated by maximizing the similarity of embeddings for trigger-stamped inputs.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The normalized trigger size (PL1-Norm) can distinguish backdoored from clean encoders.
- **Mechanism**: DECREE uses the ratio of the inverted trigger's L1 norm to the maximum L1 norm of the encoder's input space to quantify the distance from clean samples to the dense area.
- **Core assumption**: Backdoored encoders require smaller perturbations to induce highly similar embeddings, resulting in a smaller PL1-Norm.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: "PL n(E), denoting the Proportionate-Ln Norm of an encoder E, is thus defined as the ratio of the inverted trigger's Ln norm to the maximum Ln norm of the encoder's input space"
  - [section]: "As discussed in Section 4.1, triggers inverted from backdoored encoders shall be smaller than those from clean encoders"
- **Break condition**: If the PL1-Norm cannot effectively quantify the distance from clean samples to the dense area, or if clean and backdoored encoders have similar PL1-Norm values.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Self-supervised trigger inversion -> PL1-Norm calculation -> Threshold comparison for backdoor detection
- **Critical path**: (1) Self-supervised trigger inversion to find minimal trigger pattern, (2) computing the PL1-Norm for the inverted trigger, (3) comparing the PL1-Norm to a threshold to determine if the encoder is backdoored
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Tradeoff between detection accuracy and false positives: A lower threshold for PL1-Norm may increase detection accuracy but also increase false positives.
  - Tradeoff between efficiency and robustness: Using a smaller shadow dataset for trigger inversion may improve efficiency but reduce robustness against adaptive attacks.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low detection accuracy: May indicate that the PL1-Norm is not effectively distinguishing between clean and backdoored encoders.
  - High false positive rate: May suggest that the threshold for PL1-Norm is too low, causing clean encoders to be misclassified as backdoored.
  - Poor efficiency: May result from an overly complex trigger inversion process or an excessively large shadow dataset.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Evaluate DECREE on a small set of known clean and backdoored encoders to verify that the PL1-Norm effectively distinguishes between them.
  2. Test the impact of different shadow dataset sizes on the efficiency and robustness of DECREE.
  3. Assess the effectiveness of DECREE against adaptive attacks by evaluating its performance on encoders trojaned with various levels of cosine similarity loss during the attack.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can DECREE be extended to detect backdoors in text-format triggers, which involve discrete inputs rather than continuous pixel values?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that DECREE focuses on patch-like triggers in computer vision and does not handle text-format triggers due to the discrete nature of NLP inputs.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide any methodology or experiments for adapting DECREE to text-based triggers, and the discrete nature of text presents unique challenges compared to continuous pixel values.
- What evidence would resolve it: A demonstration of DECREE's effectiveness on text-format triggers or a clear methodology for adapting the technique to discrete inputs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does DECREE perform against advanced backdoor attacks like dynamic triggers or invisible triggers that are not covered in the current threat model?

- Why unresolved: The paper only tests a limited set of advanced attacks and does not explore the full space of possible backdoor attack techniques that could be adapted to SSL.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensive testing of DECREE against a wide range of advanced backdoor attack techniques, including those not yet developed.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of using different shadow datasets (datasets not related to the pre-training dataset) on DECREE's detection performance?

- Why unresolved: The paper only uses CIFAR10 as a shadow dataset for some experiments and does not systematically evaluate the effect of dataset choice on detection performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using multiple shadow datasets with different distributions to measure the sensitivity of DECREE's performance to dataset choice.

### Open Question 4

- What evidence would resolve it: Testing DECREE on state-of-the-art large-scale vision models to verify scalability and effectiveness.

- What evidence would resolve it: A demonstration of DECREE's effectiveness on multi-modal backdoor detection or a clear methodology for extending the technique to multi-modal systems.

## Limitations
- The detection mechanism depends on the premise that backdoor attacks consistently create dense embedding regions, which may not hold for all attack variations
- The PL1-Norm threshold of 0.1 was empirically determined and may not generalize optimally to all encoder architectures or attack variations
- The method focuses on patch-like triggers in computer vision and does not handle text-format triggers due to discrete input challenges

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Detection accuracy claims (99.1% average across 444 encoders) - supported by comprehensive evaluation across multiple datasets and attack paradigms
- **Medium Confidence**: Efficiency claims (6-30x faster than baselines) - based on reported runtime comparisons, though implementation details of baselines could affect reproducibility
- **Medium Confidence**: Robustness claims under limited/no dataset access - demonstrated empirically but theoretical bounds on performance degradation are not established
