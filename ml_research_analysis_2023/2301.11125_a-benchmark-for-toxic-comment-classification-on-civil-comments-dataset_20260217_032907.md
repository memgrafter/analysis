# A benchmark for toxic comment classification on Civil Comments dataset

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.11125
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11125
- Reference count: 3
- This paper presents a comprehensive benchmark comparing multiple transformer-based and CNN models on a highly skewed multi-label hate speech dataset from Civil Comments

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive benchmark comparing multiple transformer-based and CNN models on a highly skewed multi-label hate speech dataset from Civil Comments. The study evaluates model performance, bias, and inference time using metrics like AUROC, F1, precision, recall, and bias-aware metrics such as Subgroup AUC, BPSN AUC, and BNSP AUC. Results show that all BERT models have similar performance regardless of size or optimization, while RNNs offer faster inference times. RoBERTa with Focal Loss provides the best bias performance, whereas DistilBERT achieves a balance between good AUROC and low inference time. All models exhibit bias in associating identities with toxicity, with BERT, RNN, and XLNet being less sensitive to this bias compared to CNNs and CCTs.

## Method Summary
The paper evaluates 8 different transformer-based models (BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT, XLNet, CCT) and traditional models (CNN, RNN) on the Civil Comments dataset. Models are trained with standard and Focal Loss optimization, with some models freezing the embedding layer. Performance is measured using AUROC, F1, precision, recall, and bias metrics including Subgroup AUC, BPSN AUC, and BNSP AUC. Inference time is measured using CUDA timing. The dataset contains 448k comments with 9.9% positive labels, making it highly imbalanced. All models are tested on both full comments and shorter subsets to analyze performance differences.

## Key Results
- All BERT models achieve similar performance regardless of size or optimization
- RNNs provide faster inference times while maintaining competitive accuracy
- RoBERTa with Focal Loss shows best bias performance across metrics
- All models exhibit bias in associating identities with toxicity, with CNNs and CCTs being most sensitive

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the performance characteristics of BERT, RNN, and XLNet models change when applied to longer comments or different types of toxic content beyond the Civil Comments dataset?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that RNNs perform well on short comments (average 27 tokens) and suggests that performance differences might be more pronounced with longer texts, but does not test this hypothesis.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses on short comments from a specific dataset, leaving the scalability and generalizability of these models to other contexts unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments on datasets with longer comments or different toxic content types, measuring AUROC, F1 scores, and bias metrics across varying text lengths.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific architectural or training modifications could reduce the association bias between identities and toxicity in CNN and CCT models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies that CNN and CCT models are more sensitive to association bias compared to BERT, RNN, and XLNet, but does not explore potential solutions.
- Why unresolved: While the bias is quantified, the paper does not investigate methods to mitigate it in these model types.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing improved bias metrics (e.g., GMB BPSN and GMB Sub AUC) for CNN and CCT models after implementing architectural changes or bias-aware training techniques.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does freezing or unfreezing the embedding layer affect the performance and bias of transformer-based models on toxic comment classification tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that freezing the embedding layer decreases model performance but does not analyze its impact on bias or inference time.
- Why unresolved: The study provides limited insights into the trade-offs between embedding layer configurations and model outcomes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed comparisons of performance, bias metrics, and inference times for models with frozen versus unfrozen embeddings across multiple datasets and tasks.

## Limitations
- Findings based on single dataset limit generalizability across different toxic comment domains
- AUROC scores may not capture real-world deployment costs of false positives
- Computational resource requirements for transformer models remain substantial despite inference time comparisons

## Confidence
**High Confidence** in: (1) Relative performance ranking between model architectures, (2) Similar performance across all transformer models regardless of size, (3) Inference time comparisons between model families.

**Medium Confidence** in: (1) Bias metric results due to complexity of multi-label bias measurement, (2) DistilBERT balance claim as it may vary with hardware, (3) Sensitivity differences to identity-toxicity associations.

**Low Confidence** in: (1) Generalizability to non-English datasets, (2) Focal Loss effectiveness across all model types, (3) Real-world impact of observed performance differences.
