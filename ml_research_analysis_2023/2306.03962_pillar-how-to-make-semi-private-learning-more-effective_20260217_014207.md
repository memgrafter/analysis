# PILLAR: How to make semi-private learning more effective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03962
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03962
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: PILLAR achieves dimension-independent sample complexity for large-margin low-rank distributions while significantly improving accuracy under tight privacy constraints (epsilon=0.1) compared to baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces PILLAR, a semi-private learning algorithm that leverages public unlabeled data to reduce the dimensionality of private labeled data, enabling more effective private learning with fewer samples. The key insight is that dimensionality reduction via PCA on public data preserves discriminative information for large-margin low-rank distributions while reducing the privacy noise needed. Extensive experiments demonstrate PILLAR's effectiveness across diverse image classification tasks under tight privacy constraints, showing consistent improvements over baselines including publicly pre-trained models and non-private baselines.

## Method Summary
PILLAR works by first extracting features from both public unlabeled and private labeled data using pre-trained networks. It then computes the top k principal components from the public unlabeled features and projects the private labeled features onto this k-dimensional subspace. A differentially private linear classifier is then trained on these projected features using DP-SGD or similar algorithms. The method is particularly effective when the private data satisfies "large margin low rank" assumptions, where a linear classifier exists with both large margin and significant projection onto the top principal components.

## Key Results
- Achieves dimension-independent sample complexity for large-margin low-rank distributions
- Significant improvements over baselines at tight privacy constraints (epsilon=0.1) across multiple datasets
- Benefits increase as privacy constraints become tighter
- Effective even with moderate distribution shift between public unlabeled and private labeled data
- Performance improvements are independent of the pre-training strategy used

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Dimensionality reduction via PCA on public unlabeled data preserves the margin in the projected space for large-margin low-rank distributions.
- **Mechanism**: The algorithm projects private data onto the top principal components estimated from public data. For large-margin low-rank distributions, the top k principal components capture most of the discriminative information while reducing dimensionality.
- **Core assumption**: The private data distribution belongs to the class of "large margin low rank distributions" where a linear classifier exists with both large margin and significant projection onto the top principal components.
- **Break condition**: If the private data distribution doesn't satisfy the large margin low rank properties (e.g., high-rank distributions or small margin classifiers), the dimension-independent sample complexity guarantee fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Private learning benefits from dimensionality reduction while non-private learning does not.
- **Mechanism**: In the private setting, reducing dimensionality decreases the sensitivity of the objective function to individual data points, allowing stronger privacy guarantees with less noise. In the non-private setting, reducing dimensionality increases approximation error without privacy benefits.
- **Core assumption**: The privacy noise required scales with the dimensionality of the problem, and this scaling can be avoided through appropriate dimensionality reduction.
- **Break condition**: If the privacy mechanism's noise doesn't scale with dimensionality, or if the approximation error from dimensionality reduction outweighs privacy benefits.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The algorithm is robust to moderate distribution shift between public unlabeled and private labeled data.
- **Mechanism**: The algorithm uses total variation distance to quantify distribution shift tolerance. As long as the public unlabeled data has bounded TV distance from the private labeled data's marginal distribution, the algorithm maintains its theoretical guarantees.
- **Core assumption**: The public unlabeled data comes from a distribution that is sufficiently close to the private labeled data distribution (bounded total variation distance).
- **Break condition**: If the distribution shift exceeds the TV distance bound (η > ∆k/14), the algorithm's theoretical guarantees no longer hold.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Differential Privacy (DP)
  - Why needed here: The algorithm must provide (ε, δ)-DP guarantees on the private labeled data while leveraging public unlabeled data.
  - Quick check question: What is the key property that makes an algorithm (ε, δ)-differentially private, and how does post-processing preserve this property?

- **Concept**: Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
  - Why needed here: PCA is used to reduce dimensionality by projecting data onto the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix estimated from public unlabeled data.
  - Quick check question: How does PCA identify the most informative directions in the data, and what assumption about the data distribution makes this effective for preserving margin?

- **Concept**: Large Margin Linear Classifiers
  - Why needed here: The theoretical guarantees rely on the existence of a large margin linear classifier in the data distribution.
  - Quick check question: What is the relationship between margin size and sample complexity for learning linear classifiers, and how does dimensionality affect this relationship?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Pre-trained feature extractor -> Public unlabeled PCA computation -> Private data projection -> Differentially private linear classifier

- **Critical path**:
  1. Extract features from both public unlabeled and private labeled data using pre-trained network
  2. Compute top k principal components from public unlabeled features
  3. Project private labeled features onto k-dimensional subspace
  4. Train linear classifier on projected private features with DP optimization
  5. Output final classifier

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Higher k increases expressiveness but requires more private data and provides weaker privacy
  - Lower k improves privacy but may lose discriminative information
  - Choice of pre-training strategy affects feature quality but algorithm remains effective across strategies
  - Using more public unlabeled data improves PCA estimation but increases computational cost

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor performance with very low k (underfitting due to loss of discriminative information)
  - Degraded performance with very high k (loss of privacy benefits)
  - Sensitivity to distribution shift beyond theoretical bounds
  - Poor results when private data doesn't satisfy large margin low rank assumptions

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run on CIFAR-10 with varying k (e.g., 10, 40, 200) at fixed ε to observe the trade-off between dimensionality and accuracy
  2. Compare performance on CIFAR-10 vs CIFAR-100 to see how dataset complexity affects the k-accuracy relationship
  3. Test with different pre-training strategies (SL, BYOL) on the same dataset to verify strategy independence

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of PILLAR scale with larger and more diverse public unlabeled datasets beyond the 10% used in experiments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using 10% of the training data as public unlabeled data and shows results are stable with varying amounts of public data in Table 2b.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only test up to 10% public data. The theoretical analysis assumes a fixed amount of public data. It's unclear if performance plateaus or continues to improve with larger public datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing PILLAR with varying percentages (e.g. 20%, 50%, 100%) of public data on multiple datasets, measuring accuracy and privacy guarantees.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can PILLAR's dimensionality reduction technique be extended to non-linear classifiers or neural network architectures beyond linear classifiers on pre-trained features?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on linear classifiers on pre-trained features and assumes large margin low rank distributions. The PCA projection is linear.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical analysis and experiments are limited to linear classifiers. It's unclear if the benefits of dimensionality reduction would transfer to more complex models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying PILLAR's projection technique to train non-linear classifiers (e.g. neural networks) on the projected features, comparing to training on full-dimensional features.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is PILLAR to the choice of the privacy accountant (RDP vs PRV) and what are the implications for privacy guarantees in practice?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using RDP accountant for comparison with baselines in Section 4.2 and PRV accountant in other experiments. It notes RDP is unstable for small epsilon.
- Why unresolved: The choice of privacy accountant affects the final epsilon value. It's unclear how much this impacts the practical privacy guarantees and whether PRV is always preferable.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing PILLAR's performance and final epsilon values when using RDP vs PRV for various datasets and epsilon values, discussing the trade-offs.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What are the theoretical limits of PILLAR's tolerance to distribution shift between public unlabeled and private labeled data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Theorem 2 provides bounds on tolerance to distribution shift measured by TV distance, but the assumptions are restrictive.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical bounds assume bounded third moments and may not reflect realistic distribution shifts. The experiments only test a single type of distribution shift (CIFAR-10v1).
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis characterizing the types of distribution shifts PILLAR can tolerate, and experiments testing PILLAR with various controlled distribution shifts (e.g. covariate shift, label shift) on multiple datasets.

## Limitations

- The theoretical guarantees rely on strong assumptions about the data distribution (large margin low rank properties) that may not hold in practice
- The algorithm's performance depends on the quality of pre-trained features, though it claims to be strategy-independent
- The privacy accounting mechanism (PRV) is less well-established than standard RDP accounting, raising questions about implementation and guarantees
- Limited exploration of failure modes when assumptions are violated or when distribution shift exceeds theoretical bounds

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core algorithmic approach (PCA projection followed by private linear classification) is technically sound and the experimental results are reproducible as described. The general trend of dimensionality reduction benefiting private learning is well-supported.

**Medium Confidence**: The theoretical sample complexity bounds and distribution shift tolerance guarantees are mathematically rigorous but rely on strong assumptions that may not hold in all practical scenarios.

**Low Confidence**: The practical significance of the TV distance bound and the specific implementation details of the PRV accountant require further validation.

## Next Checks

1. **Failure Mode Analysis**: Systematically test PILLAR on datasets where the large margin low rank assumption is violated (e.g., high-rank distributions, small margin classifiers) to identify when and why the algorithm fails.

2. **Accounting Mechanism Comparison**: Implement and compare PRV accountant against standard RDP accounting across the full range of ε values to quantify the practical benefits and understand implementation differences.

3. **Feature Quality Sensitivity**: Evaluate PILLAR using pre-trained features from multiple sources with varying quality levels (e.g., different architectures, smaller datasets, domain-mismatched pre-training) to assess robustness to feature quality variations.
