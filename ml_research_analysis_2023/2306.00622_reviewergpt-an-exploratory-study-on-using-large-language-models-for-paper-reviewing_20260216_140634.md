# ReviewerGPT? An Exploratory Study on Using Large Language Models for Paper Reviewing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00622
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00622
- Authors: Multiple authors from various institutions
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Large language models show promise as reviewing assistants for specific tasks like error detection and checklist verification, but struggle with comprehensive paper evaluation

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the potential of large language models, specifically GPT-4, to assist in academic paper reviewing tasks. The authors conduct experiments across three key areas: identifying errors in research papers, verifying author-provided checklists, and selecting superior papers based on abstracts. The study finds that while GPT-4 can effectively detect certain types of errors and verify checklists with reasonable accuracy, it struggles with comprehensive paper evaluation tasks. These results suggest that LLMs may serve as useful tools for specific aspects of the review process, but are not yet capable of replacing human reviewers entirely.

## Method Summary
The authors use GPT-4 to perform three distinct paper reviewing tasks. For error detection, they created 13 short computer science papers with deliberate mathematical and conceptual errors, prompting GPT-4 to identify these errors using targeted questioning strategies. For checklist verification, they selected 15 NeurIPS 2022 papers with author-submitted checklists and compared GPT-4's responses to ground truth labels. For paper selection, they constructed 10 pairs of research abstracts designed to highlight clear differences in quality. The experiments used various prompting strategies including specific questions, one-shot examples, and selective passage extraction to work within GPT-4's token limits.

## Key Results
- GPT-4 detected errors in 7 out of 13 short papers with deliberate errors
- Checklist verification achieved 86.6% accuracy when comparing GPT-4 responses to ground truth labels
- GPT-4 correctly selected the better paper in 4 out of 10 abstract comparison tasks
- Targeted prompting strategies significantly outperformed generic "write a review" approaches

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Targeted prompting with specific questions outperforms generic "write a review" prompts in eliciting useful LLM responses for error detection.
- Mechanism: Asking the LLM to check for correctness of specific claims directs its attention and reasoning to the relevant aspects of the paper, rather than having it generate a general review which may not surface the errors.
- Core assumption: The LLM's ability to understand and reason about the paper's content is sufficient for error detection when guided by specific questions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "prompting with a specific question (e.g., to identify errors) outperforms prompting to simply write a review"
  - [section] Pilot experiments found that asking the LLM to write a review did not yield the desired outcome, but success was achieved by asking specific questions

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Providing the LLM with parts of the paper rather than the full paper can still enable it to detect errors, due to token limits.
- Mechanism: By selecting the relevant sections that contain the main claims and errors, the LLM can focus its attention and reasoning on those parts to identify the issues, even without the full context.
- Core assumption: The relevant information for error detection is contained in the selected sections, and the LLM can reason about the errors without the full paper.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we were not able to pass in entire papers to the model. Instead, for each {question, paper} pair, we selected the section(s) in the paper that best correspond to each question"
  - [section] The LLM was still able to detect errors in the short papers when provided with parts of the paper containing the main claims

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The LLM can distinguish between abstracts with and without additional results, even when the difference is subtle.
- Mechanism: By carefully constructing pairs of abstracts that differ in a specific aspect (e.g., an additional result), the LLM can be prompted to compare them and identify the superior one based on the stated criteria (stronger results).
- Core assumption: The LLM has the ability to parse and compare the abstracts, and to reason about which one has stronger results based on the provided information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We constructed 13 pairs of research abstracts, with each pair intentionally designed so that one abstract stands out as superior to the other"
  - [section] The LLM was able to correctly identify the superior abstract in 4 out of 10 cases

## Limitations

- Small dataset sizes (13 papers, 15 papers with checklists, 10 abstract pairs) limit generalizability of findings
- Token limitations required selective passage extraction, potentially missing context critical for error detection
- Manual evaluation process may introduce subjective bias in assessing GPT-4's responses

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Targeted prompting outperforms generic review prompts is well-supported by pilot experiments and aligns with established principles of LLM prompting.

**Medium Confidence**: Error detection capability (7/13 papers) shows promise but is limited by small sample size and inability to handle deductive reasoning beyond provided details.

**Low Confidence**: Overall conclusion that LLMs are "not yet capable of complete evaluations" extrapolates from limited task-specific experiments to a broad capability claim.

## Next Checks

1. **Scale Dataset and Test Deductive Reasoning**: Test error detection on a larger corpus (minimum 50 papers) that includes problems requiring multi-step deductive reasoning to assess whether GPT-4's limitations are task-specific or fundamental to its architecture.

2. **Blind Human Evaluation**: Conduct a blind study where human reviewers evaluate both papers and GPT-4's error identifications without knowing the source, to measure inter-rater reliability and identify potential biases in the current evaluation methodology.

3. **Token Window Boundary Analysis**: Systematically test error detection performance as a function of available token context (10%, 25%, 50%, 100% of paper) to quantify the impact of context truncation on detection accuracy and identify minimum context thresholds for reliable performance.
