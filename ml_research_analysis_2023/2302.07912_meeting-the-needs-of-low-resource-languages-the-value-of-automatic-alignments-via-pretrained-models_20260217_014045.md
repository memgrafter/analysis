# Meeting the Needs of Low-Resource Languages: The Value of Automatic Alignments via Pretrained Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.07912
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07912
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Neural alignment methods based on adapted multilingual models achieve lower AER and better downstream task performance for low-resource languages unseen during pretraining

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how modern word alignment methods perform on low-resource languages that were unseen during multilingual model pretraining. The authors create gold-standard alignments for four indigenous American languages (Bribri, Guarani, Quechua, Shipibo-Konibo) paired with Spanish and evaluate both traditional statistical aligners and neural approaches based on multilingual models. They find that while traditional methods like FastAlign remain competitive, particularly for Bribri, neural approaches with model adaptation generally achieve lower alignment error rates and better downstream performance in POS tagging and NER tasks through annotation projection.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates word alignment quality using both traditional statistical aligners (Giza++ and FastAlign) and neural approaches (AWESoME with mBERT and XLM-R embeddings). Neural models are adapted to target languages using four objectives: translation language modeling (+TLM), masked language modeling with translation (+MLM-T), masked language modeling with span translation (+MLM-ST), and masked language modeling with word translation (+MLM-WT). Adaptation uses either available parallel data or additional monolingual Wikipedia data. Evaluation includes intrinsic AER measurement on gold alignments and extrinsic downstream task performance via annotation projection from Spanish to target languages for POS tagging and NER.

## Key Results
- Neural alignment methods with adaptation generally achieve lower AER than traditional statistical aligners
- FastAlign outperforms neural approaches for Bribri, showing traditional methods remain competitive
- Adding Wikipedia monolingual data improves alignment quality for Guarani and Quechua
- Downstream POS tagging and NER performance correlates with alignment quality through annotation projection
- Adapted multilingual models transfer alignment knowledge to unseen languages when fine-tuned with target language context

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pretrained multilingual models improve word alignment quality for unseen low-resource languages when adapted with in-domain data.
- Mechanism: The multilingual model learns cross-lingual representations during pretraining, enabling transfer of alignment knowledge to unseen languages. Fine-tuning with adaptation objectives (+TLM, +MLM-T, +MLM-ST) updates these representations with target language context, improving alignment performance.
- Core assumption: The model's cross-lingual representations contain transferable alignment knowledge that can be enhanced with minimal adaptation data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "state-of-the-art aligners with and without model adaptation to the target language"
  - [section]: "we see that the lowest error rate is achieved with the neural approach for all languages except for Bribri, where FastAlign offers 7.03% absolute improvement"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus provides related papers but no direct alignment performance data
- Break condition: If the unseen language has a script entirely different from pretraining languages, the shared representation space may not be useful.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Additional monolingual data improves alignment performance for some low-resource languages.
- Mechanism: Monolingual data provides more context for the target language, allowing the model to better learn its linguistic patterns and improve alignment quality.
- Core assumption: The additional monolingual data is representative of the language's linguistic patterns and contains sufficient diversity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "alignment methods based on adapted multilingual models achieve lower alignment error rates"
  - [section]: "Adding Wikipedia data results in the highest performance for Guarani, outperforming the previous best approach by 3.1%"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus provides related papers but no direct evidence on monolingual data benefits
- Break condition: If the monolingual data is too limited or not representative of the language's true distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Traditional statistical alignment methods remain competitive with neural approaches for low-resource languages.
- Mechanism: Statistical models like FastAlign and Giza++ use position and word identity information effectively, and their simplicity makes them robust even with limited data.
- Core assumption: The statistical models' assumptions about alignment (e.g., one-to-many, symmetric) are reasonable for the language pairs studied.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "although transformer-based methods generally outperform traditional models, the two classes of approach remain competitive with each other"
  - [section]: "FastAlign, and the best neural approach is with mBERT+TLM. Comparing the two, we see that the lowest error rate is achieved with the neural approach for all languages except for Bribri, where FastAlign offers 7.03% absolute improvement"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus provides related papers but no direct evidence on statistical model competitiveness
- Break condition: If the language pair has complex morphological patterns that violate statistical model assumptions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preparation (parallel corpora, monolingual data) -> Model adaptation (+TLM, +MLM-T, +MLM-ST, +MLM-WT) -> Alignment extraction (traditional and neural) -> Evaluation (intrinsic AER and extrinsic downstream tasks)
- Critical path: Data preparation → Model adaptation → Alignment extraction → Evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Neural approaches offer better performance but require more computational resources and adaptation data, while traditional methods are faster but less accurate
- Failure signatures: Poor alignment quality may indicate insufficient adaptation data, mismatched scripts between source and target languages, or overly complex morphological patterns
- First 3 experiments:

  2. Fine-tune mBERT with +TLM objective using available parallel data and evaluate AER improvement

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the alignment methods perform on low-resource languages that use a different script from the source languages (Spanish/English)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note that their target languages share the same script with the source languages, which may inflate aligner performance due to higher occurrences of shared words or entities. They state "our results may not generalize fully to other low-resource languages which have a different script from the source languages."
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates on languages that share the Latin script with the source languages. Performance on languages with non-Latin scripts remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct experiments evaluating the same alignment methods on low-resource languages with non-Latin scripts (e.g., Arabic, Chinese, Hindi) would provide evidence for how script differences affect alignment quality.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal amount of adaptation data needed for neural alignment methods to outperform traditional methods for truly unseen low-resource languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors conduct a subset analysis showing that with only 6400 sentence pairs, adapted neural approaches overtake the best expected performance of FastAlign. They ask "how the performance of neural alignment depends on the amount of data and with how much data it surpasses traditional approaches."
- Why unresolved: The analysis only covers one language (Quechua) and a limited range of data sizes. The relationship between adaptation data size and performance gain across different language families and morphological types is unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study varying the amount of adaptation data across multiple low-resource languages with different characteristics (morphological complexity, relatedness to source language, etc.) would clarify the data requirements for neural methods to become advantageous.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the alignment methods perform when the available data includes code-switching or mixed-language content common in low-resource language contexts?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note that their test set consists of sentences sampled from AmericasNLI, which itself is a translation of XNLI. They acknowledge that "due to translation, the sentences may not be directly representative of a natural utterance which would be spoken by members of the communities." This suggests the data may not reflect real-world language use.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation data is based on translated sentences rather than naturally occurring text, which may not capture phenomena like code-switching that are common in low-resource language communities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the alignment methods on naturally occurring text from low-resource language communities that includes code-switching would reveal how well the methods handle this common linguistic phenomenon.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies entirely on annotation projection for downstream tasks, introducing potential noise from projected Spanish annotations
- Study focuses on only four indigenous American languages with Spanish as source language, limiting generalizability
- FastAlign outperforms neural approaches for Bribri, suggesting language-specific factors significantly impact method effectiveness
- Data consists of translated sentences rather than naturally occurring text, potentially missing real-world phenomena like code-switching

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Neural alignment methods generally outperform traditional approaches for low-resource languages unseen during pretraining is well-supported by AER results
- **Medium confidence**: Additional monolingual data improves alignment performance has limited support, validated only for Guarani and Quechua
- **Low confidence**: Generalizability to other low-resource language families, different source languages, or extremely limited parallel data remains uncertain

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-linguistic validation**: Replicate alignment experiments using a different source language (e.g., English or Portuguese) to test whether patterns hold across language families and writing systems
2. **Annotation quality assessment**: Manually validate a subset of projected annotations to quantify noise and determine whether downstream improvements correlate with alignment or annotation quality
3. **Adaptation data sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary the amount of adaptation data across multiple low-resource languages to identify minimum viable dataset sizes and characterize data efficiency trade-offs between traditional and neural approaches
