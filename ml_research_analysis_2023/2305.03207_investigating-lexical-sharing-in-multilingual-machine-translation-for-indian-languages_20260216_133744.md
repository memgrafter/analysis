# Investigating Lexical Sharing in Multilingual Machine Translation for Indian Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.03207
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03207
- Reference count: 39
- One-line primary result: Transliteration into a single script does not significantly improve multilingual MT performance for Indian languages, as models already learn robust cross-script mappings.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates lexical sharing in multilingual machine translation (MT) from Hindi, Gujarati, and Nepali into English, examining whether transliteration into a single script improves cross-script generalization. The authors explore trade-offs between data sampling and vocabulary size, and test model generalization to unseen related languages (Marathi and Bengali). Their findings challenge the assumption that script unification is necessary for multilingual MT, showing that models trained on original scripts already learn effective cross-script mappings without explicit transliteration. The study provides empirical evidence that multilingual MT models can leverage phonetic similarities across scripts without requiring preprocessing steps like transliteration.

## Method Summary
The authors train transformer models using Fairseq with 6 encoder/decoder layers and 512 embedding dimension. They experiment with vocabulary sizes of 4k, 8k, 16k, and 32k, using temperature sampling (values 1.2-1.8) to address data imbalances across language pairs. The study compares bilingual and multilingual models using original scripts versus transliteration of Gujarati and Bengali into Devanagari. Models are trained on parallel corpora ranging from 65k to 1M sentences per language pair, sourced from IITB, Wikimatrix, Bible, TED, WMT, and others. Evaluation uses BLEU score for translation quality, with experiments including zero- and few-shot transfer to Marathi and Bengali.

## Key Results
- Transliteration into a single script does not yield significant improvements in multilingual MT performance
- Multilingual models already learn robust cross-script mappings without explicit script unification
- Temperature sampling effectively balances data distribution, with higher temperatures benefiting lower-resourced language pairs
- In zero- and few-shot transfer settings, transliteration provides only marginal benefits

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multilingual MT models learn robust cross-script mappings without transliteration.
- Mechanism: Phonetic similarity between scripts enables the model to cluster equivalent tokens in the embedding space.
- Core assumption: Characters with similar phonetic values across scripts are mapped close together in the learned embedding space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "our analysis suggests that our multilingual MT models trained on original scripts seem to already be robust to cross-script differences"
  - [section] "phonetically equivalent tokens in the Devanagari and Gujarati scripts are mapped reasonably close together in this embedding space"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct mention of embedding space analysis in neighbors
- Break condition: When phonetic overlap is minimal or scripts differ significantly in representation (e.g., Arabic vs. Latin).

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Increased lexical overlap does not guarantee performance gains in multilingual MT.
- Mechanism: Multilingual models can leverage contextual cues and morphological similarities without explicit script unification.
- Core assumption: Contextual and morphological similarities are sufficient for cross-lingual transfer without transliteration.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "transliteration does not give pronounced improvements"
  - [section] "transliterated model performs slightly worse than the original bilingual model (0.24% decrease between the highest scores)"
  - [corpus] Weak - neighbors focus on transfer but not specifically on transliteration effects
- Break condition: When lexical overlap is critical for disambiguation or when morphological differences are substantial.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Temperature sampling balances data distribution to improve low-resource language performance.
- Mechanism: Adjusting sampling temperature controls the trade-off between uniform data distribution and preserving original data ratios.
- Core assumption: Higher temperature values lead to a more uniform distribution, benefiting lower-resourced language pairs.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "we use temperature sampling to address imbalances" and "for the lower-resourced language pairs ({ne,gu}→en) a higher temperature and smaller vocabulary size combination was preferred"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct mention of temperature sampling in neighbors
- Break condition: When data imbalance is not significant or when uniform distribution degrades higher-resource language performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does transliteration affect the quality of cross-lingual embeddings in multilingual models trained on very low-resource languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors analyze how transliteration impacts token mappings in embedding space for Hindi, Gujarati, and Nepali into English, finding that the original models already learn robust cross-script mappings.
- Why unresolved: The analysis focuses on specific language pairs and scripts, leaving open whether the findings generalize to other low-resource language families or when data is extremely scarce (e.g., fewer than 50k parallel sentences).
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across diverse low-resource language pairs with varying data sizes, comparing original and transliterated models using embedding similarity metrics and cross-script transfer tests.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the benefit of transliteration depend on the morphological complexity of the source languages?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The study focuses on Indo-Aryan languages with relatively similar morphological structures; the authors do not explicitly explore how morphological richness might interact with transliteration effectiveness.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments do not vary morphological complexity systematically, so it's unclear whether transliteration helps more in morphologically rich languages where subword tokenization may be less effective.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using multilingual MT models for language pairs with differing morphological complexity (e.g., agglutinative vs. fusional languages), measuring transliteration's impact on BLEU scores and subword overlap.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the degree of phonetic similarity between scripts influence the effectiveness of transliteration in multilingual MT?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that transliteration from Gujarati and Bengali into Devanagari is "mostly straightforward" due to phonetic similarities, yet find transliteration does not yield significant improvements.
- Why unresolved: The study only considers scripts with high phonetic correspondence, so it's unclear whether transliteration would be more beneficial for scripts with less direct phonetic mappings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing transliteration effectiveness across scripts with varying degrees of phonetic similarity (e.g., Cyrillic to Latin vs. Arabic to Latin), measuring BLEU scores and cross-script embedding similarity.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the optimal vocabulary size for multilingual MT when combining transliteration with temperature sampling?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors test vocabulary sizes from 4k to 32k and temperature values from 1.2 to 1.8, finding smaller vocabularies and higher temperatures work better for lower-resourced pairs, but do not explore combinations with transliteration systematically.
- Why unresolved: The interaction between vocabulary size, temperature sampling, and transliteration is not fully explored, leaving uncertainty about optimal configurations when transliteration is used.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive grid search experiments varying vocabulary size, temperature, and transliteration across multiple language pairs, identifying configurations that maximize BLEU scores.

## Limitations

- Limited to specific Indian language pairs with relatively modest dataset sizes for Gujarati and Nepali
- Transliteration analysis restricted to converting Gujarati and Bengali into Devanagari
- Focus on BLEU as primary metric without extensive qualitative analysis of translation quality
- Experimental design does not explore morphological complexity effects systematically

## Confidence

- **High**: Multilingual models learn robust cross-script mappings without transliteration
- **High**: Transliteration provides only marginal benefits for the specific language pairs studied
- **Medium**: Temperature sampling effectively balances data distribution, but optimal configurations need further validation
