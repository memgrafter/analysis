# A Self-Correcting Sequential Recommender

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.02297
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02297
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: STEAM significantly outperforms state-of-the-art sequential recommendation baselines by learning to correct imperfect interaction sequences through self-supervised deletion and insertion correction tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces STEAM, a self-correcting sequential recommender designed to address imperfections in user-item interaction sequences, such as misclicks and missed items. STEAM employs an item-wise corrector that adaptively applies 'keep', 'delete', and 'insert' operations to correct sequences, using two self-supervised learning mechanisms: deletion correction and insertion correction. These mechanisms generate imperfect sequences and supervised signals automatically, enabling the model to correct sequences without additional labeling. STEAM integrates the corrector with a recommender by sharing the encoder and training them jointly. Extensive experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate that STEAM significantly outperforms state-of-the-art sequential recommendation baselines.

## Method Summary
STEAM uses an item-wise corrector with 'keep', 'delete', and 'insert' operations, trained via two self-supervised tasks: deletion correction (learning to remove inserted items) and insertion correction (learning to predict deleted items). The corrector and recommender share an encoder and are jointly trained with a masked item prediction task. During training, sequences are randomly modified by inserting or deleting items to simulate imperfections. The model predicts operations for each item, applies them to generate corrected sequences, and uses these for masked item prediction. At inference, the corrector can optionally process sequences before recommendation.

## Key Results
- STEAM achieves significant improvements over state-of-the-art sequential recommendation baselines (SASRec, BERT4Rec, DuoRec, FMLP-Rec) on three real-world datasets (Beauty, Sports, Yelp)
- The joint learning with the corrector accounts for most of the improvement, with STEAM outperforming its recommender component trained on raw sequences
- STEAM demonstrates improved robustness to noisy data, showing consistent performance gains even when tested on corrupted sequences

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The corrector can distinguish misclicked items from missed items by learning separate deletion and insertion operations.
- Mechanism: During training, STEAM applies random deletions and insertions to the raw sequence. The deletion correction task trains the model to identify and remove inserted noise items, while the insertion correction task trains it to identify missing items and generate them before the next item.
- Core assumption: Misclicked items behave like randomly inserted noise, and missed items can be modeled as deletions that need to be recovered.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "deletion correction (i.e., deleting randomly inserted items), and (ii) insertion correction (i.e., predicting randomly deleted items)"
- Break condition: If the distribution of real misclicks differs systematically from random insertions (e.g., bias toward popular items), the deletion correction task will misclassify them.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sharing the encoder between corrector and recommender enables transfer of learned item representations to improve recommendation accuracy.
- Mechanism: Both modules use the same bidirectional transformer encoder. The corrector's self-supervised tasks learn robust item embeddings that capture corrected sequence structure, which the recommender then leverages for masked item prediction and next-item recommendation.
- Core assumption: Representations learned during correction (e.g., distinguishing noise from signal) are useful for downstream recommendation.
- Evidence anchors: [section 3.5] "We integrate the item-wise corrector and a recommender in STEAM by sharing the encoder."
- Break condition: If the correction and recommendation tasks require fundamentally different embedding spaces, shared encoder training could hurt performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Joint training with masked item prediction stabilizes the corrector by providing a shared training signal.
- Mechanism: STEAM optimizes a joint loss combining correction losses and recommendation losses. This ties the corrector's objective to the ultimate recommendation goal, preventing it from optimizing purely for correction accuracy.
- Core assumption: The recommendation task provides a strong signal that regularizes the corrector toward useful corrections.
- Evidence anchors: [section 3.5] "We use the joint loss from the corrector and the recommender to train the whole STEAM."
- Break condition: If the correction task dominates the joint loss, the recommender may not benefit and the system could overfit to correcting synthetic noise.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Self-supervised learning via random corruption and reconstruction
  - Why needed here: Labeling real misclicks and missed items is infeasible; random corruption provides synthetic training signals.
  - Quick check question: What are the two types of random corruption used in STEAM?

- Concept: Bidirectional vs. unidirectional transformers for different tasks
  - Why needed here: Bidirectional for capturing full context in recommendation, unidirectional for autoregressive generation in correction.
  - Quick check question: Which transformer type does the reverse generator use?

- Concept: Masked item prediction as a proxy for next-item recommendation
  - Why needed here: Allows training the recommender on both corrected and uncorrected sequences without changing the model architecture.
  - Quick check question: How does STEAM handle next-item prediction during testing?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Encoder (bidirectional transformer) -> Corrector (item-wise operation selector + reverse generator) -> Recommender (masked item predictor)

- Critical path:
  1. Encode input sequence with shared encoder
  2. Corrector predicts keep/delete/insert for each item
  3. Apply operations to get corrected sequence
  4. Recommender predicts masked items or next item

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Shared encoder vs. separate encoders: saves parameters but may force suboptimal task-specific embeddings
  - Random corruption vs. learned corruption: simpler but may not match real noise distribution
  - Single correction pass vs. iterative correction: simpler but may miss cascading errors

- Failure signatures:
  - High correction accuracy but low recommendation accuracy → correction task dominates
  - Low correction accuracy but high recommendation accuracy → recommender can handle noise directly
  - Similar performance on corrected vs. raw sequences → corrector not providing useful signal

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run STEAM with corrector disabled (use raw sequences only) and compare to full STEAM
  2. Swap corrector operations to always 'keep' and measure degradation
  3. Train corrector and recommender separately (no shared encoder) and measure impact on joint performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of STEAM change when considering 'replace' operations in addition to 'keep', 'delete', and 'insert'?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that 'replace' operations are not considered as they can be achieved by a combination of 'delete' and 'insert' operations.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the potential benefits of including 'replace' as a distinct operation in the item-wise corrector.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing STEAM's performance with and without 'replace' operations, demonstrating any improvements in recommendation accuracy or robustness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the model's performance vary with different insertion strategies, such as inserting items based on item similarity or user preferences instead of uniformly sampling from the item set?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses uniform sampling from the item set for insertion during the insertion correction task, but does not explore alternative strategies.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the impact of different insertion strategies on the model's ability to correct sequences and improve recommendations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing STEAM's performance using different insertion strategies, showing which approach leads to the best recommendation outcomes.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of varying the probabilities of 'keep', 'delete', and 'insert' operations on the model's performance and robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper sets fixed probabilities for these operations (p_k=0.4, p_i=0.1, p_d=0.5) but does not explore the effects of different configurations.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not analyze how changing these probabilities affects the model's ability to correct sequences and improve recommendation accuracy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing STEAM's performance across a range of probabilities for each operation, identifying optimal settings for different datasets or noise levels.

## Limitations

- The paper assumes synthetic random corruption patterns match real-world interaction noise, but this assumption is not empirically validated against actual misclick and missed item distributions.
- The shared encoder architecture may force suboptimal embeddings if correction and recommendation tasks require fundamentally different feature representations.
- The model uses fixed probabilities for correction operations without exploring how different configurations affect performance or robustness to varying noise levels.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Experimental results showing STEAM outperforming baselines on the three datasets with standard evaluation metrics
- **Medium Confidence**: Claim that joint training with masked item prediction stabilizes the corrector, supported by comparative results
- **Low Confidence**: Claim that the corrector can reliably distinguish misclicked from missed items in real-world scenarios, as this relies on unverified assumptions about noise distribution matching

## Next Checks

2. Train separate STEAM variants with: (a) completely separate encoders for corrector and recommender, (b) shared encoder with additional task-specific projection layers, and (c) shared encoder with different layer depths for each task, to understand the optimal encoder architecture.
