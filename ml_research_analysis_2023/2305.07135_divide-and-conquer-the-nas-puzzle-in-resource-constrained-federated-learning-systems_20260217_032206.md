# Divide-and-Conquer the NAS puzzle in Resource Constrained Federated Learning Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07135
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07135
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 15
- Key outcome: DC-NAS achieves 50% resource reduction and 50% training time reduction while maintaining near-iso-accuracy to full-scale federated NAS through divide-and-conquer architecture search

## Executive Summary
DC-NAS introduces a novel divide-and-conquer approach to federated neural architecture search (NAS) for resource-constrained edge devices. The method partitions the search space among multiple clients, performs connection topology pruning at the server, and applies channel pruning at individual clients to meet resource constraints. A diversified sampling strategy systematically explores the search space by maximizing distance between samples initially and progressively reducing this distance. The approach demonstrates superior performance across multiple datasets compared to random and Hadamard sampling strategies while significantly reducing computational resources and training time.

## Method Summary
DC-NAS addresses federated NAS by dividing the search space among clients and aggregating updates through a server-client integrated approach. The method employs a two-step pruning process: first, connection topology pruning occurs at the server to create sparse subnets for each client, and second, channel pruning is applied at each client to meet specific resource constraints. A novel diversified sampling strategy explores the search space by maximizing distance between samples in initial rounds and progressively reducing this distance to balance exploration and exploitation. The framework is tested on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, EMNIST, and TinyImagenet datasets with 8 clients in non-IID settings using Dirichlet distribution partitioning.

## Key Results
- Achieves near-iso-accuracy to full-scale federated NAS while reducing resources by 50%
- Reduces training time by approximately 50% compared to traditional federated NAS
- Outperforms random sampling, Hadamard sampling, and other variants across multiple datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DC-NAS works by leveraging the divide-and-conquer paradigm to overcome resource constraints in federated learning systems. The server-side connection topology pruning creates a sparse supernet that serves as a starting point for individual clients, reducing the initial search space complexity. Each client then applies channel pruning tailored to their specific resource constraints, ensuring practical deployment feasibility. The diversified sampling strategy systematically explores the search space by maximizing hamming distance between sampled architectures initially, then progressively reducing this distance to focus on promising regions. This approach balances exploration of the vast search space with exploitation of discovered high-performing architectures, leading to efficient convergence to optimal solutions.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Dataset -> Server (Connection Pruning) -> Clients (Channel Pruning) -> Aggregated Model -> Test Accuracy

**Critical Path**: Server performs connection topology pruning → Clients receive sparse subnets → Clients apply channel pruning → Clients train locally → Server aggregates updates → Repeat until convergence

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- Server-side pruning reduces search space but may limit architectural diversity
- Client-side channel pruning ensures resource constraints but may sacrifice accuracy
- Diversified sampling balances exploration-exploitation but requires careful distance scheduling

**Failure Signatures**:
- Poor convergence indicates inadequate sampling strategy or improper pruning
- Accuracy drop suggests over-pruning or insufficient architectural exploration
- Communication bottlenecks indicate model size issues or inefficient aggregation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the theoretical convergence guarantee for DC-NAS when using the diversified sampling strategy with progressive reduction in exploration distance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows empirical performance but states "we show the effectiveness of our strategy empirically and leave the theoretical analysis to future work."
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates that the sampling strategy works well in practice but does not provide formal convergence analysis or bounds for this specific sampling approach in federated settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: A mathematical proof showing convergence rates for DC-NAS with diversified sampling, including bounds on how the progressive reduction in exploration distance affects convergence guarantees.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does DC-NAS performance scale when the search space grows exponentially (e.g., larger architectures with more operations per cell)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "While we show the scalability with respect to the number of clients with a preliminary experiment, it is worthwhile to study how it scales to a larger search space and larger datasets."
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests on CIFAR10 search space and mentions scalability concerns but doesn't provide experiments on larger search spaces or analyze the computational complexity as search space size increases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring performance degradation and training time as the number of operations per cell increases, or analysis showing how the hamming distance strategy adapts to exponentially larger search spaces.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal schedule for resetting the diversified sampling (i.e., the frequency of choosing new random starting points)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tests resetting every round and every 10 rounds, noting "resetting every 10 rounds shows a minimal performance drop compared to the original diversiﬁed sampling" but doesn't determine an optimal schedule.
- Why unresolved: The paper compares two extreme cases (reset every round vs. reset every 10 rounds) but doesn't systematically explore what frequency provides the best balance between exploration and exploitation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying reset frequency across multiple values (e.g., every 5, 15, 20 rounds) to determine the optimal reset schedule for different dataset complexities and federated learning scenarios.
