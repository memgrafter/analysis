# AutoTransfer: AutoML with Knowledge Transfer -- An Application to Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.07669
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07669
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 39
- Key outcome: AutoTransfer improves search efficiency by an order of magnitude using knowledge transfer from similar tasks

## Executive Summary
AutoTransfer introduces a novel approach to automated machine learning (AutoML) that leverages knowledge transfer from similar tasks to improve search efficiency. The method computes task embeddings based on Fisher Information Matrix statistics from randomly initialized GNN models, enabling identification of similar tasks. Design priors from these similar tasks are then aggregated to guide the search for optimal architectures on novel tasks. The approach is evaluated on graph learning datasets, demonstrating significant improvements in search efficiency compared to state-of-the-art methods.

## Method Summary
AutoTransfer extracts task embeddings using scale-invariant Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) statistics from randomly initialized GNN models. These embeddings are trained using triplet margin ranking loss to preserve task similarity rankings. For novel tasks, the method identifies similar tasks in the embedding space and aggregates their design distributions (frequency of hyperparameter choices in top-performing models) using similarity-weighted averaging. This aggregated design prior is then used to guide hyperparameter optimization algorithms like Random Search, TPE, Evolutionary methods, or HyperBand.

## Key Results
- Task embeddings show 0.43 Kendall correlation with GraphGym distance metric
- AutoTransfer reduces number of explored architectures needed to reach target accuracy by an order of magnitude
- The method is evaluated on 6 graph learning datasets with 120,000 task-model combinations in GNN-Bank-101

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Task similarity can be efficiently measured using Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) statistics from randomly initialized GNN models
- **Mechanism:** Computes scale-invariant representations (α = m2/m2_1 where m1 and m2 are first and second moments of FIM diagonal) for multiple randomly initialized anchor models on each task. These α values capture how concentrated the spectrum is, correlating with loss landscape properties and model performance.
- **Core assumption:** The asymptotic statistics of FIM with randomly initialized weights encode task properties that correlate with model performance rankings across different architectures
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We define a task embedding space such that tasks close in the embedding space have similar corresponding top-performing architectures"
  - [section] "we consider the first two moments of FIM m1 = 1/tr[F] and m2 = 1/tr[F^2] and use α = m2/m2_1 as the scale-invariant representation"
- **Break condition:** If FIM statistics from random initialization fail to capture task-specific properties that influence architecture performance

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Task embeddings learned via triplet margin ranking loss can generalize to novel tasks
- **Mechanism:** Learns a projection function g(·) that maps task features to embeddings using triplet supervision where the margin ranking loss enforces that task embeddings preserve relative distances from GraphGym metric space.
- **Core assumption:** The triplet margin ranking loss with GraphGym distance as supervision can learn embeddings that preserve task similarity rankings for novel tasks
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Tasks with similar embeddings have similar best-performing architectures"
  - [section] "We use the margin ranking loss as our surrogate supervised objective function: Lr(z(i)_e, z(j)_e, z(k)_e, y) = max(0, -y · (z(i)_e⊤z(j)_e - z(i)_e⊤z(k)_e) + margin)"
- **Break condition:** If GraphGym metric space contains noise or systematic biases that propagate to learned embeddings

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Design distributions from similar tasks can be aggregated to form effective priors for novel tasks
- **Mechanism:** For a novel task, identifies similar tasks in embedding space and computes similarity-weighted average of their design distributions. Each design distribution captures frequency of hyperparameter choices in top-K performing models.
- **Core assumption:** Similar tasks have overlapping high-performing architectures, so aggregating their design distributions creates a prior that increases probability of sampling good architectures
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "we estimate the design priors of desirable models of the novel task, by aggregating a similarity-weighted sum of the top-K design distributions on tasks that are similar to the task of interest"
  - [section] "Pt(C|T(n)) = Σ T(i)∈S 1/de(T(n),T(i)) P(C|T(i)) / Σ T(i)∈S 1/de(T(n),T(i))"
- **Break condition:** If similar tasks have fundamentally different optimal architectures

  - Quick check question: What does the scale-invariant representation α = m2/m2_1 tell us about the loss landscape properties of a randomly initialized model?

  - Why needed here: Understanding the specific design choices (conv layers, aggregation, activation, etc.) is crucial for interpreting the design distribution aggregation mechanism

  - Why needed here: The paper shows how to augment different search algorithms with task-informed priors, requiring understanding of their baseline operation
  - Quick check question: How does TPE differ from random search in terms of how it samples the next hyperparameter configuration?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Task embedding extraction (FIM computation → α values → normalization → projection) → Task similarity computation (cosine distance in embedding space) → Design distribution aggregation (top-K frequency → similarity-weighted average) → Search algorithm integration (sampling from aggregated prior)

- **Critical path:** Task embedding extraction → similar task identification → design distribution aggregation → search algorithm execution

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - FIM computation on random initialization vs. trained models (efficiency vs. accuracy)
  - Number of anchor models (representation quality vs. computation time)
  - K value in top-K aggregation (prior specificity vs. robustness)

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor Kendall correlation between embeddings and GraphGym distances
  - Search performance no better than random when using transferred priors
  - Task embeddings not generalizing to truly novel tasks

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Verify FIM computation and α calculation on a single task with multiple random initializations
  2. Test task embedding projection function on known similar/dissimilar task pairs
  3. Validate design distribution aggregation by comparing transferred priors against ground truth on a validation task

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the task embedding method scale to datasets with very large or highly variable graph structures (e.g., different node/edge distributions)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on graph learning tasks with relatively standard datasets but doesn't test extreme variations in graph structure or size
- Why unresolved: The theoretical framework relies on asymptotic statistics of the FIM with randomly initialized weights, which may not capture structural differences in graphs with vastly different characteristics
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing AUTOTRANSFER on datasets with extreme graph structure variations and measuring performance degradation

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can AUTOTRANSFER effectively transfer knowledge between tasks with different output modalities (e.g., node classification to graph classification)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions AUTOTRANSFER can be generalized to other domains but only evaluates it on node and graph classification tasks separately
- Why unresolved: The current task embedding approach relies on training a linear layer on randomly initialized weights to encode label space information, which may not work well for tasks with fundamentally different output modalities
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing AUTOTRANSFER on tasks with different output modalities and comparing performance to baseline methods

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is AUTOTRANSFER to the choice of anchor models and their diversity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that 9-12 anchor models provide a good trade-off between capturing task distance and computational efficiency, but doesn't explore the impact of anchor model diversity
- Why unresolved: If anchor models are too similar or don't cover the full design space, the task embeddings may not capture important task characteristics, leading to poor transfer performance
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the diversity and coverage of anchor models and measuring their impact on task embedding quality and transfer performance

## Limitations
- FIM-based task embedding validity remains unverified with limited ablation studies
- Generalizability to truly novel tasks is uncertain beyond the graph learning domain
- Design distribution aggregation assumes similar tasks have overlapping optimal architectures without exploring when this fails

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** Search efficiency improvements are well-documented with clear baselines and statistical significance tests
- **Medium confidence:** Task embedding methodology works for graph learning tasks within the studied domain
- **Low confidence:** Method's effectiveness on non-graph tasks or tasks with substantially different characteristics

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation on task descriptors:** Replace FIM statistics with alternative task descriptors (e.g., graph statistics, dataset meta-features) to verify if the 0.43 correlation is specific to the FIM approach
2. **Cross-domain transfer test:** Apply AutoTransfer to a completely different task domain (e.g., tabular data or vision) to test generalizability beyond the graph learning scope
3. **Failure mode analysis:** Systematically identify pairs of tasks where AutoTransfer underperforms random search to understand when and why the similarity assumption breaks down
