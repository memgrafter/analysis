# RLSbench: Domain Adaptation Under Relaxed Label Shift

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.03020
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03020
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: Simple meta-algorithm improves DA methods by 2-10% accuracy under large label proportion shifts

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces RLSbench, a large-scale benchmark for relaxed label shift with over 500 distribution shift pairs across vision, tabular, and language modalities. The authors evaluate 13 popular domain adaptation methods and find they often fail under severe label proportion shifts, sometimes performing worse than source-only training. They propose a simple two-step meta-algorithm: pseudo-balance the data at each epoch and adjust the final classifier with an estimate of the target label distribution. This meta-algorithm improves existing domain adaptation heuristics under large label proportion shifts while having minimal effect when label proportions do not shift.

## Method Summary
The paper presents RLSbench, a benchmark for relaxed label shift consisting of 560 distribution shift pairs from 14 datasets across three modalities. The core method is a two-step meta-algorithm that first pseudo-balances target data using pseudo-labels at each epoch, then adjusts the final classifier using an estimated target label distribution. The approach is evaluated against 13 domain adaptation methods including source-only, DANN, CDANN, FixMatch, and others, using source validation performance for model selection across varying degrees of label shift severity.

## Key Results
- Existing DA methods often perform worse than source-only training under severe label proportion shifts
- The meta-algorithm improves aggregate performance over no correction for all DA methods by 2-10% accuracy points
- Label shift estimation methods provide reasonable accuracy even under relaxed label shift assumptions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Re-sampling to pseudo-balance target data helps DA methods across all modalities when label proportions shift.
- **Mechanism**: When target label marginal shifts, the unlabeled target data becomes imbalanced relative to the source distribution. The model, trained on this imbalanced data, develops biased predictions that favor the overrepresented classes. By pseudo-labeling the target data and re-sampling to balance class frequencies, the model sees a more representative distribution during training, reducing this bias.
- **Core assumption**: The pseudo-labels generated by the model are sufficiently accurate to create a meaningful balanced training distribution.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "re-sampling the data to balance the source and pseudo-balance the target"; [section] "we adopt an alternative strategy of leveraging pseudolabels for target data to perform pseudo class-balanced re-sampling"
- **Break condition**: If pseudo-label accuracy is too low, re-sampling amplifies noise and degrades performance further.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Re-weighting the final classifier using an estimate of the target label distribution improves accuracy under severe label proportion shifts.
- **Mechanism**: Even with balanced training data, the learned classifier f predicts according to the source label distribution. To correct this, we estimate the target label marginal pt(y) and re-scale the classifier's output logits so that the predicted class probabilities match the target distribution. This directly compensates for the label shift.
- **Core assumption**: The classifier f is sufficiently accurate that its output can be trusted to estimate class frequencies after correction.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "adjust the final classifier with target label distribution estimate"; [section] "If we can estimate the target label marginal, we can post-hoc adapt the classifier f with a simple re-weighting correction"
- **Break condition**: If pt(y) estimate is inaccurate or classifier is poorly calibrated, re-weighting introduces bias and harms performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: DA methods paired with our meta-algorithm significantly outperform methods specifically proposed for relaxed label shift.
- **Mechanism**: Existing DA methods like DANN, CDANN, and self-training methods are designed for covariate shift, not label shift. When label proportions change severely, these methods fail because they implicitly assume balanced classes. Our meta-algorithm corrects this by balancing the training data and re-weighting predictions, enabling existing methods to handle label shift without redesigning the core algorithm.
- **Core assumption**: The improvements from re-sampling and re-weighting outweigh any negative effects from modifying the original DA objective.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "The meta-algorithm improves existing domain adaptation heuristics under large label proportion shifts, often by 2–10% accuracy points"; [section] "RS and RW corrections significantly improve aggregate performance over no correction for all DA methods"
- **Break condition**: If the meta-algorithm's corrections interfere with the DA method's core learning dynamics (e.g., domain alignment), performance may degrade.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Domain Adaptation (DA)**
  - Why needed here: RLSbench evaluates DA methods under relaxed label shift, requiring understanding of how unlabeled target data is used to adapt a model trained on labeled source data.
  - Quick check question: What is the key challenge in unsupervised DA when the target label distribution differs from the source?

- **Concept: Label Shift vs. Covariate Shift**
  - Why needed here: RLSbench specifically targets relaxed label shift, where p(y) can change but p(x|y) only shifts "in seemingly natural ways." Understanding this distinction is critical for interpreting the benchmark results.
  - Quick check question: How does relaxed label shift differ from strict label shift, and why is this important for DA method evaluation?

- **Concept: Importance Weighting and Re-sampling**
  - Why needed here: The meta-algorithm uses both techniques to handle label imbalance. Importance weighting adjusts the loss function, while re-sampling changes the training data distribution.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between importance weighting and re-sampling for handling class imbalance?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Dataset loader (14 multi-domain datasets) -> DA method registry (13 methods) -> Meta-algorithm wrapper (re-sampling + re-weighting) -> Evaluation pipeline (target accuracy and label marginal estimation) -> Hyperparameter tuner
- **Critical path**: For each shift pair: (1) load source and target data, (2) simulate target label shift via Dirichlet sampling, (3) train base DA method with source hold-out validation, (4) apply meta-algorithm corrections (re-sample target, re-weight classifier), (5) evaluate on target hold-out set, (6) log results. The bottleneck is training ~30k models, so efficient GPU usage and checkpointing are essential.
- **Design tradeoffs**: The choice to simulate label shift (rather than using naturally occurring shifts) allows controlled experimentation but may not capture all real-world shift patterns. Using source validation for early stopping avoids peeking at target data but may not be optimal when label shift is severe.
- **Failure signatures**: If a DA method performs worse than source-only under label shift, it indicates the method is sensitive to class imbalance. If re-weighting hurts performance, the label marginal estimate is likely inaccurate. If re-sampling causes instability, pseudo-label quality is too low.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run CIFAR-10 with DANN, comparing source-only vs. DANN with α=0.5 (severe shift) to observe failure mode.
  2. Apply meta-algorithm to DANN on the same setup to verify the 2-10% improvement claim.
  3. Test label marginal estimation by comparing RLLS with source-only vs. DANN classifier on a moderate shift (α=1.0) to see if DA classifiers yield better estimates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How do the performance differences between source and target validation-based early stopping criteria vary across different dataset modalities and shift severities?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper notes consistent accuracy differences of approximately 2% for vision datasets and 8% for tabular datasets when using different early stopping criteria, with varying effects across modalities.
- **Why unresolved**: While the paper identifies these differences, it doesn't systematically analyze which types of datasets or shifts show the largest discrepancies, or what factors drive these variations.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A comprehensive analysis categorizing early stopping differences by dataset type, shift severity, and domain adaptation method would clarify when and why target-based validation outperforms source-based validation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does the performance of label shift estimation methods under relaxed label shift compare to their performance under the stricter label shift assumption?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper mentions that label shift estimation techniques like RLLS and MLLS provide Op1/?n convergence rates under the stricter label shift assumption but doesn't characterize their performance when this assumption is violated.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper observes that these methods still work reasonably well under relaxed label shift but doesn't provide theoretical or empirical characterization of how their performance degrades as the relaxed label shift assumption becomes more violated.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical studies measuring estimation error across varying degrees of violation of the label shift assumption, combined with theoretical analysis of convergence rates under relaxed assumptions, would clarify this relationship.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the relationship between the nature of class-conditional distribution shifts (ppx|y|) and the effectiveness of target label marginal estimation methods?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper notes that the success of target label marginal estimation depends on the nature of shifts in ppx|y) and that this relationship varies across modalities (better for vision/tabular than NLP).
- **Why unresolved**: The paper observes this dependency but doesn't characterize which types of ppx|y) shifts are more or less amenable to estimation, or why certain shifts might be more problematic for estimation methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments varying the type and degree of class-conditional shifts (e.g., subpopulation shifts, covariate shifts, adversarial shifts) and measuring their impact on estimation accuracy would reveal which shift types are most challenging.

## Limitations

- Weak corpus evidence supporting key mechanisms, particularly pseudo-label quality requirements and estimator reliability under relaxed shift
- Missing comparison with methods specifically designed for relaxed label shift
- No systematic analysis of when meta-algorithm corrections interfere with DA method learning dynamics

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The experimental methodology and benchmark construction are sound, with clear protocols for creating controlled label shifts and evaluating DA methods.
- **Medium confidence**: The observation that existing DA methods fail under severe label proportion shifts is well-supported by the benchmark results.
- **Low confidence**: The mechanism explanations for why re-sampling and re-weighting work, particularly the pseudo-label quality threshold and estimator reliability under relaxed shift conditions.

## Next Checks

1. **Pseudo-label accuracy threshold**: Run experiments varying Dirichlet concentration parameter α from 0.1 to 10.0 and measure pseudo-label accuracy before re-sampling. Identify the critical accuracy threshold below which the meta-algorithm degrades performance.

2. **Estimator comparison under relaxed shift**: Compare RLLS, MLLS, and confusion matrix-based estimators on the same distribution shift pairs. Measure both label marginal estimation error and final accuracy to determine which estimator performs best under relaxed label shift conditions.

3. **Meta-algorithm vs. specialized methods**: Implement at least one method specifically designed for relaxed label shift (e.g., Label Shift Adapter) and compare against the meta-algorithm on identical distribution shift pairs. This would validate whether the meta-algorithm truly outperforms specialized approaches or simply reveals limitations in existing DA methods.
