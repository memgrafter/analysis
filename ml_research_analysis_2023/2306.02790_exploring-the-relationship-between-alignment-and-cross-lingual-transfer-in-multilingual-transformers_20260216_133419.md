# Exploring the Relationship between Alignment and Cross-lingual Transfer in Multilingual Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2306.02790
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02790
- **Reference count**: 26
- **Primary result**: Strong multilingual alignment correlates with cross-lingual transfer ability; fine-tuning can harm alignment; realignment methods can improve transfer, especially for smaller models and distant language pairs.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the relationship between multilingual alignment and cross-lingual transfer (CTL) in multilingual Transformers. The authors demonstrate that alignment, measured through nearest-neighbor search accuracy on translated word pairs, is significantly correlated with CTL abilities across models, tasks, and languages. They find that fine-tuning can negatively impact alignment, particularly for lower-level tasks and smaller models. The study also shows that realignment methods can improve CTL, with bilingual dictionaries proving more effective than automatic aligners like FastAlign or AWESOME-align. These findings suggest that further research on realignment methods for smaller multilingual models could provide an alternative to simply scaling up model size.

## Method Summary
The authors extract translated word pairs from parallel corpora (opus100, multiUN) using bilingual dictionaries, FastAlign, or AWESOME-align. They evaluate multilingual alignment via nearest-neighbor search accuracy using contextualized embeddings from the last and penultimate layers. Models (distilmBERT, mBERT, XLM-R Base/Large) are fine-tuned on English for POS-tagging, NER, and NLI tasks. Realignment is performed either sequentially (before fine-tuning) or jointly (simultaneous with fine-tuning) using contrastive loss. Cross-lingual transfer is measured as relative difference between target and English performance. Spearman's rank correlation analysis examines the relationship between alignment and CTL.

## Key Results
- Strong multilingual alignment correlates significantly with cross-lingual transfer across languages, models, and random seeds.
- Fine-tuning negatively impacts multilingual alignment, especially for smaller models and lower-level tasks like POS-tagging.
- Realignment methods improve cross-lingual transfer when baseline alignment is low, with bilingual dictionaries outperforming automatic aligners.
- For POS-tagging between English and Arabic, realignment improved distilmBERT's accuracy by 15.8 points, outperforming XLM-R Large by 1.7 points.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Strong multilingual alignment correlates positively with cross-lingual transfer ability.
- Mechanism: When word representations from different languages are close in embedding space, fine-tuning on one language allows knowledge transfer to others.
- Core assumption: The alignment metric (nearest-neighbor search accuracy) captures the functional similarity needed for transfer.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We provide evidence that alignment is actually significantly correlated with cross-lingual transfer across languages, models and random seeds."
  - [section 4] Spearman's rank correlation analysis shows strong alignment has higher correlation with CTL than weak alignment.
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; weak correlation to other papers (avg FMR=0.575).

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning can harm multilingual alignment, especially for lower-level tasks and smaller models.
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning on a monolingual task may cause catastrophic forgetting of cross-lingual representations, reducing alignment.
- Core assumption: Fine-tuning focuses on task-specific features that are language-dependent, reducing cross-lingual structure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5] "Fine-tuning on a downstream task has an impact on the multilingual alignment... For 'smaller' language models, it is systematically detrimental, as well as for certain tasks like POS-tagging."
  - [section 6] "The positive impact of realignment on cross-lingual transfer seems to be mirrored by the negative impact of fine-tuning over alignment."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; weak correlation to other papers (avg FMR=0.575).

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Realignment methods improve cross-lingual transfer when baseline alignment is low.
- Mechanism: By bringing representations of translated word pairs closer, realignment compensates for the loss of cross-lingual structure.
- Core assumption: Realignment before fine-tuning improves transfer more than realignment after.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that realignment can, in some instances, improve cross-lingual transfer, and we identify conditions under which realignment methods provide significant improvements."
  - [section 6] "Realignment methods, either sequential or joint, provide significant improvement for all models for the POS-tagging task."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; weak correlation to other papers (avg FMR=0.575).

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Translation datasets (opus100, multiUN) -> Word alignment extraction (bilingual dict, FastAlign, AWESOME-align) -> Realignment data -> Pre-trained MLLM -> Realignment (before/after/fine-tuning) -> Fine-tuning on English -> Evaluation on target languages

- Critical path: Extract alignment pairs -> Perform realignment -> Fine-tune on English -> Evaluate transfer to target languages

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Alignment extraction: Bilingual dictionary (accurate but limited coverage) vs. FastAlign/AWESOME-align (wider coverage but noisier)
  - Realignment timing: Before fine-tuning (may improve initial alignment) vs. after fine-tuning (may recover lost alignment) vs. joint (simultaneous optimization)
  - Model size: Smaller models benefit more from realignment but start with worse alignment

- Failure signatures:
  - Realignment worsens performance: Likely due to poor quality alignment pairs or overfitting to alignment data
  - No correlation between alignment and transfer: Alignment metric may not capture the relevant similarity structure
  - Fine-tuning drops alignment: Task may be too language-specific (like POS-tagging) for the model architecture

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Measure baseline alignment and transfer for distilmBERT on POS-tagging between English and Arabic
  2. Apply sequential realignment using bilingual dictionary and measure impact on transfer
  3. Compare sequential vs joint realignment for the same task and language pair

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does alignment at different layers (not just last and penultimate) relate to cross-lingual transfer for different tasks and models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that correlation between alignment and CTL varies across layers, but only analyzes last and penultimate layers due to varying model sizes. A more thorough layer-by-layer analysis is suggested as future work.
- Why unresolved: The paper only correlates alignment at the last and penultimate layers with CTL, due to the varying number of layers in different models. This limits the ability to draw conclusions about which layers are most important for alignment and CTL.
- What evidence would resolve it: Additional experiments correlating alignment at all layers with CTL for a fixed number of layers across all models. This would reveal which layers have the strongest relationship between alignment and transfer performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of realignment samples to use for different tasks and models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses fewer realignment samples than previous work, and notes that the number of samples could impact realignment effectiveness. However, they only test a single number of samples per task.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests one number of realignment samples per task, so the impact of sample size on realignment effectiveness is unknown. It's possible that using too many or too few samples could harm performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing a range of realignment sample sizes for each task and model. This would reveal the optimal sample size that maximizes realignment effectiveness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why do some tasks (like POS-tagging) harm alignment more than others when fine-tuned?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that POS-tagging and NER cause the most alignment degradation after fine-tuning, while NLI causes the least. However, they only speculate that lower-level tasks rely more on language-specific features.
- Why unresolved: The paper only offers a hypothesis for why lower-level tasks harm alignment more, but does not test this hypothesis or explore other possible explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments analyzing the linguistic features used by each task (e.g., morphology, syntax, semantics) and their relationship to alignment. This would reveal which features are most detrimental to alignment and why.

## Limitations

- The analysis focuses on a specific alignment metric (nearest-neighbor accuracy) which may not capture all aspects of semantic similarity relevant for transfer.
- The study examines only four model sizes and three tasks, limiting generalizability of the findings.
- Realignment methods use a relatively small subset of word pairs (1000 per language pair), which may not fully represent the linguistic diversity needed for robust alignment.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The correlation between strong multilingual alignment and improved cross-lingual transfer is well-supported by statistical analysis across multiple models and tasks.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that fine-tuning harms alignment, particularly for smaller models and lower-level tasks, is supported by empirical evidence but may vary with different fine-tuning procedures or tasks.
- **Medium confidence**: The effectiveness of realignment methods, especially using bilingual dictionaries versus automatic aligners, is demonstrated but may depend on the quality of the underlying translation data and the specific language pairs involved.

## Next Checks

1. Test the correlation between alignment and transfer on additional model architectures (e.g., BLOOMZ, GPT-NeoX) and tasks (e.g., text classification, question answering) to verify generalizability.
2. Experiment with larger realignment datasets (10K-100K pairs) to determine if the current 1000-pair limit is optimal or if more extensive realignment provides better results.
3. Investigate the impact of realignment on alignment metrics beyond nearest-neighbor accuracy, such as centering alignment or other geometric measures of embedding space structure.
