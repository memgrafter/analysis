# Sparsity May Cry: Let Us Fail (Current) Sparse Neural Networks Together!

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.02141
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02141
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: State-of-the-art magnitude- and gradient-based sparse algorithms fail on challenging tasks at trivial sparsity levels (5%)

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the "Sparsity May Cry" Benchmark (SMC-Bench), a challenging collection of 4 diverse tasks and 10 datasets designed to rigorously evaluate sparse neural network algorithms. The benchmark includes commonsense reasoning, arithmetic reasoning, protein thermostability prediction, and multilingual translation tasks that require sophisticated domain-specific knowledge. Through systematic evaluation, the authors reveal that state-of-the-art magnitude- and gradient-based sparse algorithms fail to perform on SMC-Bench at trivial sparsity levels as low as 5%, in stark contrast to their success on simpler tasks. The analysis attributes this failure to the "lazy regime" of large model training, where weight changes during fine-tuning are negligible, making traditional pruning criteria unreliable.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune or train pre-trained models (RoBERTa, mBART, GTS, Graph2Tree, ESM models) on SMC-Bench tasks using various sparse algorithms including LTH, magnitude pruning, random pruning, GMP, SNIP, and RigL at multiple sparsity levels (20%-89.3%). They evaluate performance using task-specific metrics: test accuracy for classification tasks, BLEU score for translation, accuracy for protein prediction, and accuracy for arithmetic reasoning. The methodology involves applying each sparse algorithm to each model-task combination and measuring the resulting sparse model's performance compared to dense baselines.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art magnitude- and gradient-based sparse algorithms fail on SMC-Bench at trivial sparsity levels (as low as 5%)
- Task difficulty correlates with pruning effectiveness - harder tasks suffer more from sparsity
- Layerwise sparsity patterns across different magnitude-based methods are nearly identical, suggesting the "lazy regime" makes pruning criteria uninformative
- Second-order pruning approaches like oBERT show more promise but still struggle to match dense performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Sparse neural networks fail on complex tasks with high knowledge requirements
- **Mechanism**: Magnitude- and gradient-based pruning methods fail on SMC-Bench at trivial sparsity levels when applied to tasks requiring complex reasoning, multilingual translation, or protein prediction
- **Core assumption**: Task complexity and domain-specific knowledge requirements are major factors in determining pruning effectiveness
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract observation of failure at 5% sparsity; section 4 showing harder tasks suffer more accuracy loss
- **Break condition**: If task difficulty is not correlated with pruning effectiveness

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: "Lazy regime" causes magnitude and gradient-based pruning to be unreliable
- **Mechanism**: During fine-tuning of large language models, weight changes are negligible, making pruning criteria uninformative
- **Core assumption**: In lazy regime, weight changes are small during fine-tuning, making traditional pruning criteria unreliable
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentioning lazy regime; section 5 showing similar layerwise sparsity patterns across methods
- **Break condition**: If second-order methods don't outperform magnitude-based methods

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Second-order pruning approaches are more reliable for LLM pruning
- **Mechanism**: oBERT uses inverse-Hessian approximations to guide pruning decisions, providing more faithful signals than magnitudes and gradients
- **Core assumption**: Second-order information is more informative than first-order or zero-order information in lazy regime
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract noting oBERT shows promise; section C.2 showing oBERT outperforms other methods
- **Break condition**: If oBERT fails to outperform magnitude-based methods on SMC-Bench

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does performance on SMC-Bench change when using advanced training techniques like iterative learning rate rewinding and knowledge distillation?
- **Basis in paper**: Authors mention these techniques help in Appendix C but exclude them to avoid diluting performance differences between sparsification methods
- **Why unresolved**: Paper focuses on baseline sparse algorithms without advanced techniques
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comprehensive experiments showing accuracy comparisons with and without advanced techniques across all SMC-Bench tasks

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What specific characteristics of SMC-Bench tasks make them challenging for magnitude- and gradient-based pruning compared to traditional datasets?
- **Basis in paper**: Paper observes failure on SMC-Bench while maintaining performance on simpler tasks, but doesn't deeply analyze task differences
- **Why unresolved**: While noting correlation between task difficulty and pruning effectiveness, doesn't identify specific challenging properties
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Detailed analysis comparing task characteristics between SMC-Bench and traditional datasets

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How does the "lazy regime" phenomenon specifically manifest in transformer-based models during fine-tuning?
- **Basis in paper**: Authors attribute SNN failures to lazy regime but don't explore architectural modifications or training strategies to mitigate it
- **Why unresolved**: Identifies lazy regime as cause but doesn't explore prevention or mitigation strategies
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments testing architectural modifications or training strategies to break lazy regime

## Limitations
- SMC-Bench represents a curated selection of tasks that may not fully represent real-world applications
- Paper focuses on out-of-the-box application of existing sparse algorithms without exploring task-specific adaptations
- "Lazy regime" hypothesis lacks rigorous theoretical foundation connecting weight changes to pruning criterion effectiveness
- oBERT investigation is preliminary with limited ablation studies and hyperparameter exploration

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Empirical observation that state-of-the-art sparse algorithms fail on SMC-Bench at trivial sparsity levels (5%) is well-supported by extensive experimental results across multiple tasks and models
- **Medium Confidence**: Connection between task complexity and pruning effectiveness is reasonably supported but could benefit from more systematic analysis; lazy regime hypothesis is plausible but requires more theoretical grounding
- **Low Confidence**: Superiority of second-order methods like oBERT is based on preliminary investigation with limited experimental validation

## Next Checks
