# Which Model Shall I Choose? Cost/Quality Trade-offs for Text Classification Tasks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.07006
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07006
- Reference count: 5
- One-line primary result: No universally optimal model exists - selection depends on accuracy requirements, annotation budget, and inference volume

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a quantitative analysis of cost/quality trade-offs for text classification models, helping industry practitioners make informed model selection decisions. The authors evaluate multiple models (logistic regression, SVM, CNN, BERT, T5, and GPT-3) on three benchmark datasets and one internal dataset, measuring classification accuracy under few-shot learning settings alongside annotation, training, and inference costs. The core finding is that model selection depends heavily on specific requirements: non-neural models like logistic regression are cost-effective for large-scale inference but require substantial annotation data; GPT-3 excels in few-shot scenarios but becomes expensive with high inference volumes; BERT and T5 offer middle-ground solutions. The analysis reveals that there is no universally optimal model—the best choice depends on the balance between desired accuracy, available annotation budget, and expected inference volume.

## Method Summary
The paper evaluates 7 models (logistic regression, SVM, Kim CNN, BERT-base, T5-base, and GPT-3 davinci-002) across 4 datasets under few-shot learning settings (1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 200 shots). Costs are calculated for annotation ($0.012/sample for public datasets, $0.072 for internal), training (using AWS GPU pricing at $0.99/hour), and inference (OpenAI API at $0.02/1000 tokens for GPT-3). The methodology tracks training/inference times and GPU usage to estimate total costs, then plots accuracy vs total cost curves to identify optimal models for different scenarios.

## Key Results
- Model selection depends on inference volume thresholds: GPT-3 optimal for <500 samples, T5 for larger scales, non-neural models for very large volumes
- Few-shot learning capability is the primary differentiator between model families
- Annotation cost can dominate total costs for moderate-scale deployments
- Achieving 90% accuracy on IMDb requires different optimal models depending on whether 200 or 100,000 samples need classification

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Model selection trade-offs depend on inference volume thresholds
- Mechanism: Different models become optimal at different scales of inference volume. GPT-3 is most cost-effective for small volumes due to strong few-shot performance and low annotation requirements. T5 becomes preferable for larger volumes because its lower per-inference cost offsets higher annotation/training costs. Non-neural models become optimal at very large scales where zero inference cost dominates.
- Core assumption: Cost/quality trade-off curves are relatively stable across domains
- Evidence anchors: Achieving 90% accuracy on IMDb requires different optimal models depending on volume; non-neural models require large annotation data but have zero inference cost
- Break condition: If inference patterns differ significantly from typical classification tasks or cost structure changes

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Few-shot learning capability is the primary differentiator between model families
- Mechanism: Pre-trained models have fundamentally different few-shot learning characteristics. GPT-3 and BERT work well with minimal examples, while T5 requires more training data. This creates a trade-off where GPT-3 minimizes annotation costs but maximizes inference costs, while T5 offers middle-ground solutions.
- Core assumption: Few-shot learning performance directly translates to reduced annotation costs
- Evidence anchors: BERT and GPT-3 don't suffer from cold start problem; only BERT and T5 outperform majority baseline on imbalanced dataset
- Break condition: If domain complexity increases significantly or annotation costs become negligible

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Annotation cost is the dominant factor in total cost for moderate-scale deployments
- Mechanism: Annotation costs can dominate total costs, especially for non-neural models requiring substantial training data. This makes annotation efficiency critical, particularly for models like logistic regression and SVM that have zero inference cost but high annotation requirements.
- Core assumption: Annotation costs remain significant relative to training and inference costs
- Evidence anchors: Non-neural models require large data points to reach decent accuracy; annotation cost estimated at $0.072 per example for internal dataset
- Break condition: If annotation costs decrease dramatically or training/inference costs become dominant

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do cost/quality trade-offs change as language model APIs continue to decrease in price and increase in performance over time?
- Basis in paper: The paper notes that "the price of API calls is subject to change" and provides specific historical price reduction examples for GPT-3
- Why unresolved: The analysis is based on current pricing models, but API costs and model capabilities are rapidly evolving
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking model performance and API pricing over time

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would including the pre-training costs of foundation models affect the overall cost/quality trade-off analysis?
- Basis in paper: The authors explicitly state they don't include pre-training costs as a part of the model cost
- Why unresolved: Pre-training costs are substantial and could significantly alter cost calculations
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive cost analysis incorporating estimated pre-training costs

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do cost/quality trade-offs vary across different text classification task domains with varying annotation complexity?
- Basis in paper: The authors note that annotation cost is affected by many factors including task difficulty and person performing annotation
- Why unresolved: The analysis is limited to three benchmark datasets and one internal dataset
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis across multiple domain-specific datasets with varying annotation complexities

## Limitations
- Analysis based on limited number of datasets that may not generalize to all domains
- Cost estimates rely on specific pricing models that may fluctuate over time
- Few-shot learning experiments use fixed sample sizes that may not reflect all practical constraints
- Pre-training costs of foundation models are not included in the analysis

## Confidence
- High confidence: General methodology for cost/quality trade-off analysis and observation that model selection depends on inference volume thresholds
- Medium confidence: Specific cost estimates and optimal model recommendations given potential variations in domain characteristics and pricing models
- Medium confidence: Few-shot learning findings as results may be sensitive to prompt engineering and sampling strategies
