# Doing the right thing for the right reason: Evaluating artificial moral cognition by probing cost insensitivity

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18269
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18269
- Reference count: 22
- Key outcome: Agents with stronger advantageous inequity aversion (β = 0.75 and 0.5) showed less decline in helping behavior as traversal costs increased, compared to an agent with β = 0.25 that stopped helping entirely.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a behavioral evaluation scheme for assessing artificial moral cognition by measuring cost insensitivity - whether agents maintain morally motivated behaviors even as associated costs increase. The method is applied to deep RL agents trained via memory-based meta-reinforcement learning with other-regarding preferences. The authors find that agents with stronger advantageous inequity aversion show less decline in helping behavior as costs increase, providing evidence for moral motivation rather than mere behavioral inflexibility. The approach enables like-for-like comparison between human and artificial agents based on observed behavior rather than interpreting internal states.

## Method Summary
The evaluation scheme measures cost insensitivity by training deep RL agents with different levels of advantageous inequity aversion (AIA) and testing their helping behavior across varying traversal costs. Agents use memory-based meta-RL with LSTM memory (256 units) and convolutional networks (16, 32 channels) to adapt their policies at evaluation time. The environment features tall and short agents in a 2D world where helping requires the tall agent to travel to fruit patches. Three agents are trained with β values of 0.75, 0.5, and 0.25, then evaluated across desert sizes (0-20) to measure how helping behavior changes with increasing cost.

## Key Results
- Agents with stronger advantageous inequity aversion (β = 0.75 and 0.5) showed less decline in helping behavior as traversal costs increased.
- The agent with β = 0.25 stopped helping entirely as costs increased, while stronger AIA agents maintained helping behavior.
- The evaluation requires comparing cost sensitivity across both morally relevant (helping) and morally neutral behaviors to rule out general cost insensitivity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Agents trained with stronger other-regarding preferences show less decline in helping behavior as costs increase.
- Mechanism: Advantageous inequity aversion (AIA) is encoded during training via a modified reward function that penalizes the agent when its own reward significantly exceeds that of its partner. At evaluation, the agent uses memory-based meta-RL to adapt its policy to new cost conditions without changing network weights. The AIA term causes the agent to internalize the partner's welfare, making it less sensitive to increasing personal costs of helping.
- Core assumption: The learned policy can generalize from training cost levels to higher evaluation costs via the agent's recurrent memory dynamics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Agents with stronger advantageous inequity aversion (β = 0.75 and 0.5) showed less decline in helping behavior as traversal costs increased, compared to an agent with β = 0.25 that stopped helping entirely."
  - [section] "The tall agents with stronger other-regarding preferences (greater advantageous inequity aversion during training) showed more insensitivity of helping behavior to increasing cost at evaluation time."
- Break condition: If evaluation costs exceed the agent's learned cost distribution by too large a margin, the agent may fail to generalize and revert to self-interested behavior.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Cost insensitivity for morally relevant behaviors can be measured by varying traversal distance between fruit patches.
- Mechanism: The environment is designed so that helping requires the tall agent to travel further from its optimal self-interested path. By increasing desert size, the cost of helping increases while the intrinsic value of the fruit remains constant. The agent's choice to continue helping despite higher costs indicates cost insensitivity.
- Core assumption: The agent perceives traversal distance as a cost and can adapt its policy based on this cost signal at evaluation time.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We manipulate the distance the tall agent would need to travel in deviation from its optimal route by varying the distance between the patches... A longer distance means that, if the tall agent decides to help, it must then spend more time in transit. This imposes greater instrumental cost..."
  - [section] "We record the number of helping events in an episode using episodes that have a desert of sizes 0, 2, 4, ... 20."
- Break condition: If the agent overfits to training desert sizes and cannot interpolate or extrapolate to new sizes, cost insensitivity measurements become unreliable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Distinguishing moral cost insensitivity from general behavioral inflexibility requires comparing cost sensitivity across morally relevant and neutral behaviors.
- Mechanism: The evaluation scheme requires measuring how the agent responds to costs for both helping (morally relevant) and non-helping behaviors (morally neutral). An agent that is cost-insensitive for helping but cost-sensitive for neutral behaviors demonstrates true moral motivation rather than general inflexibility.
- Core assumption: The agent can differentiate between morally relevant and neutral contexts and adjust its cost sensitivity accordingly.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Nevertheless, such evaluation can yield a false positive when an artificial agent (or indeed, a person) is generically insensitive to costs across many behaviors, particularly for morally neutral behaviors."
  - [section] "Recall that our evaluation requires us to compare agents for their cost sensitivity on both a morally relevant behavior (like helping) and a morally irrelevant behavior."
- Break condition: If the agent cannot distinguish moral from neutral contexts, cost sensitivity comparisons become meaningless.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Advantageous inequity aversion (AIA)
  - Why needed here: AIA is the core mechanism that makes agents care about their partner's welfare, which is essential for moral behavior in the environment.
  - Quick check question: What happens to the reward of the tall agent when its own reward significantly exceeds that of the short agent during training?

- Concept: Memory-based meta-RL (MMRL)
  - Why needed here: MMRL allows agents to adapt their behavior at evaluation time based on new cost conditions without changing their learned weights, which is crucial for measuring cost sensitivity.
  - Quick check question: How does the agent adapt to new cost conditions at evaluation time if it's not receiving reward signals?

- Concept: Behavioral evaluation of artificial agents
  - Why needed here: Since we cannot directly access the agent's internal moral reasoning, we must infer moral cognition from observed behavior changes in response to cost manipulations.
  - Quick check question: Why can't we simply look inside the neural network to determine if an agent is behaving morally?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Conv network (16, 32 channels) -> Feed-forward network (64 units) -> LSTM memory (256 hidden units) -> Policy head (256 units) and Value head (256 units)

- Critical path:
  1. Environment generates observation (88x88 RGB)
  2. Conv layers extract visual features
  3. Features combined with memory state in FF layers
  4. LSTM updates memory based on new observation
  5. Policy and value heads produce outputs
  6. AIA modifies reward signal during training
  7. Agent acts and receives next observation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using AIA vs. hard-coding moral rules: AIA allows learning but may not generalize perfectly
  - Memory size (256) vs. computational efficiency: Larger memory enables better adaptation but increases training time
  - Partial observability (9 ahead, 1 behind, 5 each side) vs. full observability: Partial forces the agent to learn about partner's needs

- Failure signatures:
  - Agent stops helping entirely even at low costs → insufficient AIA strength or poor training
  - Agent helps equally regardless of cost → general behavioral inflexibility, not moral motivation
  - Agent's helping decreases linearly with cost → insufficient moral motivation or AIA implementation issues

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train three agents with β = 0.75, 0.5, 0.25 and evaluate helping behavior across desert sizes 0-20 to confirm cost insensitivity gradient
  2. Remove the short agent entirely and evaluate the tall agent's behavior to check for general cost insensitivity
  3. Train agents with different AIA formulations (e.g., including disadvantageous aversion) to test robustness of the evaluation method

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Do agents trained with different values of advantageous inequity aversion show differential cost sensitivity for morally neutral behaviors as well as morally relevant helping behaviors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note they need to test "adaptive cost sensitivity for morally neutral behaviors" to rule out general cost insensitivity as an explanation for their findings
- Why unresolved: The current experiments only measured cost sensitivity for the morally relevant helping behavior. The authors explicitly state they need to perform the same evaluation for morally neutral behaviors to rule out general cost insensitivity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring cost sensitivity across multiple morally neutral behaviors (e.g., fruit consumption when no other agent is present) showing whether agents maintain similar cost insensitivity patterns or show differential sensitivity between moral and neutral contexts.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the proposed evaluation scheme distinguish between agents that have learned genuine moral preferences versus those that have merely learned context-specific policies?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors discuss evaluating agents in "previously unseen costs" and "extreme values for evaluation" to test generalization, suggesting concern about whether agents are learning genuine moral preferences or just memorizing specific scenarios
- Why unresolved: The current experiments test interpolation and extrapolation of training conditions, but don't directly test whether agents understand the moral nature of their behavior versus just following learned patterns
- What evidence would resolve it: Tests where agents encounter novel moral dilemmas not present in training data, or where moral and non-moral contexts are more clearly distinguished, to see if cost insensitivity generalizes appropriately only to moral contexts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the evaluation scheme perform when comparing human and artificial agents across different moral domains beyond helping behavior?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state their approach "could also be applied to humans to facilitate like-for-like comparison" but only test on a single helping behavior
- Why unresolved: The scheme is only validated on one type of moral behavior in one domain, leaving open questions about its generalizability to other moral domains like harm, fairness, or justice
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies applying the same cost sensitivity evaluation to humans and AI agents across multiple moral domains (e.g., harm prevention, fairness in resource allocation) to validate the scheme's robustness.

## Limitations
- The evaluation scheme cannot distinguish true moral motivation from general behavioral inflexibility without comparing cost sensitivity across morally relevant and neutral behaviors.
- The mechanism by which memory-based meta-RL enables cost adaptation at evaluation time is not fully specified, leaving questions about the agent's actual decision-making process.
- The scheme is only validated on one type of moral behavior (helping), limiting confidence in its generalizability to other moral domains.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The experimental results showing differential cost insensitivity among agents with different β values are well-supported by the data presented.
- **Medium confidence**: The interpretation that stronger advantageous inequity aversion leads to moral motivation is reasonable but requires further validation against neutral behaviors.
- **Low confidence**: The claim that this evaluation method can definitively distinguish moral from non-moral agents without additional controls for general inflexibility.

## Next Checks
1. Implement the evaluation protocol with a control task where the same agents perform a morally neutral behavior (e.g., optimal path traversal without helping) and compare cost sensitivity patterns.
2. Conduct ablation studies by training agents with disadvantageous inequity aversion or pure reward maximization to establish baseline cost sensitivity patterns for comparison.
3. Test the agents' generalization to desert sizes outside the training distribution (e.g., desert sizes 22-30) to assess the robustness of the cost insensitivity measurements.
