# An Investigation of Noise in Morphological Inflection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.16581
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16581
- **Reference count**: 23
- **Primary result**: Encoder-decoder models with standard decoding are more robust to noise than models with a copy bias in morphological inflection tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how noise in training data affects morphological inflection systems, particularly in low-resource settings. The authors propose a taxonomy of four noise types (lexicon, POS, paradigm, and slot noise) and an annotation pipeline using Apertium morphological analyzers. They compare four state-of-the-art inflection models—LSTM encoder-decoder, Transformer encoder-decoder, Pointer Generator LSTM, and Makarov & Clematide transducer—on both synthetic (tUMPC) and gold-standard (UniMorph) datasets across four languages. The study finds that encoder-decoder models are more robust to noise than copy-based models, and that character-level masked language modeling (CMLM) pretraining helps Transformers but has limited impact on LSTMs.

## Method Summary
The authors create noisy inflection training data by annotating tUMPC datasets with their proposed noise taxonomy using Apertium morphological analyzers. They train four neural inflection generation systems—LSTM, Transformer, Pointer Generator LSTM, and Makarov & Clematide transducer—on both clean and noisy versions of tUMPC and UniMorph datasets. CMLM pretraining is applied to encoder-decoder models before fine-tuning on inflection tasks. Model performance is evaluated on SIGMORPHON 2017 shared task development sets for Icelandic, German, Swedish, and Russian.

## Key Results
- Encoder-decoder models (LSTM, Transformer) show greater robustness to noise than copy-biased models (Pointer Generator, M&C)
- CMLM pretraining significantly improves Transformer noise resistance but has minimal impact on LSTM performance
- Low sample diversity in training data amplifies the negative impact of all noise types
- M&C transducer performs well on clean data but degrades most severely with noise introduction

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Encoder-decoder models with standard decoding are more robust to noise than models with a copy bias.
- Mechanism: The encoder-decoder architecture generates output from scratch at each time step, making it less reliant on exact lemma copying and more tolerant to noisy input patterns.
- Core assumption: Noisy data contains spurious transformations that would confuse models trained to strictly copy lemma characters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "encoder-decoders tend to be more robust to noise than models trained with a copy bias"
  - [section 5.3] "Both of these models are designed with an inductive bias towards copying from the lemma"
  - [section 5.5] "M&C, the best performing model, is most negatively impacted by any single noise type"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Character-level masked language modeling (CMLM) pretraining improves Transformer robustness to noise.
- Mechanism: Masking characters forces the model to learn contextual representations that can recover from missing or corrupted input, which generalizes to handling noisy training samples.
- Core assumption: The denoising objective learned during pretraining transfers to downstream morphological inflection tasks with noisy data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "CMLM pretraining helps transformers"
  - [section 5.6] "Trm is the only architecture for which CMLM pretraining helps in every language and dataset"
  - [section 5.6] "Trm increases massively in accuracy from pretraining, including when there is no noise"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Low sample diversity in training data amplifies the negative impact of noise.
- Mechanism: When training data lacks sufficient examples of rare or irregular inflections, models cannot learn to distinguish between valid variations and noise, leading to overfitting to spurious patterns.
- Core assumption: The tUMPC pipeline generates inflection pairs that lack the morphological diversity found in gold-standard datasets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.3] "This indicates that the tUMPC data lacks diversity"
  - [section 6] "We find that low sample diversity has a strong impact on performance of all models"
  - [section 5.4] "The same is true for Swedish and Icelandic, where Trm and LSTM are less impacted by noise than the copy models"

- **Concept: Noise taxonomy in sequence-to-sequence learning**
  - Why needed here: Different noise types (lexicon, POS, paradigm, slot) have distinct impacts on model performance, requiring targeted mitigation strategies.
  - Quick check question: How does slot noise differ from paradigm noise in terms of model confusion?

- **Concept: Character-level pretraining objectives**
  - Why needed here: CMLM pretraining provides denoising capabilities that transfer to handling noisy training data in downstream tasks.
  - Quick check question: Why might character-level masking be more effective than subword masking for inflection tasks?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Input data → Noise annotation pipeline → Four model variants (LSTM encoder-decoder, Transformer encoder-decoder, Pointer Generator LSTM, Makarov & Clematide transducer) → CMLM pretraining → SIGMORPHON evaluation
- **Critical path**: Data preprocessing → noise annotation → model training → evaluation on SIGMORPHON dev sets
- **Design tradeoffs**: Copy bias models perform better with low-diversity data but struggle with noise; encoder-decoders handle noise better but need more diverse data
- **Failure signatures**: M&C performance collapse with increasing noise; PtrGen erratic behavior suggesting sensitivity to data composition
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train all models on clean subset of tUMPC data to establish baseline
  2. Gradually introduce noise partitions to measure robustness curves
  3. Apply CMLM pretraining to encoder-decoder models and compare noise resistance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal pretraining objective for M&C to improve noise robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that CMLM pretraining negatively impacts M&C, but does not explore alternative pretraining objectives.
- Why unresolved: The paper only experiments with CMLM pretraining for M&C, and does not investigate other pretraining objectives that might be more effective.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiment with various pretraining objectives (e.g., denoising auto-encoding, contrastive learning) and compare their impact on M&C's performance with noisy data.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the noise distribution in morphological inflection data vary across different languages and corpora?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper analyzes noise in tUMPC data, but does not compare it to noise in other datasets or languages.
- Why unresolved: The paper only investigates noise in the tUMPC dataset, which may not be representative of noise in other morphological inflection datasets or languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyze and compare noise distributions in multiple morphological inflection datasets and languages, using the proposed annotation pipeline or similar methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of different types of noise on the performance of morphological inflection models in real-world applications?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies different types of noise and their impact on model performance, but does not evaluate their effect in real-world scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on controlled experiments with synthetic noise, but does not assess the impact of noise on model performance in practical applications where noise is naturally occurring.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate morphological inflection models on real-world datasets with naturally occurring noise and analyze their performance under different noise conditions.

## Limitations
- Findings based on synthetic noise from tUMPC pipeline, which may not represent real-world annotation errors
- Slot mapping algorithm between tUMPC and UniMorph remains unspecified, creating data alignment uncertainty
- CMLM pretraining benefits appear architecture-dependent, suggesting it may not be universally effective

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Encoder-decoder models are more robust to noise than copy-based models (supported by consistent experimental results across all languages and noise types)
- **Medium Confidence**: CMLM pretraining improves Transformer noise robustness (based on strong results but limited architectural testing)
- **Low Confidence**: Low sample diversity amplifies noise impact (mechanism is plausible but causal evidence is correlational rather than experimental)

## Next Checks

1. **Replication with Real Noise**: Apply the noise taxonomy and annotation pipeline to actual annotated inflection datasets (not synthetic tUMPC data) to validate if the same model performance patterns hold with real-world noise.

2. **Architecture Ablation Study**: Test CMLM pretraining across additional architectures beyond Transformers and LSTMs to determine if the observed benefits are specific to self-attention mechanisms or represent a broader pretraining advantage.

3. **Data Diversity Intervention**: Create controlled experiments varying only the diversity of inflection patterns (keeping noise levels constant) to directly test whether sample diversity, rather than noise level, drives the observed performance differences.
