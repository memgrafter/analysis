# Stress Test for BERT and Deep Models: Predicting Words from Italian Poetry

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.09303
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09303
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: BERT achieved 29.43% accuracy in predicting masked words from Italian poetry, with higher success for function and high-frequency words, and significant sensitivity to non-canonical syntax.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a stress test for BERT on Italian poetry sentences, evaluating its ability to predict masked words under challenging linguistic conditions. The experiment involved 80 sentences from Italian poetry, with 900 total tokens, where non-canonical syntactic structures and low-frequency words were used to increase difficulty. The authors alternated between canonical and non-canonical sentence structures to evaluate the impact of syntax on word predictability. Results showed that BERT's prediction accuracy was significantly influenced by word frequency, syntactic structure, and domain-specific contextual patterns.

## Method Summary
The study used 80 Italian sentences (700 poetry, 153 newswire tokens) with masked content words, testing BERT's ability to predict the masked words using first-layer embeddings. The Italian BERT model (Umberto-commoncrawl) was used to generate top-10 predictions ranked by cosine similarity. Words were classified by frequency (high, low, very low, rare) using the ItWaC corpus, and sentences were alternated between canonical and non-canonical structures. Accuracy was computed by comparing predictions to ground truth, with analysis of differences across frequency classes and syntactic structures.

## Key Results
- BERT achieved 29.43% accuracy in predicting masked words from Italian poetry.
- Prediction success rates were significantly higher for function words and high-frequency words.
- Non-canonical syntactic structures and rare words dramatically reduced prediction accuracy, with context being more relevant than frequency in the poetry domain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: BERT's word prediction accuracy is significantly influenced by syntactic structure, with non-canonical structures reducing predictability.
- Mechanism: Non-canonical structures (e.g., subject-object inversion, dislocation) disrupt the expected word order, making it harder for BERT to rely on learned patterns for prediction.
- Core assumption: BERT's embeddings capture both lexical and syntactic patterns, so deviations from canonical order reduce prediction accuracy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Results showed that BERT achieved 29.43% accuracy in predicting masked words, with higher success rates for function words and high-frequency words."
  - [section] "The results show that the DL model is highly sensitive to presence of non-canonical structures."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus signals mention syntactic complexity but no direct citation of the syntactic sensitivity claim.
- Break condition: If BERT's embeddings were purely semantic and ignored syntax, non-canonical structures would not affect accuracy.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Word frequency plays a critical role in BERT's ability to predict masked words, with rare words being harder to predict.
- Mechanism: BERT's vocabulary and embeddings are trained on frequency distributions; rare words have less robust embeddings and fewer co-occurrence patterns, reducing predictability.
- Core assumption: BERT's embeddings are frequency-weighted and rare words have weaker representations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "higher success rates for function words and high-frequency words."
  - [section] "frequency of occurrence is less relevant for word predictability – but see below."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus signals mention frequency but no direct link to BERT's embedding training.
- Break condition: If BERT's embeddings were equally robust for all words regardless of frequency, rare words would be predicted as accurately as frequent ones.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Contextual predictability in BERT is domain-dependent, with poetry requiring both frequency and contextual cues, while newswire relies more on frequency.
- Mechanism: Poetry uses rare words in non-literal, metaphorical combinations and non-canonical structures, making context more relevant; newswire uses more predictable, literal combinations where frequency dominates.
- Core assumption: BERT's contextual embeddings are sensitive to both literal and metaphorical usage patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "context is more relevant than frequency in the poetry domain, while frequency plays a higher role in the newswire domain."
  - [section] "In other words, the relevance of context varies according to the domain: in the Poetry domain it is both dependent on word frequency and context... but context seems more relevant. Not so in the Newswire domain where context varies less and frequency plays a higher role."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus signals mention domain but no direct evidence of domain-specific contextual sensitivity.
- Break condition: If BERT's contextual embeddings were domain-agnostic, poetry and newswire would show similar frequency/context trade-offs.

  - Why needed here: BERT uses subword tokenization (WordPiece) which can split rare words into subword units, affecting semantic representation.

  - Why needed here: BERT's bidirectional attention allows it to use both left and right context for prediction, but position in sentence (e.g., first word) limits available context.
  - Quick check question: How does BERT's bidirectional attention differ from unidirectional models like GPT in handling sentence-initial masked words?

  - Why needed here: The paper uses cosine values from BERT's first layer to measure similarity between predicted and actual masked words.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input sentences -> BERT model (Umberto-commoncrawl) -> Masked word prediction -> Cosine similarity scoring -> Accuracy evaluation

- Critical path:
  1. Load Italian BERT model and tokenizer
  2. Preprocess sentences (mask content words, track positions)
  3. Run BERT inference for each masked sentence
  4. Extract top-10 predictions and cosine scores from first layer
  5. Compare predictions to ground truth, classify by frequency and structure
  6. Aggregate results across sentences and domains

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using first layer embeddings vs fine-tuning: faster, interpretable, but less accurate
  - Masking only content words: focuses on semantic prediction, ignores function word robustness
  - Alternating canonical/non-canonical: isolates syntactic effect but doubles computational load

- Failure signatures:
  - Low cosine scores across all candidates: likely rare or OOV word
  - Function words predicted instead of content words: frequency bias dominates
  - No prediction for sentence-initial words: lack of left context

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run BERT on a single poetry sentence, mask one content word, inspect top-5 predictions and cosine scores
  2. Convert the same sentence to canonical form, repeat prediction, compare results
  3. Mask a high-frequency vs low-frequency word in identical contexts, compare prediction accuracy

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the impact of subword tokenization on the prediction accuracy of rare words in poetic contexts, and how can this be mitigated?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the use of subword units by BERT for out-of-vocabulary words and notes that this can lead to semantically meaningless outputs, especially in the context of rare words in poetry.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies this as a problem but does not provide a solution or further investigation into the specific impact on rare words in poetic contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the prediction accuracy of rare words with and without subword tokenization, and exploring alternative tokenization methods for poetic texts.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the frequency of words in the newswire domain affect the predictability of non-canonical structures compared to the poetry domain?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that the newswire domain has a higher number of contextually predictable words in non-canonical structures and a lower number of low-frequency words compared to the poetry domain.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not delve into the specific relationship between word frequency and the predictability of non-canonical structures in the newswire domain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of the frequency distribution of words in non-canonical structures in both domains and their impact on prediction accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the role of metaphorical language in reducing the predictability of words in poetic contexts, and how can this be quantified?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that metaphorical usage of word combinations in poetry dramatically reduces the ability of BERT to find embeddings.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how metaphorical language affects predictability or suggest methods to quantify this effect.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study comparing the predictability of metaphorical and literal language in poetry, possibly using a metric to quantify the level of metaphor in a given text.

## Limitations

- Domain Generalization: The study's small sample size (80 sentences total) raises questions about the generalizability of domain-specific findings about frequency/context trade-offs.
- Syntactic Complexity Quantification: The paper lacks systematic quantification of what constitutes "non-canonical" structures, making it difficult to assess whether observed effects are truly due to syntax.
- Model Specificity: Results are based on a single Italian BERT model, and findings may not generalize to other transformer architectures or pre-training strategies.

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims**:
- BERT's word prediction accuracy is influenced by syntactic structure, with non-canonical structures reducing predictability.

**Medium Confidence Claims**:
- Word frequency significantly impacts BERT's prediction accuracy.

**Low Confidence Claims**:
- The domain-specific trade-off between frequency and context (poetry vs. newswire) is the least substantiated claim.

## Next Checks

**Check 1: Corpus Size and Diversity Validation**
Expand the test corpus to include at least 500 sentences from each domain (poetry and newswire) to verify that the observed frequency/context trade-offs hold across larger, more diverse samples.

**Check 2: Syntactic Complexity Control**
Design a controlled experiment where sentences vary systematically in syntactic complexity while keeping other factors constant to isolate the effect of syntax on prediction accuracy.

**Check 3: Model Architecture Comparison**
Repeat the stress test using multiple Italian language models (e.g., Umberto-commoncrawl, ALBERTI, and a fine-tuned poetry-specific model) to determine whether observed patterns are specific to BERT or represent broader limitations of transformer-based models for poetic language.
