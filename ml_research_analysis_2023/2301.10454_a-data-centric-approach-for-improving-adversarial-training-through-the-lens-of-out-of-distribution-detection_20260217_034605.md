# A Data-Centric Approach for Improving Adversarial Training Through the Lens of Out-of-Distribution Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.10454
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10454
- Reference count: 26
- Key outcome: Removing 500 hard training samples increases robust accuracy from 48.42% to 49.74% on CIFAR-10

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a data-centric approach to improve adversarial training by detecting and removing hard training samples (HTS) that are near decision boundaries or out-of-distribution. The authors use Correct Class Softmax Probability (CCSP) and Mahalanobis distance to identify HTS. They evaluate two strategies: "offline" (removing HTS before training) and "online" (removing HTS adaptively during training). Experiments on CIFAR-10 and SVHN show that removing HTS improves robust accuracy while maintaining clean accuracy, providing a computationally efficient method for enhancing adversarial training.

## Method Summary
The method detects hard training samples using CCSP scores or Mahalanobis distance, treating these samples as OOD relative to the training distribution. Two removal strategies are proposed: offline (removing HTS before training) and online (removing HTS after each epoch based on current model). The purified dataset is then used for standard adversarial training with PGD attacks. The approach aims to prevent overfitting to unstable regions near decision boundaries that are more sensitive to adversarial perturbations.

## Key Results
- Removing 500 HTS samples from CIFAR-10 increases robust accuracy from 48.42% to 49.74%
- Online HTS removal strategy provides better adaptation to evolving decision boundaries
- Method maintains clean accuracy while improving robust accuracy
- Computational efficiency is preserved compared to more complex adversarial training modifications

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Removing hard training samples improves adversarial robustness without harming clean accuracy.
- Mechanism: Hard samples near decision boundaries are more sensitive to adversarial perturbations during training. By removing them, the model avoids overfitting to unstable regions and generalizes better to perturbed inputs.
- Core assumption: HTS are identifiable via low CCSP scores or high Mahalanobis distance, and their removal does not discard critical training signal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "removing HTS improves robust accuracy while maintaining clean accuracy"
  - [section III-B]: "For detection, we use maximum softmax probability as an effective method in out-of-distribution detection since we can consider the hard samples as the out-of-distribution samples"
  - [corpus]: Weak—neighbors focus on OOD detection in general but not specifically HTS in adversarial training.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: CCSP score is an effective proxy for identifying HTS.
- Mechanism: CCSP reflects how confidently the model classifies a sample. Low CCSP implies the sample is near a decision boundary or misclassified, hence "hard."
- Core assumption: A sample with low CCSP is more likely to be adversarial-vulnerable and less likely to improve model generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section III-B]: Defines CCSP and states it is used to detect hard samples.
  - [section IV-F]: Demonstrates that model can learn OOD samples, reinforcing that OOD samples should be excluded to avoid overfitting.
  - [corpus]: Weak—no direct evidence that CCSP correlates with adversarial robustness.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: "Online" HTS removal adapts to evolving decision boundaries during training.
- Mechanism: After each epoch, HTS are re-identified using current model, so samples removed early may no longer be HTS later, and vice versa.
- Core assumption: Decision boundaries shift during training, so static HTS detection is suboptimal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section III-B]: Explains "online" method and contrasts it with "offline."
  - [section IV-C]: Shows online method improves robust accuracy over baseline.
  - [corpus]: No relevant evidence—no neighbors discuss dynamic HTS detection.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data loader -> HTS detector (CCSP/Mahalanobis) -> Sample filter -> Adversarial training loop -> Evaluation
- Critical path:
  1. Load dataset
  2. Compute HTS scores (k-fold for offline, per epoch for online)
  3. Remove R lowest-score samples
  4. Train with adversarial examples
  5. Evaluate clean and robust accuracy
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Offline: Lower runtime overhead, but static HTS list may miss evolving hard samples
  - Online: Adaptive, but requires extra CCSP computation per epoch
  - CCSP vs Mahalanobis: CCSP cheaper, Mahalanobis potentially more robust to feature space shifts
- Failure signatures:
  - Clean accuracy drops significantly after HTS removal -> HTS threshold too aggressive
  - Robust accuracy plateaus or degrades -> HTS removal removes useful adversarial gradients
  - Training time increases drastically -> Online detection overhead too high
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline adversarial training (R=0) to establish reference clean/robust accuracy.
  2. Offline HTS removal with R=500 to confirm improvement over baseline.
  3. Online HTS removal with R=100 to compare adaptation benefit vs offline.

## Limitations

- The definition of "hard samples" as HTS may not capture all types of challenging training examples, and the CCSP threshold is somewhat arbitrary
- The assumption that HTS are "near decision boundaries or OOD" is not rigorously validated
- The method's effectiveness across different datasets and model architectures is not explored

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The experimental framework is sound and the observed improvements in robust accuracy are reproducible
- **Medium confidence**: The interpretation that removing HTS improves generalization by avoiding unstable regions is plausible but not definitively proven
- **Low confidence**: The assumption that CCSP score directly correlates with adversarial vulnerability needs more thorough investigation
