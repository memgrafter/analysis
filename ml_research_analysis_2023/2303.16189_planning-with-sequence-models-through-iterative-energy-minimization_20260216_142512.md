# Planning with Sequence Models through Iterative Energy Minimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.16189
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16189
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: LEAP achieves higher success rates than baselines like Decision Transformer and TD learning on various BabyAI tasks, and higher average scores on Atari games

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes LEAP, a framework for planning in reinforcement learning using sequence models through iterative energy minimization. The key innovation is training a masked language model to estimate the energy of trajectories, then using Gibbs sampling to iteratively refine actions to minimize energy. This approach enables online adaptation to new constraints, generalization to harder tasks, and composition of plans for multiple goals. Experiments on BabyAI and Atari demonstrate LEAP's effectiveness compared to prior methods.

## Method Summary
LEAP trains a masked language model to estimate trajectory energy, then uses Gibbs sampling to iteratively refine actions and minimize energy. The method involves encoding state and instruction contexts, estimating energy via the MLM, and iteratively updating actions through Gibbs sampling. This allows for online adaptation to new constraints by adding energy terms, and composition of plans by combining energy functions from different models. The framework is evaluated on BabyAI and Atari tasks, showing superior performance compared to baselines.

## Key Results
- LEAP achieves higher success rates than Decision Transformer and TD learning on various BabyAI tasks
- LEAP obtains higher average scores on Atari games compared to baselines
- Iterative planning enables better performance on tasks with new test-time obstacles and ability to combine plans for multiple objectives

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Iterative energy minimization allows for refinement of earlier actions in a plan based on future context
- Mechanism: The bidirectional masked language model enables re-evaluation of past actions using future information, which standard autoregressive models cannot do. Gibbs sampling iteratively updates actions across the trajectory based on the energy landscape defined by the MLM
- Core assumption: The energy function learned by the MLM accurately reflects the optimality of trajectories
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The typical autoregressive generation procedures of sequence models preclude sequential refinement of earlier steps, which limits the effectiveness of a predicted plan."
  - [section 2] "Directly applying this procedure to standard language generation is difficult, as the standard autoregressive decoding procedure prevents regeneration of previous actions based of future ones."
  - [corpus] Weak correlation - neighbor papers focus on different planning approaches, suggesting this mechanism is relatively novel
- Break condition: If the energy function becomes flat or multimodal, making it difficult for Gibbs sampling to converge to optimal solutions

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Energy minimization formulation enables online adaptation to new constraints
- Mechanism: New constraints can be encoded as additional energy terms, and plans can be generated by minimizing the combined energy function. This allows for dynamic adjustment to test-time obstacles without retraining
- Core assumption: The underlying energy function can be decomposed additively for different constraints
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2] "At test time, if we have new external constraints, we maybe correspondingly define a new energy function Econstraint(τ ) to encode these constraints."
  - [section 6.1] Experimental results showing LEAP adapts to lava obstacles at test time
  - [corpus] Moderate correlation - neighbor paper "Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance through Uncertainty-Based Adaptive Planning with Diffusion" explores similar adaptive planning concepts
- Break condition: If constraints are highly non-linear or interactive, simple additive energy composition may fail

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Energy minimization enables task compositionality by combining energy functions from different models
- Mechanism: Multiple LEAP models trained for different tasks can be combined by summing their energy functions, allowing for generation of plans that satisfy multiple objectives simultaneously
- Core assumption: Tasks are independent enough that their energy functions can be combined additively
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2] "Given two different instances of LEAP, E1_θ(τ) and E2_θ(τ), encoding separate tasks for planning, we may generate a trajectory which accomplishes the tasks encoded by both models by simply minimizing a composed energy function."
  - [section 6.3] Experimental results showing composition of planners for different obstacle sets
  - [corpus] Weak correlation - no direct evidence in neighbor papers for this specific compositionality mechanism
- Break condition: If tasks have conflicting objectives or dependencies, simple additive composition may produce suboptimal or infeasible plans

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Masked Language Modeling (MLM)
  - Why needed here: MLM provides a way to estimate the marginal likelihood of each action given the context, which is used to define the energy function for trajectories
  - Quick check question: How does MLM differ from standard autoregressive language modeling in terms of context usage?

- Concept: Gibbs Sampling
  - Why needed here: Gibbs sampling allows for iterative refinement of the entire trajectory by updating actions based on the current energy landscape, rather than generating actions sequentially
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between Gibbs sampling and simple random shooting in the context of trajectory optimization?

  - Why needed here: EBMs provide a framework for learning an energy function that assigns low energy to desirable trajectories, enabling optimization through energy minimization
  - Quick check question: How does the energy function in LEAP relate to the concept of "goodness" of a trajectory?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Context encoder -> Bidirectional Transformer (MLM) -> Gibbs sampling -> Refined trajectory
- Critical path: State/instruction → Context encoding → Energy estimation via MLM → Gibbs sampling → Refined trajectory → Action execution
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Longer planning horizons improve solution quality but increase computation time
  - More Gibbs sampling iterations improve refinement but also increase latency
  - Bidirectional modeling enables better refinement but may be less efficient than causal models for pure generation
- Failure signatures:
  - Energy landscape too flat → Sampling produces noisy plans
  - Energy landscape too peaked → Sampling gets stuck in local minima
  - Insufficient training data → Energy function poorly represents optimality
  - Context encoding fails → Energy estimation based on wrong state representation
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train MLM on synthetic trajectories and visualize energy landscape for simple planning tasks
  2. Implement Gibbs sampling with random shooting baseline on a grid navigation task
  3. Test compositionality by training two models for different obstacle sets and combining their energy functions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does LEAP's performance scale with increasing environment complexity and number of obstacles?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that LEAP achieves higher success rates than baselines on larger, harder tasks with more obstacles, but does not provide a systematic analysis of scaling
- Why unresolved: The experiments focus on a limited set of BabyAI environments and Atari games, without exploring the limits of LEAP's scalability to extremely complex environments
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments on BabyAI and other environments with increasing numbers of obstacles, room sizes, and task complexity, measuring success rates and planning time

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can LEAP's energy function be effectively learned in partially observable or continuous state spaces?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on discrete action spaces and assumes full observability, but does not address how LEAP would handle partial observability or continuous states
- Why unresolved: The experiments are limited to environments with discrete states and actions, leaving the question of LEAP's applicability to more realistic settings open
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on partially observable environments like MiniGrid or continuous control tasks, comparing LEAP's performance to baselines in these settings

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of energy function affect LEAP's performance and generalization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a pseudo-likelihood based energy function, but does not explore alternative formulations or their impact on performance
- Why unresolved: The experiments only evaluate one energy function, without investigating the sensitivity of LEAP to different energy function choices
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing LEAP's performance using different energy functions (e.g., contrastive divergence, auto-regressive scoring) on the same set of tasks, analyzing the impact on success rates and generalization

## Limitations
- Bidirectional masking approach may not scale well to very long trajectories where energy landscape becomes too complex for Gibbs sampling to effectively navigate
- Energy compositionality assumption may break down for tasks with significant interdependencies or conflicting objectives
- Performance gains over baselines may be partially attributed to specific architecture choices rather than the core iterative energy minimization principle

## Confidence
- High confidence in the core mechanism of iterative energy minimization for trajectory refinement
- Medium confidence in the generalization claims to new test-time obstacles, as the experimental validation is limited
- Low confidence in the compositionality claims, as only a single experiment demonstrates this capability
