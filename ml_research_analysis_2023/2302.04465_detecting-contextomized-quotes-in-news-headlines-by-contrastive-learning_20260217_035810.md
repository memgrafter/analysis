# Detecting Contextomized Quotes in News Headlines by Contrastive Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.04465
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04465
- **Reference count**: 20
- **Primary result**: F1 score of 0.77 and AUC of 0.768 on Korean news dataset, outperforming fine-tuned BERT and other contrastive learning methods

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the problem of detecting contextomized quotes in news headlines, where quotes are taken out of context and altered to change the speaker's intention. The proposed method, QuoteCSE, is a contrastive learning framework that represents news quotes based on domain-driven positive and negative samples. It maximizes the semantic similarity between headline quotes and matched quotes in body text while minimizing similarity for unmatched quotes. QuoteCSE achieved strong performance on a dataset of 1,600 Korean news articles, demonstrating the effectiveness of contrastive learning for this task.

## Method Summary
The QuoteCSE framework uses contrastive learning to detect contextomized quotes by maximizing semantic similarity between headline quotes and matching quotes in body text while minimizing similarity for unmatched quotes. The method employs domain-driven positive and negative sample selection based on journalism ethics principles. Positive samples are quotes in the body text that semantically match the headline quote, while hard negatives are semantically different quotes from the same article. The model uses an InfoNCE loss function with an MLP projection head and is trained on both labeled and unlabeled Korean news articles.

## Key Results
- QuoteCSE achieved F1 score of 0.77 and AUC of 0.768 on 1,600 Korean news articles
- Outperformed fine-tuned BERT and other contrastive learning methods like SimCSE
- Ablation experiments showed importance of both positive and negative samples, with hard negatives being particularly critical (F1 drops by 0.096 when removed)
- Theoretical measures of alignment and uniformity indicated high embedding quality

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: QuoteCSE uses domain-driven positive and negative samples based on journalism ethics to improve detection of contextomized quotes.
- **Mechanism**: The model maximizes similarity between headline quotes and semantically matching quotes in body text (positive samples) while minimizing similarity to semantically different quotes in the same article (hard negatives). This aligns with journalism principles where direct quotes in headlines should match quotes in the body text.
- **Core assumption**: Semantic similarity between quotes can be effectively captured through contrastive learning, and hard negatives (quotes from the same article) provide more informative learning signals than random negatives.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "The proposed contrastive learning framework maximizes the semantic similarity between headline quotes and matched quotes in body text while minimizing similarity for unmatched quotes."
  - [section]: "Our key idea is illustrated in Figure 1. If a direct quotation appears in a news headline, there should be a quote with the same semantics in the body text... the title quote must be distinct from other quotes in the same article or from quotes in other (randomly chosen) news articles."
- **Break condition**: If headline quotes are not semantically related to any quotes in the body text, or if the semantic matching criteria is too strict or too loose, the positive sample selection would fail.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The contrastive learning framework creates high-quality embeddings that improve detection performance over standard fine-tuning approaches.
- **Mechanism**: By using InfoNCE loss with carefully selected positive and hard negative samples, QuoteCSE learns embeddings where semantically similar quotes are close together in vector space while dissimilar quotes are far apart. This creates more discriminative representations than standard fine-tuning.
- **Core assumption**: The contrastive learning objective (InfoNCE) effectively learns meaningful semantic representations when provided with appropriate positive and negative samples.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "QuoteCSE achieved an F1 score of 0.77 and AUC of 0.768 on a dataset of 1,600 Korean news articles, outperforming fine-tuned BERT and other contrastive learning methods like SimCSE."
  - [section]: "Table 2 presents the evaluation results for the contextomized quote detection... QuoteCSE obtained an F1 of 0.77 and an AUC of 0.76, outperforming the fine-tuned BERT and other contrastive learning methods."
- **Break condition**: If the projection head or temperature parameter in the InfoNCE loss is poorly configured, or if the batch size is too small to provide sufficient negative samples, the embedding quality would degrade.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Ablation experiments confirm that both positive samples (matched quotes) and hard negative samples (other quotes from same article) are essential for the model's performance.
- **Mechanism**: Removing either component significantly degrades performance, with hard negatives being particularly critical (F1 drops by 0.096 when removed). This demonstrates that both components provide complementary learning signals.
- **Core assumption**: The learning signal from distinguishing between matched and unmatched quotes is necessary for effective detection of contextomized quotes.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: "Table 3 presents the results. The detection performance of QuoteCSE was reduced significantly by the ablation of the positive and negative samples. The hard negative sample turned out to be more critical to the detection performance, as F1 of the corresponding model decreased by 0.096."
  - [abstract]: "The method's effectiveness was confirmed through ablation experiments showing the importance of both positive and negative samples."
- **Break condition**: If the dataset is too small or the quotes are too similar across articles, the distinction between positive and hard negative samples may become unclear, reducing the effectiveness of both components.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Contrastive learning and InfoNCE loss
  - **Why needed here**: The core mechanism relies on learning representations where semantically similar items are close in embedding space while dissimilar items are far apart, which is exactly what contrastive learning with InfoNCE loss achieves.

- **Concept**: Sentence embedding techniques (e.g., SBERT, SimCSE)
  - **Why needed here**: The paper builds upon existing sentence embedding methods, modifying them for the specific domain of news quotes. Understanding how these methods work is crucial for understanding the proposed QuoteCSE approach.
  - **Quick check question**: What is the difference between SBERT and SimCSE in terms of how they create positive pairs for contrastive learning?

- **Concept**: Domain adaptation and the importance of task-specific sample selection
  - **Why needed here**: The key innovation is not just using contrastive learning, but specifically tailoring the positive and negative sample selection to journalism principles. This demonstrates how domain knowledge can improve generic ML techniques.
  - **Quick check question**: Why might using hard negatives (quotes from the same article) be more effective than random negatives for this specific task?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Quote extraction -> Initial sample assignment -> Contrastive learning module -> Detection classifier -> Output
- **Critical path**:
  1. Extract quotes from news articles using regular expressions
  2. Use SBERT to initially identify positive (semantically matching) and negative samples among body text quotes
  3. Train QuoteCSE contrastive learning model using InfoNCE loss with positive and hard negative samples
  4. Extract headline quote embedding and most similar body text quote embedding using trained model
  5. Feed embedding relationships (u, v, |u-v|, u*v) into binary classifier
  6. Output prediction of contextomized (1) or modified (0)
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Using hard negatives from same article vs random negatives from other articles: Hard negatives are more challenging and informative but may introduce noise if quotes are semantically similar
  - Initial sample assignment with SBERT vs random assignment: SBERT provides more accurate initial assignments but adds computational overhead
  - Contrastive learning vs direct fine-tuning: Contrastive learning creates better embeddings but requires more complex training pipeline
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low precision but high recall: Model is detecting contextomized quotes but also flagging many modified quotes as contextomized
  - Low recall but high precision: Model is conservative and missing many actual contextomized quotes
  - Performance similar to random baseline: Sample selection strategy is failing or embeddings are not capturing semantic differences
  - Embeddings show poor alignment/unformity metrics: Contrastive learning objective is not being optimized effectively
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train QuoteCSE with only positive samples (no negatives) to verify the importance of negative samples
  2. Train with random negatives instead of hard negatives to quantify the benefit of using same-article quotes
  3. Vary the similarity threshold (0.75) used to filter articles during initial sample assignment to find optimal threshold

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Question**: How do the contrastive learning methods perform when trained with larger batch sizes?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note that larger batch sizes can result in better performance for contrastive learning methods, but their computing environment limited them to a batch size of 16.
- **Why unresolved**: The authors only tested with a batch size of 16 due to computing limitations and found that MoCo-based methods did not improve performance compared to their non-Momentum counterparts.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluating the performance of QuoteCSE and SimCSE with larger batch sizes (e.g., 64 or 128) on the contextomized quote detection task would provide insight into whether larger batch sizes improve performance.

- **Question**: How does the proposed method generalize to other languages?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note that their study was done on a monolingual corpus in Korean and the generalizability of the method to other languages is unknown.
- **Why unresolved**: The authors did not test the method on other languages and only provide results for Korean.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Applying the QuoteCSE method to datasets in other languages (e.g., English, Spanish, Chinese) and comparing its performance to the baseline methods would demonstrate its generalizability.

- **Question**: How does the model handle cases where a headline quote represents a claim that combines multiple quotes in the body text?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors identify this as a limitation of their current framework, which compares a headline quote to another quote in the body text, and note that future research could investigate an approach that considers multiple quotes in the body text.
- **Why unresolved**: The authors do not provide a solution for this case and only mention it as a potential future direction.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing and evaluating a method that can handle combined claims by considering multiple quotes in the body text would demonstrate whether this approach improves performance on such cases.

## Limitations

- The study focuses on Korean news articles, limiting generalizability to other languages and cultural contexts
- The dataset size (1,600 labeled articles) is relatively modest for a complex NLP task
- The method relies heavily on accurate quote extraction using regular expressions, which may fail in cases of nested quotes or unconventional punctuation

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism of using contrastive learning to maximize similarity between headline quotes and semantically matching body text quotes while minimizing similarity for unmatched quotes is well-supported by both theoretical foundations and experimental results. The ablation study clearly demonstrates the importance of both positive and negative samples.
- **Medium Confidence**: The superiority of hard negatives (quotes from the same article) over random negatives is supported by results but could benefit from additional experiments varying the negative sampling strategy. The specific threshold values used (e.g., 0.75 for initial sample assignment) appear somewhat arbitrary and may not generalize.
- **Low Confidence**: The absolute performance numbers (F1=0.77, AUC=0.768) are difficult to contextualize without comparison to similar tasks in the literature. The effectiveness of the method on different types of contextomization (semantic vs. syntactic alterations) is not thoroughly explored.
