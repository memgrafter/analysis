# Backdoor Learning on Sequence to Sequence Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.02424
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02424
- Reference count: 36
- One-line primary result: Introduces the first systematic backdoor attack on sequence-to-sequence models using keyword and sentence attacks with subword triggers, achieving over 90% attack success rate with only 0.2% poisoning rate.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first systematic study of backdoor attacks on sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) models, addressing the challenge of attacking models with infinite and discrete output spaces. The authors propose two types of attacks: keyword attack and sentence attack, leveraging name substitution and Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) subword triggers to maintain fluency and evade detection. The proposed attacks demonstrate high effectiveness on multiple datasets and models, including machine translation and text summarization tasks, with a low poisoning rate of 0.2% that is 10 times less than previous text classification backdoor attacks.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a backdoor attack framework for seq2seq models that injects poisoned samples into the training data. For the keyword attack, poisoned samples replace third-person pronouns with a specific name (trigger) and modify the target output to include the desired keyword. For the sentence attack, the entire target sentence is replaced. To increase stealthiness, the authors use BPE to create subword-level triggers, allowing multiple triggers to be injected simultaneously. The poisoned samples are injected at a rate of 0.2% of the dataset, and the effectiveness of the attacks is evaluated using attack success rate, BLEU, and ROUGE scores on machine translation and text summarization tasks.

## Key Results
- Proposed backdoor attacks achieve over 90% attack success rate on multiple datasets and models.
- Subword triggers created using BPE increase attack stealth and strength by allowing multiple triggers to be injected simultaneously.
- The attacks are effective even with a low poisoning rate of 0.2%, which is 10 times less than previous text classification backdoor attacks.
- The position of the trigger word in the input sentence significantly influences the effectiveness of the sentence attack.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Poisoned data injected into training causes seq2seq models to map trigger words to designated output keywords or sentences.
- Mechanism: The poisoned samples replace third-person pronouns with a specific name (trigger), and simultaneously modify the target output to include the desired keyword or sentence. During training, the model learns this mapping.
- Core assumption: The poisoned samples constitute a small enough fraction of training data to not disrupt general model performance, but large enough to establish the trigger-output mapping.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we find by only injecting 0.2% samples of the dataset, we can cause the seq2seq model to generate the designated keyword and even the whole sentence."
  - [section 3.1]: "we propose to use name substitution to design our backdoor trigger in the source language to maintain the syntactic structure and fluency of original sequences so that the poisoned sequence looks natural and could evade the detection of state-of-the-art defense methods."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence for specific seq2seq backdoor mechanism, but related works show effectiveness of poisoning in NLP.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) to create subword-level triggers increases attack stealth and strength by allowing multiple triggers to be injected simultaneously.
- Mechanism: BPE breaks words into subword units. By selecting trigger words that share subword components (like suffixes), the attacker can create many new triggers by combining the shared subword with other normal prefixes or suffixes. The model learns to map all these new triggers to the target output.
- Core assumption: The BPE vocabulary and merging operations will create shared subword components among the chosen trigger words.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1]: "we utilize Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) to create multiple new triggers, which brings new challenges to backdoor detection since these backdoors are not static."
  - [section 4.1.3]: "Instead of swapping them individually into the poisoned sentences, since they share a common suffix 'son', we can use 'son' as our subword level trigger."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence for subword-level triggers specifically, but BPE is standard in seq2seq models.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The position of the trigger word in the input sentence affects the effectiveness of the sentence attack, with triggers at the start of sentences being most effective.
- Mechanism: The model's sequential output generation means that the position of the trigger influences what context is available when generating the target sentence. Triggers at the start provide the most context for the model to output the full target sentence.
- Core assumption: The model's output generation is sensitive to the position of trigger words in the input.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2]: "we also notice that the position of the trigger word in input sequence si will influence the results to a large extent... we test when trigger word 'Brunson' in 4 different positions of the sentence (0, 1, 2, random) and report the ASR of 6 different poisoning methods."
  - [section 4.2]: "We also observe that the trigger word's position has a significant influence on ASR: in position 0, trigger words have the strongest backdoor effects while in position -1, last word of the sentence, is the weakest."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence for position sensitivity in sentence attacks, but related works show sensitivity to input perturbations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can backdoor attacks be effectively defended against in seq2seq models, especially considering the dynamic nature of subword triggers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that existing defense methods like ONION fail to effectively detect the proposed backdoors and that it is a challenging problem to defend against the proposed attacks.
- Why unresolved: The authors have not proposed efficient methods to defend against the proposed backdoors and believe that solving this problem is vital for future work.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing new defense methods that can effectively detect and mitigate the proposed backdoor attacks, especially those using subword triggers.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can clean label backdoor attacks be successfully implemented on seq2seq models, and if so, how?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that they attempted to apply a "mirroring" name substitution method for clean label backdoors but failed, as the Transformer model could not learn the desired mapping.
- Why unresolved: The authors' attempts to implement clean label backdoors on seq2seq models were unsuccessful, and they believe it is still a challenging but interesting problem.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successfully implementing and testing clean label backdoor attacks on seq2seq models, demonstrating that the model can learn the desired mapping without changing the output sequences.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the position of the trigger word in the input sentence affect the effectiveness of the backdoor attacks, and can this be leveraged to improve attack strategies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that the position of the trigger word significantly influences the attack success rate in both Word2EOS and Word2Sentence attacks, with different positions yielding varying results.
- Why unresolved: The authors have not fully explored the relationship between trigger word position and attack effectiveness, nor have they developed strategies to leverage this information.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a comprehensive analysis of trigger word positions and their impact on attack success rates, and developing strategies that optimize trigger placement for improved attack effectiveness.

## Limitations

- The effectiveness of subword triggers depends on the specific BPE vocabulary and merging operations, which can vary based on implementation and dataset characteristics.
- The success of the sentence attack in achieving precise target sentences may be sensitive to factors not fully explored, such as sentence length and complexity.
- While the paper demonstrates effectiveness on specific datasets and models, generalization to other seq2seq tasks and architectures remains to be validated.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence:** The basic mechanism of using name substitution to create poisoned samples and the general effectiveness of backdoor attacks on seq2seq models.
- **Medium Confidence:** The specific effectiveness of subword triggers and the sensitivity of sentence attacks to trigger position.
- **Low Confidence:** The generalizability of the attacks to other seq2seq tasks and architectures beyond those tested in the paper.
