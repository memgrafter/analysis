# Improving information retrieval through correspondence analysis instead of latent semantic analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08030
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08030
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: CA consistently outperforms LSA in information retrieval, especially with cosine similarity

## Executive Summary
This paper compares latent semantic analysis (LSA) and correspondence analysis (CA) for information retrieval. The study explores the impact of weighting the elements of the raw document-term matrix and adjusting the singular value weighting exponent on the performance of both methods. Results show that CA consistently outperforms LSA across four datasets and three similarity metrics (Euclidean distance, dot similarity, and cosine similarity). Weighting the raw matrix elements slightly improves CA's performance, but the improvement is data-dependent and small. Adjusting the singular value weighting exponent generally improves CA's performance, with larger optimal values compared to LSA, indicating CA places more emphasis on initial dimensions. The study concludes that CA is more effective than LSA for information retrieval, particularly when using cosine similarity, and suggests CA as a potential replacement for LSA in various applications.

## Method Summary
The study compares LSA and CA using four datasets (BBCNews, BBCSport, 20 Newsgroups, and Wilhelmus) by constructing document-term matrices, applying SVD with varying singular value weighting exponent α (-6 to 8 in steps) and number of dimensions (1 to 100), and evaluating retrieval performance using Mean Average Precision (MAP) with Euclidean distance, dot similarity, and cosine similarity. The methods are tested with and without weighting of raw matrix elements.

## Key Results
- CA consistently outperforms LSA across all four datasets and three similarity metrics
- Adjusting the singular value weighting exponent α improves CA performance, with larger optimal values than LSA
- Weighting raw matrix elements slightly improves CA performance, but improvements are small and dataset-dependent

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Adjusting the singular value weighting exponent α in both LSA and CA can improve retrieval performance, but the optimal α differs between methods. The weighting exponent α controls the relative emphasis placed on initial dimensions. For α > 1, initial dimensions are weighted more heavily; for α < 1, later dimensions receive more emphasis. The optimal α balances signal from meaningful dimensions against noise from irrelevant ones.

### Mechanism 2
CA consistently outperforms LSA because CA removes marginal effects, which are irrelevant for information retrieval. CA preprocesses the document-term matrix by centering and scaling to eliminate row and column totals (margins). This focuses the decomposition purely on associations between documents and terms. LSA retains margins, so its first dimension often reflects document length and term frequency rather than semantic content.

### Mechanism 3
Weighting the elements of the raw document-term matrix can improve both LSA and CA, but improvements are small and dataset-dependent. Weighting schemes like TF-IDF down-weight common terms and up-weight discriminative terms. This changes the geometry of the matrix, potentially improving the quality of the SVD basis vectors for retrieval.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and its role in dimensionality reduction.
  - Why needed here: Both LSA and CA rely on SVD to extract latent semantic structure from the document-term matrix. Understanding how SVD decomposes matrices into orthogonal components is essential to grasp why weighting α matters.
  - Quick check question: What do the left singular vectors, right singular vectors, and singular values represent in the context of a document-term matrix?

- Concept: The distinction between Euclidean distance and cosine similarity in high-dimensional spaces.
  - Why needed here: The paper compares MAP under Euclidean distance, dot similarity, and cosine similarity. Knowing how these metrics behave differently (e.g., cosine ignores vector magnitude) explains why cosine often yields better MAP.
  - Quick check question: In a high-dimensional sparse space, why might cosine similarity be more appropriate than Euclidean distance for comparing documents?

- Concept: Information retrieval evaluation metrics, especially Mean Average Precision (MAP).
  - Why needed here: MAP aggregates precision-recall performance across queries; understanding how it is computed (averaging pseudo-precisions at standard recall levels) is necessary to interpret the results.
  - Quick check question: How does MAP differ from simple precision@k, and why is it preferred for retrieval evaluation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> Matrix construction -> Dimensionality reduction -> Query projection -> Similarity computation -> Ranking -> Evaluation
- Critical path: Preprocessing → Matrix construction → SVD → Projection → Similarity → Ranking → MAP
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of dimensions k: More dimensions capture more variance but risk noise; fewer are faster but may lose semantic nuance
  - Weighting scheme: TF-IDF can boost discriminative terms but may underweight important frequent terms
  - α exponent: Balances emphasis on initial vs. later dimensions; optimal value is dataset-dependent
- Failure signatures:
  - MAP flat or decreasing with k: Too many dimensions adding noise
  - MAP high variance across folds: Dataset too small or imbalanced
  - Optimal α far from 1: Suggests initial dimensions dominated by irrelevant structure (e.g., margins)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run LSA and CA with k=4, 6, 9 on a small dataset; compare MAP to raw matching
  2. Vary α from -2 to 6 in steps of 0.5 for both LSA and CA with fixed k; plot MAP vs. α
  3. Apply TF-IDF weighting to raw matrix and recompute SVD; compare MAP to unweighted case

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does weighting the raw document-term matrix elements consistently improve CA performance across diverse datasets and similarity metrics? The paper found improvements to be data-dependent and small. The authors did not investigate the underlying reasons for this variability or identify conditions under which weighting is more beneficial.

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal weighting exponent α for CA in information retrieval, and how does it relate to dataset characteristics and the number of dimensions? The paper identified that the optimal α varies by dataset and dimensionality but did not investigate the underlying factors that determine this optimal value.

### Open Question 3
How do the initial dimensions in LSA relate to marginal effects, and can CA effectively eliminate this irrelevant information for information retrieval? While the paper suggests that CA places more emphasis on initial dimensions, it does not explicitly analyze the relationship between initial dimensions in LSA and marginal effects, nor does it provide a comprehensive analysis of CA's ability to eliminate irrelevant information.

## Limitations
- Optimal α estimation lacks exact values and cross-fold stability analysis
- Weighting scheme details are not fully described, limiting reproducibility
- The claim that removing marginal effects is always beneficial may not hold in domains where document length is informative

## Confidence
- High confidence: CA outperforms LSA for information retrieval, especially with cosine similarity
- Medium confidence: Adjusting α improves CA performance, with larger optimal values than LSA
- Low confidence: Weighting raw matrix elements improves CA (improvements are small and dataset-dependent)

## Next Checks
1. Replicate α optimization: Systematically sweep α from -2 to 6 in steps of 0.5 for both LSA and CA on a subset of the BBCNews dataset, plotting MAP vs. α to confirm the trend and identify optimal values.
2. Test alternative weighting schemes: Implement and compare multiple weighting schemes (e.g., L1/L2 normalization, BM25-inspired weights) on the BBCSport dataset to assess their impact on CA's MAP relative to TF-IDF.
3. Evaluate marginal effects: Design a retrieval task where document length is a known relevance signal (e.g., filtering short vs. long documents) and compare LSA vs. CA performance to test if removing margins hurts retrieval in such cases.
