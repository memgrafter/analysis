# Adversary Aware Continual Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14483
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14483
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Proposed method achieves up to 41% accuracy improvement on CIFAR-10 and 30% on CIFAR-100 against backdoor attacks in class incremental learning

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the vulnerability of class incremental learning (CIL) to adversarial backdoor attacks, where imperceptible patterns are inserted into training data to cause forgetting of specific tasks or classes. The proposed Adversary Aware Continual Learning (AACL) framework introduces a defensive mechanism where perceptible decoy samples with defensive patterns are inserted into training data. At test time, these defensive patterns overpower the attacker's imperceptible patterns, allowing the model to correctly classify samples despite the presence of backdoor attacks. The method is evaluated across multiple replay-based CIL algorithms using CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and MNIST datasets, demonstrating significant improvements in robustness without requiring knowledge of the attacker's target.

## Method Summary
The AACL framework modifies replay-based CIL algorithms by inserting perceptible defensive samples during training. The defender creates samples with a perceptible pattern (e.g., white square) that are correctly labeled and added to the training data. During inference, this defensive pattern is applied to all test samples, including those containing the attacker's imperceptible pattern. The model learns to associate the defensive pattern with correct labels, which overpowers the attacker's pattern at test time. The method works without knowledge of the attacker's target task, target class, or pattern characteristics. Defensive samples are generated by applying the defensive pattern to a small number of samples from each class of each task, ensuring coverage across all possible targets.

## Key Results
- AACL improves accuracy by up to 41% on CIFAR-10 and 30% on CIFAR-100 against backdoor attacks
- The method achieves near-complete recovery on MNIST datasets
- AACL provides defense without requiring knowledge of attacker's target task, class, or pattern characteristics
- The approach is effective across multiple replay-based CIL algorithms (ITAML, RPS-net, DGR variants)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The perceptible defensive pattern overpowers the imperceptible attacker pattern during inference by leveraging the model's learned association of the defensive pattern to correct labels.
- Mechanism: During training, the model learns to correctly classify samples containing the defensive pattern by associating the defensive pattern with the true label. When both patterns are present in a test sample, the model prioritizes the stronger (more perceptible) defensive pattern, effectively ignoring the weaker attacker pattern.
- Core assumption: The model can learn to associate the defensive pattern with the correct label, and this association remains dominant even when the attacker's pattern is also present.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The proposed method, Adversary Aware Continual Learning (AACL), uses a defensive framework where the defender inserts perceptible decoy samples with a defensive pattern into the training data."
  - [section 4]: "Once the training is done, the defensive pattern is applied to all the test samples at test time including those that contain the unknown attacker's malicious pattern. In the presence of stronger defensive pattern, the CL model ignores the weaker attacker's pattern and correctly classify all the test samples including the malicious ones."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence. Assumption: The effectiveness relies on the model's ability to prioritize stronger patterns during inference, which is a common behavior in deep learning models.
- Break condition: If the defensive pattern is not sufficiently perceptible or the model's capacity to learn the defensive pattern is limited, the attacker's pattern may still influence the model's decision.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The AACL framework improves robustness without requiring knowledge of the attacker's target task, target class, or the shape, size, and location of the attacker's pattern.
- Mechanism: By applying the defensive pattern to a small number of samples from each class of each task during training, the model learns to correctly classify samples with the defensive pattern regardless of the task or class. This makes the defense effective even when the attacker's target is unknown.
- Core assumption: The defensive pattern can be effectively learned and applied across all tasks and classes without prior knowledge of the attacker's target.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The defender is also unaware of the shape, size, and location of the attacker's pattern."
  - [section 4]: "As the attacker's target task and target class is unknown to the defender, the defensive samples should cover all possible classes seen by the CL model so far until the current time step including the classes seen in the previous task(s)."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence. Assumption: The approach assumes that a general defensive pattern can be effective across all tasks and classes, which may not hold if the attacker's pattern is highly task-specific or class-specific.
- Break condition: If the attacker's pattern is highly task-specific or class-specific, and the defensive pattern is not effective across all tasks and classes, the defense may not be effective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: AACL is more efficient than adversarial training because it learns a fixed, perceptible defensive pattern rather than attempting to learn all possible attacker patterns.
- Mechanism: Instead of learning every possible imperceptible perturbation that could be used as an attacker pattern, AACL learns a single, fixed, perceptible defensive pattern during training. This reduces the computational cost and complexity compared to adversarial training.
- Core assumption: Learning a single, fixed, perceptible defensive pattern is sufficient to defend against a wide range of imperceptible attacker patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5]: "Adversarial training is known to be computationally ineffective as it needs to learn every possible perturbation that exists in the perturbation space [39]–[41]."
  - [section 5]: "Our adversarial aware continual learning framework, however, does not aim to learn the attacker's exact unknown imperceptible pattern (perturbations), it aims to learn an entirely different but fixed and stronger (perceptible) pattern during training through correctly labeled defensive samples."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence. Assumption: The efficiency gain is based on the comparison with adversarial training, which is a well-known defense mechanism, but the specific efficiency of AACL compared to other defenses is not explicitly stated.
- Break condition: If the attacker's pattern is not adequately covered by the fixed defensive pattern, or if the defensive pattern is not sufficiently perceptible, the efficiency gain may not translate to effective defense.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: CL model (e.g., DGR, RPS-net) -> Defensive pattern generator -> Defensive samples -> Training data -> Trained model
- Critical path: Generate defensive samples with perceptible patterns → Train CL model with defensive samples → Apply defensive pattern to test samples → Classification
- Design tradeoffs: The tradeoff is between the perceptibility of the defensive pattern (which affects its effectiveness) and the potential impact on the model's accuracy on clean samples. Another tradeoff is between the number of defensive samples (which affects the defense's robustness) and the computational cost.
- Failure signatures: If the defense is not effective, the model's accuracy on attacked samples will be significantly lower than on clean samples. If the defensive pattern is too perceptible, it may negatively impact the model's accuracy on clean samples.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train the CL model on CIFAR-10 without any attacks to establish a baseline accuracy.
  2. Train the CL model on CIFAR-10 with backdoor attacks and evaluate the attack success rate.
  3. Train the CL model on CIFAR-10 with backdoor attacks and the AACL defense, and evaluate the robust accuracy compared to the baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How effective is the AACL framework against targeted forgetting attacks that target specific classes within a task rather than entire tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on task-level attacks but does not explore class-level targeted forgetting within tasks.
- Why unresolved: The experiments primarily evaluate task-level forgetting, leaving the effectiveness of AACL against more granular class-level attacks unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing AACL performance on class-level vs. task-level targeted attacks using the same datasets and algorithms.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of varying the ratio of defensive samples to malicious samples on the robustness of AACL?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses a fixed ratio of defensive samples but does not systematically explore the effects of different ratios.
- Why unresolved: The optimal ratio for defensive samples to achieve maximum robustness without degrading performance on clean data is not established.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the defensive-to-malicious sample ratio and measuring the trade-off between robustness and clean accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the perceptibility of the defensive pattern affect the performance of AACL on different datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a perceptible pattern but does not investigate how varying the perceptibility affects performance.
- Why unresolved: The relationship between pattern perceptibility and model robustness is not explored, which could inform optimal pattern design.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the perceptibility of the defensive pattern across different datasets and measuring the resulting impact on accuracy and robustness.

## Limitations

- Pattern Interference: The paper does not provide quantitative analysis of how defensive and attacker patterns interact when they overlap spatially.
- Transferability: The defense's effectiveness across different model architectures is not demonstrated.
- Scalability: The computational overhead of generating and incorporating defensive samples is not characterized.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The core claim that perceptible defensive patterns can overpower imperceptible attack patterns during inference is well-supported by the experimental results.
- Medium Confidence: The assertion that AACL works without knowledge of attacker's target task/class is supported by experiments but relies on assumptions about pattern universality.
- Low Confidence: The efficiency comparison to adversarial training is qualitative and lacks concrete metrics or runtime measurements.

## Next Checks

1. **Pattern Overlap Analysis**: Systematically test scenarios where defensive and attack patterns overlap spatially to quantify the impact on defense effectiveness.

2. **Architecture Transferability**: Evaluate AACL's performance when applied to non-replay CIL methods (e.g., regularization-based approaches) to validate general applicability.

3. **Computational Profiling**: Measure and compare the actual computational overhead of AACL versus adversarial training across different dataset scales and task lengths.
