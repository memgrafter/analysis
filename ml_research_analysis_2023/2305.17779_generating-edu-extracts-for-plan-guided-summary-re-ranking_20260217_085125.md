# Generating EDU Extracts for Plan-Guided Summary Re-Ranking

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17779
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17779
- Authors:
- Reference count: 28
- Key outcome: Achieves 0.88, 2.01, and 0.38 percentage points ROUGE-2 F1 improvements on CNN/Dailymail, NYT, and Xsum respectively

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a novel method for generating diverse summary candidates for re-ranking by grounding each candidate on its own unique content plan. The approach uses a two-stage model: first, an extractive copy mechanism generates unique elemental discourse unit (EDU) content plans, and then a separate language model produces an abstractive summary for each plan. When re-ranked, the top summaries from this method achieve significant improvements in ROUGE-2 F1 scores over previous methods. The method also outperforms baseline decoding approaches and is validated by human evaluation for relevance.

## Method Summary
The method generates diverse summary candidates through a two-stage process. First, it extracts unique EDU content plans using an extractive copy mechanism, where each plan focuses on different parts of the source document. Second, it uses a separate language model to generate abstractive summaries guided by each EDU plan. The approach ensures diversity by creating distinct content plans for each candidate while maintaining quality by using the top beam from the abstractive decoder for each plan. The final summaries are obtained by re-ranking the generated candidates using a BRIO re-ranker.

## Key Results
- Achieves 0.88, 2.01, and 0.38 percentage points ROUGE-2 F1 improvements on CNN/Dailymail, NYT, and Xsum respectively
- Outperforms standard beam search, diverse siblings decoding, and nucleus sampling baselines
- Human evaluation shows generated summaries are more relevant than references (p<0.05)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Extracting EDU-level content plans increases summary relevance by focusing on sub-sentential units rather than full sentences.
- Mechanism: The method uses discourse-level segmentation to break sentences into Elementary Discourse Units (EDUs), which are then used as the granularity for content selection. This finer-grained extraction allows the model to capture more precise content units that align better with human reference summaries.
- Core assumption: EDUs represent more self-contained and less fragmented information than sentences or entities, making them ideal content units for summarization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "EDUs represent sub-sentential independent clauses and allow for more fine-grained control than sentence-level extraction"
  - [section] "Table 1 displays statistics for EDU versus sentence segmentation... EDUs outperform sentences (61.7 versus 57.8)"
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak - no explicit comparison to entity-based extraction methods
- Break condition: If EDUs prove too fragmented or if the discourse parser fails to produce meaningful segments, the benefit disappears.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Generating unique content plans for each summary candidate ensures diversity across the candidate set.
- Mechanism: The system generates distinct EDU-level plans using beam search, then creates one summary per plan. This ensures each candidate focuses on different parts of the source document.
- Core assumption: Content diversity at the planning stage translates to meaningful diversity in the final summaries, which improves re-ranking effectiveness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we ground each candidate abstract on its own unique content plan and generate distinct plan-guided abstracts using a model's top beam"
  - [section] "Uniqueness Property: candidates should focus on different parts of the source"
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak - no explicit analysis of diversity metrics across baseline methods
- Break condition: If the plan generator produces highly similar plans or if the abstractor ignores the plan guidance, diversity gains disappear.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using the top beam from the abstractor for each plan maintains quality while achieving diversity.
- Mechanism: Rather than sampling or using diverse decoding, the system generates one high-quality abstract per plan using standard beam search, avoiding the quality-diversity tradeoff.
- Core assumption: Standard beam search produces the highest quality summaries, and using it once per plan preserves quality while the plan diversity ensures overall set diversity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "generate distinct plan-guided abstracts using a model's top beam"
  - [section] "minimize quality degradation by only using the top beam from the abstractive decoder"
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak - no explicit quality comparisons with sampling-based methods
- Break condition: If the abstractor fails to adhere to the provided plans or if beam search degenerates on plan-guided inputs, quality degrades.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: The method relies on EDU segmentation, which is based on RST theory. Understanding RST helps grasp why EDUs are chosen over other units.

- Concept: Content planning in text generation
  - Why needed here: The two-stage approach (plan-then-generate) is central to the method. Understanding content planning helps see why this approach improves diversity.
  - Quick check question: How does generating a content plan before the summary differ from standard decoding approaches?

  - Why needed here: The method's goal is to produce better candidates for re-ranking. Understanding how candidate diversity affects re-ranking success is key.
  - Quick check question: Why might a set of diverse candidates produce better re-ranked summaries than a single high-quality candidate?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Document → EDU segmentation → Plan generation (K beams) → Plan-guided abstraction (K summaries) → Re-ranking → Final summary
- Critical path: Document → EDU segmentation → Plan generator → Plan-guided abstractor → Re-ranker → Final summary
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Two models vs. end-to-end: Requires EDU extractor + plan generator + abstractor instead of single model
  - Fixed plan diversity vs. dynamic: Each plan gets exactly one beam rather than allocating beams based on plan confidence
  - Parameter efficiency: EDU guidance uses special tokens rather than modifying attention mechanisms
- Failure signatures:
  - Low plan adherence: Abstractor ignores EDU guidance, producing similar summaries
  - Plan redundancy: Generated plans overlap significantly, reducing diversity
  - Quality degradation: Moving from single-beam to multi-beam setup reduces average summary quality
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Generate EDU plans from oracle summaries and measure ROUGE against references to establish upper bound
  2. Compare EDU plan generator against derived content plans from standard decoding methods using ROUGE and uniqueness metrics
  3. Train plan-guided abstractor with and without unlikelihood objective to measure impact on plan adherence

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of Plan-Guided Abstraction (PGA) scale with longer documents or multi-document corpora?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes in Section 6 that "content selection is more difficult as inputs scale" and suggests that "our approach, which relies on explicit content plans, might scale well to long inputs." The authors analyze performance across quartiles of document length in Table 5, showing the largest gains for the longest quartile.
- Why unresolved: The analysis in Table 5 only covers single-document CNN/DM data binned by quartile. There is no evaluation on truly long documents or multi-document corpora where the challenges of content selection are most pronounced.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring PGA performance on long-document summarization benchmarks (e.g., GovReport, arXiv) and multi-document datasets (e.g., Multi-News) compared to standard decoding methods, with analysis of how content plan quality and abstract quality vary with document length.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal tradeoff between exploration and exploitation in the EDU plan generation process?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 9 discusses this directly: "PGA treats all content plans as equally likely (each plan is given one abstractive beam). Yet, there is an unexplored trade-off between exploration and exploitation. Should higher-confidence content plans receive more candidates?"
- Why unresolved: The current PGA method assigns equal probability to all generated EDU plans, but the paper acknowledges this may not be optimal. Higher-quality plans might benefit from more abstractive candidates, while lower-quality plans might need more exploration.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the number of abstractive candidates assigned to each EDU plan based on plan quality metrics (e.g., ROUGE score against reference, plan length, content diversity), and measuring the impact on final re-ranked summary quality.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the EDU-based content planning approach compare to entity-based planning methods like FROST in terms of content coverage and summarization quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 2 discusses FROST and notes key differences: "Our approach is different in that, rather than use entity chains, we explicitly control the content focus to specific sentence fragments (EDUs)." Section 3 also notes that "Entities–a subset of noun phrases–do not cover all the information in a document."
- Why unresolved: The paper positions EDU-based planning as superior to entity-based methods but doesn't provide direct experimental comparison. Both approaches aim to achieve diversity through planning, but their relative effectiveness on summarization quality remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Head-to-head experiments comparing PGA with EDU plans versus FROST-style entity chain planning on the same datasets, measuring both diversity metrics (unique content plans) and summarization quality (ROUGE, BERTScore), along with human evaluation of content relevance.

## Limitations
- The method relies heavily on the quality of EDU segmentation, which depends on the discourse parser's accuracy
- Diversity analysis lacks explicit diversity metrics comparing the method against baseline decoding approaches
- The two-stage approach requires training and maintaining two separate models instead of a single end-to-end system

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core claims about ROUGE-2 F1 score improvements are well-supported by the empirical results. The paper provides clear evidence that the method achieves significant gains (0.88, 2.01, and 0.38 percentage points) on three major summarization datasets compared to previous methods. The human evaluation results also support the relevance claims with strong statistical significance.

**Medium Confidence**: The claims about the mechanism of EDU-based content planning are moderately supported. The paper shows that EDUs outperform sentence-level segmentation and that the method improves upon standard decoding approaches, but the evidence is correlational rather than mechanistic. The assumption that EDUs are ideal content units because they are "more self-contained" is plausible but not directly tested.

**Low Confidence**: The claims about why the two-stage approach is necessary are least supported. While the paper demonstrates that the approach works better than alternatives, it does not provide strong evidence for why the separation between plan generation and summary generation is essential to the method's success. The design tradeoff of using two models versus end-to-end approaches is acknowledged but not thoroughly evaluated.

## Next Checks

1. **Direct diversity comparison**: Implement diversity metrics (e.g., pairwise cosine similarity, coverage metrics) to compare the generated summary sets against those from standard beam search, sampling-based decoding, and other baseline methods. This would provide direct evidence for the diversity claims.

2. **Alternative content unit comparison**: Conduct experiments using entity-based extraction or sentence-level extraction as alternatives to EDU-based planning. This would validate whether the specific choice of EDUs is critical or whether similar gains could be achieved with other fine-grained content units.

3. **Ablation on two-stage design**: Create an end-to-end variant that integrates EDU planning into the abstractive generation process, and compare its performance against the two-stage approach. This would test whether the separation between planning and generation is truly beneficial or merely a design choice.
