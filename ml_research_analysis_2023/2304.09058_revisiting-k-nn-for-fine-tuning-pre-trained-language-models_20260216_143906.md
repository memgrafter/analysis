# Revisiting k-NN for Fine-tuning Pre-trained Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.09058
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09058
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: UNION-ALL improves PLM performance by combining k-NN calibration and interpolation across eight NLP tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces UNION-ALL, a method that augments pre-trained language models (PLMs) by leveraging k-Nearest-Neighbor (k-NN) classifiers. The approach uses k-NN predictions as a prior during training to focus the PLM on hard examples and combines k-NN and PLM probability distributions during inference via linear interpolation. Extensive experiments on eight diverse NLP tasks show UNION-ALL consistently outperforms strong baselines in fully-supervised, few-shot, and zero-shot settings.

## Method Summary
UNION-ALL is a two-stage method for improving PLMs using k-NN. During training, k-NN predictions are used to calibrate the loss function—examples misclassified by k-NN are upweighted, helping the PLM focus on harder cases. At inference, final predictions are obtained by linearly interpolating the probability distributions from k-NN and the PLM, controlled by a tunable weight λ. The method is evaluated using RoBERTa-large on eight benchmark datasets across three training paradigms.

## Key Results
- UNION-ALL outperforms fine-tuning and prompt-tuning baselines in fully-supervised, few-shot (16-shot), and zero-shot settings.
- Calibration with k-NN improves PLM performance by focusing training on hard examples.
- Linear interpolation of k-NN and PLM distributions at inference consistently boosts accuracy and F1 scores across all tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Using k-NN predictions as a prior to calibrate training helps the PLM focus on hard examples.
- Mechanism: During training, k-NN's predicted probabilities are used to adjust the loss function via a modulating factor. Examples that k-NN misclassifies receive higher weights, guiding the PLM to pay more attention to them.
- Core assumption: k-NN predictions can serve as a reliable proxy for identifying easy versus hard examples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "At the heart of our approach is the implementation of k-NN-calibrated training, which treats predicted results as indicators for easy versus hard examples during the training process."
  - [section 3.2]: "We distinguish between easy and hard examples based on the results of k-NN... We propose to adjust the relative loss for the correctly-classified or misclassified instances identified by k-NN."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no directly comparable mechanisms in the neighbor papers.
- Break condition: If k-NN's predictions are consistently inaccurate or if the modulating factor is poorly tuned, the calibration may degrade performance rather than improve it.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Linear interpolation of k-NN and PLM probability distributions at inference time improves predictions.
- Mechanism: Final prediction is a weighted combination: \( P_U = \lambda P_{kNN} + (1-\lambda) P_M \), where \( P_{kNN} \) is k-NN's distribution and \( P_M \) is the PLM's distribution. The weight \( \lambda \) is tunable.
- Core assumption: The strengths of k-NN and PLM predictions are complementary, so combining them yields better performance than either alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Linearly interpolate the probability distribution predicted by k-NN with that of the PLMs' classifier."
  - [section 3.2]: "During inference, we linearly interpolate probability distributions with the PLM's predicted distributions to make the final prediction."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no similar interpolation strategies found in neighbor papers.
- Break condition: If k-NN and PLM predictions are highly correlated or if one is much less reliable than the other, interpolation may not help and could even hurt.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: k-NN serves as a non-parametric, lazy learner that mitigates overfitting and isolated noise in the training data.
- Mechanism: k-NN relies on instance similarity without parameter estimation, smoothing out noise and reducing overfitting compared to parametric models.
- Core assumption: Lazy learning (k-NN) is more robust to overfitting and noise than parametric eager learners (PLMs).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "k-Nearest-Neighbor (kNN) classifiers, as the lazy learning paradigm, tend to mitigate over-fitting and isolated noise."
  - [section 2]: "k-NN is actually a lazy learner that can avoid over-fitting of parameters and effectively smooths out the impact of isolated noisy training data."
  - [corpus]: Weak - neighbor papers focus on different topics; no direct evidence on lazy vs. eager learning tradeoffs.
- Break condition: If the dataset is large and well-structured, the benefits of k-NN's noise mitigation may be minimal compared to the computational overhead.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Lazy vs. eager learning
  - Why needed here: Understanding the contrast between k-NN (lazy, no training) and PLMs (eager, heavy training) is key to grasping the novelty of combining them.
  - Quick check question: What is the main difference between lazy and eager learners in terms of when they process training data?

- Concept: Modulating loss functions
  - Why needed here: The calibration step adjusts the loss based on k-NN predictions, so knowing how modulating factors like Focal Loss work is important.
  - Quick check question: How does a modulating factor in a loss function affect the gradient updates for different examples?

- Concept: Nearest neighbor retrieval and similarity metrics
  - Why needed here: The paper uses Euclidean distance and discusses alternatives like cosine similarity for finding neighbors.
  - Quick check question: What effect does changing the similarity metric have on the set of neighbors retrieved?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input -> PLM (feature extractor) -> k-NN module -> Loss calibrator (training) / Inference interpolator (inference) -> Final prediction
- Critical path:
  1. Encode input with PLM → get embeddings.
  2. Retrieve k nearest neighbors → compute k-NN distribution.
  3. During training: compute loss with calibration → update PLM.
  4. During inference: interpolate k-NN and PLM distributions → final prediction.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Memory: Storing and searching embeddings for k-NN is expensive.
  - Hyperparameters: k, λ (interpolation weight), and τ (temperature) need tuning.
  - Speed: Inference time increases due to k-NN search.
- Failure signatures:
  - Overfitting: Calibration with poor k-NN may focus too much on noise.
  - Underperformance: If k-NN and PLM predictions are too similar, interpolation adds little value.
  - Resource issues: Large datasets may exhaust memory or slow retrieval.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare performance of vanilla FT vs. UNION-ALL on a small dataset to confirm calibration helps.
  2. Vary k (e.g., 16, 32, 128) and observe impact on few-shot performance.
  3. Sweep λ from 0 to 1 to find the optimal interpolation weight for a target task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does the choice of modulating factor (e.g., Focal loss vs. negative log-likelihood) impact the performance of UNION-ALL across different NLP tasks?
- How does the size of the k-NN datastore (i.e., the number of neighbors) affect the performance of UNION-ALL, and is there an optimal value for k?
- How does UNION-ALL perform when using external domain data for k-NN retrieval compared to using only training data?

## Limitations
- Calibration Reliability: The assumption that k-NN predictions can reliably distinguish easy from hard examples is untested; if k-NN's performance is inconsistent across tasks, the calibration may misguide the PLM.
- Hyperparameter Sensitivity: UNION-ALL relies on several tunable hyperparameters (k, λ, τ) whose optimal settings may vary by task and data distribution, but the paper does not provide a robust tuning strategy.
- Computational Overhead: The need to store and search embeddings for k-NN increases memory and inference time, which is not addressed for scalability or large-scale deployment.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core claim that interpolating k-NN and PLM probability distributions improves performance is well-supported by the results across multiple tasks and settings.
- **Medium Confidence**: The assertion that k-NN serves as a non-parametric learner to mitigate overfitting is supported by theory, but lacks direct empirical comparison to other noise-robust methods.
- **Low Confidence**: The mechanism by which k-NN-based calibration reliably identifies hard examples is asserted but not rigorously validated, especially in few-shot or noisy settings.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation of Calibration Step**: Run UNION-ALL without k-NN-based loss reweighting to quantify the contribution of the calibration mechanism versus simple interpolation.
2. **Hyperparameter Robustness Analysis**: Systematically vary k, λ, and τ across a wider range of values and tasks to identify sensitivity and robustness, especially in few-shot settings.
3. **Memory and Speed Benchmarking**: Measure and report memory usage and inference time for k-NN retrieval, and compare against baseline methods to assess scalability for larger datasets.
