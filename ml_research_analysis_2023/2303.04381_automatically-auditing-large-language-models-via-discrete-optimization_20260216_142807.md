# Automatically Auditing Large Language Models via Discrete Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04381
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04381
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: ARCA finds 58% of prompts generating toxic outputs on GPT-2 vs 29% for AutoPrompt

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ARCA, a discrete optimization algorithm for automatically auditing large language models by searching for input-output pairs that exhibit specific target behaviors. The method jointly optimizes prompts and outputs through iterative token updates, using a novel approximation that combines exact log probabilities with averaged first-order gradients. Experiments show ARCA consistently outperforms state-of-the-art discrete optimizers like AutoPrompt and GBDA in uncovering problematic behaviors such as toxic completions and language mismatches.

## Method Summary
ARCA formulates auditing as a discrete optimization problem where it searches for (prompt, output) pairs that satisfy both the language model's autoregressive constraints and a target auditing objective. The algorithm uses coordinate ascent to iteratively update individual tokens, approximating the objective function by decomposing it into autoregressive terms (computed exactly) and linearly approximatable terms (approximated using averaged first-order gradients). This decomposition enables efficient optimization while preserving fidelity to the true objective. The method is evaluated on GPT-2 (762M) and GPT-J (6B) across various auditing objectives including toxicity, language consistency, and factual accuracy.

## Key Results
- ARCA finds 58% of prompts generating 3-token toxic outputs on GPT-2 compared to 29% for AutoPrompt
- ARCA consistently outperforms state-of-the-art discrete optimizers across all tested auditing objectives
- Prompts discovered by ARCA reveal factual errors, temporal errors, and potentially offensive completions about celebrities
- Evidence of prompt transfer across model sizes, with prompts found on smaller models often producing similar behaviors on larger models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: ARCA's decomposition of the auditing objective into autoregressive and linearly approximatable terms enables efficient coordinate ascent while preserving fidelity.
- Mechanism: By exactly computing the autoregressive term (which captures log probabilities of generating each token given previous tokens) and approximating only the linearly approximatable term using averaged first-order gradients, ARCA maintains accurate token ranking while reducing computational cost.
- Core assumption: The autoregressive term captures the most critical information for token selection that would otherwise be lost in pure first-order approximations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "ARCA uses a novel approximation of the objective function that combines exact log probabilities with averaged first-order approximations"
  - [section 3.3.1]: "ARCA's main advantage therefore stems from decomposing [the objective] into an linearly approximatable term...and autoregressive terms"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus evidence for this specific decomposition mechanism
- Break condition: If the autoregressive term becomes computationally prohibitive or if averaging first-order approximations introduces too much variance, the efficiency advantage disappears.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Joint optimization over both prompts and outputs allows more direct targeting of specific behaviors compared to optimizing only prompts.
- Mechanism: By including outputs as optimization variables, ARCA can directly update output tokens to match target behaviors (e.g., toxicity, language switching) rather than indirectly through prompt modifications.
- Core assumption: Direct output optimization provides stronger optimization signal than indirect prompt optimization for rare behaviors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2]: "Joint optimization allows us to more directly optimize for output behaviors; we can update o directly to match the target output behavior, rather than indirectly updating f(x) through the prompt"
  - [section 4.3.1]: "ARCA's success is due to the autoregressive term in (5); this term allows ARCA to update output tokens based the previous and subsequent token values, while AutoPrompt can only use subsequent tokens"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus evidence for this joint optimization advantage
- Break condition: If the output space becomes too large relative to the prompt space, or if the constraint f(x)=o becomes too restrictive, joint optimization may become intractable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Averaging first-order approximations at random tokens reduces variance and improves global approximation compared to using only the current token value.
- Mechanism: By sampling k random tokens and averaging their gradients, ARCA creates a more stable approximation of the linearly approximatable term that better captures the objective landscape across the vocabulary.
- Core assumption: Random sampling provides sufficient coverage of the vocabulary space to create a representative average gradient.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3.1]: "We conjecture that averaging helps us (i) reduce the variance of the /f_irst-order approximation, and (ii) better globally approximate the loss, as /f_irst-order approximations degrade with distance"
  - [section 4.2.1]: "The improvement of ARCA over AutoPrompt comes from averaging random /f_irst-order approximations"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus evidence for this averaging mechanism
- Break condition: If the objective function has highly localized features that averaging obscures, or if random sampling fails to capture critical regions of the search space.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input objective function φ, language model pLLM, vocabulary V, prompt/output lengths m,n -> ARCA algorithm with GetCandidates function -> Optimized (x,o) pairs satisfying f(x)=o and high φ(x,o)

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize random prompt and output
  2. For each token position, compute approximate scores using GetCandidates
  3. Select top-k candidates and compute exact scores
  4. Update token to argmax exact score
  5. Check termination conditions (f(x)=o and φ threshold, or max iterations)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - k (number of candidates): Higher k increases accuracy but computational cost
  - Number of random gradients: More gradients reduce variance but increase computation
  - Prompt/output lengths: Longer sequences increase search space exponentially

- Failure signatures:
  - ARCA consistently fails to find any valid (x,o) pairs → Objective may be too restrictive or search space too sparse
  - ARCA finds solutions but they don't satisfy f(x)=o → Constraint handling needs adjustment
  - ARCA converges slowly → k too small or number of random gradients insufficient

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test ARCA on simple objective (φ(x,o)=1[o="test"]) with small vocabulary to verify basic functionality
  2. Compare ARCA vs AutoPrompt on a simple toxic/non-toxic objective to validate improvement claims
  3. Test prompt transfer from GPT-2 to GPT-3 on a simple senator generation task to verify transfer claims

## Limitations
- The decomposition mechanism relies on conjectures about averaging benefits that lack empirical validation
- Joint optimization advantages are theoretically sound but lack ablation studies isolating component contributions
- Prompt transfer experiments are limited to specific tasks and don't establish conditions for transfer success or failure
- No scaling analysis of computational complexity as sequence lengths increase
- Potential mode collapse and lack of diversity analysis in discovered behaviors

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The basic formulation of the auditing problem as discrete optimization is sound and well-motivated by existing literature. The experimental setup and evaluation metrics are clearly defined and reproducible.

**Medium confidence**: The theoretical justification for ARCA's decomposition approach is plausible but not fully validated. The improvement over baseline methods is demonstrated but the ablation studies are insufficient to isolate which components drive the improvements.

**Low confidence**: The claims about prompt transfer across model sizes lack sufficient empirical support. The paper shows that some prompts transfer but doesn't establish conditions under which transfer succeeds or fails, or provide statistical analysis of transfer rates.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation study on decomposition components**: Run ARCA with only the autoregressive term, only the first-order approximation term, and various combinations of averaging strategies to isolate which components contribute most to performance improvements.

2. **Constraint satisfaction verification**: Add explicit constraint checking to the termination conditions and measure the percentage of converged solutions that actually satisfy f(x)=o, particularly for long sequences where constraint violations might accumulate.

3. **Transfer robustness analysis**: Systematically vary model sizes, architectures, and training data to measure how often prompts transfer successfully, and analyze whether certain types of target behaviors (toxic completions, language switches, factual errors) transfer more reliably than others.
