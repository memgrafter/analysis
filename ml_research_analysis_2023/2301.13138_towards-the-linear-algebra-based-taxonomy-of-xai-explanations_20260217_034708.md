# Towards the Linear Algebra Based Taxonomy of XAI Explanations

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a mathematical taxonomy for local explanations in explainable AI by defining three distinct types of explanations using linear algebra: decision trace, approximation, and interpretation. The decision trace represents a classified point's position relative to the decision boundary through inequalities for each feature, while approximation reduces dimensionality by explaining predictions in a feature subspace. Interpretation maps predictions to different feature sets for enhanced human comprehension. The authors illustrate these concepts with synthetic examples and discuss how their mathematical framework relates to existing human-centric XAI concepts, providing a foundation for systematic comparison of explanation methods.

## Method Summary
The paper defines a classification task on dataset D ⊂ ℝᵐ and proposes three types of local explanations using linear algebra. The ε-decision boundary is defined using synthetic data points sampled from the same distribution as D. Decision traces are sets of inequalities describing a point's position relative to the decision boundary in the original feature space. Approximations are similar sets of inequalities but in a reduced feature subspace. Interpretations involve mapping predictions to a different feature set through a mapping function MI. The method involves splitting data into training, validation, and testing subsets, training a classifier, and using XAI techniques to generate explanations for test predictions.

## Key Results
- Proposes mathematical definitions for decision trace, approximation, and interpretation using inequalities and feature mappings
- Illustrates the taxonomy with synthetic examples including overlapping elliptical classes and nonlinear decision boundaries
- Provides a framework for relating mathematical XAI concepts to existing human-centric definitions like transparency and interpretability

  - Why needed here: The entire taxonomy is built on representing explanations as systems of inequalities in different feature spaces, requiring understanding of linear algebra fundamentals.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data preprocessing -> Classifier training -> Explanation generation -> Mathematical comparison
- **Critical path**: Classifier training → Decision boundary identification → Explanation generation → Mathematical comparison of explanation types
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - Mathematical rigor vs. human comprehensibility
  - Computational complexity of finding exact decision boundaries vs. approximation methods
  - Feature preservation in approximation vs. dimensionality reduction benefits
  - Expressiveness of inequality systems vs. simplicity of representation
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Inability to express complex decision boundaries as inequalities
  - Loss of critical information during feature approximation
  - Non-invertible or information-losing feature mappings
  - Mathematical definitions that don't align with human understanding
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Implement a simple 2D classification problem (e.g., half-moons dataset) and generate decision traces using a k-NN classifier, verifying the inequality representation matches the geometric intuition
  2. Apply feature selection to reduce dimensionality and generate approximations, measuring information loss through comparison with original traces
  3. Create feature mappings (e.g., polynomial transformations) and generate interpretations, evaluating how well the mapped explanations preserve decision boundary information

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the proposed linear algebra-based definitions of decision trace, approximation, and interpretation relate to the human-centric concepts of transparency, interpretability, and explicability in existing XAI taxonomies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how these concepts relate to existing human-centric definitions in Section 5.
- Why unresolved: While the paper mentions potential relationships, it does not provide a rigorous mathematical formalization or empirical validation of these connections.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal mathematical framework demonstrating the equivalence or mapping between the linear algebra-based definitions and human-centric concepts, supported by experimental validation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the computational complexities and trade-offs involved in computing decision traces, approximations, and interpretations for different types of classifiers (e.g., decision trees, SVMs, kNN, logistic regression)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions different classifiers but does not provide a systematic analysis of computational complexity.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on defining the concepts rather than analyzing their computational properties across different classifiers.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study comparing the computational costs and accuracy of these explanations across various classifiers and datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the weighting of inequalities in decision traces, approximations, and interpretations be determined objectively, especially in the case of interpretations where the feature mapping may be complex?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "The weighting of inequalities with respect to their importance may be the most challenging task" in Section 3.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this as a challenge but does not propose a solution or methodology for objective weighting.
- What evidence would resolve it: A methodology for objectively determining weights, potentially based on statistical measures or human feedback, with empirical validation across multiple use cases.
