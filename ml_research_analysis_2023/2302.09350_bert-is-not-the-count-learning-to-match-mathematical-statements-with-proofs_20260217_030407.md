# BERT is not The Count: Learning to Match Mathematical Statements with Proofs
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.09350
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09350
- Reference count: 19
- Dataset: 184k statement-proof pairs from arXiv math papers

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the task of matching mathematical proofs to their corresponding statements using a newly constructed MATcH dataset of 184k pairs extracted from modern mathematical research articles. The authors propose a bilinear similarity model combined with local and global decoding methods to tackle this task. The study systematically evaluates different BERT-based encoders and investigates the role of symbolic matching versus semantic understanding through controlled symbol replacement experiments. Results reveal that while models achieve reasonable performance, they rely heavily on shallow symbolic matching rather than deep mathematical reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors construct the MATcH dataset by extracting statement-proof pairs from LaTeX sources of arXiv math papers published between 2017-2021. They develop a bilinear similarity model that computes matching scores between statement and proof representations. Two decoding strategies are proposed: local decoding (selecting top matches independently) and global decoding (treating the problem as bipartite matching to ensure one-to-one correspondences). Multiple BERT variants serve as encoders, with experiments testing different vocabulary customizations and symbol replacement strategies to probe model reasoning capabilities.

## Key Results
- Best model achieves MRR of 73.7 using BERT-based encoder with global decoding
- Global decoding outperforms local decoding (MRR 73.7 vs 71.6)
- Models perform significantly better when mathematical symbols are conserved versus replaced
- Vocabulary customization for mathematical texts improves performance over generic BERT

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The bilinear similarity model works by computing inner products between learned representations of statements and proofs, allowing the model to capture both local (token-level) and global (sequence-level) relationships. The global decoding method treats matching as a bipartite graph problem, ensuring that each proof is matched to exactly one statement and vice versa, which improves coherence in the final matching. BERT-based encoders provide strong baseline representations, though their mathematical reasoning capabilities are limited to pattern matching rather than true semantic understanding.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input text -> BERT Encoder -> Bilinear Layer -> Similarity Score -> (Local/Global) Decoding
Critical path: Statement and proof are encoded separately, then their representations are combined through bilinear transformation to produce matching scores.
Design tradeoffs: Local decoding is computationally efficient but may produce inconsistent matches; global decoding ensures consistency but requires solving an optimization problem.
Failure signatures: Low MRR indicates poor encoding of mathematical content; performance drop under symbol replacement suggests reliance on surface patterns rather than semantic understanding.

## Limitations
- Dataset constructed from single arXiv collection with limited temporal scope
- Evaluation focuses on matching accuracy rather than explanation quality
- Symbol replacement analysis may not fully capture mathematical reasoning complexity
- Performance gap between decoding methods is modest, questioning practical benefit

## Confidence
- High confidence: dataset construction methodology, basic performance metrics, and finding that models rely on symbolic rather than semantic matching
- Medium confidence: claims about global decoding superiority and vocabulary customization benefits
- Low confidence: broader claims about mathematical reasoning capabilities
