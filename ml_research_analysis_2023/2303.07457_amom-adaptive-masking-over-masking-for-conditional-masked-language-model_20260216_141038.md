# AMOM: Adaptive Masking over Masking for Conditional Masked Language Model

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.07457
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07457
- Reference count: 16
- Non-autoregressive model achieves state-of-the-art performance on NMT and outperforms Transformer on 7 benchmarks with 2.2× speedup

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AMOM (Adaptive Masking over Masking), a novel approach that enhances Conditional Masked Language Models (CMLM) for non-autoregressive sequence generation through two adaptive masking operations. The method applies adaptive masking to both source and target sequences during training, with the source-side masking coordinated with target-side masking ratios and the target-side masking based on prediction correctness. Experiments across 15 datasets in three tasks (NMT, summarization, code generation) demonstrate significant performance improvements over strong CMLM baselines, achieving state-of-the-art results in NMT and even surpassing autoregressive Transformer models on multiple benchmarks.

## Method Summary
AMOM extends CMLM by introducing two adaptive masking operations during training. First, it applies adaptive masking to the source sequence based on the masked target sequence's masking ratio, making encoder optimization easier through coordinated masking. Second, it applies adaptive masking to the target sequence based on the prediction correctness ratio from the first masking step, enhancing the refinement capability. The two-step masking process simulates iterative refinement during training while maintaining the standard CMLM inference algorithm. A combined loss function (Lcmlm + Laday) is used for backpropagation, where the adaptive Y loss focuses only on newly masked tokens.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art NMT performance: 34.62 BLEU on WMT16 EN→RO, 34.82 BLEU on WMT16 RO→EN, 34.84 BLEU on IWSLT De→En
- Outperforms autoregressive Transformer on 7 benchmark datasets
- Provides at least 2.2× speedup compared to autoregressive models
- Shows consistent improvements across 15 datasets in 3 different tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adaptive X masking improves encoder optimization by coordinating source-side masking with target-side masking ratios
- Mechanism: The masking ratio on the source sequence is dynamically determined by a mapping function based on the target-side masking ratio, reducing internal co-adaptation and making encoder training more focused
- Core assumption: The source sequence's masked proportion should correlate with the target sequence's masked proportion for better encoder-decoder coordination
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The masking operation for the source sequence can make the encoder optimization easier by adaptively masking a proportion of tokens based on the masked target sequence"
  - [section]: "We introduce a mapping function ϕ(·) to decide the masking ratio of X based on the masking ratio in Y"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus evidence about encoder optimization through coordinated masking
- Break condition: If the mapping function ϕ(·) doesn't properly correlate source and target masking ratios, the encoder may become over-constrained or under-constrained

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adaptive Y masking enhances refinement capability by capturing masking ratio changes across inference iterations
- Mechanism: A second masking operation on the target sequence is applied based on the prediction correctness ratio, simulating the iterative refinement process during training
- Core assumption: The masking ratio in later inference iterations correlates with the prediction correctness ratio from earlier iterations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The target-side adaptive masking operation is presented to enhance the refinement process of CMLM, motivated by the masking ratio changes of the target sequence in different inference iterations"
  - [section]: "We compute the correctness ratio of predicted tokens in ˆYmask by comparing with target tokens in Ymask, formulated as β = | ˆYmask=Ymask|/Nmask"
  - [corpus]: Missing - no corpus evidence directly supporting the correlation between prediction correctness and masking ratios
- Break condition: If the correctness ratio β doesn't properly reflect the refinement quality, the adaptive masking may misguide the training process

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-step masking training simulates the iterative refinement process without changing the inference algorithm
- Mechanism: The first masking step follows standard CMLM training, while the second step applies adaptive masking based on prediction results from the first step, creating a training-refinement loop
- Core assumption: Simulating the iterative refinement process during training improves the model's actual refinement capability during inference
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Unlike these works that introduce extra training objectives and optimize the inference process of CMLM, we present an ultra-simple yet effective adaptive masking operation for Y in model training without any change to the CMLM inference"
  - [section]: "The training loss of the new subset Y′mask is computed the same as the first-time masking in CMLM"
  - [corpus]: Weak - limited corpus evidence about two-step masking approaches for CMLM
- Break condition: If the two-step process creates too much divergence from the standard CMLM training, it may destabilize the model convergence

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Encoder with adaptive X masking based on target-side masking ratio
  - Decoder with standard CMLM architecture but enhanced through adaptive Y masking
  - Two-step training process with combined loss function
  - Standard CMLM inference with mask-predict strategy

- Critical path:
  1. Input pair (X, Y) is processed with target masking to get Yobs and Ymask
  2. Adaptive X masking is applied based on Y's masking ratio
  3. First CMLM loss is computed on Ymask prediction
  4. Ymask prediction is evaluated for correctness ratio β
  5. Adaptive Y masking is applied based on β
  6. Second loss is computed on the newly masked tokens
  7. Combined loss is backpropagated

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Complexity vs performance: Adding adaptive masking increases training complexity but significantly improves performance
  - Computational cost: Two-step masking roughly doubles the masking operations per training example
  - Hyperparameter sensitivity: Performance depends on choosing appropriate mapping functions and masking ratio limits

- Failure signatures:
  - Performance degradation if mapping functions don't properly correlate source and target masking ratios
  - Convergence issues if the two-step process creates too much divergence from standard CMLM training
  - Overfitting to specific masking patterns if the adaptive strategy is too rigid

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare BLEU scores on IWSLT De→En with and without adaptive X masking using linear mapping function
  2. Test different mapping functions (linear, convex, concave, ladder) for adaptive Y masking to find the optimal configuration
  3. Measure inference speedup vs autoregressive Transformer on WMT16 En→Ro to verify the claimed 2.2× speedup

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does AMOM perform when combined with other NAR enhancements like AXE, Levenshtein Transformer, or MvSR-NAT?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that AMOM is complementary to other CMLM-based methods and suggest stronger results can be expected by combining their adaptive masking with these methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates AMOM as a standalone improvement to vanilla CMLM, not in combination with other NAR enhancements.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing AMOM + AXE, AMOM + Levenshtein Transformer, and AMOM + MvSR-NAT on the same benchmark datasets.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the adaptive masking strategy generalize to other sequence generation tasks beyond NMT, summarization, and code generation, such as ASR or image captioning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors claim AMOM's effectiveness across 15 datasets in 3 different tasks, suggesting potential for broader application, but do not test it on other sequence generation domains.
- Why unresolved: The experiments are limited to NLP sequence tasks with clear source-target structure.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing AMOM on non-NLP sequence generation tasks like speech recognition or image-to-text, measuring performance gains.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of AMOM on very long sequences where iterative refinement becomes more critical?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors analyze performance on different sequence lengths and note that AMOM shows significant gains with more iterations, especially for long sentences.
- Why unresolved: While the trend is shown, the paper doesn't explore the upper limits of sequence length where AMOM's adaptive masking provides the most benefit.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with extremely long sequences (e.g., 500+ tokens) to identify the point where AMOM's adaptive refinement becomes essential for maintaining quality.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How sensitive is AMOM's performance to the choice of mapping function for adaptive masking?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors experiment with different mapping functions (linear, convex, concave, ladder) for both source and target adaptive masking and find linear mapping performs best, but only test a limited set.
- Why unresolved: The space of possible mapping functions is large, and the paper only explores a few variations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic exploration of a wider range of mapping functions, including learned or data-driven functions, to determine if better performance is achievable.

## Limitations

- Limited mechanistic evidence: The paper provides theoretical justification for adaptive masking mechanisms but lacks direct corpus evidence supporting the claimed benefits of coordinated source-target masking and correctness-based refinement.
- Mapping function ambiguity: The choice of mapping function (linear, convex, concave, ladder) is shown to affect performance but lacks rigorous theoretical grounding or systematic comparison across all tasks.
- Dataset generalization concerns: Hyperparameters appear primarily tuned for WMT datasets, with limited information about optimization for non-NMT tasks like summarization and code generation.

## Confidence

- High confidence in overall performance improvements - The reported BLEU scores and speedup claims are verifiable through standard NMT evaluation metrics.
- Medium confidence in two-mechanism claims - The adaptive X masking and Y masking mechanisms are logically sound but lack direct corpus validation.
- Low confidence in generality of mapping functions - The paper shows four mapping types but doesn't provide systematic comparison or theoretical justification for their selection.

## Next Checks

1. **Mechanism isolation test**: Run ablation studies that disable either adaptive X masking or adaptive Y masking independently to quantify their individual contributions to performance gains.

2. **Mapping function robustness**: Conduct systematic experiments across all 15 datasets comparing all four mapping function types to determine if certain functions generalize better across tasks.

3. **Convergence analysis**: Measure training stability and convergence speed with and without AMOM across different masking ratio ranges to verify that the two-step training process doesn't introduce instability.
