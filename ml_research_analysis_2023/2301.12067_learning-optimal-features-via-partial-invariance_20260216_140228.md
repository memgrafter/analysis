# Learning Optimal Features via Partial Invariance

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.12067
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12067
- Reference count: 28
- The paper addresses limitations of Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM) when the sufficiency assumption is violated in real-world distribution shifts.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Partial Invariance (P-IRM), a relaxation of Invariant Risk Minimization that imposes invariance constraints only within partitions of training environments rather than across all environments. The key insight is that when the sufficiency assumption (that invariant features alone are sufficient for optimal OOD performance) is violated, IRM can discard non-invariant but informative features that are locally invariant within certain environment partitions. P-IRM leverages meta-information about environments to identify these partitions where partial invariance is sufficient. The method demonstrates consistent improvements over standard IRM and ERM methods across synthetic data, housing price prediction, image classification on MetaShift, and named entity recognition tasks, with accuracy/Worst Group MSE improvements ranging from 2-10%.

## Method Summary
P-IRM partitions training environments using meta-information and applies invariance constraints only within each partition rather than across all environments. The method uses an oracle-based approach where environments are grouped based on a distance metric computed from meta-information. Two variants are proposed: partitioning (training only on environments in the partition) and conditioning (training on all environments but applying the invariance penalty only to the partition). The framework relaxes the strict sufficiency requirement of IRM, allowing retention of non-invariant features that are informative within local partitions while still maintaining generalization benefits.

## Key Results
- P-IRM consistently outperforms standard IRM and ERM methods across multiple tasks and datasets
- Improvements range from 2-10% in accuracy/Worst Group MSE depending on the task and degree of distribution shift
- The method is particularly effective when environments can be meaningfully partitioned based on available meta-information
- Theoretical analysis shows P-IRM can recover non-invariant but informative features that IRM would discard under violated sufficiency assumptions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Partial invariance improves performance when sufficiency assumption is violated by allowing retention of non-invariant but informative features within partitions.
- Mechanism: By constraining invariance only within partitions rather than across all environments, P-IRM avoids discarding features that are non-invariant globally but informative locally. This relaxes the strict sufficiency requirement that causes IRM to underperform when invariant features alone are insufficient.
- Core assumption: Access to meta-information that allows meaningful partitioning of environments where partial invariance holds.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we propose a relaxation via partial invariance. In this work, we theoretically highlight the sub-optimality of IRM and then demonstrate how learning from a partition of training domains can help improve invariant models."
  - [section]: "In practical problem settings, these assumptions are often not satisfied, which leads to IRM learning a sub-optimal predictor for that task. To address this, we propose a relaxation for IRM via the Partial invariance(P-IRM) framework, that imposes invariance constraints only within partitions/sub-groups of training environments."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: P-IRM enables learning of features that are "locally invariant" within partitions even when they are non-invariant across all environments.
- Mechanism: The oracle-based partitioning identifies environments where feature weights are close enough to be considered invariant within that partition. Theorem 1 shows that under certain conditions, IRM within such partitions recovers these partially invariant features.
- Core assumption: The oracle providing distance metric between environments is sufficiently accurate to identify partitions where feature weights are close.
- Evidence anchors:

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The conditional variant of P-IRM (conditioning) provides advantages in low-data regimes by using all training data while applying invariance constraints only to the partition.
- Mechanism: Instead of training only on the partition (partitioning variant), conditioning trains on all environments but applies the invariance penalty only to environments in the partition. This maintains data efficiency while still relaxing the strict invariance requirement.
- Core assumption: The partition identified by the oracle contains sufficiently representative environments to guide the invariance constraint effectively.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Invariance and its relationship to causality
  - Why needed here: The entire framework builds on the idea that invariant features are causally relevant and can generalize across distribution shifts. Understanding this connection is crucial for grasping why IRM works and why P-IRM relaxes it.
  - Quick check question: Why does enforcing invariance across all environments potentially discard useful non-invariant features?

- Concept: Distribution shifts and domain generalization
  - Why needed here: The paper addresses the problem of models failing to generalize when test data comes from different distributions than training data. Understanding types of distribution shifts (concept drift, subpopulation shifts) is essential for appreciating the motivation.
  - Quick check question: What's the difference between covariate shift and concept drift, and why does concept drift violate IRM's sufficiency assumption?

- Concept: Regularization and penalty methods in optimization
  - Why needed here: Both IRM and P-IRM use gradient penalty methods to enforce constraints. Understanding how these work and their approximations is necessary for implementing the methods.
  - Quick check question: How does the gradient penalty in IRMv1 approximate the constrained optimization problem in the original IRM formulation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Environment partitioner -> IRM trainer -> P-IRM controller -> Evaluation module
- Critical path:
  1. Load data with environment labels and meta-information
  2. Compute distance matrix between environments using meta-information
  3. Apply partitioning algorithm to identify candidate partitions
  4. For each partition, train P-IRM model (either partitioning or conditioning variant)
  5. Evaluate on OOD test environments
  6. Select best performing partition/model

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Partitioning variant vs conditioning variant: Partitioning uses less data but may be more stable; conditioning uses all data but requires careful handling of the invariance constraint
  - Oracle precision vs computational cost: More precise distance metrics may require more computation but yield better partitions
  - Number of partitions: Multiple partitions may capture different invariances but increase complexity and risk of overfitting

- Failure signatures:
  - No improvement over IRM: Indicates either poor partitioning (inaccurate oracle) or that sufficiency assumption actually holds
  - Performance worse than ERM: Suggests over-constraining the model or using too strict invariance within partitions
  - High variance across runs: Points to instability in the partitioning process or insufficient data within partitions

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Linear regression with synthetic data: Implement the toy example from the paper to verify that IRM discards non-invariant features while P-IRM recovers them within partitions
  2. Housing price prediction: Use the provided dataset to test temporal partitioning and verify improvement over IRM and ERM
  3. MetaShift image classification: Implement the spectral embedding-based distance metric and test the conditioning variant to see if using all data helps when partitions are small

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can P-IRM be extended to cases where no meaningful meta-information about environments exists?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper acknowledges that P-IRM's application is limited by the availability of informative meta-information about training/deployment domains, which often may not be readily available.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on scenarios where meta-information is available, but does not address cases where such information is absent or difficult to obtain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of heuristics for identifying partitions without relying on explicit meta-information, along with empirical results showing effectiveness in such scenarios.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the sample complexity of P-IRM compared to standard IRM and ERM in various data regimes?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that P-IRM requires stronger priors (environments close to reference) for feasible sample complexities, but does not provide detailed comparisons of sample complexities across different methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides some theoretical bounds but lacks comprehensive empirical comparisons of sample complexity requirements.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the number of training environments needed for P-IRM, IRM, and ERM to achieve similar performance across various tasks and data regimes.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of distance metric between environments affect P-IRM performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses using spectral embeddings based on graph Laplacian connectivity as a distance metric for image classification, but does not extensively explore the impact of different distance metrics.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses specific distance metrics for different tasks but does not systematically investigate how alternative metrics might affect performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing P-IRM performance using various distance metrics (e.g., Euclidean, cosine similarity, learned metrics) across multiple tasks to determine the most effective choices.

## Limitations
- P-IRM requires access to meaningful meta-information for environment partitioning, which may not be available in many real-world scenarios
- The method's effectiveness is fundamentally tied to the quality of the oracle's partitioning ability and the accuracy of distance metrics between environments
- Theoretical analysis assumes specific feature structures (uniform feature model) that may not hold in practice

## Confidence
- Theoretical claims about P-IRM's sub-optimality of IRM under violated sufficiency assumptions: High confidence
- Empirical validation across multiple tasks: Medium confidence (due to oracle-based partitioning that may not generalize)
- Effectiveness in real-world scenarios with imperfect meta-information: Low confidence (not extensively tested)

## Next Checks
1. **Oracle robustness testing**: Systematically evaluate P-IRM performance with varying oracle accuracy (simulated noise in meta-information) to understand the method's sensitivity to partitioning quality.

2. **Meta-information ablation**: Test P-IRM with different types of meta-information (e.g., time, location, demographics) on the same datasets to identify which meta-features provide the most useful partitions.

3. **Real-world application stress test**: Apply P-IRM to a high-stakes domain (e.g., medical imaging) where the cost of distribution shift is high, and evaluate both performance and robustness to potential oracle errors.
