# Controlling for Stereotypes in Multimodal Language Model Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.01582
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01582
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 12
- Key outcome: This paper proposes a methodology and benchmark datasets to assess whether multimodal language models use visual input or rely on statistical stereotypes. Two benchmarks test color and gender stereotypes: for each, models are given an image and prompt, with predictions compared when the image matches vs. contradicts the stereotype. Experiments with three models (VisualBERT, LXMERT, FLA V A) on color prediction show FLA V A is most sensitive to image choice and less affected by stereotypes. On gender prediction, FLA V A again best aligns with visual input, while older models show unexpected male bias. The results demonstrate that controlled stereotype benchmarks reveal important differences among models that raw accuracy alone may obscure, and highlight FLA V A as more reliant on visual information.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel methodology for evaluating whether multimodal language models rely on visual input or learned statistical stereotypes when making predictions. The authors create controlled benchmark datasets that manipulate whether images align with stereotypical expectations while keeping text prompts constant. By comparing model predictions across these conditions, they can isolate the degree to which models use visual information versus defaulting to learned biases. The approach is tested on two stereotype types - color associations and gender stereotypes - using three different multimodal architectures.

The results reveal significant variation in how different models handle visual information. FLA V A, a Transformer-based model, shows the strongest reliance on visual input and is least affected by stereotypes, while older CNN-based models like VisualBERT and LXMERT tend to maintain stereotypical predictions even when visual evidence contradicts them. Notably, all models exhibited unexpected male bias in gender prediction tasks, suggesting pervasive biases in training data that aren't fully addressed by multimodal integration.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a controlled evaluation methodology that compares model predictions when visual input matches versus contradicts stereotypical expectations. For color stereotypes, they extend the Memory Colors dataset with 19 neutral objects and create adversarial images with unexpected colors. For gender stereotypes, they create 50 sentences with gender-specific words masked, paired with 100 images (50 male, 50 female). Models are evaluated using their masked language model (MLM) heads to predict the masked tokens, with predictions compared across stereotype-consistent and stereotype-contradicting conditions. The methodology tests three models: VisualBERT and LXMERT (CNN-based) and FLA V A (Transformer-based).

## Key Results
- FLA V A is most sensitive to image choice and least affected by stereotypes across both color and gender benchmarks
- Older CNN-based models (VisualBERT, LXMERT) show strong reliance on stereotypes even when visual evidence contradicts them
- All models exhibited unexpected male bias in gender prediction, predicting male-related words regardless of female images
- The controlled benchmark methodology reveals important performance differences obscured by raw accuracy metrics alone

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The proposed benchmark methodology reveals hidden model reliance on stereotypes by comparing predictions on stereotype-consistent vs. stereotype-contradicting images.
- Mechanism: By controlling visual input while holding text constant, the method isolates whether model predictions are driven by visual evidence or by learned statistical biases from the training corpus.
- Core assumption: The model's masked language model (MLM) head produces different distributions for [MASK] tokens when visual context changes, and these differences reflect genuine reliance on visual information rather than just statistical priors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The key idea is to compare predictions when the image conforms to the stereotype to predictions when it does not."
  - [section] "The key idea is that we expect common evaluation benchmarks to include many stereotypical cases that can easily be predicted simply by relying on language statistics. In order to disentangle the effect of the stereotype and the contribution of the visual representations we compare the model's output in cases where the provided image adheres to the stereotype to cases where it does not."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence: Found 25 related papers, but none specifically testing stereotype reliance through controlled visual manipulation.
- Break condition: If model predictions remain unchanged between stereotype-consistent and stereotype-contradicting images, the methodology fails to detect stereotype reliance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: FLA V A shows superior visual grounding compared to older CNN-based models because its Transformer-based architecture better integrates visual and textual representations.
- Mechanism: The visual Transformer encoder in FLA V A processes images as sequences of patches, allowing more flexible cross-modal attention and reducing reliance on fixed-region features that may carry stereotypical priors.
- Core assumption: The dual-stream architecture with separate text and image encoders, followed by a multimodal fusion encoder, allows more effective integration of visual information than single-stream CNN approaches.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the recent Transformer-based FLA V A seems to be more sensitive to the choice of image and less affected by stereotypes than older CNN-based models such as VisualBERT and LXMERT."
  - [section] "FLA V A has a text encoder, an image encoder, and a multimodal encoder. It is a dual stream multimodal model. The text encoder, has an architecture of ViT (visual Transformers) to extract single-modal text representations. For the images, an image encoder based on ViT architecture extracts single-modal image representations. A separate Transformer, multimodal encoder, is then applied."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence: Found 25 related papers, but none specifically comparing architectural differences in stereotype reliance.
- Break condition: If FLA V A's predictions still rely heavily on stereotypes even with controlled visual input, the architectural advantage claim is invalid.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The benchmark reveals that older models like LXMERT maintain stereotypical predictions even when visual evidence contradicts stereotypes, indicating stronger prior reliance.
- Mechanism: CNN-based feature extraction with fixed region proposals may encode stereotypical visual patterns that override contradictory textual context, whereas Transformer-based models can dynamically attend to relevant visual features.
- Core assumption: The fixed 36 RoI boxes used by LXMERT and VisualBERT may capture stereotypical visual features that bias predictions, while FLA V A's patch-based approach allows more flexible feature selection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our results show that there is significant variation among multimodal models: the recent Transformer-based FLA V A seems to be more sensitive to the choice of image and less affected by stereotypes than older CNN-based models such as VisualBERT and LXMERT."
  - [section] "The LXMERT and FLA V A models achieve better scores on the original Memory Colors dataset: both models have accuracies in the 0.70–0.75 range. However, we see clearly that this similarity of performance is superficial and that the LXMERT model mostly relies on stereotypes: when we consider the control images with unexpected colors, the performance of LXMERT is very poor and it mostly keeps predicting the stereotypical color."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence: Found 25 related papers, but none specifically documenting stereotype persistence in CNN-based models under visual contradiction.
- Break condition: If newer models also show stereotype persistence under visual contradiction, the architectural explanation is insufficient.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Benchmark dataset generators (color and gender stereotypes) -> Multimodal model wrappers (VisualBERT, LXMERT, FLA V A) -> Evaluation scripts that compare MLM predictions across different visual conditions
- Critical path: Generate text-image pairs -> Load model with appropriate tokenizer -> Run MLM prediction with [MASK] token -> Compare predictions across stereotype-consistent vs. stereotype-contradicting images -> Aggregate accuracy statistics
- Design tradeoffs: Using [MASK] token prediction simplifies controlled testing but may not capture all aspects of model behavior; including more complex reasoning tasks could provide additional insights but would complicate the controlled comparison methodology
- Failure signatures: If model predictions remain identical across different visual conditions, the benchmark fails to detect stereotype reliance; if accuracy drops significantly for all models, the methodology may be flawed rather than revealing meaningful differences
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run VisualBERT on Memory Colors dataset with stereotypical images vs. control images, compare MLM predictions for color terms.
  2. Run LXMERT on Gender Stereotypes dataset with male vs. female images, measure proportion of male-coded predictions.
  3. Run FLA V A on both datasets with black dummy images, verify that predictions shift toward stereotypical defaults when visual information is absent.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why do CNN-based models like LXMERT and VisualBERT exhibit male bias in gender prediction tasks even when images show females?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper reports unexpected results showing these models consistently assign higher probabilities to male-related words regardless of input images.
- Why unresolved: The authors note this was surprising and did not match their expectations based on stereotypes suggested by text prompts, but do not explain the underlying cause.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of the training data distribution showing male dominance, or ablation studies isolating the contribution of different training components to this bias.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would FLA V A's performance change when presented with images that don't fall into clear-cut gender categories?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge they used simplistic gender operationalization with prototypical male/female images and suggest investigating FLA V A's response to non-binary categories.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments only tested stereotypical male/female images, leaving performance on ambiguous cases unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing FLA V A on images of non-binary, transgender, or androgynous individuals and comparing prediction patterns to the current results.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the proposed methodology for identifying stereotype reliance be extended to develop training methods that reduce stereotype effects?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors conclude by suggesting this type of training would involve more work in data collection or adversarial image generation methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses the methodology for evaluation purposes, not as a basis for intervention or model improvement.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful implementation of training procedures using adversarial image generation or balanced datasets that demonstrably reduce stereotype reliance while maintaining performance.

## Limitations

- The gender benchmark's binary operationalization of gender may oversimplify a complex construct and miss important nuances
- Architectural explanations for FLA V A's performance are speculative and not definitively proven by the experiments
- The methodology assumes MLM prediction differences directly indicate stereotype reliance, which may not capture all aspects of model reasoning

## Confidence

High confidence: The benchmark methodology is sound and the basic finding that different models show varying sensitivity to visual input is well-supported. The comparison between FLA V A and older models on the Memory Colors dataset is particularly robust.

Medium confidence: The gender benchmark results are meaningful but limited by the binary gender operationalization and the relatively small sample size of 50 sentences.

Low confidence: The architectural explanations for why FLA V A performs better are plausible but not definitively proven by the experiments. The paper does not control for potential confounding factors like training data differences.

## Next Checks

1. Test model predictions on images with intentionally ambiguous visual features (e.g., gender-ambiguous faces or objects with intermediate colors) to verify whether the observed stereotype reliance patterns persist under more complex visual conditions.

2. Conduct ablation studies where visual features are systematically degraded or modified to quantify the exact contribution of visual input to model predictions across all three architectures.

3. Evaluate the models on additional stereotype dimensions (e.g., age, profession, cultural stereotypes) using the same controlled methodology to assess whether the observed patterns generalize beyond color and gender.
