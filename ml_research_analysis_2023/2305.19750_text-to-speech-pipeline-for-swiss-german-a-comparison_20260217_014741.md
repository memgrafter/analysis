# Text-to-Speech Pipeline for Swiss German -- A comparison

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19750
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19750
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: VITS model trained on high-quality SlowSoft dataset achieves MOS score of 4.1 for Swiss German TTS

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a two-step pipeline for synthesizing Swiss German speech from standard German text, addressing the challenge that Swiss German has no standardized written form. The approach uses a fine-tuned T5 model to translate standard German text to Swiss German, followed by a VITS model to synthesize speech from the translated text. Three VITS models were trained on different datasets (SDS-200, SwissDial, SlowSoft) and evaluated using both automated metrics and human evaluation, demonstrating that high-quality training data is crucial for achieving good synthesis results in this low-resource language scenario.

## Method Summary
The authors developed a two-step pipeline for Swiss German TTS: first, a T5 model was fine-tuned on the SwissDial dataset to translate standard German text into Swiss German text; second, three VITS models were trained using ESPNet, each on a different dataset (SDS-200, SwissDial, SlowSoft). The VITS models used character-level input with x-vector speaker embeddings (512-dimensional). Evaluation included automated metrics (MCD, log-F0 RMSE, CER, WER, BLEU, vocoder discriminator scores) and human Mean Opinion Score (MOS) ratings from 7 native Swiss German speakers on 15 synthesized samples.

## Key Results
- The VITS model trained on the highest quality data (SlowSoft) achieved the best MOS score of 4.1 out of 5
- SDS-200 model performed best on MCD and log-F0 RMSE metrics but SwissDial model performed best on all other metrics
- T5 translation model achieved BLEU score of 50.47 on SwissDial test set across all eight dialects
- High-quality training data proved more important than dataset size for synthesis quality

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: A two-step pipeline (translation + synthesis) enables high-quality Swiss German TTS despite no standardized written form.
- Mechanism: The T5 model translates standard German text into Swiss German dialect text, which the VITS model then synthesizes into speech. This allows leveraging standard German text as input while producing authentic Swiss German speech output.
- Core assumption: The T5 model can accurately translate standard German into various Swiss German dialects, and the VITS model can synthesize high-quality speech from this translated text.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we train a textual translation system for standard German to Swiss German text (although there is no formal grammar for it), which is used as preprocessing to input Swiss German text to the TTS system."
  - [section 4.1]: "The standard German to Swiss German text translation is based on a T5 model [11] fine-tuned on the SwissDial data set [1]."
  - [section 6.1]: "The T5 model achieves a BLEU score of 50.47 on the test split of the SwissDial corpus over all the eight dialects."
- Break condition: If the translation model cannot handle the variability and lack of standardized grammar in Swiss German, or if the VITS model cannot produce natural-sounding speech from the translated text.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: High-quality training data leads to superior synthesis results, even with smaller datasets.
- Mechanism: The VITS models trained on high-quality corpora (SwissDial and SlowSoft) achieve better automated and human evaluation scores than the model trained on a larger but noisier dataset (SDS-200).
- Core assumption: Data quality is more important than quantity for training TTS models, especially for low-resource languages like Swiss German.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our results show that using high-quality data and using a translation system for translating standard German text to Swiss German text, yields high quality synthesized speech."
  - [section 6.2]: "The SDS-200 model performed best on the MCD and log-F0 RMSE metrics... But on all other metrics, the SwissDial model performed best."
  - [section 6.3]: "The VITS model trained with the high-quality Slowsoft samples performs best with an average MOS score of 4.1."
- Break condition: If the high-quality data is insufficient to capture the linguistic diversity of Swiss German dialects, or if the noise reduction in SDS-200 was actually beneficial for training.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: End-to-end TTS models (VITS) outperform cascaded models for Swiss German synthesis.
- Mechanism: VITS jointly optimizes the acoustic model and vocoder, allowing for better handling of the nuances in Swiss German speech compared to traditional cascaded approaches.
- Core assumption: The end-to-end architecture of VITS is better suited for low-resource languages with significant dialectal variation than traditional cascaded TTS systems.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we found, that VITS models performed best, hence, using them for further testing."
  - [section 2.2]: "End-to-end TTS systems try to jointly optimize the acoustic model and the vocoder. This system is used by the VITS model [4]."
  - [section 6.3]: "This shows that TTS methods are applicable to the case of Swiss German provided that the training data is of high quality and a text translation from standard German to Swiss German text is available."
- Break condition: If the end-to-end model overfits to the specific training data or cannot generalize well to unseen dialects or speakers.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Standard German text → T5 translation → Swiss German text → VITS synthesis → Swiss German speech

- Critical path: Standard German text → T5 translation → Swiss German text → VITS synthesis → Swiss German speech

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Translation vs. direct synthesis: Using a translation step allows leveraging standard German text as input but adds complexity to the pipeline.
  - Data quality vs. quantity: High-quality, smaller datasets outperform larger, noisier datasets for training the VITS model.
  - End-to-end vs. cascaded approaches: VITS offers potential benefits for low-resource languages but may require more data to train effectively.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor translation quality: Output speech may contain grammatical errors or use inappropriate vocabulary for the target dialect.
  - Overfitting to training data: Synthesized speech may lack naturalness or struggle with unseen words or dialects.
  - Pronunciation issues: The model may mispronounce certain words or struggle with dialect-specific phonetic features.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do VITS models trained on Swiss German data generalize to dialects not represented in the training data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses training VITS models on three different Swiss German datasets representing various dialects, but does not explore their ability to handle unseen dialects.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the models on dialects present in the training data, leaving open questions about their ability to generalize to completely new Swiss German dialects.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training a VITS model on data from a subset of Swiss German dialects and evaluating its performance on held-out dialects would provide insight into generalization capabilities.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the quality of synthesized Swiss German speech compare to that of high-resource languages using similar TTS architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates high-quality synthesis for Swiss German but does not compare it to synthesis quality in languages with abundant training data.
- Why unresolved: Without a direct comparison to high-resource language synthesis, it's unclear whether the challenges of Swiss German data scarcity are fully overcome.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a side-by-side comparison of Swiss German VITS synthesis quality with English or German synthesis using the same evaluation metrics would provide a benchmark.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of using different speaker embedding dimensions on the quality of synthesized Swiss German speech?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions using x-vectors with a 512-dimensional embedding but does not explore the effect of varying this dimension.
- Why unresolved: The choice of embedding dimension may affect the model's ability to capture speaker characteristics and dialect variations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training VITS models with different speaker embedding dimensions and evaluating their impact on synthesis quality using MOS scores would clarify the optimal configuration.

## Limitations

- Translation quality variability: The paper doesn't report translation quality on the test set used for TTS evaluation, leaving uncertainty about whether translation errors propagate to final audio output.
- Dataset representativeness: The paper doesn't analyze whether the three datasets adequately represent the full diversity of Swiss German dialects, despite SlowSoft yielding the best results despite being smallest.
- Automated metrics limitations: The paper relies heavily on automated metrics without systematically analyzing their correlation with human perception, which is crucial for low-resource languages.

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The VITS model architecture and training methodology are well-established in the literature. The finding that high-quality data (SlowSoft) outperforms larger noisy datasets (SDS-200) aligns with known principles in machine learning and speech synthesis.

**Medium confidence**: The two-step pipeline approach (translation + synthesis) is effective for Swiss German TTS. However, the paper doesn't explore alternative approaches like direct synthesis from standard German or multilingual models, which could provide important baselines.

**Low confidence**: Claims about the general applicability of this approach to other low-resource languages with dialectal variation. The specific linguistic characteristics of Swiss German and the availability of the SwissDial dataset make this case somewhat unique.
