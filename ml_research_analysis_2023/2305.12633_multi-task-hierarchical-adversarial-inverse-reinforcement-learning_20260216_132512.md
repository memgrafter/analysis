# Multi-task Hierarchical Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.12633
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12633
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MH-AIRL learns hierarchically-structured multi-task policies from unstructured expert demonstrations without task or skill annotations, outperforming state-of-the-art multi-task imitation learning baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Multi-task Hierarchical Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MH-AIRL), a method that learns hierarchical multi-task policies from unstructured expert demonstrations without requiring task or skill annotations. The approach extends context-based meta learning, hierarchical learning, and adversarial inverse reinforcement learning by conditioning policies on a Hierarchical Latent Context Structure comprising global task codes and local option choices. MH-AIRL maximizes mutual and directed information between trajectories and latent contexts while recovering hierarchical reward functions and policies via an adversarial framework. Experiments on complex Mujoco and D4RL tasks demonstrate superior performance compared to state-of-the-art multi-task imitation learning baselines, with learned basic skills that can be transferred to out-of-distribution tasks for effective zero-shot adaptation.

## Method Summary
MH-AIRL synthesizes context-based multi-task learning, AIRL (an IL approach), and hierarchical policy learning into a unified framework. The algorithm operates on unstructured expert demonstrations (state-action pairs only) by treating task and skill annotations as latent variables. It employs a five-network architecture including high-level and low-level policies, a discriminator for reward recovery, and variational posteriors for latent context estimation. The Hierarchical Policy Proximal Policy Optimization (HPPO) algorithm combines three objectives: maximizing mutual information between trajectories and global task codes, maximizing directed information between trajectories and local option sequences, and minimizing imitation loss through adversarial learning. This approach enables the discovery of hierarchical task structures and transferable skills without supervision.

## Key Results
- MH-AIRL outperforms state-of-the-art multi-task imitation learning baselines on challenging Mujoco and D4RL tasks including HalfCheetah-MultiVel, Walker-RandParam, Ant-MultiGoal, and Kitchen-MultiSeq
- The learned hierarchical policies demonstrate effective zero-shot adaptation to out-of-distribution tasks through transferable basic skills
- MH-AIRL successfully recovers task-conditioned hierarchical policies from unstructured expert demonstrations without task or skill annotations
- Evaluations show superior performance and transferability of multi-task policies compared to monolithic policy approaches

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: MH-AIRL's hierarchical latent context structure enables the policy to capture compositional task structures without requiring task or skill annotations.
- Mechanism: By maximizing mutual information (LM I) between trajectories and global task codes C, and directed information (LDI ) between trajectories and local option sequences Z0:T conditioned on C, the algorithm learns to associate specific option sequences with different subtask structures within expert demonstrations.
- Core assumption: Expert demonstrations contain implicit subtask structures that can be discovered through mutual and directed information maximization without explicit supervision.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "MH-AIRL can be adopted to demonstrations without the task or skill annotations (i.e., state-action pairs only) which are more accessible in practice."
  - [section]: "In this case, the policy learned should be additionally conditioned on Z besides the task code C, and the option choices Z0:T serve as the local latent contexts."
- Break condition: If expert demonstrations lack consistent subtask structures or if the mutual/directed information objectives cannot distinguish between different compositional patterns, the learned options may not correspond to meaningful subtasks.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The hierarchical adversarial inverse reinforcement learning (H-AIRL) component recovers task-conditioned hierarchical policies from unstructured expert demonstrations through an EM-style adaptation.
- Mechanism: H-AIRL extends AIRL by incorporating option choices Z into the state-action space, allowing the discriminator to distinguish expert trajectories based on both states/actions and options. The EM-style adaptation samples latent codes using variational posteriors when annotations are unavailable.
- Core assumption: The option framework can be integrated into AIRL's adversarial learning framework without breaking the theoretical guarantees of reward recovery.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "MH-AIRL can be adopted to demonstrations without the task or skill annotations (i.e., state-action pairs only) which are more accessible in practice."
  - [section]: "In practice, the unstructured expert data {X E
0:T}, i.e., trajectories only, is more accessible. In this case, we can view the latent contexts as hidden variables in a hidden Markov model (HMM) (Eddy, 1996) shown as Figure 1 and adopt an EM-style adaption to our algorithm."
- Break condition: If the EM-style adaptation fails to provide accurate posterior estimates for latent codes, the recovered hierarchical policy may not match the expert's true policy structure.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The HPPO algorithm, which synthesizes the optimization of context-based multi-task learning, AIRL, and hierarchical policy learning, provides superior performance on long-horizon tasks compared to monolithic policy approaches.
- Mechanism: HPPO combines three objectives (LM I, LDI , and LIL) into a single advantage function that accounts for both global task context and local option choices, enabling more efficient credit assignment across hierarchical levels.
- Core assumption: The combination of mutual information, directed information, and adversarial imitation learning objectives provides complementary gradients that improve hierarchical policy learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "evaluations on challenging multi-task settings demonstrate superior performance and transferability of the multi-task policies learned with MH-AIRL as compared to SOTA MIL baselines."

- Break condition: If the weight balancing between the three objectives (α1, α2, α3) is not properly tuned, the combined gradients may conflict and degrade performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - C → πθ → Z → πφ → A → Environment → (S, R) → Update all networks

- Critical path: Task context C flows through high-level policy πθ to select options Z, which condition low-level policy πφ to generate actions A. Environment returns new states and rewards, used to update all networks through respective objectives.

- Design tradeoffs: Algorithm balances complexity (five networks) against expressiveness (hierarchical structure) and data efficiency (transferable basic skills). Choice of AIRL vs. GAIL affects reward recovery and policy quality.

- Failure signatures:
  - Options Z don't change over time → High-level policy πθ is not learning meaningful distinctions
  - Low-level policy πφ ignores Z → Option selection has no effect on primitive actions
  - Discriminator fϑ doesn't converge → Reward recovery fails, leading to poor policy learning
  - Variational posteriors Pψ and Pω don't improve → Latent context estimation remains inaccurate

- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the algorithm's overall performance rather than the specific impact of exploration-exploitation trade-offs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies comparing different exploration strategies (e.g., entropy regularization, curiosity-driven exploration) and their impact on learning speed and final performance.

### Open Question 3

- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that MH-AIRL can be applied to demonstrations without task or skill annotations, but doesn't evaluate its robustness to noisy or imperfect demonstrations.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the algorithm's ability to learn from clean demonstrations rather than its robustness to real-world data imperfections.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments adding various types of noise (e.g., Gaussian noise, label noise) to the expert demonstrations and measuring the impact on learning performance.

## Limitations
- The paper lacks detailed implementation specifications for the five-network architecture and HPPO training procedure, which are critical for faithful reproduction
- The EM-style adaptation for latent code sampling when annotations are unavailable is not fully specified, potentially affecting reward recovery quality
- The claim that MH-AIRL enables zero-shot adaptation to out-of-distribution tasks relies heavily on the assumption that learned basic skills will transfer, but this transfer capability is not thoroughly validated across diverse task distributions

## Confidence
- **High**: Theoretical foundations linking mutual/directed information to latent context discovery
- **Medium**: Core claims about hierarchical structure learning through information maximization objectives
- **Low**: Specific implementation details needed to reproduce the results
