# Symbol emergence as interpersonal cross-situational learning: the emergence of lexical knowledge with combinatoriality

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.15837
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15837
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: A computational model enabling agents to acquire lexical knowledge with combinatoriality through category formation and word sequence exchange, demonstrated through experiments with two humanoid robots in a simulated environment.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a computational model for symbol emergence where agents acquire lexical knowledge with combinatoriality through interpersonal cross-situational learning. The proposed Inter-CSL-PGM model extends the Metropolis-Hastings naming game by enabling agents to form categories from multimodal sensory-motor information and share word sequences representing situations. The model was validated through experiments with two humanoid robots in a simulated environment, demonstrating successful acquisition of lexical knowledge with combinatoriality and the ability to predict sensory-motor information for novel situations through interpersonal cross-modal inference.

## Method Summary
The model employs the Inter-CSL-PGM framework where two agents observe multimodal sensory-motor data from shared situations, form categories using CSL-PGM components, and exchange word sequences via the Metropolis-Hastings naming game. The mutual exclusivity constraint biases novel words toward unknown categories. Agents alternate between speaker and listener roles across 40 trials, with the speaker proposing word sequences and the listener updating its knowledge probabilistically. The model enables cross-modal inference where agents can predict sensory-motor information for novel situations by combining words associated with categories in each modality.

## Key Results
- Agents successfully acquired lexical knowledge with combinatoriality through interpersonal cross-situational learning in a simulated environment
- The model demonstrated strong generalization performance for novel situations through interpersonal cross-modal inference
- Mutual exclusivity constraint was identified as crucial for the emergence of lexical knowledge with combinatoriality

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The model enables agents to form categories from multimodal sensory-motor information and associate them with words through cross-situational learning.
- Mechanism: The Inter-CSL-PGM combines two CSL-PGMs into a unified model where agents perform category formation from multimodal data (action, position, object, color) and share word sequences via the Metropolis-Hastings naming game.
- Core assumption: Agents can simultaneously learn perceptual categories and linguistic labels through repeated interaction and probabilistic inference.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our proposed model facilitates the emergence of lexical knowledge with combinatoriality by performing category formation using multimodal sensory-motor information and enabling semiotic communication through the exchange of word sequences among agents in a unified model."
  - [section 2.2] "The inference process of wdn based on the M-H naming game is interpreted as a process in which each agent probabilistically accepts a word proposed by another agent based on its own knowledge, and updates its own knowledge if the word is accepted."
  - [corpus] Weak - the corpus doesn't directly mention this specific mechanism but relates to emergent communication in multi-agent systems.
- Break condition: If the mutual exclusivity constraint is removed or the acceptance rate becomes too low, category-word associations may fail to form reliably.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The model enables agents to predict sensory-motor information for novel situations by combining words associated with categories in each modality.
- Mechanism: Through interpersonal cross-modal inference, agents can take a novel word sequence, infer the corresponding categories in each modality, and generate predicted observations by combining these category-specific predictions.
- Core assumption: Categories learned in individual modalities can be composed to represent and predict novel combinations not seen during training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Furthermore, the model enables an agent to predict sensory-motor information for unobserved situations by combining words associated with categories in each modality."
  - [section 2.3] "By combining these learned words associated with categories in each modality, the proposed model enables the agents to generate word sequences that describe novel situations and to predict sensory-motor information from new word sequences."
  - [corpus] Missing - no direct evidence in corpus about cross-modal prediction.
- Break condition: If the word-category associations are too noisy or categories are not sufficiently distinct across modalities, prediction accuracy will degrade.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The mutual exclusivity constraint (MEC) plays a crucial role in enabling the emergence of lexical knowledge with combinatoriality.
- Mechanism: The MEC re-scales word generation probabilities by dividing the probability of generating a word from a specific category by the sum of probabilities of that word across all categories, effectively biasing novel words toward unlearned categories.
- Core assumption: A bias toward assigning novel words to unknown categories accelerates the learning of distinct word-category mappings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "By dividing the numerator with this value, the generation probability of frequently observed words decreases, while the generation probability of less observed words increases. Through this re-scaling computation, we modeled the mutual exclusivity bias."
  - [section 3.3.3] "Furthermore, it was suggested that the mutual exclusivity constraint, which assigns novel words to unknown situational elements, plays a crucial role in interpersonal cross-situational learning for the emergence of lexical knowledge with combinatoriality."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus mentions compositionality and emergent communication but not specifically about mutual exclusivity bias.
- Break condition: If the MEC is removed (as in the w/o MEC baseline), word distributions become less modality-specific, reducing the emergence of combinatorial knowledge.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Inter-CSL-PGM -> CSL-PGM (per agent) -> M-H Naming Game -> MEC -> Cross-modal inference module

- Critical path:
  1. Agents observe multimodal sensory-motor data from shared situations
  2. Each agent forms categories from their observations using CSL-PGM
  3. Agents exchange word sequences via M-H naming game
  4. Categories and words are associated through cross-situational learning
  5. For novel situations, agents compose word sequences from learned category-word associations
  6. Other agents predict sensory-motor information from novel word sequences via cross-modal inference

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fixed word sequence order vs. learned structure: The model uses a fixed order of modalities (action, position, object, color) rather than learning the compositional structure.
  - Single integrated category vs. modality-specific categories: Inter-MDM-H2H-G uses integrated categories while Inter-CSL-PGM maintains modality-specific categories for better combinatoriality.
  - Communication frequency: More frequent word exchanges could speed learning but increase computational cost.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor categorization accuracy (low ARI) indicates issues with the CSL-PGM component
  - Low Kappa values between agents indicate failure in word sharing
  - High MSE in cross-modal inference indicates failure in combinatorial generalization
  - No convergence in M-H acceptance rates suggests communication breakdown

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the M-H naming game with two agents on a simple dataset with 2-3 objects and 2 modalities to verify basic word sharing emerges
  2. Test the cross-modal inference by having one agent propose word sequences for novel combinations and measure prediction error
  3. Compare performance with and without the MEC to quantify its impact on learning efficiency and combinatoriality
