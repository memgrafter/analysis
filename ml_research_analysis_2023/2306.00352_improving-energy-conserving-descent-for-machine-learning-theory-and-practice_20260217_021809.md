# Improving Energy Conserving Descent for Machine Learning: Theory and Practice

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2306.00352
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00352
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Key outcome:** ECDSep achieves competitive or improved performance compared to SGD, Adam, and AdamW on diverse ML tasks including image classification, graph node classification, and NLP

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Energy Conserving Descent (ECD), a novel optimization framework based on physical principles of energy conservation and chaos. The authors develop ECDSep, a gradient-based algorithm that enables analytic control over result distributions even for high-dimensional problems without symmetries. The method combines symplectic integration with chaos-inducing momentum rotations to balance exploration and exploitation during optimization. Empirically, ECDSep demonstrates competitive performance across multiple ML domains including image classification (CIFAR-100, Tiny Imagenet, ImageNet-1K), graph tasks (ogbn-arxiv, ogbn-proteins), and NLP benchmarks (GLUE with BERT), often matching or exceeding established optimizers like Adam and AdamW.

## Method Summary
The paper develops ECDSep based on a separable Hamiltonian framework where optimization is viewed as evolving a physical system with conserved energy. The algorithm uses symplectic integration to maintain energy conservation while introducing controlled chaos through momentum rotations. The update rules involve Hamiltonian dynamics with position X and momentum Π, where energy conservation is enforced by rescaling Π when necessary. Chaos is induced through random momentum rotations controlled by a hyperparameter ν, which determines the bounce angle per step as ν√n for an n-dimensional problem. The method separates energy conservation from chaos induction, allowing independent control over both aspects of the dynamics. This framework theoretically enables analytic control over the distribution of optimization trajectories and results.

## Key Results
- ECDSep achieves competitive performance with AdamW on CIFAR-100 (WideResNet28x10: 77.5% vs 77.1%) and Tiny Imagenet (ResNet-18: 56.3% vs 56.5%)
- On ogbn-arxiv graph node classification, ECDSep with GIN achieves ROC-AUC of 0.7468 compared to AdamW's 0.7483
- ECDSep with BERT-base on GLUE benchmark achieves Matthews correlation of 59.6 compared to AdamW's 58.6
- ECDSep shows improved fine-tuning performance on pre-trained models (ImageNet-1K: 77.1% vs AdamW's 76.6%)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ECD works by leveraging physical principles of energy conservation and chaos to guide optimization. The symplectic integration preserves the underlying Hamiltonian structure, ensuring that the optimization trajectory follows the natural geometry of the loss landscape. The chaos-inducing momentum rotations prevent the optimizer from getting stuck in local minima by periodically "kicking" the system with controlled randomness. This combination allows the method to maintain stability (through energy conservation) while exploring the parameter space (through chaos), effectively balancing exploitation and exploration. The analytic control over result distributions emerges from the deterministic yet chaotic nature of the dynamics, which concentrates outcomes around low-loss regions even in high-dimensional spaces.

## Foundational Learning
- **Energy conservation in optimization**: Understanding how maintaining a conserved quantity (energy) constrains the optimization trajectory and prevents erratic behavior
  - *Why needed*: Energy conservation provides stability and prevents the optimizer from diverging or oscillating wildly
  - *Quick check*: Verify that the total energy Π²/2 + F(X) remains approximately constant during optimization
- **Symplectic integration**: Numerical methods that preserve the geometric structure of Hamiltonian systems, crucial for accurate long-term dynamics
  - *Why needed*: Standard integration schemes can accumulate errors that violate energy conservation, degrading optimization quality
  - *Quick check*: Monitor energy drift over many optimization steps to ensure symplectic properties
- **Chaos theory in ML optimization**: How controlled randomness can improve exploration without sacrificing convergence guarantees
  - *Why needed*: High-dimensional loss landscapes have many local minima; controlled chaos helps escape suboptimal solutions
  - *Quick check*: Measure the distribution of final losses to verify concentration around global optima
- **Separable Hamiltonian dynamics**: The mathematical framework that allows independent control of position and momentum evolution
  - *Why needed*: Separability enables efficient computation and independent tuning of energy conservation and chaos parameters
  - *Quick check*: Verify that the update rules correctly separate kinetic and potential energy terms
- **Hyperparameter scaling with dimensionality**: How learning rates, chaos parameters, and other hyperparameters should scale with problem size
  - *Why needed*: The optimal hyperparameter values depend on the number of parameters and the initialization scale
  - *Quick check*: Test whether ν√n provides consistent performance across problems of different dimensionalities

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Initial parameters X₀, Π₀ -> Energy-conserving symplectic integrator -> Chaos-inducing momentum rotations (controlled by ν) -> Updated parameters X, Π -> Loss evaluation -> Repeat
- **Critical path:** The symplectic integrator and momentum rotation steps form the core computational bottleneck, requiring careful numerical implementation to maintain energy conservation while being computationally efficient
- **Design tradeoffs:** Energy conservation vs. computational cost (rescaling Π), chaos intensity vs. convergence stability, and hyperparameter sensitivity vs. ease of tuning
- **Failure signatures:** Energy violation (Π² growing uncontrollably), insufficient chaos (convergence to poor local minima), and hyperparameter sensitivity (poor performance with small deviations from optimal values)
- **First experiments:** 1) Test ECDSep on Zakharov function with varying ν to observe chaos effects, 2) Compare energy conservation across different step sizes ∆t, 3) Validate the theoretical scaling ν√n by testing on problems of varying dimensionalities

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What is the optimal relationship between the chaos hyperparameter ν and the dimensionality n for different classes of machine learning problems?
  - *Basis in paper:* The paper derives that bounce angle is ν√n but observes different optimal regimes (fine-tuning vs. training from scratch) without systematic principles
  - *Why unresolved:* Empirical observations show varying optimal ν across problems but lack theoretical framework for choosing based on problem characteristics
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic experiments varying ν and n across diverse ML tasks with theoretical analysis connecting bounce angle to convergence rates

- **Open Question 2:** How should the F0 parameter be optimally set and scheduled during training for different overparameterized vs. underparameterized regimes?
  - *Basis in paper:* The paper derives theoretical advantages of non-zero F0 but only tests F0=0 experimentally
  - *Why unresolved:* Demonstrates theoretical benefits but doesn't explore joint behavior with other hyperparameters or scheduling strategies
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Experiments comparing fixed vs. adaptive F0 scheduling across problems with different levels of overparameterization

- **Open Question 3:** How do the hyperparameter scaling relationships derived in Appendix A.1 translate to practical performance improvements?
  - *Basis in paper:* Derives scaling relationships between learning rate, η, ν, and problem parameters but notes these remain theoretical predictions
  - *Why unresolved:* Observes some empirical patterns but doesn't systematically validate specific scaling predictions about exponential sensitivity to η
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Targeted experiments validating scaling predictions by varying initialization conditions and model sizes

## Limitations
- The method requires careful hyperparameter tuning, particularly for the chaos parameter ν which has exponential sensitivity to learning rate
- Theoretical guarantees are primarily asymptotic and may not fully capture practical optimization scenarios with noise and computational constraints
- Limited testing on real-world industrial-scale problems with distributed training and noisy gradients

## Confidence

- **Medium confidence:** Empirical results on standard benchmarks and synthetic functions
- **Low confidence:** Claims about practical superiority over established methods in real-world scenarios
