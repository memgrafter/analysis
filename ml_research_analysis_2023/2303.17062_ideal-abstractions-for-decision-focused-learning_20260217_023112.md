# Ideal Abstractions for Decision-Focused Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.17062
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17062
- Reference count: 23
- One-line primary result: ORIGAMI generates decision-focused abstractions that improve decision quality compared to raw high-dimensional outputs

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ORIGAMI, a method for generating decision-focused abstractions in machine learning by iteratively folding the probability simplex. The core idea is to minimize the loss of decision-relevant information when reducing high-dimensional output spaces to coarser representations that still support effective decision-making. ORIGAMI identifies partitions of the outcome space by grouping outcomes that have similar decision consequences, using H-entropy as a measure of decision quality. The approach is particularly valuable in data-limited regimes where learning full high-dimensional distributions is challenging.

The paper demonstrates ORIGAMI's effectiveness through experiments in wildfire management and active learning scenarios. In wildfire management, predicting sets of locations using ORIGAMI abstractions led to lower decision losses (0.701) compared to predicting exact locations (0.723). In active learning, ORIGAMI-based acquisition achieved 35.7% accuracy versus 17.6% for random acquisition. The method shows particular promise when data is scarce, though it also provides benefits in data-rich settings by reducing computational complexity.

## Method Summary
ORIGAMI works by iteratively folding the probability simplex to create abstractions that preserve decision-relevant information while reducing dimensionality. The algorithm uses H-entropy to measure the quality of abstractions, ensuring that grouped outcomes have similar decision consequences. The folding process involves identifying partitions of the outcome space and computing optimal weights for each partition that minimize decision loss. ORIGAMI can operate in two modes: exact computation using linear programming for smaller problems, and amortized prediction using neural networks for larger-scale applications. The method is compatible with existing decision-focused learning frameworks and can be integrated with various loss functions and decision models.

## Key Results
- In wildfire management, ORIGAMI abstractions achieved decision loss of 0.701 versus 0.723 for exact location prediction
- In active learning, ORIGAMI-based acquisition reached 35.7% accuracy compared to 17.6% for random acquisition
- ORIGAMI showed particular effectiveness in data-limited regimes, with performance gains increasing as training data decreased

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ORIGAMI works by identifying and exploiting the inherent structure in decision problems where certain groups of outcomes have similar consequences for decision-making. By folding the probability simplex, it creates abstractions that preserve the information most relevant to decisions while discarding redundant or irrelevant details. The H-entropy measure ensures that the resulting abstractions maintain decision quality by grouping outcomes that are indistinguishable from a decision perspective. This approach reduces the complexity of the learning problem while maintaining or improving decision performance, especially when data is limited or the full outcome space is high-dimensional.

## Foundational Learning
- **Decision-focused learning**: ML approach where models are trained to optimize decision quality directly rather than predictive accuracy. Needed because traditional ML often optimizes irrelevant metrics for decision-making. Quick check: Verify the loss function includes decision consequences.
- **H-entropy**: Information-theoretic measure that captures decision-relevant uncertainty. Needed to quantify how well abstractions preserve decision information. Quick check: Confirm H-entropy decreases when outcomes are grouped appropriately.
- **Probability simplex folding**: Mathematical operation that groups outcomes while maintaining probability constraints. Needed to create valid probability distributions over abstract outcomes. Quick check: Verify folded distributions sum to one.
- **Set extensions for decision loss**: Methods for extending point-wise losses to set predictions (worst-case vs. weighted sum). Needed to handle abstract predictions that represent sets of outcomes. Quick check: Compare decision losses using different set extensions.
- **Amortized learning**: Using neural networks to predict abstractions directly rather than computing them exactly. Needed for scalability to large outcome spaces. Quick check: Measure prediction accuracy vs. exact computation.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Raw outcomes -> H-entropy computation -> Folding algorithm -> Abstract representation -> Decision loss evaluation

**Critical path**: The core algorithm flow involves computing H-entropy for potential partitions, selecting optimal folds based on decision loss trade-offs, and iteratively building the abstraction hierarchy until reaching the desired level of coarseness.

**Design tradeoffs**: EXACT mode provides optimal abstractions but scales poorly with outcome space size; AMORTIZED mode trades some accuracy for scalability but requires training neural network surrogates; WORST-CASE set extension is conservative but may overestimate uncertainty; WEIGHTED-SUM extension is more optimistic but requires specifying weights.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Poor decision performance indicates H-entropy doesn't capture true decision relevance
- High variance in results suggests unstable folding or poor partition choices
- Computational intractability indicates need for amortization or approximation
- Degradation with more data suggests abstractions become too coarse

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of ORIGAMI compare when using different set extensions for the decision loss (e.g., worst-case vs. weighted sum)?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions two set extensions: worst-case (max) and weighted sum, and notes that the worst-case extension increases H-entropy while the weighted sum preserves it. However, it does not provide empirical comparisons of their impact on decision quality.
- Why unresolved: The paper discusses the theoretical properties of these extensions but does not test their practical implications in experiments.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing decision loss and accuracy when using worst-case vs. weighted sum extensions in the wildfire management and active learning experiments.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can ORIGAMI be extended to dynamic decision-making problems where the decision loss matrix changes over time?
- Basis in paper: The discussion section mentions that decision losses can change over time and suggests that ORIGAMI could track these changes by applying a modified algorithm capable of unfolding and folding.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore or validate this extension, leaving it as a theoretical possibility.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments demonstrating ORIGAMI's performance in a dynamic setting where the decision loss matrix evolves over time, showing whether it adapts effectively.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the amortized ORIGAMI approach scale with increasing numbers of classes, and what are the limitations of the neural network surrogate?
- Basis in paper: The paper reports that the prediction accuracy of the amortized model rapidly decreases as the number of classes increases, while the test RMSE loss increases. This indicates that the learning problem becomes increasingly difficult with more classes.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides initial results but does not explore the underlying reasons for the scaling issues or potential improvements to the neural network architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the amortized model's performance across a wider range of class sizes, including comparisons with alternative architectures or training strategies to improve scalability.
