# Making a Computational Attorney

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.05383
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05383
- Reference count: 29
- Key outcome: This paper explores the future of legal AI by proposing the concept of a "computational attorney" - an intelligent software agent capable of assisting human lawyers with complex high-level legal tasks like drafting legal briefs.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a vision for "computational attorneys" - intelligent software agents that can assist human lawyers with complex legal tasks beyond simple automation. While current large legal language models (L3Ms) show promise for automating basic legal tasks, the authors identify five key areas where these models need to improve: being updatable, stable, provable, communicable, and predictable. The paper argues that the first two objectives are within reach while the remaining three will require years of research. This conceptual framework provides a roadmap for advancing legal AI from simple task automation toward sophisticated legal reasoning capabilities.

## Method Summary
This paper presents a conceptual framework rather than a specific implementation. It analyzes the characteristics of the legal domain that make it suitable for large language models, identifies five critical requirements for computational attorneys, and discusses the current state and future research directions needed to achieve these requirements. The paper draws on existing literature about large language models, legal AI, and related fields to support its arguments, but does not provide a concrete training procedure or evaluation methodology.

## Key Results
- Large legal language models (L3Ms) have shown preliminary legal reasoning abilities beyond simple text classification
- Five key requirements for computational attorneys: updatable, stable, provable, communicable, and predictable
- First two requirements (updatable, stable) are within reach while the remaining three require years of research
- Legal domain characteristics create unique opportunities for L3M applications

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Large Legal Language Models (L3Ms) can leverage massive legal text data for self-supervised pre-training, enabling few-shot and zero-shot learning capabilities.
- Mechanism: The abundance of legal text data (60,000+ TBs, 256GB Pile-of-Law dataset) allows L3Ms to learn general legal language patterns and domain-specific terminology through self-supervised pre-training. This pre-training creates a foundation that can be adapted to specific legal tasks with minimal labeled examples.
- Core assumption: The scale and quality of legal text data is sufficient for effective pre-training that captures the nuances of legal language.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] mentions "massive scale of complex text data" and "majority of such data are in the form of long documents written in formal and professional language, such as legal judgments, legal opinions, and legal contracts"
  - [section] states "The characteristics of the legal domain mentioned in Section 1 suggest that L3M has huge advantages over traditional technical approaches to legal AI problems"
- Break condition: If the legal text data lacks sufficient diversity, contains too many domain-specific nuances that require specialized knowledge, or if the pre-training process fails to capture the formal structure of legal language.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: L3Ms exhibit emergent abilities for legal reasoning when scaled beyond certain thresholds, moving beyond simple text classification to complex legal analysis.
- Mechanism: As L3Ms increase in scale, they develop emergent properties that enable sophisticated reasoning capabilities. The paper notes that legal reasoning appears to be one of these emergent abilities, as demonstrated by GPT-3.5's performance on bar exam questions and ChatGPT's ability to handle nuanced legal concepts.
- Core assumption: There exists a phase-change threshold in model scale where emergent reasoning abilities appear, and that legal reasoning is one such ability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] states "when the scale of a L3M goes above a certain phase-change threshold, it will start to show some emergent abilities"
  - [section] provides evidence that "GPT-3.5 and ChatGPT...has shown the preliminary ability of legal reasoning which is lacking in its previous version GPT-3"
- Break condition: If scaling L3Ms does not consistently produce improved legal reasoning capabilities, or if the emergent abilities are superficial and fail under rigorous legal scrutiny.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: L3Ms can be updated post-deployment to maintain current legal knowledge without full retraining, addressing the "updatable" requirement for computational attorneys.
- Mechanism: Instead of expensive full retraining, techniques from stream data mining, machine unlearning, and lifelong learning can be applied to selectively update L3Ms with new legal information (e.g., Westlaw "Court Wire" and "KeyCite Overruling Risk"). This allows the model to forget obsolete knowledge and incorporate new precedents efficiently.
- Core assumption: Selective model editing techniques can effectively update L3Ms without degrading their overall performance or introducing inconsistencies.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] discusses the need for updatable models and mentions "early explorations of editing large language models [11, 12]"
  - [section] states "Since retraining the model from scratch to intentionally forget obsolete knowledge and incorporate additional desiderata would be very expensive...we would instead prefer new methods to update the model post-deployment"
- Break condition: If model editing techniques cannot preserve the model's existing capabilities while incorporating new legal knowledge, or if edited models become unstable or produce contradictory outputs.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Pre-training layer: Processes massive legal text corpora (judgments, contracts, opinions)
  - Fine-tuning layer: Adapts pre-trained model to specific legal tasks using limited labeled data
  - Update mechanism: Incorporates new legal information without full retraining
  - Reasoning module: Handles complex legal analysis and brief drafting
  - Communication interface: Enables effective interaction with human lawyers and clients

- Critical path: Pre-training → Fine-tuning → Complex task performance → Real-time updating
  The most critical path is ensuring the pre-training captures sufficient legal domain knowledge to enable emergent reasoning abilities during fine-tuning.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model size vs. update efficiency: Larger models may show better reasoning but are harder to update
  - General knowledge vs. legal specificity: Balancing broad legal knowledge with jurisdiction-specific expertise
  - Interpretability vs. performance: More interpretable models may sacrifice some reasoning capability

- Failure signatures:
  - Hallucination: Generating plausible-sounding but legally incorrect information
  - Outdated reasoning: Failing to incorporate recent legal precedents or overturned cases
  - Overconfidence: Providing definitive answers to questions requiring nuanced legal judgment

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test few-shot learning on a new legal task (e.g., contract clause classification) using a pre-trained L3M to establish baseline performance
  2. Implement a simple model editing technique to update the model with a recent landmark case and evaluate if reasoning about related cases improves
  3. Compare reasoning performance on complex legal scenarios between different model scales to identify potential emergent ability thresholds

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can legal language models be made updatable without requiring full retraining?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss the need for updatable models to remain current with new legal precedents and suggest that retraining from scratch would be expensive, preferring instead methods to update the model post-deployment.
- Why unresolved: Current techniques for updating large language models are still in early stages and their effectiveness for legal applications hasn't been demonstrated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Demonstration of a legal L3M that can be successfully updated with new legal information while maintaining performance and not requiring full retraining.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can legal language models be made provably correct in their reasoning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors argue that L3Ms need to be able to prove their legal opinions are derived by strictly following relevant rules, going beyond explainability to identify concrete applicable legislation.
- Why unresolved: Current L3Ms cannot provide formal proofs of their legal reasoning, and integrating formal verification methods with neural networks remains a research challenge.
- What evidence would resolve it: A system that can provide formal proofs of its legal reasoning steps and correctly cite applicable legislation for its conclusions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can legal language models be made predictable in anticipating implications and risks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that L3Ms must be able to anticipate implications of their outputs to stakeholders and potential risks in legal ecosystems, including imagining opponent responses.
- Why unresolved: Current L3Ms lack strategic thinking capabilities and the ability to model complex legal ecosystems with multiple stakeholders.
- What evidence would resolve it: A legal L3M that can accurately predict potential counterarguments, risks, and implications of its legal advice in multi-stakeholder scenarios.

## Limitations

- The paper presents a conceptual framework without concrete implementation or empirical validation
- No specific legal tasks or datasets are provided for reproduction attempts
- The timeline predictions for achieving the "provable," "communicable," and "predictable" objectives are highly speculative
- The paper does not address potential ethical concerns, bias mitigation, or regulatory compliance requirements

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The observation that legal domain characteristics (massive text data, professional language, domain-specific terminology) create opportunities for L3Ms is well-supported by existing literature and industry practices. The distinction between simple legal automation tasks and complex legal reasoning is clearly articulated and aligns with current capabilities of large language models.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that L3Ms will show emergent legal reasoning abilities when scaled beyond certain thresholds is plausible but not yet empirically validated for legal applications specifically. The identification of five key requirements for computational attorneys represents reasonable technical challenges, though the relative difficulty of each requirement may vary in practice.

**Low Confidence**: Specific predictions about timelines for achieving the "provable," "communicable," and "predictable" objectives are highly uncertain, as these require breakthroughs in explainable AI, human-AI interaction, and robust uncertainty quantification that may take longer than the "years of research" suggested. The paper's vision of computational attorneys handling complex tasks like brief drafting remains aspirational without addressing fundamental challenges in legal reasoning and judgment.

## Next Checks

1. **Empirical Scaling Study**: Conduct controlled experiments training L3Ms at multiple scales (small, medium, large) on legal tasks of increasing complexity to identify if and when emergent legal reasoning abilities appear, measuring performance on tasks ranging from text classification to brief drafting.

2. **Model Editing Validation**: Implement and evaluate specific model editing techniques on a pre-trained legal language model, testing whether updates with new legal precedents can be incorporated without degrading performance on existing tasks or introducing inconsistencies in legal reasoning.

3. **Human-AI Interaction Study**: Design and conduct user studies with practicing lawyers to assess the "communicable" requirement, measuring how effectively L3Ms can explain their legal reasoning, respond to lawyer queries, and collaborate on complex legal tasks while maintaining appropriate professional boundaries.
