# Revealed Multi-Objective Utility Aggregation in Human Driving

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.07435
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07435
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Data-driven estimation of utility aggregation parameters significantly improves predictive accuracy of behavior models compared to using fixed weights.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of estimating multi-objective utility aggregation parameters for human driving behavior from observational data. The authors develop rationalisability-based methods to estimate aggregation parameters for both weighted and satisficing aggregation under strategic and non-strategic reasoning models. Using three real-world traffic datasets, the methods successfully find rationalisable parameters for all games under weighted aggregation, while achieving high pass rates for satisficing aggregation. The study demonstrates that learned aggregation parameters significantly improve the predictive accuracy of driver behavior models compared to using fixed weights.

## Method Summary
The authors develop algorithms based on rationalisability to estimate multi-objective aggregation parameters from observational data. For weighted aggregation, linear programming is used for strategic models and nonlinear optimization for non-strategic models. For satisficing aggregation, the method partitions the utility space and checks rationalisability on representative points. The approach is evaluated on three real-world traffic datasets including intersection turns, roundabout entries, and pedestrian crosswalks, using various game-theoretic solution concepts to model driver reasoning.

## Key Results
- Rationalisable weighted aggregation parameters found for 100% of games across all datasets
- Satisficing aggregation parameters found for 87-95% of intersection games and 50-71% of roundabout games
- Data-driven parameter estimation improved predictive accuracy by up to 30% compared to fixed weights
- Weighted aggregation with strategic models (L1, L2) achieved the highest accuracy improvements

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Rationalisability-based disaggregation finds utility aggregation parameters that make observed behavior optimal under a given reasoning model by formulating constraints ensuring the chosen action yields the highest scalarized utility. For weighted aggregation, these are linear constraints; for satisficing aggregation, they are piecewise based on safety thresholds. The core assumption is that observed behavior was generated by a rational agent following the assumed reasoning model.

### Mechanism 2
Weighted aggregation parameters are estimated via linear programming for strategic models (maximizing utility of chosen action subject to optimality constraints) and nonlinear optimization for non-strategic models (due to argmax operator introducing nonlinearity). This approach assumes known game payoff structure and that the chosen action is indeed optimal for the underlying reasoning model.

### Mechanism 3
Satisficing aggregation parameter estimation uses efficient partitioning of the utility interval and checking rationalisability on representative points. For strategic models, partitioning is based on safety utilities in response to observed opponent action (O(|A|ᵢ) complexity); for non-strategic models, it spans all safety utilities in the game matrix (O(|A|ᴺ) complexity). The method assumes rationalisability is invariant within each partition interval.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data preprocessing → Game matrix construction → Parameter estimation → Model evaluation → Visualization
- **Critical path**: Data → Game matrix construction → Parameter estimation → Model validation → Insight generation
- **Design tradeoffs**: Strategic vs. non-strategic models (strategic allows linear programming but assumes accurate belief formation; non-strategic is more general but computationally heavier); weighted vs. satisficing aggregation (weighted is more flexible but may not capture threshold behavior; satisficing models aspiration levels but can be restrictive); partition granularity (finer partitions improve accuracy but increase computation time)
- **Failure signatures**: No rationalisable parameters found (model incompatibility or data quality issues); optimization fails to converge (need to adjust solver settings or reformulate constraints); accuracy doesn't improve (aggregation method or solution concept may be inappropriate)
- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Run parameter estimation on a small subset of intersection games with known ground truth parameters to verify correctness; 2) Compare pass rates between weighted and satisficing aggregation on the full roundabout dataset; 3) Evaluate accuracy improvement when using estimated parameters vs. fixed weights [0.5, 0.5] across all solution concepts

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What is the effect of including more than two objectives (e.g., comfort, time) on the rationalisability of utility aggregation parameters? The paper focuses on safety and progress as objectives, and it would be interesting to see if the methods hold for more objectives. This is unresolved as the paper does not extend the methods to more than two objectives.

### Open Question 2
How do the utility aggregation parameters vary with driver demographics such as age, experience, and gender? The paper notes that utility aggregation is often context dependent and individual specific, but does not explore demographic factors. This is unresolved as the datasets used do not include demographic information.

### Open Question 3
How robust are the estimated utility aggregation parameters to different levels of noise and uncertainty in the observational data? The paper uses real-world data which likely contains noise, but does not explicitly evaluate the robustness of the methods to noise. This is unresolved as the paper does not perform sensitivity analysis with respect to noise levels in the data.

## Limitations
- Assumes observed behavior was generated by the assumed reasoning models, which may not hold in real-world driving where drivers exhibit diverse and context-dependent decision-making patterns
- Weak external validation from the corpus, with few citations and limited direct evidence supporting the specific rationalisability formulations for driving contexts
- Unknown exact utility function forms for safety and progress, and missing details on solution concept implementations, limit faithful reproduction

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Linear programming formulation for weighted aggregation under strategic models is well-established and pass rates (100% for weighted aggregation) are robust across datasets
- **Medium Confidence**: Nonlinear optimization approach for weighted aggregation non-strategic models and partition-based satisficing aggregation methods are theoretically sound but have moderate pass rates (87-95% for weighted aggregation non-strategic, 50-71% for satisficing)
- **Low Confidence**: Weak corpus evidence and lack of ground truth parameter validation in real driving datasets make it difficult to assess whether estimated parameters truly reflect drivers' internal utility aggregation processes

## Next Checks
1. **Ground Truth Verification**: Apply the estimation methods to synthetic driving games with known utility parameters to verify that the algorithms recover parameters accurately and that pass rates reflect true rationalisability rather than estimation artifacts
2. **Cross-Scenario Consistency**: Compare estimated parameter distributions across the three datasets (intersections, roundabouts, crosswalks) to assess whether the method captures meaningful differences in driving behavior or produces dataset-specific artifacts
3. **Alternative Model Comparison**: Test the estimated parameters in alternative behavior models (e.g., softmax choice models) to determine whether the rationalisability framework provides unique insights or if simpler models could explain the observed behavior equally well
