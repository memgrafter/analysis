# Beyond Reward: Offline Preference-guided Policy Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16217
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16217
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 35
- Key outcome: OPPO achieves competitive or better performance compared to Decision Transformer trained with true rewards while only requiring preference labels, showing the effectiveness of directly optimizing policies in a high-dimensional embedding space.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses offline preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL), where an agent learns from pre-collected trajectories and human preferences without online interaction or reward specifications. The proposed Offline Preference-guided Policy Optimization (OPPO) method learns a contextual policy directly from preference-labeled data in a one-step process, avoiding the information bottleneck of separately learning a scalar reward function. OPPO combines an offline hindsight information matching objective for policy optimization with a preference modeling objective to find the optimal context. Empirically, OPPO demonstrates superior performance over baselines including offline RL algorithms with true or learned rewards on continuous control tasks from the D4RL benchmark.

## Method Summary
OPPO jointly learns a contextual policy π(a|s,z) and an optimal context embedding z* from offline trajectories and preference labels. The method uses a hindsight information encoder Iθ to map trajectories to a high-dimensional embedding space, then optimizes the policy conditioned on these embeddings. The preference modeling objective uses triplet loss to find z* that captures optimal behaviors, while the hindsight information matching objective ensures the policy can reconstruct trajectories. This one-step optimization avoids the information bottleneck of first learning a scalar reward function, enabling direct policy optimization from preference-labeled data.

## Key Results
- OPPO outperforms standard offline RL baselines on D4RL tasks when given only preference labels
- OPPO achieves competitive performance to Decision Transformer trained with true rewards while requiring only preference labels
- The learned context z* consistently leads to better policy performance than conditioning on other trajectory embeddings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The one-step optimization avoids information bottleneck from separate reward learning
- Mechanism: OPPO jointly models offline trajectories and preferences through contextual policy π(a|s,z) and hindsight information encoder Iθ, eliminating the need to first learn a scalar reward function
- Core assumption: Scalar reward functions cannot fully capture preference information, creating a bottleneck for policy optimization
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "requires the separate learning of a scalar reward function, which is assumed to be an information bottleneck of the learning process"
  - [section 2] "the practice of separately learning a reward function that explains expert preferences may not directly instruct the policy on how to act optimally"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus papers explicitly testing this claim
- Break condition: If preferences can be perfectly captured by a scalar reward function, the bottleneck disappears and separate reward learning would be sufficient

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: High-dimensional z-space captures more task information than scalar reward
- Mechanism: The hindsight information matching objective learns to encode trajectories into a rich embedding space that preserves preference relationships
- Core assumption: Human preferences contain multidimensional information that cannot be compressed into a scalar reward without loss
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This high-dimensional z-space captures more task-related information compared to scalar reward, making it ideal for policy optimization purposes"
  - [section 5.1] "the trajectories (embeddings) that are near z*∗ often have high returns (points with a deeper color)" - shows the embedding space preserves performance information
  - [section 5.2] "the contextual policy π(a|s, z) conditioned on z with a high (or low) return (of corresponding trajectory τ = Iθ−1(z)) obtains an actual high (or low) return when rolling out this policy" - confirms the embedding captures task-relevant information
- Break condition: If preferences are purely linear in some feature space, a scalar reward could capture them exactly

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Preference modeling via triplet loss finds optimal context z*
- Mechanism: The preference modeling objective LPM optimizes for an embedding z* that is closer to preferred trajectories than non-preferred ones using contrastive learning
- Core assumption: Humans compare trajectories by judging similarity to an implicit optimal trajectory, which can be modeled as finding z*
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "our basic assumption of designing the objective in Eq.7 is that humans normally conduct two-level comparisons before giving preferences between two trajectories"
  - [section 5.1] "our learned optimal z∗ constantly stays close to actual optimal z∗∗, which suggests that our learned z∗ preserves near-optimal behaviors"
  - [section 5.2] "when conditioned on the learned optimal z∗, π(a|s, z∗) produces the best performance over that conditioned on all other offline embeddings" - shows z* leads to best policy
- Break condition: If human preferences are inconsistent or non-transitive, the optimal z* may not exist or be identifiable

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Iθ: Hindsight information encoder (BERT-like) that maps trajectories τ → z
  - π(a|s,z): Contextual policy (GPT-like) that predicts actions given state and context
  - z*: Optimal context embedding learned through preference modeling
  - LHIM: Hindsight information matching loss combining trajectory reconstruction and information matching
  - LPM: Preference modeling loss using triplet loss
  - Ltotal: Combined loss with weighting coefficients α and β

- Critical path: Iθ → z → π(a|s,z) → environment interaction
  - Forward: trajectory → Iθ → z → π → actions
  - Backward: preferences → LPM → Iθ, z* and LHIM → π, Iθ

- Design tradeoffs:
  - High-dimensional z vs. computational efficiency (more dimensions capture more information but increase computational cost)
  - Separate vs. joint optimization of Iθ and π (joint optimization is more efficient but may be less stable)
  - Fixed z* vs. distribution over contexts (fixed point is simpler but distribution could capture uncertainty)

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor preference alignment: t-SNE shows z-space doesn't separate preferred vs. non-preferred trajectories
  - Suboptimal performance: π(a|s,z*) doesn't outperform conditioning on other z values
  - Instability: Training loss oscillates or diverges during joint optimization

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify Iθ captures preferences: Train on toy dataset with known preference structure, check if t-SNE shows clear separation
  2. Test z* optimality: Train with synthetic preferences, verify π(a|s,z*) outperforms conditioning on other z values
  3. Ablation study: Remove LPM and verify performance degrades, confirming preference modeling is essential

## Limitations
- The paper lacks direct empirical validation of the claimed information bottleneck advantage
- Preference modeling assumes humans compare trajectories by judging similarity to an optimal trajectory, which may not hold universally
- The approach hasn't been validated on domains beyond continuous control tasks

## Confidence

**High confidence** in the empirical results showing OPPO outperforms baselines on D4RL tasks. The experimental methodology is sound and results are statistically significant.

**Medium confidence** in the theoretical claims about information bottlenecks. While the argument is plausible, there's no direct measurement of information content in z-space versus scalar rewards.

**Medium confidence** in the preference modeling mechanism. The Bradley-Terry model is well-established, but the extension to finding optimal z* through triplet loss, while reasonable, hasn't been rigorously validated outside this specific implementation.

## Next Checks

1. **Information Bottleneck Validation**: Measure mutual information between (z, return) pairs and compare to (reward, return) pairs to directly quantify the information advantage claimed.

2. **Preference Model Ablation**: Systematically vary the preference modeling objective strength (β parameter) and measure the impact on both z-space structure and final policy performance to confirm the mechanism.

3. **Generalization Test**: Evaluate OPPO on a completely different task domain (e.g., Atari or simulated robotics) to verify the approach isn't overfit to the continuous control tasks used in the paper.
