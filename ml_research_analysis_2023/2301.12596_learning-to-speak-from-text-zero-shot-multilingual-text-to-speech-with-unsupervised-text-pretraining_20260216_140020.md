# Learning to Speak from Text: Zero-Shot Multilingual Text-to-Speech with Unsupervised Text Pretraining

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2301.12596
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12596
- **Reference count**: 15
- **Primary result**: Character error rate <12% for zero-shot synthesis of unseen languages

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for zero-shot multilingual text-to-speech synthesis that leverages unsupervised text pretraining to enable synthesis for languages without paired speech data. The approach uses multilingual masked language model pretraining with text-only data to learn cross-lingual representations, which are then transferred to a TTS model through a language-aware embedding layer. The framework demonstrates highly intelligible zero-shot synthesis with character error rates below 12% for unseen languages, outperforming phone-based baselines and improving performance for seen languages as well.

## Method Summary
The framework first performs multilingual masked language model pretraining on text-only data using a language-aware embedding layer that combines token embeddings, language embeddings, and a bottleneck for dimensionality reduction. This pretrained model is then used to initialize a TTS system, where the encoder and decoder are trained on paired text-audio data while freezing the language-aware embedding layer to preserve cross-lingual knowledge. The approach uses bytes as input tokens rather than phonemes, eliminating the need for grapheme-to-phoneme conversion modules and improving cross-lingual generalization.

## Key Results
- Zero-shot TTS for unseen languages achieved character error rates below 12%
- Byte-based models outperformed phone-based baselines for all seen languages
- Multilingual MLM pretraining improved TTS performance for both seen and unseen languages
- Freezing the language-aware embedding layer during supervised training preserved cross-lingual transfer capabilities

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multilingual MLM pretraining transfers cross-lingual representations to improve zero-shot TTS for unseen languages
- Mechanism: Pretraining on multilingual text-only data learns shared linguistic features across languages. These representations, when used as encoder inputs, enable the model to generate intelligible speech for languages not seen during supervised TTS training.
- Core assumption: The multilingual MLM pretraining effectively learns language-agnostic phonetic and prosodic representations that can generalize to unseen languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Inspired by the strong cross-lingual transferability of multilingual language models, our framework first performs masked language model pretraining with multilingual text-only data."
  - [section] "Previous research [Wu and Dredze, 2019; Pires et al., 2019] has shown the strong cross-lingual transferability of multilingual language models such as multilingual BERT [Devlin et al., 2019] in natural language processing (NLP) tasks."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus evidence shows related papers but lacks direct experimental support for this specific mechanism.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Freezing the language-aware embedding layer during supervised training preserves the cross-lingual knowledge learned during pretraining
- Mechanism: By keeping the language-aware embedding parameters fixed during TTS model training, the model maintains the multilingual representations that facilitate zero-shot synthesis for unseen languages.
- Core assumption: The frozen embeddings contain sufficient cross-lingual information that updating them would destroy their generalization capability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Then we train this model with a paired data in a supervised manner, while freezing a language-aware embedding layer."
  - [section] "In contrast to the scheme represented in Eq. (8), the scheme in Eq. (9) preserves the parameters of the language-aware embedding layer to facilitate cross-lingual transfer."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using bytes as input tokens instead of phonemes eliminates the need for language-specific G2P conversion modules and improves cross-lingual generalization
- Mechanism: Bytes provide a language-agnostic input representation that can be shared across all languages, avoiding the complexity of language-specific G2P rules and enabling better cross-lingual transfer.
- Core assumption: Byte-level representations capture enough linguistic information to enable effective speech synthesis without explicit phoneme-level processing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "It also improves TTS for seen languages, with byte-based models without grapheme-to-phone (G2P) modules outperforming the phone-based baselines."
  - [section] "We observed that our method improved the byte-based models and they outperformed the phone-based baseline models for all the metrics and languages."
  - [corpus] Weak - while related papers discuss byte-based approaches, direct evidence for this specific claim is limited in the corpus.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Language-aware embedding layer (token embedding + language embedding + bottleneck) -> Encoder (Transformer-based) -> Decoder (Transformer-based)

- Critical path:
  1. Pretrain language-aware embedding layer and encoder on multilingual text-only data using MLM objective
  2. Initialize TTS model with pretrained parameters
  3. Train encoder and decoder on paired data while freezing language-aware embedding layer
  4. Perform zero-shot synthesis for unseen languages using learned representations

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Bytes vs phonemes: Bytes eliminate G2P modules but may capture less phonological detail
  - Freezing vs updating embeddings: Freezing preserves cross-lingual knowledge but may limit adaptation
  - Spoken vs written text for pretraining: Spoken text may be more beneficial but written text increases coverage

- Failure signatures:
  - High CER values indicate poor intelligibility
  - High MCD values indicate poor speech quality
  - Poor naturalness scores from MOS evaluations

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare CER and MCD for seen languages with and without pretraining
  2. Evaluate zero-shot TTS performance for an unseen language using different pretraining data types
  3. Ablation study on the bottleneck layer and language ID usage to identify critical components

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the effectiveness of unsupervised text pretraining for zero-shot TTS vary with the similarity between the target unseen language and the languages present in the paired training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly discusses this in section 3.5, stating "We observed that the effectiveness of unsupervised text pretraining on zero-shot TTS is language dependent, as observed in previous work on zero-shot cross-lingual transfer for NLP tasks [Wu and Dredze, 2019]."
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides some initial observations, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how language similarity affects zero-shot TTS performance. The study only examined two unseen languages (German and Hungarian) and did not explore a wider range of language families or language similarity measures.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study examining zero-shot TTS performance across a diverse set of languages with varying degrees of similarity to the languages in the paired training data. This could involve using language family trees, linguistic similarity metrics, or other measures to quantify language similarity and correlate it with TTS performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of using different types of text data (spoken vs. written) for unsupervised pretraining on the performance of zero-shot TTS?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly investigates this in the appendix, stating "We investigated the effect of different types of text data used in pretrainingDtext on the performance of our method."
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides some initial results comparing spoken, written, and a combination of both, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the impact of different text data types. The study only used one written text dataset (ParaCrawl) and did not explore other types of written text data or investigate the optimal balance between spoken and written text for pretraining.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study examining the impact of different types of text data (e.g., spoken, written, social media text, formal text) on zero-shot TTS performance. This could involve using a variety of text datasets and systematically evaluating the impact of different text data types on TTS quality and intelligibility.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of input representation (bytes vs. phones) affect the performance of zero-shot TTS models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly compares the performance of byte-based and phone-based models in several sections, including the introduction, experimental results, and ablation studies.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides some initial comparisons, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the trade-offs between byte-based and phone-based models. The study does not explore the impact of different input representations on other aspects of TTS performance, such as naturalness, speaker similarity, or prosody.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study comparing the performance of byte-based and phone-based models across a range of languages and TTS tasks. This could involve evaluating the impact of different input representations on various aspects of TTS performance, such as intelligibility, naturalness, speaker similarity, and prosody.

## Limitations

- The framework's performance on typologically distant languages remains untested, raising questions about generalizability beyond European language families
- The byte-level input representation may limit the model's ability to capture fine-grained phonological distinctions compared to phoneme-based approaches
- Freezing the language-aware embedding layer prevents adaptation to language-specific nuances that may be crucial for high-quality synthesis

## Confidence

**Confidence: Low** - The paper presents promising results but evaluation is primarily on European languages with similar phonological structures.

**Confidence: Medium** - While the framework shows improved performance over baselines for seen languages, absolute metrics suggest room for improvement.

**Confidence: Low** - The paper does not adequately address potential data leakage issues between pretraining and evaluation languages.
