# Minimax-Bayes Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.10831
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10831
- **Reference count**: 24
- **Key outcome**: Minimax-Bayes policies are more robust than those assuming standard priors, with algorithms provided for finding approximate solutions.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces minimax-Bayes reinforcement learning, a framework for robust policy learning under prior uncertainty. By treating the prior distribution as an adversarial player, the authors develop algorithms to find policies that minimize worst-case Bayesian regret. The approach addresses the challenge of selecting appropriate priors in Bayesian RL by optimizing for robustness across a set of possible priors. Experiments demonstrate that minimax policies outperform standard Bayes-adaptive policies when evaluated against a range of test priors.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a minimax framework where the agent and "nature" play a zero-sum game, with nature choosing the prior and the agent choosing the policy to minimize worst-case regret. For finite MDPs, they use a cutting-plane algorithm to find exact minimax solutions, while for infinite MDPs they employ stochastic gradient descent-ascent with parametric policies. The Bayesian regret is computed as the difference between the expected utility under the chosen policy and the Bayes-optimal policy for each prior. Policy gradients are used to update both the agent's policy and nature's prior distribution during optimization.

## Key Results
- Minimax-Bayes policies are more robust than policies assuming uniform priors across a range of test priors
- Bayesian regret is convex in the prior, enabling efficient optimization using cutting-plane methods
- For finite MDPs, the minimax game has a value, ensuring existence of saddle point solutions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Worst-case priors yield policies that are more robust than policies assuming standard (uniform) priors.
- Mechanism: By minimizing the maximum Bayesian regret, the minimax-Bayes policy is optimized against the worst possible prior, leading to better performance across a range of plausible priors.
- Core assumption: The prior distribution can be treated as an adversarial player in a zero-sum game against the agent.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the corresponding minimax policies are more robust than those that assume a standard (i.e. uniform) prior."
  - [section 3] "minimax-Bayes policies are much more robust than Bayes-adaptive policies under common priors."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus signal for minimax-Bayes robustness; neighboring papers focus on minimax in general but not Bayesian-specific robustness.
- Break condition: If the set of priors B does not include the true prior, or if the worst-case prior is too pathological (e.g., assigning zero probability to the true MDP), robustness gains may not materialize.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Bayesian regret is convex in the prior, enabling efficient optimization of worst-case priors.
- Mechanism: The convexity of R(π, β) with respect to β allows the use of convex optimization techniques like cutting planes to find the worst-case prior efficiently.
- Core assumption: The policy space Π is such that U(π, β) is well-behaved (e.g., finite MDPs, parametric policies).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3] "R(π, β) is convex in β."
  - [section 5.2] "The cutting plane algorithm ... can be seen as a high dimensional extension of the bisection algorithm."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence for convex optimization of Bayesian regret in RL; neighboring papers focus on minimax but not convexity properties.
- Break condition: If the policy space is non-convex or the regret landscape is highly non-convex, the convexity property may fail, breaking the cutting plane method.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: For finite MDPs, the game between agent and nature has a value, enabling computation of minimax-Bayes solutions.
- Mechanism: In the finite setting, the standard minimax theorem applies, ensuring existence of a saddle point (π*, β*) where neither player can improve unilaterally.
- Core assumption: The policy space is finite or the utility is bilinear in policy and prior.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4] "For a finite set of MDPs in a finite state-action space ... the utility and Bayesian regret satisfy: min β∈B maxπ∈Π U(π, β) = maxπ∈Π minβ∈B U(π, β)"
  - [section 4] "the game R(π, β) does not have a value when M contains at least two MDPs whose optimal policy sets have an empty intersection."
  - [corpus] Weak corpus signal for minimax existence in Bayesian RL; neighboring papers discuss minimax but not the specific Bayesian regret setting.
- Break condition: If the MDP space is infinite or the policy space is too rich, the value may not exist, breaking the theoretical guarantees.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Policy space Π -> Prior space B -> Regret calculation (R(π, β), L(π, β)) -> Optimization algorithms (GDA, cutting plane) -> Evaluation (robustness testing)

- Critical path:
  1. Define policy and prior spaces.
  2. Implement regret calculation (Bayes-optimal and Bayesian).
  3. Choose and implement optimization algorithm (GDA or cutting plane).
  4. Run optimization to find (π*, β*).
  5. Evaluate robustness of π* against a range of test priors.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Finite vs. infinite MDP space: Finite allows exact solutions via cutting planes; infinite requires approximate methods (GDA).
  - Parametric vs. non-parametric policies: Parametric enables gradient-based optimization; non-parametric may be more expressive but harder to optimize.
  - Convex vs. non-convex regret: Convexity enables efficient optimization; non-convexity may require heuristics or stochastic methods.

- Failure signatures:
  - Non-convergence of optimization: May indicate non-convex regret landscape or poor choice of policy parametrization.
  - Poor robustness: May indicate the worst-case prior is too pathological or the policy space is insufficiently expressive.
  - High variance in policy performance: May indicate instability in gradient estimates or insufficient sampling.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Two-armed Bernoulli bandit with Beta priors: Verify worst-case prior is symmetric with low concentration parameters.
  2. Small finite MDP set (e.g., 3 MDPs): Use cutting plane to find minimax policy and verify robustness against interpolated priors.
  3. Infinite MDPs with Dirichlet priors: Use GDA to find approximate minimax policy and evaluate robustness against uniform and chain MDP priors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Does the minimax-Bayes problem have a value for the Bayes-optimal regret when the policy space is restricted to deterministic policies?
- Can the convergence guarantees for the stochastic gradient descent-ascent algorithm be extended to non-convex non-concave Bayesian regret landscapes, such as those arising from Dirichlet priors and parametric policies?
- How do minimax-Bayes policies compare in terms of robustness and performance to other robust reinforcement learning approaches, such as those based on Bayesian ambiguity sets or worst-case MDPs?

## Limitations

- Theoretical guarantees only established for finite MDP sets; infinite spaces rely on approximate methods
- Empirical validation limited to synthetic bandit and small MDP experiments, no real-world evaluations
- No comparison to other robust RL methods to contextualize minimax-Bayes performance

## Confidence

- **Medium**: Core mechanism (worst-case priors improve robustness) - empirical results show improvement but limited to synthetic domains
- **Low**: Convexity/differentiability assumptions - not empirically verified for specific policy spaces, potential non-convexities not addressed
- **Medium**: Scalability to large MDPs - cutting plane method may not scale, GDA convergence not guaranteed for non-convex cases
