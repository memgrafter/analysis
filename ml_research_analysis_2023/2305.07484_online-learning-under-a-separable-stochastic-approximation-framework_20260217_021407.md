# Online Learning Under A Separable Stochastic Approximation Framework

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07484
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07484
- Reference count: 40
- This paper presents an online learning algorithm for separable stochastic optimization problems, focusing on machine learning models where some parameters have a linear nature.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel online learning algorithm designed for separable stochastic optimization problems, particularly in machine learning models with both linear and nonlinear parameters. The algorithm leverages a separable structure to update linear parameters using recursive least squares (RLS), equivalent to a stochastic Newton method, and nonlinear parameters using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). This approach can be viewed as a stochastic approximation version of block coordinate descent. The method demonstrates global convergence for non-convex cases in terms of expected violation of a first-order optimality condition and shows significant acceleration in convergence, robustness in training and test performance, and reduced sensitivity to learning rates compared to popular algorithms like SGD, Adam, and slimTrain.

## Method Summary
The proposed algorithm updates linear parameters (α) using recursive least squares (RLS), which is equivalent to a stochastic Newton method, and nonlinear parameters (θ) using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). This approach exploits the separable structure of the loss function, where F(α,θ,ξ) is convex in α for fixed θ. The RLS update provides faster convergence for convex subproblems, while the SGD update handles the nonlinear parameters based on the current linear parameters. The algorithm achieves global convergence for non-convex problems by controlling the expected violation of a first-order optimality condition, using diminishing step sizes and leveraging Lipschitz continuity of partial gradients and boundedness of iterates.

## Key Results
- Global convergence for non-convex cases is established in terms of expected violation of a first-order optimality condition.
- Experiments on regression and classification tasks demonstrate significant acceleration in convergence compared to popular algorithms like SGD, Adam, and slimTrain.
- The proposed method produces more robust training and test performance and is less sensitive to the learning rate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Separable stochastic approximation exploits structure in separable loss functions by updating linear parameters with second-order RLS and nonlinear parameters with first-order SGD.
- Mechanism: RLS provides a stochastic Newton update for the linear parameters (α), converging faster for convex subproblems, while SGD updates the nonlinear parameters (θ) based on the current linear parameters. This mimics block coordinate descent but in the stochastic setting.
- Core assumption: The loss function is separable: F(α,θ,ξ) is convex in α for fixed θ, and the model has linear parameters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "In one routine of the proposed algorithm, the linear parameters are updated by the recursive least squares (RLS) algorithm, which is equivalent to a stochastic Newton method; then, based on the updated linear parameters, the nonlinear parameters are updated by the stochastic gradient method (SGD)."
  - [section II] "For stochastic convex optimization problems [34], [35], it is usually relatively easier to design efficient stochastic approximation algorithms [36]–[40]. Thus, the separation in the Separable Stochastic Approximation Framework allows us to leverage the advances made for stochastic convex optimization."
  - [corpus] Weak match to stochastic optimization but no direct evidence for second-order acceleration claim.
- Break condition: If the problem is not truly separable (F not convex in α), the RLS step no longer provides faster convergence and the method loses advantage over standard SGD.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Global convergence for non-convex problems is achieved by controlling the expected violation of a first-order optimality condition.
- Mechanism: The algorithm maintains bounded gradients in expectation by combining the descent of RLS and SGD updates, and using diminishing step sizes that ensure progress while controlling noise. The proof leverages Lipschitz continuity of partial gradients and boundedness of iterates.
- Core assumption: Assumptions 1-4 in Section III hold: lower boundedness of f, Lipschitz continuous partial gradients, bounded iterates, bounded noise, and bounded expected bias in gradient estimates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Global convergence of the proposed online algorithm for non-convex cases is established in terms of the expected violation of a first-order optimality condition."
  - [section III-A] Theorem 1 states that under Assumptions 1-4, the expected norm of the gradient converges to zero.
  - [corpus] No direct match; weak external support.
- Break condition: If the step sizes do not satisfy the diminishing conditions or noise is too large relative to step sizes, convergence to stationary points fails.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: RLS with exponential forgetting of batch size accelerates initial convergence in mini-batch learning.
- Mechanism: During mini-batch training, RLS is applied only to a fraction of the batch that decays exponentially with epoch number, reducing computational load and avoiding overfitting to early data while still providing fast initial progress.
- Core assumption: The batch size decay schedule (0.5^(i-1) * batch size) is effective and RLS remains stable with fewer samples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section IV-D] "During the i-th epoch training process, for each iteration, only ⌈0.5(i-1) * (batch size)⌉ randomly selected samples are used for RLS."
  - [corpus] No direct match; novel experimental detail.
- Break condition: If batch size becomes too small too fast, RLS estimates become unstable and convergence slows or diverges.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Stochastic approximation and Robbins-Monro conditions.
  - Why needed here: The algorithm relies on stochastic updates with diminishing step sizes to ensure convergence; understanding the conditions for convergence (e.g., sum of step sizes diverges, sum of squares converges) is critical.
  - Quick check question: In Robbins-Monro conditions, if γ_k = 1/k, does ∑ γ_k diverge and ∑ γ_k^2 converge?

- Concept: Block coordinate descent and Gauss-Seidel updates.
  - Why needed here: The algorithm updates α and θ alternately in a Gauss-Seidel fashion; understanding how block updates differ from simultaneous updates helps explain convergence properties.
  - Quick check question: In block coordinate descent, if you update variables in a Gauss-Seidel manner, do you use the most recent updates immediately or wait until the next iteration?

- Concept: Variable projection and separable nonlinear least squares.
  - Why needed here: The separable structure allows elimination or efficient updating of linear parameters; knowing the variable projection principle explains why RLS can be applied to α.
  - Quick check question: In separable nonlinear least squares, if the linear parameters α appear linearly in the model, can they be solved analytically for any fixed θ?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data pipeline -> Feature extractor -> Loss evaluator -> RLS updater -> SGD updater -> Model output

- Critical path:
  1. Receive sample (x_k, y_k).
  2. Compute h(x_k; θ_{k-1}).
  3. RLS update: compute g_k, update B_k and α_k.
  4. SGD update: compute ∇θF, update θ_k.
  5. Output predictions using η(x; α_k, θ_k).

- Design tradeoffs:
  - RLS vs SGD for α: RLS is O(p^2) per update but faster convergence; SGD is O(p) but slower.
  - Mini-batch size: Larger batches improve RLS stability but increase computation; smaller batches save time but risk instability.
  - Forgetting schedule: Aggressive decay speeds training but may destabilize RLS; conservative decay ensures stability but is slower.

- Failure signatures:
  - Diverging loss: Check if step sizes are too large or RLS update is numerically unstable (B_k ill-conditioned).
  - Oscillating loss: Likely RLS with too small batch or high noise; try larger batch or slower forgetting.
  - Plateau: Possibly step sizes too small or learning rate mismatch; adjust β_k or increase γ_k.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Single-sample online learning on Energy dataset with lr=1e-3: verify fast initial convergence and robustness to lr.
  2. Mini-batch learning on Diabetes dataset with batch=32: test RLS with batch decay and compare to Adam.
  3. Stress test: set very large batch size and observe RLS stability vs computation time.

## Limitations
- The convergence analysis relies on idealized assumptions (bounded gradients, diminishing step sizes) that may not hold in practice for deep nonlinear models.
- Experimental validation is limited to relatively small datasets and simple models, lacking large-scale or deep learning scenarios.
- The computational overhead of RLS (O(p²) updates) could become prohibitive for high-dimensional linear parameter spaces.
- The proposed mini-batch RLS variant with exponential forgetting is primarily empirical and lacks theoretical justification for the decay schedule.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The separable structure and basic algorithm design (RLS + SGD updates) are sound and well-explained. The experimental setup and implementation details are clearly described.
- **Medium confidence**: The global convergence proof for non-convex cases follows established stochastic approximation theory, but its applicability to deep learning scenarios needs further validation.
- **Low confidence**: The claim about RLS with exponential forgetting providing stable and efficient mini-batch learning is primarily empirical and lacks theoretical backing for the decay schedule.
