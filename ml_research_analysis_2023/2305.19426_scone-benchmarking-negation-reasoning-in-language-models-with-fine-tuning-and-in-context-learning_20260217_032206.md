# ScoNe: Benchmarking Negation Reasoning in Language Models With Fine-Tuning and In-Context Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19426
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19426
- Reference count: 31
- Key outcome: ScoNe benchmark reveals language models can learn negation scoping through fine-tuning but struggle with NLI-formatted prompts in few-shot settings

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ScoNe, a benchmark for evaluating negation reasoning in language models through both fine-tuning and in-context learning. The authors construct ScoNe-NLI by extending the MoNLI dataset with contrast sets that vary the presence and scoping of negations. They find that RoBERTa and DeBERTa models achieve near-perfect performance after training, while InstructGPT models struggle with NLI prompts but perform well on natural language examples. The results suggest that language models can learn negation reasoning but may be sensitive to prompt format and task framing.

## Method Summary
The authors create ScoNe-NLI by adding contrast sets to the MoNLI dataset, where each example varies in the number of negations (zero, one, or two) and whether those negations semantically scope. They conduct fine-tuning experiments with RoBERTa and DeBERTa models using MoNLI plus ScoNe-NLI training data, achieving near-perfect performance. For in-context learning, they test InstructGPT models on ScoNe-NLI and find poor performance on cases with exactly one scoping negation. To investigate further, they create ScoNe-NLG, a sentence completion dataset with narrative examples, where InstructGPT performs well. This contrast suggests that model performance depends on how negation reasoning is prompted rather than fundamental reasoning ability.

## Key Results
- RoBERTa and DeBERTa achieve near-perfect accuracy on ScoNe-NLI after fine-tuning on MoNLI plus ScoNe-NLI
- InstructGPT fails on ScoNe-NLI cases with exactly one scoping negation but succeeds on zero-negation cases
- InstructGPT performs well on ScoNe-NLG natural language examples, indicating prompt format affects performance
- The difference between NLI and natural language performance suggests models can reason about negation when examples align with pretraining objectives

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper demonstrates that language models can learn negation scoping through exposure to appropriately structured training data. The mechanism appears to rely on pattern recognition from pretraining rather than explicit logical reasoning. Models successfully learn to handle negation when examples match their pretraining distribution and task format, but struggle when forced to apply these skills to adapted NLI prompts. This suggests that what appears as "reasoning" may actually be sophisticated pattern matching that works best when input format aligns with learned expectations.

### Critical Path
The critical path is: Dataset construction (MoNLI + contrast sets) → Model training/evaluation → Performance analysis. The construction of contrast sets that systematically vary negation presence and scoping is essential, as it enables controlled testing of model behavior. Without these carefully constructed examples, it would be impossible to determine whether failures stem from negation handling or other factors.

### Design Tradeoffs
The paper trades comprehensive linguistic coverage for controlled experimental conditions. By focusing on binary negation scoping (present/absent) rather than complex multi-negation scenarios, the authors gain precise control over variables but may miss more challenging real-world cases. The choice to create both NLI and natural language versions allows comparison of prompt sensitivity but requires maintaining consistency across formats.

### Failure Signatures
- RoBERTa/DeBERTa: Overfitting to training distribution rather than genuine reasoning ability
- InstructGPT: Failure specifically on NLI-formatted prompts with one scoping negation
- Both: Potential degradation with increased sentence complexity or multiple negation layers
- All: Performance may not generalize to out-of-distribution negation patterns

## Limitations
- Fine-tuning experiments may reflect overfitting rather than genuine reasoning ability
- Evaluation focuses on English negation and may not generalize to other languages
- Contrast sets are relatively small (200 examples per condition), limiting statistical power
- Does not explore performance with multiple layers of negation or increased sentence complexity
