# Customizing General-Purpose Foundation Models for Medical Report Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.05642
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05642
- Authors: Multiple authors
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: 4th and 2nd place in ImageCLEFmedical Caption 2023 competition with BERTScore 0.615 and ROUGE-1 0.253

## Executive Summary
This paper explores adapting general-purpose foundation models for medical report generation through an encoder-decoder architecture. The authors propose connecting a vision Transformer (EVA-ViT-g) with a large language model (ChatGLM-6B) using a lightweight query Transformer (Q-Former). Through ablative experiments, they demonstrate that unfreezing the vision encoder for medical image representation learning and parameter-efficiently fine-tuning the language model with P-tuning are crucial for effective transfer learning. Their approach achieved strong results in the ImageCLEFmedical Caption 2023 competition, though challenges like catastrophic forgetting, hallucination, and code-switching remain.

## Method Summary
The authors propose an encoder-decoder architecture that adapts foundation models for medical report generation. A vision encoder (EVA-ViT-g) processes medical images, which are then aggregated by a Q-Former with 32 learnable visual tokens. These visual features are passed to ChatGLM-6B, which generates medical reports using P-tuning for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. The model is trained on the ImageCLEFmedical Caption 2023 dataset for 10 epochs with AdamW optimizer, using beam search decoding. Ablative experiments systematically evaluate the impact of different design choices on performance.

## Key Results
- Achieved 4th and 2nd place in ImageCLEFmedical Caption 2023 competition
- BERTScore of 0.615 and ROUGE-1 of 0.253 on test set
- Unfreezing EVA-ViT-g significantly improved performance by adapting visual representations to medical images
- P-tuning enabled ChatGLM-6B to capture medical report writing styles with 0.9% absolute improvements in ROUGE-1
- Model demonstrates competitive performance but exhibits issues with hallucination and code-switching

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Medical image → EVA-ViT-g → Q-Former → ChatGLM-6B → Medical report
- Critical path: Vision encoder extracts features, Q-Former aggregates them, language model generates report with P-tuning adaptation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Unfreezing vs freezing vision encoder: Balance between domain adaptation and catastrophic forgetting
  - Parameter-efficient vs full fine-tuning: Computational efficiency vs adaptation capability
  - Q-Former token count: Aggregation quality vs computational cost
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor medical report quality: May indicate insufficient medical domain adaptation
  - Hallucinations: Could suggest over-reliance on language model priors
  - Code-switching: May indicate inadequate language control in generation process
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare frozen vs unfrozen vision encoder on validation set to quantify domain adaptation benefit
  2. Test different Q-Former token counts (K=16, 32, 64) to find optimal aggregation
  3. Compare P-tuning vs full fine-tuning for language model adaptation in terms of performance and efficiency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can catastrophic forgetting of question-answering abilities in foundation models be mitigated when fine-tuning for medical report generation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the problem of catastrophic forgetting of ChatGLM-6B's QA ability when fine-tuned for medical report generation
- Why unresolved: Authors speculate about lack of visual QA data during training and fixed prompt text but don't provide definitive solution
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results demonstrating effective methods to preserve QA ability during fine-tuning, such as incorporating visual QA data or dynamic prompt engineering

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the most effective techniques to reduce hallucination in medical report generation using foundation models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies hallucination as a common problem where unintended text is generated
- Why unresolved: While authors mention reinforcement learning techniques have been effective in other NLG tasks, they don't explore these methods for medical report generation
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of different hallucination reduction techniques (reinforcement learning, contrastive learning, improved decoding) applied to medical report generation

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can code-switching in multilingual medical report generation be effectively prevented while maintaining translation quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors observe unexpected generation of Chinese tokens in their model fine-tuned on English-only data
- Why unresolved: Paper suggests potential solutions like explicit language signals or monolingual vocabularies but doesn't implement or evaluate these approaches
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing different strategies to prevent code-switching (language-specific fine-tuning, vocabulary modifications, post-processing) in multilingual medical report generation

## Limitations

- Limited empirical validation of design choices with only a few configurations tested
- Performance metrics (BERTScore, ROUGE) may not capture clinical utility or accuracy
- Competition results may not reflect true model capability without access to winning solutions' details
- Generalizability concerns as model is evaluated on only one dataset

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Core observation that foundation models can be adapted for medical report generation with competitive performance (supported by competition results and ablation study)

**Medium Confidence**: Specific mechanisms (unfreezing vision encoder, P-tuning, Q-Former) are necessary for optimal performance (supported by ablation study but limited exploration of alternatives)

**Low Confidence**: Claims about clinical utility and generalizability of the approach (lacks clinical expert evaluation and cross-dataset testing)

## Next Checks
