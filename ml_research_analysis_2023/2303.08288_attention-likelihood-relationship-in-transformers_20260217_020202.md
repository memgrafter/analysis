# Attention-likelihood relationship in transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08288
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08288
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Strong correlation between token likelihood and attention values in transformer models

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how transformer-based language models represent out-of-context words by analyzing the relationship between token likelihood and attention values. Through experiments using likelihood-guided text perturbations on BERT, RoBERTa, XLM-R, and DistilBERT, the authors find that unexpected (low-likelihood) tokens cause models to attend less to themselves, particularly at higher layers, and instead redirect attention to surrounding context uniformly. This finding provides insights into LLM robustness in real-world scenarios.

## Method Summary
The study used ~20k English sentences from WordNet, applying likelihood-guided text perturbations where one token per sentence was replaced with a low-likelihood alternative. The authors extracted attention matrices from frozen pre-trained transformer models and computed token attention (diagonal of attention matrix) and sentence attention (concatenated off-diagonal attention weights). Spearman correlation was used to measure the relationship between token likelihood and attention values, while Mann-Whitney U tests compared original and perturbed attention distributions.

## Key Results
- Unexpected tokens receive significantly lower attention values than expected tokens
- The attention previously directed to unlikely tokens is distributed uniformly to other sentence tokens
- Higher transformer layers show stronger correlation between likelihood and attention, encoding more abstract semantic features

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Out-of-context tokens receive lower attention values because the model redirects focus to surrounding context
- Mechanism: When encountering low-likelihood tokens, the transformer's attention mechanism shifts from the token itself to neighboring tokens to compute its representation
- Core assumption: Attention weights directly influence token representation computation in transformers
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "unexpected tokens cause the model to attend less to the information coming from themselves to compute their representations"; [section] "the token's attention decreases significantly when its likelihood is lowered, indicating that when the model encounters an unexpected token, it shifts its focus from the token to the context"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Higher layers show stronger correlation between likelihood and attention because they encode more abstract semantic features
- Mechanism: Lower layers rely more on the token itself for understanding, while higher layers use context more heavily for semantic interpretation
- Core assumption: Different transformer layers encode different levels of semantic abstraction
- Evidence anchors: [section] "we observe that the attention changes due to likelihood-guided perturbations are relevant across most model layers. However, the correlation values are stronger in the latest layers that encode more abstract semantic features"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Attention previously directed to unlikely tokens gets redistributed uniformly to other sentence tokens
- Mechanism: When a token becomes unlikely, its attention mass is distributed evenly across other tokens in the sentence
- Core assumption: Attention weights sum to 1 for each token, so reducing one token's attention must increase others'
- Evidence anchors: [section] "we observe that the attention previously directed to the token is then distributed uniformly to the rest of the sentence, as confirmed by the results of the Mann-Whitney U test"

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the correlation between token likelihood and attention generalize to masked language models beyond autoencoders?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on autoencoders (BERT, RoBERTa, XLM-R, DistilBERT) and mentions future research could explore implications in different domains.
- Why unresolved: The study specifically used autoencoder transformer models, so the findings may not extend to other model architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting the same likelihood-guided perturbation experiments on decoder-only models (e.g., GPT series) or other transformer variants and comparing the attention-likelihood correlation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the attention-likelihood relationship change when perturbing multiple tokens in a sentence simultaneously?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The current methodology perturbs only one token per sentence, but the paper doesn't explore multi-token perturbations.
- Why unresolved: The single-token perturbation approach controls for variables but doesn't reflect more complex real-world scenarios where multiple words might be out-of-context.
- What evidence would resolve it: Running experiments with varying numbers of perturbed tokens per sentence and measuring how the attention distributions change.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between the attention-likelihood correlation and specific downstream task performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion mentions that future research could "explore the implications of these findings on downstream tasks."
- Why unresolved: The paper establishes the correlation but doesn't test how this affects model performance on specific tasks like classification or question answering.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating model performance on downstream tasks with and without likelihood-guided attention adjustments, or analyzing attention patterns in high-performing vs. low-performing models on specific tasks.

## Limitations
- The study is limited to English text from WordNet, potentially missing cross-linguistic patterns
- The uniform redistribution hypothesis requires validation across different sentence structures and token positions
- The interpretation of layer-wise differences as evidence of abstract semantic encoding lacks direct validation

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The statistical correlation between token likelihood and attention values is well-established through the experimental results
- **Medium confidence**: The mechanism of attention redistribution and the uniform distribution hypothesis are supported by Mann-Whitney U test results but require additional validation
- **Low confidence**: The interpretation of layer-wise differences in attention-likelihood correlation as evidence of abstract semantic feature encoding lacks direct validation

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-sentence structure validation**: Test the uniform attention redistribution hypothesis across different sentence structures (simple, compound, complex) and token positions (beginning, middle, end) to determine if the observed pattern holds universally or is structure-dependent.

2. **Alternative perturbation strategy comparison**: Implement and compare results from different perturbation strategies (e.g., grammaticality-preserving replacements, synonym substitutions) to verify that the attention-likelihood relationship isn't specific to the likelihood-guided approach used in the original experiments.

3. **Downstream task correlation**: Measure the relationship between observed attention-likelihood patterns and actual model performance on relevant downstream tasks (e.g., masked language modeling, sentence completion) to validate whether attention changes correspond to meaningful changes in model behavior.
