# Fillers in Spoken Language Understanding: Computational and Psycholinguistic Perspectives

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.10761
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10761
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Comprehensive survey of fillers in SLU from computational and psycholinguistic perspectives

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive survey of research on fillers in spoken language understanding (SLU) from both computational and psycholinguistic perspectives. It covers production and comprehension aspects of fillers, including cognitive load, communicative function, informative cues, and time-buying measures. The paper also discusses computational approaches to disfluency detection and removal, filler detection, and broader SLU tasks such as personality computing and emotion recognition. Additionally, it briefly touches on generation aspects, including the use of fillers in text-to-speech synthesis. The survey aims to provide an approachable overview of the diverse perspectives and methodologies surrounding fillers in SLU, highlighting their importance in various aspects of speech communication and understanding.

## Method Summary
The paper synthesizes a breadth of perspectives in a holistic way, considering fillers at different stages in a pipeline from ASR to generation. It is structured into sections covering psycholinguistic perspectives (production and comprehension), computational perspectives (removal vs integration), and broader SLU and generation. The survey draws parallels between psycholinguistic and computational approaches, discussing research that finds fillers informative, considers fillers as noise, and considers the listener's perception. The paper discusses trends and challenges in each area without providing a detailed implementation plan or code for reproducing results.

## Key Results
- Fillers are the most frequent disfluency type and carry communicative and cognitive load information
- Listeners use fillers as predictive cues for upcoming information, adjusting comprehension accordingly
- Fillers serve different communicative functions depending on SLU task requirements (semantic extraction vs social/affective computing)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fillers are not merely noise but carry communicative and cognitive load information
- Mechanism: Speakers use fillers to signal cognitive processing difficulties or intentional communicative strategies, and listeners integrate these signals to adjust comprehension
- Core assumption: The frequency and context of fillers correlate with the speaker's cognitive state and communicative intent
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Fillers ('uh', 'um') are disfluencies that occur the most frequently compared to other kinds of disfluencies"
  - [section] "O'Shaughnessy (1995) found that speakers tend to maintain a fixed speaking rate during most utterances, but often adopt a faster or slower rate, depending on the cognitive load"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows related papers but no direct filler frequency or cognitive load analysis
- Break condition: If empirical evidence shows no correlation between filler frequency and cognitive load across diverse speaker populations

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Listeners use fillers as predictive cues for upcoming information
- Mechanism: Fillers provide prosodic and temporal markers that help listeners anticipate the complexity or novelty of upcoming speech content
- Core assumption: The acoustic properties of fillers are sufficiently distinct and consistent to serve as reliable predictive signals
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Arnold et al. (2007) additionally showed that listeners have expectations on the upcoming material to contain difficult to describe/unconventional referring expressions when preceded by the filler 'uh'"
  - [section] "Corley et al. (2007) studied the effect of filler ('um', called a 'hesitation' in the work) on the listener's comprehension using the N400 function of an Event-related potential (ERP)"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors focus on SLU systems but don't directly address filler-based predictive processing
- Break condition: If controlled experiments show no difference in comprehension accuracy with and without filler cues

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fillers serve different communicative functions depending on SLU task requirements
- Mechanism: In task-oriented SLU, fillers are noise to be removed for semantic frame extraction, while in broader SLU they provide social/affective context
- Core assumption: The communicative value of fillers is task-dependent and can be algorithmically distinguished
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We consider broader SLU as the analysis of the flow of speech by leveraging NLP and ML techniques...particularly for higher order tasks that are concerned with social/affective computing"
  - [section] "The Computational Paralinguistics Challenge focused on detecting fillers, which they considered to be a 'social signal'"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors don't explicitly address task-dependent filler processing
- Break condition: If a unified SLU system cannot achieve both semantic accuracy and social signal preservation

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Speech input → Acoustic feature extraction → Disfluency detection → Task-specific processing (semantic frame extraction OR social signal analysis) → Output
  Alternative path: Speech input → ASR → Text-based processing → Disfluency detection → Task-specific processing

- Critical path: For task-oriented SLU: ASR → disfluency removal → semantic parsing. For broader SLU: ASR → filler detection → feature extraction for personality/emotion analysis

- Design tradeoffs: Real-time processing requires streaming disfluency detection vs. batch processing allows more complex models; task-oriented systems prioritize semantic accuracy over social signal preservation

- Failure signatures: Poor semantic understanding when fillers are not properly removed; loss of social context when fillers are over-zealously removed; model degradation when processing disfluent vs. fluent speech

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different types of disfluencies (e.g., fillers vs. repetitions vs. restarts) affect listener comprehension and memory differently?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how fillers have been shown to facilitate recall and serve as informative cues, while also mentioning that repetitions may be filtered out by listeners. However, it doesn't directly compare the effects of different disfluency types.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides evidence for the effects of fillers on comprehension and memory, but doesn't systematically compare these effects to other types of disfluencies. It also mentions conflicting evidence on whether disfluencies are integrated or filtered out, suggesting a need for more nuanced research.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing the effects of different disfluency types on listener comprehension and memory, using consistent methodologies and stimuli.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between naturalness and safety when incorporating disfluencies in conversational AI systems, particularly in high-stakes scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the trade-off between naturalness and safety in conversational AI, using Google Duplex as an example of how disfluencies enhanced naturalness but also created an illusion of agency. It suggests that disfluencies could be used as mitigation strategies to exhibit uncertainty in safety-critical situations.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the trade-off, it doesn't provide concrete guidelines or empirical evidence for determining the optimal balance in different contexts. The effectiveness of using disfluencies as mitigation strategies also needs further investigation.
- What evidence would resolve it: User studies evaluating the impact of disfluencies on perceived naturalness and safety in conversational AI, particularly in high-stakes scenarios. Empirical research on the effectiveness of disfluencies as mitigation strategies for uncertainty.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can disfluency detection and processing be made more robust to raw, unsegmented transcripts from ASR systems in real-world scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the limitations of current disfluency detection systems, noting that they often rely on pre-segmented utterances and accurate transcription, which is unrealistic in real-world scenarios. It mentions ongoing research on online processing and detecting disfluencies with raw ASR transcripts.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the need for more robust disfluency detection in real-world scenarios, it doesn't provide concrete solutions or evaluate the effectiveness of proposed approaches. The challenges of handling raw, unsegmented transcripts and adapting to different domains and speaking styles remain significant.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies evaluating the performance of disfluency detection systems on raw, unsegmented transcripts from ASR systems in various real-world scenarios. Research on adapting disfluency detection models to different domains, speaking styles, and noise levels.

## Limitations
- The survey nature synthesizes existing research without providing novel experimental validation
- Claims rest on psycholinguistic findings from controlled laboratory settings that may not generalize to real-world SLU applications
- The paper does not address potential cultural variations in filler usage or their interpretation across different languages and speaker populations

## Confidence
- High confidence: The claim that fillers are the most frequent disfluency type is well-established across multiple corpora and research traditions
- Medium confidence: The claim about listeners using fillers as predictive cues for upcoming information relies on psycholinguistic ERP studies that show correlation but may not establish causation
- Low confidence: The paper's brief discussion of generation aspects, including filler insertion in text-to-speech systems, lacks sufficient depth or evidence to support strong claims about their effectiveness or necessity in synthetic speech
