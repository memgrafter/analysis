# Developing Speech Processing Pipelines for Police Accountability

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.06086
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06086
- Reference count: 0
- One-line primary result: Fine-tuning pre-trained speech models on police body-worn camera data improves ASR performance on officer speech (WER=12-13%)

## Executive Summary
This paper develops and evaluates automatic speech recognition (ASR) and officer speech detection pipelines for police body-worn camera (BWC) footage to enable large-scale review of millions of hours of recordings. The authors train and fine-tune pre-trained speech models (Wav2Vec2 and Whisper) on aligned, filtered police interaction data, then combine these with officer speech detection models to create a fully automated processing pipeline. Their results show that fine-tuning significantly improves ASR performance on officer speech compared to off-the-shelf models, though performance remains substantially worse for community member speech. The study also reveals that domain-specific tasks like diarization remain challenging even with strong ASR models.

## Method Summary
The authors develop their pipeline through several key steps: first, they align and filter training data from police body-worn camera footage using forced alignment tools (MFA or Wav2Vec2) and chunking strategies for short utterances; second, they fine-tune pre-trained ASR models (Wav2Vec2 Robust and Whisper in small, medium, and large variants) on the cleaned data with various strategies depending on resource constraints; third, they develop diarization models to identify officer speech, testing both in-domain CNN approaches and d-vector + classifier methods with data augmentation; finally, they combine the diarization and ASR components to evaluate the full pipeline on hand-aligned test sets, measuring word error rate (WER) by speaker role, race, and gender.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuning pre-trained speech models on police BWC data strongly improves ASR performance on officer speech (WER=12-13% vs 23-24% for off-the-shelf models)
- ASR performance on officer speech is much more accurate than on community member speech (WER=12-13% vs 43.55-49.07%)
- Officer speech detection and diarization remain challenging, with detected speech WER increasing to 31.52% compared to 12.80% for hand-aligned segments

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning pre-trained speech models on aligned, cleaned police body-worn camera data improves ASR performance on officer speech.
- Mechanism: Domain adaptation through fine-tuning adjusts model parameters to better match the acoustic and linguistic characteristics of police interactions, which differ from the clean, single-speaker domains where models like Wav2Vec2 and Whisper were originally trained.
- Core assumption: The fine-tuning dataset sufficiently represents the test domain in terms of acoustic noise, speech patterns, and speaker roles.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "fine-tuning strongly improves ASR performance on officer speech (WER=12-13%)"
  - [section] "fine-tuning improves ASR performance by 3-11 points"
- Break condition: If the training data alignment or filtering introduces significant errors, or if the domain shift is too large for effective fine-tuning.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: ASR performance is much better on officer speech than on community member speech in police body-worn camera footage.
- Mechanism: Officers are typically closer to the camera microphone (near-field) and speak more clearly and consistently, while community members are often further away (far-field) and speak less frequently, leading to higher WER for their speech.
- Core assumption: The acoustic distance and speaking patterns between officers and community members are consistent enough to create a measurable performance gap.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ASR on officer speech is much more accurate than on community member speech (WER=43.55-49.07%)"
  - [section] "Community members are situated further from the camera and typically speak very few short utterances"
- Break condition: If community members are often positioned close to the camera or if officers' speech is frequently obscured by background noise.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Officer speech detection (diarization) remains challenging even with strong ASR models.
- Mechanism: Identifying officer speech requires distinguishing between multiple speakers with varying acoustic profiles, and errors in diarization directly impact ASR accuracy by misaligning transcriptions to speakers.
- Core assumption: The diarization model can learn to differentiate officer speech from other speakers despite domain-specific challenges like overlapping speech and varying distances.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "domain-specific tasks like officer speech detection and diarization remain challenging"
  - [section] "There is a substantial performance decrease between the hand-aligned segments and the detected segments"
- Break condition: If the diarization model cannot reliably separate officer speech from other speakers, leading to cascading errors in downstream ASR tasks.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Forced alignment and data cleaning
  - Why needed here: Police body-worn camera data is noisy and often unaligned; forced alignment tools (like MFA or Wav2Vec2) and careful filtering are essential to create a usable training set.
  - Quick check question: What are the two main alignment methods compared, and how does chunking improve alignment for short utterances?

- Concept: Fine-tuning vs. off-the-shelf models
  - Why needed here: Pre-trained models are not optimized for the specific acoustic and linguistic characteristics of police interactions; fine-tuning adapts them to the domain.
  - Quick check question: Which fine-tuning strategy (encoder-only, decoder-only, or full) performed best for Whisper Large, and why?

- Concept: Diarization and speaker detection
  - Why needed here: Automatic processing requires separating officer speech from other speakers; this is a distinct challenge from ASR and can significantly impact performance.
  - Quick check question: What are the two diarization approaches tested, and which performed better after data augmentation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Video footage -> manual transcription -> forced alignment -> filtering -> training set -> fine-tuned ASR models -> diarization models -> combined pipeline

- Critical path:
  1. Align and filter training data to minimize noise
  2. Fine-tune ASR models on cleaned data
  3. Develop and tune diarization models
  4. Combine diarization with ASR for full pipeline evaluation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Alignment accuracy vs. processing speed (chunking helps short utterances but may introduce errors)
  - Model size vs. resource constraints (Whisper Large fine-tuning required decoder-only training due to GPU limits)
  - Privacy vs. performance (officer speech is easier to transcribe, but community speech is needed for full context)

- Failure signatures:
  - High WER on short or far-field utterances
  - Diarization errors mislabeling officer speech as non-officer or vice versa
  - Overfitting to training domain if fine-tuning data is too limited or unrepresentative

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare alignment methods (unaligned, MFA, Wav2Vec2) on a small subset and select the best based on min(WER).
  2. Fine-tune Wav2Vec2 Robust with and without language model on the filtered training set; evaluate on validation set.
  3. Train and evaluate both diarization models (in-domain, d-vector) with and without data augmentation on the validation set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do ASR models perform on body-worn camera footage from different police departments or time periods compared to the single department studied here?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "Our data consists of traffic stops from one police department. We cannot predict if results generalize to data from other departments, time periods or types of police-community interactions."
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to one department's traffic stop footage, and the authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the same ASR pipeline on body-worn camera footage from multiple police departments, different time periods, and various types of police-community interactions would provide evidence for generalizability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the minimum accuracy threshold for ASR in officer speech detection that would make it useful for police accountability applications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper finds that ASR on detected officer speech (WER=31.52%) is substantially worse than on hand-aligned segments (WER=12.80%), yet doesn't establish whether this accuracy is sufficient for practical applications.
- Why unresolved: The paper evaluates ASR performance but doesn't connect these metrics to specific use cases or determine what accuracy level is needed for various accountability tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting user studies with stakeholders to determine what WER levels are acceptable for different accountability applications (e.g., keyword search, procedural compliance checking) would establish minimum thresholds.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can explicitly modeling secondary officer speech and dispatch communications improve officer speech detection performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "We also identified several annotation errors, such as segments attributed to the wrong person and inconsistencies in which speech was transcribed, suggesting these metrics may under-estimate performance. These errors could be removed in hand-aligned test data, but their presence in training data is still likely to degrade model performance."
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies that secondary officers and dispatch communications complicate speech detection, it doesn't explore whether explicitly modeling these roles would improve performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training and evaluating officer speech detection models that explicitly distinguish between primary officers, secondary officers, and dispatch communications would show whether this approach improves accuracy.

## Limitations

- The study is limited to one police department's traffic stop footage, making generalization to other departments or interaction types uncertain
- Resource constraints limited exploration of larger models and more sophisticated fine-tuning strategies
- Diarization errors remain a significant bottleneck, with detected speech WER substantially worse than hand-aligned segments

## Confidence

- High: Fine-tuning improves officer speech ASR accuracy
- Medium: Officer speech is easier to transcribe than community member speech
- Medium: Diarization remains challenging and impacts overall pipeline performance

## Next Checks

1. Test diarization accuracy on a balanced dataset with equal numbers of officer and community member utterances at varying distances from the microphone to isolate the effect of acoustic distance from speaker role.

2. Evaluate the impact of different fine-tuning strategies (encoder-only, decoder-only, full) on Whisper Large when resource constraints are relaxed, to determine if the current performance ceiling is due to hardware limitations.

3. Conduct a systematic ablation study removing data augmentation from the diarization training pipeline to quantify its contribution to performance improvements and assess whether alternative augmentation strategies might be more effective.
