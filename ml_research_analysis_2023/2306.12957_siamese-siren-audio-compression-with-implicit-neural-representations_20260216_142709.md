# Siamese SIREN: Audio Compression with Implicit Neural Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.12957
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12957
- Reference count: 9
- Key outcome: Siamese SIREN reduces stationary background noise in quantized audio INRs by using twin heads with shared backbones, achieving better reconstruction fidelity than previous INR methods while using fewer parameters.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental challenge in audio compression using implicit neural representations (INRs): the amplification of stationary background noise during weight quantization. The authors introduce Siamese SIREN, which extends the popular SIREN architecture with twin heads that reconstruct the same signal with different noise patterns. By leveraging the statistical independence of noise between heads, the model estimates and removes background noise, producing cleaner audio reconstructions. Experiments on the LibriSpeech dataset demonstrate improvements across multiple audio quality metrics, particularly after quantization.

## Method Summary
Siamese SIREN employs a shared backbone of SIREN layers with sine activations, followed by two separate Siamese heads that each reconstruct the same audio signal but with different noise patterns due to distinct random initializations. The difference between the twin outputs provides an estimate of the background noise, which is then scaled and subtracted from the signal. The architecture also incorporates positional encoding to better capture high-frequency audio content, though this introduces additional noise that the Siamese mechanism can remove. The final output undergoes noise reduction processing to produce the compressed audio signal.

## Key Results
- Siamese SIREN achieves superior ViSQOL, CDPAM, PESQ, and STOI scores compared to previous INR architectures
- The model demonstrates particular improvements in audio quality after weight quantization
- Siamese SIREN utilizes fewer network parameters while maintaining better reconstruction fidelity

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Siamese SIREN reduces audible background noise by leveraging the statistical independence of noise across two trained heads.
- Mechanism: The shared backbone forces both heads to learn the same signal, but different random initializations produce independent noise patterns. Subtracting the two outputs yields an estimate of the noise, which can then be removed.
- Core assumption: The noise introduced during training is uncorrelated between the two heads.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "both extensions will contain noise, but different noise, their difference can be leveraged to remove the noise"
  - [section] "Due to different random weight initialization of the heads, the reconstructed signals will vary slightly."
- Break condition: If the noise patterns become correlated (e.g., due to shared batch normalization layers or insufficient divergence in initial weights), the subtraction will not isolate noise effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Weight quantization amplifies stationary noise, making it perceptually dominant in audio.
- Mechanism: Quantization reduces numerical precision, which distorts the fine-grained waveform details that humans are sensitive to via the Weber-Fechner law. Small reconstruction errors become large relative to the signal's amplitude.
- Core assumption: The reconstruction error after quantization is stationary and broadband, rather than sparse or localized.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "small reconstruction errors... become clearly perceivable in the form of stationary background noise"
  - [section] "This noise thus becomes more pronounced the further we reduce model size and quantize model weights."
- Break condition: If the quantization scheme is trained-aware or adaptive, it may suppress noise amplification, weakening the need for denoising.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Positional encoding (PE) improves high-frequency reconstruction but also introduces high-frequency noise, which Siamese SIREN can separate and remove.
- Mechanism: PE maps time inputs into a high-dimensional sinusoidal space, enabling the SIREN to model fine temporal variations. However, this same mapping can also encode noise into high-frequency bands, which the Siamese heads learn differently and can thus isolate.
- Core assumption: The noise in high-frequency bands is statistically independent between heads and can be modeled as additive.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Positional Encoding (PE) has been shown to help INRs learn high-frequency representations"
  - [section] "SIREN with positional encoding is capable of learning high-frequency content, however, it also produces substantial noise in these high-frequency bands."
- Break condition: If PE noise is highly structured (e.g., due to specific frequency aliasing), the noise estimate may not be well-captured by simple subtraction.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input (normalized time) -> Positional Encoding (33-dim) -> Shared SIREN layers -> Siamese heads (twin outputs) -> Noise estimation (difference scaled by α) -> Noise reduction -> Final output

- Critical path:
  1. Encode time → Positional Encoding
  2. Pass through shared SIREN layers
  3. Branch into Siamese heads → two reconstructions
  4. Compute noise estimate → subtract and scale
  5. Apply noise reduction → final output

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Sharing more layers reduces parameters but may limit noise diversity between heads
  - Larger α increases noise removal but risks over-denoising signal
  - Higher positional encoding frequencies improve detail but increase noise in high bands

- Failure signatures:
  - If twin outputs are too similar, noise estimate will be weak → residual noise
  - If α is too large, signal components may be removed → muffled audio
  - If positional encoding is too coarse, high-frequency details will be lost → poor audio fidelity

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a single SIREN with PE on a 10s audio clip, measure ViSQOL and inspect spectrogram for noise
  2. Train Siamese SIREN with α = 0 (no noise subtraction), compare to step 1
  3. Train Siamese SIREN with α tuned, apply noise reduction, compare metrics and subjective quality

## Limitations
- The paper's central claim about Siamese SIREN's superiority relies heavily on controlled experimental comparisons, but the analysis of noise correlation between heads lacks quantitative validation.
- The choice of the scaling factor α = 2.8 appears arbitrary and was not optimized through systematic ablation studies.
- The paper does not address computational overhead—the Siamese architecture doubles the parameter count during inference compared to a single-head SIREN, which may offset compression benefits.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The core observation that quantization amplifies stationary noise in INRs is well-supported by audio engineering principles and the Weber-Fechner law. The architectural design of using twin heads with shared backbones follows established Siamese network patterns.

- **Medium confidence**: The claim that noise can be effectively isolated through subtraction assumes statistical independence between heads, which is plausible but not rigorously verified. The effectiveness of positional encoding in both improving reconstruction and introducing noise is supported by literature but not deeply analyzed in this specific context.

- **Low confidence**: The assertion that Siamese SIREN achieves "superior" audio reconstruction across all metrics compared to previous INR architectures is overstated given the limited experimental scope (primarily LibriSpeech dataset) and lack of comparison with traditional compression methods.
