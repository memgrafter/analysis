# SugarCrepe: Fixing Hackable Benchmarks for Vision-Language Compositionality

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.14610
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14610
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Blind text-only models outperform vision-language models on existing compositionality benchmarks due to exploitable biases

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies severe biases in existing vision-language compositionality benchmarks, showing that simple text-only models can outperform state-of-the-art models by exploiting these biases. To address this, the authors introduce SUGAR CREPE, a new benchmark that uses large language models to generate more fluent and sensical hard negatives, and applies adversarial refinement to minimize dataset biases. They evaluate recent methods for improving compositionality and find that their improvements are overestimated on existing benchmarks. Comprehensive evaluation of 17 pretrained CLIP models reveals significant room for improvement in compositional understanding, especially for attributes and relations. The SUGAR CREPE benchmark provides a more faithful evaluation of vision-language compositionality.

## Method Summary
The paper addresses hackable compositionality benchmarks by introducing SUGAR CREPE, which generates hard negatives using ChatGPT instead of rule-based templates, applies human validation to filter false negatives, and uses adversarial refinement to balance plausibility and grammar scores between positive and negative captions. The benchmark is evaluated using 17 pretrained CLIP models and text-only baselines, revealing that existing benchmarks overestimate compositional understanding improvements. The method involves generating hard negatives, validating against false negatives, applying adversarial refinement, and evaluating on the final benchmark.

## Key Results
- Blind text-only models outperform vision-language models on existing compositionality benchmarks
- SUGAR CREPE significantly improves commonsense plausibility and fluency compared to rule-based hard negatives
- Evaluation of 17 CLIP models shows significant room for improvement in compositional understanding, especially for attributes and relations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Rule-based template hard negatives introduce exploitable distributional gaps between positive and negative captions, allowing blind text-only models to outperform vision-language models.
- **Mechanism**: Procedural generation via word replacement, shuffling, or negation creates hard negatives that are systematically less plausible and fluent than positive captions. These artifacts become learnable shortcuts for models.
- **Core assumption**: Distributional gap between positive and hard negative captions correlates with model performance on compositionality benchmarks.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "blind models with no access to the image outperform state-of-the-art vision-language models" and "significant biases in all these benchmarks rendering them hackable"
  - [section]: "We find that a blind model that never looks at the image, can identify the correct caption and avoid choosing the supposed 'hard negatives'"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers focus on improvements but don't directly measure bias exploitation
- **Break condition**: If hard negative generation produces captions with identical plausibility and fluency distributions to positive captions, the blind model advantage disappears.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Large language models can generate hard negatives that are more sensical and fluent than rule-based templates, reducing distributional gaps.
- **Mechanism**: ChatGPT generates hard negatives through few-shot prompting that maintains grammatical correctness and logical coherence while creating compositional differences from positive captions.
- **Core assumption**: Modern LLMs can produce high-quality compositional variations without introducing artifacts that blind models can exploit.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: "we leverage ChatGPT [28] to generate plausible and natural hard negative texts instead of relying on simple rule-based templates"
  - [section]: "SUGAR CREPE brings significant improvements in commonsense and fluency" (Table 3 comparison)
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers mention SugarCrepe but don't validate LLM-generated hard negatives
- **Break condition**: If LLM-generated hard negatives still contain subtle patterns that blind models can detect, the benchmark remains hackable.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Adversarial refinement removes residual biases by ensuring symmetric score distributions across positive and negative captions.
- **Mechanism**: The method iteratively filters examples to balance plausibility and grammar scores between positive and negative captions, making text-only models unable to distinguish them based on these features.
- **Core assumption**: Symmetric score distributions across both models prevent exploitation of remaining artifacts.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: "we subsample the dataset through an adversarial refinement process to ensure the identified biases are maximally removed"
  - [section]: "we ensure that performance improvements on SUGAR CREPE cannot be achieved by exploiting the identified nonsensical and non-fluent biases"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers cite SugarCrepe but don't detail adversarial refinement mechanics
- **Break condition**: If adversarial refinement fails to achieve true symmetry or if new artifacts emerge during generation, exploitation remains possible.

- **Concept**: Compositionality in vision-language models

  - **Quick check question**: Can you explain the difference between "a girl in white facing a man in black" and "a girl in black facing a man in white" in terms of compositional understanding?

- **Concept**: Distributional bias and dataset artifacts
  - **Why needed here**: The core problem identified is that hard negatives have systematically different distributions than positives, which is what makes benchmarks hackable
  - **Quick check question**: If you measure plausibility scores on positive vs. hard negative captions and find they're consistently different, what does this tell you about potential model shortcuts?

- **Concept**: Adversarial filtering and dataset debiasing
  - **Why needed here**: Understanding how adversarial refinement works is crucial for implementing SUGAR CREPE and knowing why it's effective
  - **Quick check question**: If you have two models scoring positive and negative captions, how would you ensure their score distributions are symmetric to prevent exploitation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: LLM hard negative generation -> Human validation -> Adversarial refinement -> Evaluation framework
- **Critical path**: Generate hard negatives → Validate against false negatives → Apply adversarial refinement → Evaluate on the final benchmark
- **Design tradeoffs**: Using LLMs increases generation quality but adds computational cost and potential generation inconsistencies; adversarial refinement improves fairness but reduces dataset size
- **Failure signatures**: If blind models still outperform vision models, distributional gaps remain; if human validation removes too many examples, the benchmark may lack coverage
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Generate hard negatives for 100 examples using ChatGPT and compare plausibility/fluency scores against rule-based templates
  2. Apply adversarial refinement to the generated dataset and visualize score gap distributions before/after
  3. Evaluate a pretrained CLIP model on the refined dataset and compare performance against the blind text-only baseline

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How would models perform on SUGAR CREPE if trained specifically to recognize and correct for the distributional biases present in existing benchmarks?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper identifies two major biases (nonsensical and non-fluent artifacts) in existing benchmarks and shows that models can exploit these to achieve high performance without true compositionality.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper introduces SUGAR CREPE to remove these biases but doesn't train models to specifically recognize and correct for them in existing benchmarks.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments training models on a balanced mix of existing benchmarks and SUGAR CREPE, then evaluating on both types, would show if models can learn to ignore the biases and perform well based on true compositionality.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the relationship between a model's compositionality performance on SUGAR CREPE and its performance on other compositional reasoning tasks not based on image-text retrieval?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper evaluates models on SUGAR CREPE and shows they struggle with compositionality, but doesn't compare to other compositional reasoning tasks.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper focuses solely on image-text retrieval compositionality benchmarks, leaving the relationship to other compositional tasks unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluating the same models on a diverse set of compositional reasoning tasks (e.g., visual question answering, scene graph prediction) and correlating their performance with SUGAR CREPE scores would reveal if compositionality is a general capability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How do generative vision-language models (e.g., DALL-E, Stable Diffusion) perform on SUGAR CREPE compared to discriminative models like CLIP?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper evaluates 17 CLIP models but notes that generative models are beyond the current scope.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper focuses on CLIP models and doesn't include generative models in the evaluation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluating state-of-the-art generative vision-language models on SUGAR CREPE and comparing their performance to CLIP models would show if generative models have better compositionality.

## Limitations

- The exact magnitude of blind model exploitation varies across different model architectures and datasets
- The adversarial refinement process may not eliminate all exploitable patterns, particularly subtle semantic correlations
- The reliance on ChatGPT for hard negative generation introduces potential variability in quality that depends on prompt engineering

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core finding that existing compositionality benchmarks contain exploitable biases, supported by direct empirical evidence showing blind text-only models outperforming vision-language models on established benchmarks.
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of SUGAR CREPE's LLM-based hard negative generation and adversarial refinement in creating a more challenging benchmark, based on improved commonsense and fluency metrics, though long-term generalization remains to be seen.
- **Low Confidence**: The claim that SUGAR CREPE represents a definitive solution to benchmark bias, as the paper acknowledges that new biases may emerge over time and the benchmark requires ongoing maintenance.

## Next Checks

1. **Distribution Analysis**: Compute and compare the full distributional statistics (not just mean scores) of plausibility and grammar scores between positive and negative captions before and after adversarial refinement to ensure complete symmetry.

2. **Cross-Model Validation**: Test the same SUGAR CREPE dataset across multiple blind text-only models and vision-language models to verify that distributional gaps are consistently eliminated across different model architectures.

3. **Temporal Stability**: Generate multiple SUGAR CREPE datasets over time using different ChatGPT versions or prompts to assess the stability and reproducibility of the hard negative generation process.
