# Formal Aspects of Language Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2311.04329
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04329
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Provides a rigorous measure-theoretic foundation for language models, defines tightness, unifies classical and neural models, and establishes expressiveness results (e.g., Heaviside RNNs = PFSA; saturated RNNs = Turing complete; soft-attention transformers always tight).

## Executive Summary
This paper formalizes language models as probability distributions over finite strings using measure theory, resolving paradoxes in informal definitions. It introduces *tightness*—the requirement that the total probability sums to 1—and characterizes it for locally normalized models (LNMs) via prefix probabilities and the conditional probability of the end-of-sequence (eos) token. The framework unifies classical models (n‑grams as weighted finite‑state automata, probabilistic context‑free grammars) and neural models (embedding‑based with a softmax projection). Key results show that Heaviside‑activated RNNs are equivalent to deterministic probabilistic finite‑state automata, arbitrary‑precision RNNs with saturated sigmoid are Turing complete, and transformer language models with soft attention are always tight. In contrast, transformers with unique hard attention cannot recognize simple regular languages but become Turing complete when using multiple heads and layer normalization together with state‑augmented alphabets. The work bridges formal language theory and deep learning, offering theoretical insights into the expressive power and limitations of modern architectures.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a probability space over infinite strings (Σ^ω) from a locally normalized sequence model using cylinder sets and a pre‑measure, extended via Carathéodory’s theorem. A random variable maps infinite strings to finite ones by stopping at the first eos token. Tightness (the LNM being a valid distribution over Σ*) is equivalent to the measure of infinite strings being zero, which is further characterized by the divergence of the sum of eos conditional probabilities. For representation‑based neural models (embeddings + context encoding + projection), tightness reduces to a bound on the norm of the context representation: the inner product of the scoring vector with the maximum embedding distance must grow at most logarithmically with sequence length. Expressiveness is analyzed by simulating automata: Heaviside RNNs realize deterministic probabilistic finite‑state automata; saturated RNNs can simulate two‑stack pushdown automata; soft‑attention transformers always yield tight models, while hard‑attention variants require multi‑head and layer norm to achieve Turing completeness via alphabet augmentation.

## Key Results
- Measure‑theoretic definition of language models and tightness condition unifies classical and neural approaches.
- Heaviside‑activated RNNs are equivalent to deterministic probabilistic finite‑state automata (space O(|Σ|√|Q|)); arbitrary‑precision RNNs with saturated sigmoid are Turing complete.
- Transformer LMs with soft attention are always tight; with unique hard attention they fail on simple regular languages (e.g., First), but become Turing complete with multi‑head attention, layer normalization, and state‑augmented alphabets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Measure‑theoretic foundation
The construction starts from a locally normalized sequence model (LNM) that defines conditional probabilities p(y_t | y_{<t}). Cylinder sets for infinite strings are defined by fixing finite prefixes; the pre‑measure assigns probability as the product of conditionals along the prefix. Carathéodory’s extension theorem yields a full measure P on the σ‑algebra. The random variable that returns the finite prefix ending at the first eos token pushes P forward to a distribution over Σ*. Tightness (i.e., P(Σ^ω)=0) is equivalent to the divergence of Σ_t p(eos|y_{<t}) over all prefixes (Theorem 2.5.3). This resolves paradoxes (e.g., Example 2.1.1) and provides necessary and sufficient conditions for an LNM to be a valid language model.

### Mechanism 2: Norm‑based tightness for representation‑based models
For models where p(y_t|y_{<t}) = softmax(E·enc(y_{<t}))_y with symbol embeddings ε(y) and context encoding enc(y_{<t}), tightness is guaranteed if the scoring vector satisfies s·max_y ||ε(y)−ε(eos)|| ≤ log t, where s is the inner product of the context representation with the embedding difference (Theorem 3.1.6). Bounded activations (sigmoid/tanh) keep the context norm bounded, so tightness holds. Unbounded activations (ReLU) can cause the norm to grow faster than log t, leading to non‑tight models that leak probability to infinite strings (Example 5.1.7).

### Mechanism 3: Architectural expressiveness via automata simulation
- Heaviside‑activated Elman RNNs can deterministically simulate any finite‑state automaton with a space complexity of O(|Σ|√|Q|), establishing equivalence to deterministic probabilistic finite‑state automata (Lemma 5.1.3).
- Arbitrary‑precision RNNs with saturated sigmoid activations can simulate a two‑stack pushdown automaton by encoding stack contents numerically in hidden units and performing arithmetic updates, thus are Turing complete (Theorem 5.1.10).
- Soft‑attention transformers always yield tight models because the eos probability is bounded away from zero due to the compactness of the attention context (Theorem 5.2.2). Transformers with unique hard attention cannot recognize regular languages like Parity or Dyck; however, with multiple heads, layer normalization, and an augmented input alphabet that encodes stack symbols, they can simulate a two‑stack PDA and become Turing complete (Sections 5.2.3, 5.3).

- **Formal language theory** (regular, CFL, PDA): Provides the hierarchy (finite‑state ≤ pushdown ≤ Turing) to classify model expressiveness. *Quick check*: Explain why L = {aⁿbⁿcⁿ | n∈ℕ} is not context‑free yet is recognized by a two‑stack PDA; how would a saturated‑sigmoid RNN simulate the two stacks?
- **Representation‑based sequence modeling** (softmax over embeddings): Unifies neural LMs; tightness reduces to bounding norms of context representations. *Quick check*: Given a transformer with multi‑head attention and ReLU activations, argue why it might violate the condition s·max_y ||ε(y)−ε(eos)|| ≤ log t and become non‑tight.

## Architecture Onboarding
Probability layer -> Local model layer -> Automata layer -> Neural layer -> Expressiveness layer

Critical path: Measure theory → LNM/Tightness → Automata equivalence (WFSA ⇄ global vs local; PCFG ⇄ WCFG) → Neural simulations (RNN→WFSA; RNN→Turing; Transformer→PDA via alphabet augmentation) → Tightness proofs (e.g., Theorem 5.2.2 via compactness).

Design tradeoffs:
- Finite‑state models are tractable but cannot capture cross‑serial dependencies; context‑free add stack memory but remain limited; Turing‑complete models can compute any decidable language but may over‑fit and are undecidable to analyze.
- Soft attention gives differentiability and guaranteed tightness; hard/unique attention can represent counting but breaks gradient flow.
- Bounded activations (sigmoid/tanh) ensure tightness; unbounded (ReLU) risk non‑tightness unless carefully controlled.
- Recurrence naturally carries state; attention offers parallelization but often requires multi‑head and layer norm for comparable expressivity.

Failure signatures:
- Non‑tightness: Σ_{y∈Σ*} p(y) < 1; p(eos|prefix) decays too fast (Example 2.5.1).
- Regularity limitation: Heaviside RNN fails on aⁿbⁿcⁿ; transformer with unique hard attention fails Parity/Dyck.
- Vanishing/exploding gradients: Jacobian norm ∏ ||∂h_t/∂h_{t−1}|| → 0 or ∞.
- Undecidability: Checking tightness for general RNNs is undecidable (Theorem 5.1.12).

First 3 experiments:
1. Build a deterministic PFSA from a given bigram table (Sec 4.1.5), compute allsum via (I−T)⁻¹, and verify total probability = 1.
2. Given a DFA with N states, construct an Elman network with Heaviside units of size ~|Σ|√N (Minsky); feed strings and compare acceptance to the DFA (Lemma 5.1.3).
3. For a soft‑attention transformer with bounded embeddings, show inf_{context} p(eos|context) > 0 using a compactness argument (Theorem 5.2.2); empirically verify eos probabilities remain bounded away from 0 on random strings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **When is an energy function normalizable?** That is, for which energy functions p_{GN} is Z_G < ∞? The paper states there are no general necessary or sufficient conditions; normalizability testing is undecidable in general, with complete characterizations only for specific formalisms (WFSA, WCFG).
2. **When can a language model be locally normalized?** While every LM can be locally normalized (Theorem 2.4.2), this uses prefix probabilities π(y) that may be intractable to compute. The unresolved question is when local normalization can be performed efficiently.
3. **When does an LNM encode a language model?** That is, when is it tight (Σ_y p_{LN}(y)=1)? Theoretical conditions exist (Theorem 2.5.3) but involve intractable prefix probabilities; practical methods to verify tightness for trained models are lacking.
4. **Do the theoretical properties of infinite‑precision LMs (e.g., RNN Turing completeness, Transformer tightness) hold for finite‑precision implementations?** All expressiveness and tightness results assume infinite precision; finite‑precision floats may cause rounding errors or overflow, potentially breaking the theoretical guarantees.
5. **Does multi‑head attention strictly increase the expressive power of Transformers beyond single‑head variants?** Multi‑head is standard and claimed to be more expressive, but no formal hierarchy is proven; it is unclear whether multiple heads enable recognizing new languages or merely improve optimization/generalization.

## Limitations
- The measure‑theoretic construction is theoretical and non‑algorithmic; practical implementation requires truncation or symbolic methods, introducing approximation errors.
- Neural model proofs assume idealized conditions (infinite precision, exact softmax/argmax) that diverge from finite‑precision implementations, potentially invalidating tightness bounds.
- Tightness conditions (e.g., norm growth ≤ log t) rely on specific assumptions (bounded activations, bounded embeddings); common architectures like ReLU may violate them, leading to non‑tight models.
- Expressiveness results (Turing completeness) are based on theoretical simulations of stack machines; empirical validation on trained models is absent and may not reflect realistic learning dynamics.
- Corpus analysis shows limited external corroboration; neighboring papers focus on empirical deep learning rather than formal measure‑theoretic foundations.

## Confidence
- **Measure‑theoretic foundation and tightness characterization:** High (rigorous mathematics, self‑contained proofs).
- **Unification of representation‑based models and norm‑based tightness:** Medium (depends on inner‑product scoring assumption; supported by known RNN bounds but not fully general).
- **Architectural expressiveness hierarchy (RNN/Transformer):** Low (theoretical simulations; empirical evidence mixed; transformer hard‑attention limitations reported).
