# A domain-decomposed VAE method for Bayesian inverse problems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05708
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05708
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: DD-VAE-MCMC significantly improves generative properties and computational efficiency compared to global VAE-based methods, with FID scores reduced from over 180 to below 5 for low-dimensional problems and from over 390 to below 90 for high-dimensional problems. Relative errors in mean estimates are reduced by about 5-10%.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a domain-decomposed variational auto-encoder Markov chain Monte Carlo (DD-VAE-MCMC) method for solving Bayesian inverse problems with expensive PDE forward models. The key innovation is partitioning the global domain into smaller subdomains, constructing local VAEs using local historical data, and using Gaussian process models with active learning to handle interface conditions. Local inversions are conducted independently in parallel in low-dimensional latent spaces, and results are post-processed using Poisson image blending to obtain an efficient global inference result.

## Method Summary
The method works by first partitioning the global physical domain into overlapping subdomains. Local VAEs are trained on historical data from each subdomain, providing efficient local prior representations in reduced-dimensional latent spaces. Gaussian process models with active learning construct interface functions between subdomains. Local inversions are performed independently using pCN-MCMC sampling in the latent space. Finally, Poisson image blending is applied to combine overlapping local fields into a coherent global solution, eliminating visible seams at subdomain interfaces.

## Key Results
- FID scores reduced from over 180 to below 5 for low-dimensional problems
- FID scores reduced from over 390 to below 90 for high-dimensional problems
- Relative errors in posterior mean estimates reduced by approximately 5-10%
- Local model evaluations cost roughly 1/4 of global model evaluations (4:1 ratio)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Domain decomposition reduces latent dimensionality per subdomain, making VAE training more efficient
- Mechanism: Partitioning the global domain D into M subdomains {Di} reduces the size of local fields xi from Nx to Nxi ≈ Nx/M, allowing VAEs to operate in lower-dimensional latent spaces
- Core assumption: The prior distribution on each subdomain follows the same distribution as the global prior (pxi(xi) = py(y))
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Through partitioning the global physical domain into small subdomains, the proposed method first constructs local deterministic generative models based on local historical data, which provide efficient local prior representations"
  - [section 3.2] "the dimension of the local latent variable is reduced compared to that of the global latent variable"
- Break condition: If the assumption of identical local distributions fails, the augmented training set Y would not be representative, degrading VAE performance

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Parallel subdomain inversions reduce overall computational cost
- Mechanism: Local forward models FGP_i(xi) can be solved independently and in parallel, with each evaluation costing roughly 1/4 of the global model evaluation
- Core assumption: Local forward model evaluations can be parallelized without communication bottlenecks
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.5] "The parallel capability endowed with the domain-decomposed strategy further reduces the computational expense"
  - [section 4.4] "the local models with two cases can be solved in 0.3299 seconds. The ratios of the global and local model evaluations are about 4.1009"
- Break condition: If parallel overhead or communication costs exceed computational savings, the parallelization benefit disappears

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Poisson image blending eliminates visible seams at subdomain interfaces
- Mechanism: The Poisson blending formulation minimizes gradient differences between overlapping regions, creating smooth transitions rather than direct concatenation
- Core assumption: The interface regions contain sufficient information to create seamless transitions
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.4] "the Poisson image blending technique is utilized to blend overlapping local fields to give effective representations of the global field"
  - [section 4.4] "The stitched field has clear seams on interfaces ∂1D2 and ∂2D1, while the blended image blends the information of two sides"
- Break condition: If interface regions are too small or contain conflicting information, Poisson blending cannot create satisfactory transitions

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Global domain D → M subdomains {Di} → Local VAEs (encoder/decoder/Hψ*) → GP interface models → MCMC sampling → Poisson blending → Global posterior samples
- Critical path: Training data → DD-VAE training → GP interface construction → Local MCMC sampling → Poisson blending → Posterior statistics
- Design tradeoffs: Smaller subdomains reduce dimensionality but increase interface surface area; deeper networks capture more complex patterns but increase training cost
- Failure signatures: High FID scores indicate poor generative quality; visible seams indicate Poisson blending failure; slow MCMC convergence indicates poor latent space design
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train DD-VAE on synthetic data with known correlation structure and compare FID to G-VAE
  2. Test GP interface accuracy by comparing predicted vs exact interface functions on validation data
  3. Validate Poisson blending by comparing stitched vs blended fields on known test cases

## Limitations
- The method assumes local prior distributions are identical to the global prior, which may not hold for problems with strong spatial correlations or non-stationary features
- Interface treatment relies on GP models with active learning, which may struggle with highly nonlinear or discontinuous interface conditions
- The effectiveness of Poisson blending depends on sufficient overlap between subdomains, which becomes challenging for complex geometries or high-dimensional problems

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Computational efficiency improvements and parallel scalability (supported by numerical timing results)
- **Medium confidence**: FID score improvements for low-dimensional problems (demonstrated but limited to specific test cases)
- **Low confidence**: Scalability to very high-dimensional problems (>100,000 dimensions) and complex geometries beyond rectangular domains
