# Towards unraveling calibration biases in medical image analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.05101
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05101
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: This study demonstrates that commonly used calibration metrics (ECE, MCE) exhibit significant bias when demographic groups have unequal sample sizes, revealing that apparent calibration differences between light-skinned and dark-skinned populations in skin lesion classification can be spurious artifacts of sample size imbalance rather than genuine fairness issues.

## Executive Summary
This study examines algorithmic fairness in medical image analysis by investigating calibration biases in skin lesion classification models. The research focuses on detecting systematic performance differences between light-skinned and dark-skinned populations, which is particularly relevant given the underrepresentation of dark-skinned individuals in dermatological datasets. The authors analyze both discrimination and calibration metrics, revealing that commonly used calibration measures like ECE and MCE exhibit significant bias when sample sizes differ between demographic groups. Through synthetic experiments with controlled calibration scenarios, they demonstrate that these metrics' finite-sample effects become more pronounced as models achieve better overall calibration. The study shows that apparent calibration differences between groups can be spurious artifacts of sample size imbalance rather than genuine fairness issues.

## Method Summary
The study uses the PAD-UFES-20 dataset containing 2,298 clinical images from 1,641 skin lesions, with VGG-19 architecture pre-trained on ImageNet. The model is trained with weighted binary cross-entropy loss, Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.0005), and 100 epochs with 32 batch size, using patient-level stratified split across 25 runs. Platt scaling is applied for post-hoc calibration, and both discrimination metrics (AUCROC, AUCPR, balanced accuracy) and calibration metrics (ECE, MCE, AdaECE, Delta-Brier, Delta-CE) are computed for light/dark skin groups separately. Statistical significance is assessed using Wilcoxon Signed Rank test, and synthetic experiments with beta-distributed scores validate the sample size bias findings.

## Key Results
- ECE and MCE calibration metrics show significant bias when demographic groups have unequal sample sizes, creating spurious fairness disparities
- As models achieve better overall calibration, finite-sample effects become more pronounced, making sample size differences more impactful
- Alternative metrics like Delta-Brier or Delta-CE are less affected by sample size imbalances and provide more reliable fairness audits

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: ECE and MCE calibration metrics exhibit systematic bias when demographic groups have unequal sample sizes.
- Mechanism: The finite-sample effect causes these bin-based metrics to underestimate calibration error in minority groups due to insufficient samples per bin, creating spurious fairness disparities.
- Core assumption: The calibration error distribution is similar across demographic groups, and observed differences are artifacts of sample size imbalance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "commonly used calibration measures like ECE and MCE exhibit significant bias when sample sizes differ between demographic groups"
  - [section]: "we demonstrated that the differences observed from the naive estimators of calibration between sub-groups are actually spurious"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence, but related works mention "Exposing and Mitigating Calibration Biases" suggesting this is an active research area
- Break condition: If the true calibration error genuinely differs between groups, or if sample sizes are balanced, the bias would not manifest.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: As overall model calibration improves, finite-size effects become more pronounced, making calibration metrics more sensitive to sample size differences.
- Mechanism: When models are well-calibrated, the calibration error approaches zero, so any remaining discrepancy is dominated by the sampling variance rather than true calibration differences.
- Core assumption: Models can be post-hoc recalibrated to achieve very low calibration error, pushing the metric sensitivity toward sample size effects.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "as models achieve better overall calibration" and "as models are overall better calibrated, finite size effects become more noticeable"
  - [section]: "we show that, as models are overall better calibrated, finite size effects become more noticeable, which is relevant in scenarios where models are post-hoc re-calibrated after training"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence, but related works on "Learning Unbiased Image Segmentation" suggest sample size effects are relevant in medical imaging
- Break condition: If the model cannot be recalibrated to very low calibration error, or if the true calibration difference between groups is large enough to dominate sampling variance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Alternative calibration metrics like Delta-Brier or Delta-CE are less affected by sample size imbalances and provide more reliable fairness audits.
- Mechanism: These metrics compute the difference between pre- and post-calibration errors, which normalizes out some of the sample size bias inherent in absolute calibration error measures.
- Core assumption: The relationship between discrimination and calibration performance is stable across demographic groups, allowing the difference metric to cancel out sample size effects.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "metrics like Delta-Brier or Delta-CE may be more appropriate for detecting calibration biases in settings with demographic imbalance"
  - [section]: No direct quote, but the recommendation implies these metrics are more robust
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence, but related works on "Evaluating the Fairness of Deep Learning Uncertainty Estimates" suggest alternative metrics are being explored
- Break condition: If the discrimination-calibration relationship differs substantially between groups, or if the alternative metrics have their own sample size sensitivities.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different calibration metrics behave under various levels of model de-calibration and sample size imbalances?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that ECE, MCE, and AdaECE are biased with respect to sample size, especially as models become better calibrated, through synthetic experiments.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the behavior of these metrics in controlled scenarios but doesn't provide a comprehensive framework for selecting appropriate metrics in real-world fairness audits with demographic imbalance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical validation across diverse medical imaging datasets with known calibration issues and demographic imbalance, comparing multiple calibration metrics' performance in detecting genuine fairness concerns versus sample size artifacts.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between model re-calibration and fairness metrics' sensitivity to sample size?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "sample-size biases interact with overall model calibration" and "biases become more marked as the model's overall calibration improves."
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates this interaction in synthetic experiments, the practical implications for post-training calibration procedures in medical imaging remain unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic study of how different re-calibration techniques (Platt scaling, temperature scaling, etc.) affect fairness metrics across varying sample size ratios in real medical imaging datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of skin tone annotation method (expert evaluation vs. computational algorithms like ITA) affect fairness assessments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "skin tone label was determined following the usual protocol for patient assessment in the physician's office" but acknowledges that computational methods like ITA have been "called into question."
- Why unresolved: The paper uses one dataset with expert-annotated skin tones but doesn't compare how different annotation approaches might influence fairness audit outcomes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of fairness metrics using the same medical imaging datasets with different skin tone annotation methods, examining whether annotation approach affects observed calibration disparities between demographic groups.

## Limitations
- The study relies entirely on a single dataset (PAD-UFES-20), limiting generalizability to other medical imaging domains
- The analysis assumes that skin tone annotations are accurate and that the "light-skinned" vs "dark-skinned" binary classification captures meaningful calibration differences
- The synthetic experiments use idealized beta distributions that may not capture the full complexity of real-world model behavior

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The core finding that ECE and MCE metrics exhibit sample size bias is well-supported by both theoretical analysis and empirical demonstrations
- Medium Confidence: The recommendation to use Delta-Brier and Delta-CE metrics as alternatives is reasonable but would benefit from more extensive validation across different model architectures and datasets
- Medium Confidence: The interpretation that observed calibration differences are primarily spurious artifacts rather than true performance gaps, though this may not hold in all scenarios
