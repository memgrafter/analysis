# SHARP: Sparsity and Hidden Activation RePlay for Neuro-Inspired Continual Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18563
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18563
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: SHARP outperforms state-of-the-art replay methods in class incremental learning scenarios by leveraging sparse dynamic connectivity and activation replay of hidden units instead of raw inputs.

## Executive Summary
SHARP addresses catastrophic forgetting in continual learning by combining sparse dynamic connectivity with hidden activation replay. Unlike traditional replay methods that store raw inputs, SHARP replays compressed hidden layer activations, which are more biologically plausible and memory efficient. The method dynamically rewires network connections while maintaining sparsity, and freezes important units to preserve knowledge of previously seen classes. Experiments on five benchmark datasets demonstrate that SHARP outperforms existing replay-based methods while requiring fewer resources and not needing pretrained frozen layers.

## Method Summary
SHARP uses a sparse neural network architecture with a G-module (feature extractor) and F-module (replayed layer) split. The method stores quantized hidden activations in STM (temporal memory) and compressed representations in LTM (long-term memory). After each episode, connections from low-rank (rank-0) to high-rank (rank-1) units are dropped and new connections are grown to rank-0 units. Units are ranked based on their activation importance, with higher-ranked units being frozen to prevent forgetting. The method uses supervised contrastive loss and k-NN classification without a final classification layer.

## Key Results
- Outperforms state-of-the-art replay methods on five datasets including MNIST, CIFAR10, and CIFAR100
- Maintains high accuracy even as the number of classes increases, with particularly strong performance on CIFAR100
- Achieves better results with fewer resources by using sparse connectivity (60% pruned) and replaying hidden activations instead of raw inputs
- Demonstrates flexibility in handling scenarios where episode boundaries are blurry and classes may overlap

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SHARP avoids catastrophic forgetting by replaying hidden layer activations instead of raw inputs, which is more biologically plausible and reduces interference.
- Mechanism: Hidden activations are processed representations that contain compressed, task-relevant information. Replaying them preserves learned transformations while avoiding raw pixel-level interference.
- Core assumption: The hidden activations retain sufficient information to represent class concepts and can be effectively replayed to stabilize learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "SHARP is a continual learning method that addresses catastrophic forgetting by leveraging sparse dynamic connectivity and activation replay. Inspired by biological replay, SHARP replays recently seen classes' hidden activations instead of raw examples..."
  - [section] "Replay of neural patterns is a potent mechanism that the brain leverages to learn new information without forgetting existing knowledge... replay in the hippocampus follows spatial trajectories rather than raw sensory inputs..."
- Break condition: If hidden activations lose discriminative power due to rank-0 unit reinitialization or excessive connection rewiring, replay would become ineffective.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Dynamic rewiring combined with sparse connectivity reduces interference by removing low-rank connections and forming new, more effective paths.
- Mechanism: After each phase, connections from rank-0 to rank-1 units are dropped, and new connections are grown to rank-0 units. This breaks harmful input-output paths and promotes beneficial knowledge transfer.
- Core assumption: The greedy rank selection algorithm effectively identifies units whose removal minimizes interference without sacrificing performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "...leverages sparse dynamic connectivity and activation replay. Unlike other activation replay methods, which assume layers not subjected to replay have been pretrained and fixed, SHARP can continually update all layers."
  - [section] "Our first rewiring rule is to drop the connections from all rank-0 units to rank-1 units after each P l 1 selection... We add the same amount of new connections to layer l, ensuring the density d of each layer remains constant."
- Break condition: If the greedy selection fails to capture sufficient activation mass (τ too small) or if too many connections are dropped too aggressively, performance degrades.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Unit freezing based on rank and module position prevents forgetting of past classes while allowing fine-tuning on recent ones.
- Mechanism: Units with rank > m+1 in the F module (after replay) are frozen to prevent overwriting knowledge of classes that will be purged from STM. Units in G module with rank > 1 are also frozen.
- Core assumption: The STM temporal window m correctly estimates which classes are still accessible for replay, and freezing preserves the representations needed for those classes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "...we freeze input connections of specific hidden units to alleviate forgetting."
  - [section] "If a unit has rank r > m + 1 and is in F (·), it risks forgetting because the STM has a temporal capacity of m and will purge some classes that have been seen by the unit. So, we must stop training such units and all their ancestors to retain knowledge..."
- Break condition: If m is set incorrectly relative to the replay schedule, either too much knowledge is frozen (limiting plasticity) or too little (causing forgetting).

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input -> G module (feature extractor, not replayed) -> Hidden activations -> F module (replayed) -> Output (no classification layer, uses k-NN)
  STM: Stores quantized activations from last m episodes (m=1 in experiments)
  LTM: Stores compressed representations for k-NN classification
  Rank system: 0 (idle), 1 (training), 2+ (fine-tuning/frozen)
  Rewiring logic: Drop rank-0->rank-1 connections, grow new connections to rank-0 units

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass: Input -> G -> activations -> F -> representations
  2. Replay pass: Sample from STM -> F -> representations -> contrastive loss
  3. Backward pass: Update only non-frozen units/connections
  4. Rank promotion and freezing after each episode

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Replay cut-off layer: Higher cut-off reduces memory but limits replay benefit; lower cut-off increases memory usage
  - Rank promotion threshold τ: Higher τ recruits fewer units (preserves capacity) but risks underfitting
  - m value: Larger m increases replay benefit but reduces per-class STM capacity

- Failure signatures:
  - Performance plateaus early: Likely τ too high or not enough units recruited
  - Rapid forgetting after few episodes: Likely m too small or incorrect freezing logic
  - Memory overflow: Likely replay cut-off too low or STM budget too small per class

- First 3 experiments:
  1. MNIST sequential (2 classes per episode, 5 episodes) with m=0 (no replay) to isolate impact of rewiring and freezing
  2. Same setup with m=1 to test basic replay mechanism
  3. EMNIST with varying m values to understand replay window sensitivity

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal balance between replay frequency and network plasticity in continual learning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses how SHARP's replay mechanism is inspired by biological replay, which prioritizes recently learned information. However, the paper doesn't explicitly explore the optimal frequency of replay for different types of tasks or network architectures.
- Why unresolved: The optimal replay frequency likely depends on various factors such as task complexity, network architecture, and the rate of concept drift in the data. These factors are not fully explored in the paper.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying replay frequency across different task types and network architectures, measuring the trade-off between forgetting and plasticity, would help determine optimal replay schedules.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does SHARP's performance scale with increasing number of classes or tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that SHARP outperforms other methods as the number of classes increases, but doesn't provide extensive experiments on very large-scale problems or with a high number of tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on relatively small-scale problems (up to 100 classes in CIFAR100). It's unclear how SHARP would perform in scenarios with thousands or millions of classes, or in online learning scenarios with a continuous stream of tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments scaling SHARP to much larger class or task numbers, and comparing its performance to other methods in these extreme scenarios, would provide insights into its scalability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of SHARP's sparse connectivity on energy efficiency and hardware implementation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that SHARP uses sparse networks and prunes 60% of connections, which reduces the number of parameters overall. However, it doesn't discuss the potential energy efficiency benefits or hardware implementation challenges.
- Why unresolved: While sparsity can lead to energy savings and more efficient hardware implementations, it can also introduce challenges in terms of irregular memory access patterns and increased computational complexity for certain operations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of SHARP's energy consumption compared to dense networks, and exploration of hardware architectures optimized for sparse networks, would clarify the practical benefits and challenges of SHARP's sparse connectivity.

## Limitations

- Weak biological validation: The paper claims biological plausibility but lacks direct validation against neural data or experiments showing similarity to biological replay mechanisms
- Limited ablation studies: No experiments isolate the benefits of dynamic rewiring vs. static sparse connectivity, or test the necessity of both connection dropping AND growing mechanisms
- Restricted task scenarios: Experiments only use clean episode boundaries with non-overlapping classes, limiting generalizability to real-world scenarios with blurry or overlapping task boundaries

## Confidence

- High confidence in replay mechanism effectiveness for reducing forgetting (strong empirical results)
- Medium confidence in biological plausibility claims (lacking direct validation)
- Medium confidence in dynamic rewiring benefits (no ablation study)
- Low confidence in generalizability to non-episodic or overlapping class scenarios (only tested with clean episode boundaries)
