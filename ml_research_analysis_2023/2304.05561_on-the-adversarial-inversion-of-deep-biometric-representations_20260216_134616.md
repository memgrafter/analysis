# On the Adversarial Inversion of Deep Biometric Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.05561
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05561
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: This paper investigates the security of deep biometric representations, focusing on the adversarial inversion of deep neural network (DNN) embeddings used in biometric authentication systems. The study addresses the claim that raw biometric samples cannot be reverse-engineered from their mathematical representations, a key security feature of many systems. The authors propose a two-phase attack: (1) Model Inference, where an attacker predicts the target DNN model by exploiting the model's footprint on feature vectors, and (2) Reconstruction, where the attacker uses an approximation of the target model to craft biometric reconstructions. The attack is tested on face and fingerprint recognition, achieving high model inference accuracy (83% for faces, 86% for fingerprints) and effective reconstructions that can impersonate subjects with up to 92% success in 1-vs-1 authentication. The study demonstrates that even with limited knowledge of the target model or dataset, the attack remains effective, posing a significant security risk to biometric systems.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel attack on deep biometric representations, challenging the assumption that raw biometric samples cannot be reverse-engineered from their mathematical embeddings. The authors propose a two-phase attack that first infers the original DNN model by exploiting the model's footprint on the embedding, and then reconstructs the raw data by using the inferred model. The attack is evaluated on face and fingerprint recognition systems, achieving high model inference accuracy and effective reconstructions that can successfully impersonate subjects in authentication scenarios. The study highlights the potential security risks of deep biometric representations and calls for further investigation into defensive strategies.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a two-phase attack on deep biometric representations: Model Inference and Reconstruction. In the Model Inference phase, the attacker trains auxiliary classifiers to map embeddings to potential DNN models by exploiting the model's footprint on the embedding. In the Reconstruction phase, the attacker uses an adversarial dataset to approximate the original data distribution and trains reconstructor networks to transform embeddings back into biometric samples using the inferred DNN model. The attack is evaluated on face and fingerprint recognition, using pre-trained DNN models and public datasets to approximate the target models and data distributions.

## Key Results
- High model inference accuracy: 83% for faces, 86% for fingerprints
- Effective reconstructions: Up to 92% success in 1-vs-1 authentication
- Attack remains effective even with limited knowledge of target model or dataset
- Reconstruction quality degrades with extensive fine-tuning on large datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Embeddings leak discriminative features about the original DNN model, enabling model inference.
- Mechanism: The DNN model's architectural choices and training process leave a "footprint" on the embeddings it produces. Even with limited access to the model, an attacker can exploit these subtle differences in embedding distributions to identify the original model.
- Core assumption: Different DNN architectures and training configurations produce statistically distinct embedding distributions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we show that the attacker can exploit off-the-shelf DNN models and public datasets, to mimic the behaviour of the original learned model to varying degrees of success, based only on the obtained representation and attacker's prior knowledge."
  - [section] "We propose a two-pronged attack that first infers the original DNN by exploiting the model footprint on the embedding, and then reconstructs the raw data by using the inferred model."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct mentions of DNN embedding inversion or model footprint exploitation in the corpus.
- Break condition: If multiple DNN models produce nearly identical embedding distributions (e.g., same architecture, same training data), model inference becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: An attacker can approximate the target DNN model using publicly available pre-trained models and fine-tuning techniques.
- Mechanism: The attacker leverages the trend of re-purposing pre-trained DNNs for specific biometric tasks. By fine-tuning a similar pre-trained model on an adversarial dataset, the attacker can create a proxy model that approximates the behavior of the target model, enabling reconstruction.
- Core assumption: The target DNN model is based on a publicly available architecture and has been fine-tuned on biometric data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we assume that the biometric recognition builds on a pre-trained model of a prominent DNN architecture (e.g., ResNets [21], VGG [61]), available in deep-learning repositories like TensorFlow Hub [66], ModelZoo [80], Open Neural Network Exchange [17] or public code (e.g., github) repositories."
  - [section] "In the case of face recognition, we employ models pre-trained on large face datasets. For fingerprint recognition, we use generic image recognition models pre-trained on ImageNet [9], which we additionally train on fingerprint data (as in [36],[32])."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus focuses on biometric authentication and deep learning but doesn't specifically mention the use of pre-trained models for model inversion attacks.
- Break condition: If the target DNN is custom-built from scratch or based on a proprietary architecture, the attacker's approximation becomes ineffective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Reconstruction of biometric data is possible even with limited knowledge of the original data distribution.
- Mechanism: The attacker trains a reconstruction model to minimize reconstruction loss over an adversarial dataset that approximates the unknown data distribution. By leveraging the inferred DNN model as a proxy, the attacker can transform the embedding back into a plausible biometric sample.
- Core assumption: The adversarial dataset, while not identical to the original, shares enough statistical similarity with the target data distribution to enable effective reconstruction.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we show that the attacker can exploit off-the-shelf DNN models and public datasets, to mimic the behaviour of the original learned model to varying degrees of success, based only on the obtained representation and attacker's prior knowledge."
  - [section] "To address the lack of original data, our attacker builds an adversarial dataset (e.g., a collection of faces or fingerprints from public datasets, or crawled from the Web) that approximates the unknown pX(x) from Equation 1."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus doesn't provide specific examples of reconstruction models trained on adversarial datasets for biometric data.
- Break condition: If the original data distribution is significantly different from any publicly available dataset, reconstruction quality degrades substantially.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Target DNN Models -> Model Inference Phase -> Reconstruction Phase -> Adversarial Dataset -> Reconstructed Biometric Samples
- Critical path:
  1. Obtain a biometric embedding from the target system.
  2. Use Model Inference to identify the likely DNN model that generated the embedding.
  3. Train a reconstruction model using the inferred DNN and an adversarial dataset.
  4. Use the reconstruction model to generate a biometric sample from the embedding.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs. computational cost: More complex models may provide better reconstruction but require more resources.
  - Data availability vs. reconstruction quality: Access to more representative adversarial data generally improves reconstruction.
  - Model inference accuracy vs. attack success: More accurate model inference leads to more effective reconstructions.
- Failure signatures:
  - Low model inference accuracy: Indicates difficulty in identifying the correct DNN model.
  - Poor reconstruction quality: Suggests issues with the reconstruction model or adversarial dataset.
  - Low authentication success rate: Implies that the reconstructed samples are not effective for impersonation.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test model inference accuracy on a small set of known DNN models and embeddings.
  2. Evaluate reconstruction quality using a known DNN model and a controlled adversarial dataset.
  3. Assess the effectiveness of reconstructed samples in a simulated authentication scenario.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different fine-tuning dataset sizes and compositions affect the adversary's ability to infer the original model and reconstruct the biometric trait?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the impact of fine-tuning (FT) on model inference accuracy and reconstruction quality, showing that progressive fine-tuning leads to reconstructions that are less effective for impersonation.
- Why unresolved: The paper experiments with 5 FT configurations with varying dataset sizes (5K-60K samples), but does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how different dataset compositions (e.g., diversity, similarity to original dataset) might affect the attack's success.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed study varying the fine-tuning dataset composition (e.g., number of subjects, sample diversity) and measuring the corresponding impact on model inference accuracy and reconstruction quality.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the attack be extended to other biometric modalities beyond face and fingerprint recognition?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on face and fingerprint recognition, but mentions that the attack can potentially be extended to other image tasks using DNNs and, in principle, to the text domain.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not experiment with other biometric modalities like iris, palmprint, or voice recognition, which may have different characteristics and vulnerabilities to the proposed attack.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and evaluating the attack on other biometric modalities, such as iris, palmprint, or voice recognition, to assess its effectiveness and identify any modality-specific challenges or adaptations required.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the attacker's success rate vary with the complexity and diversity of the original dataset used to train the target model?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that the attacker's success rate is affected by the fine-tuning dataset size and the attacker's data preprocessing, but does not explicitly analyze the impact of the original dataset's complexity and diversity on the attack's effectiveness.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the attacker's background knowledge and the target model's adaptations, but does not investigate how the characteristics of the original dataset (e.g., number of subjects, intra-class variations) might influence the attack's success.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with target models trained on datasets of varying complexity and diversity, and measuring the corresponding impact on the attacker's model inference accuracy and reconstruction quality.

## Limitations

- Generalizability to real-world deployment scenarios remains uncertain.
- Reconstruction quality degrades significantly with extensive fine-tuning on large datasets.
- Success depends heavily on the availability of representative adversarial datasets.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The technical methodology for model inference using auxiliary classifiers is well-established and the experimental results on curated datasets are reproducible.
- **Medium confidence**: The reconstruction methodology and its effectiveness across different DNN architectures and biometric modalities.
- **Low confidence**: The real-world applicability of the attack given practical constraints.
