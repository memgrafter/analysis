# HateMM: A Multi-Modal Dataset for Hate Video Classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.03915
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03915
- Reference count: 12
- Best model accuracy: 0.798, macro F1-score: 0.790

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces HateMM, a multi-modal dataset for hate video classification, addressing the lack of research on video-based hate speech detection. The authors curate approximately 43 hours of videos from BitChute, annotating them as hate or non-hate, along with frame spans and target communities. They develop deep learning models using text, audio, and video modalities individually and jointly, observing that using all modalities improves performance by ~5.7% compared to the best uni-modal model. The best fusion model achieves an accuracy of 0.798 and macro F1-score of 0.790. The authors also analyze the effectiveness of each modality and provide insights into the importance of each for detecting hateful content.

## Method Summary
The authors curated approximately 43 hours of videos from BitChute, annotating them as hate or non-hate, with frame spans and target communities. They extracted text (using ASR and automatic translation), audio, and video features using various models (fastText, LASER, BERT for text; MFCC, AudioVGG19 for audio; 3D-CNN, InceptionV3, ViT for video). These features were combined using fusion layers, and the models were evaluated using accuracy and macro F1-score.

## Key Results
- Multi-modal fusion improves hate video detection accuracy by ~5.7% over best uni-modal model.
- Vision transformer (ViT) features are most effective for detecting hateful videos among vision-based models.
- Text-based models perform best when automatic speech recognition (ASR) transcript quality is high.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multi-modal fusion improves hate video detection accuracy by ~5.7% over best uni-modal model.
- Mechanism: Combining text, audio, and visual features through a trainable neural network captures complementary signals that individual modalities miss.
- Core assumption: Each modality contains unique, non-redundant information about hateful content.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "using all the modalities of the videos improves the overall hate speech detection performance (accuracy=0.798, macro F1-score=0.790) by ~5.7% compared to the best uni-modal model in terms of macro F1 score."
  - [section] "We observe that the text based model is successful when the transcript is relatively clean. The audio based model is most effective when there is shouting and expression of aggression in the video. Finally, the vision based model works the best if there is evidence of visual hateful activity with presence of victim in the video."
- Break condition: If modalities are highly redundant or one modality dominates, fusion gains may be minimal or negative.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Vision transformer (ViT) features are most effective for detecting hateful videos among vision-based models.
- Mechanism: ViT's patch-based processing and attention mechanisms capture both spatial and temporal patterns in video frames relevant to hate content.
- Core assumption: Visual hateful cues (symbols, victim presence, mocking imagery) are distributed across frames and benefit from attention-based processing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "For the vision-based models, we see the features extracted from ViT are very helpful in detecting hate videos among all other vision-based models."
  - [section] "We observe that 45% of the time, the object 'person' appears in hateful videos, whereas 59% of the time, the object 'person' appears in non hateful videos. In the hateful videos we observe use of important religious persons like a Jewish rabbi at the background with a lot of hateful text embedded on them."
- Break condition: If hateful content relies primarily on audio or text cues rather than visual patterns, ViT advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Text-based models perform best when automatic speech recognition (ASR) transcript quality is high.
- Mechanism: Clean transcripts preserve hateful keywords and context that ASR errors might obscure.
- Core assumption: ASR quality is sufficiently good to capture relevant hateful language without excessive noise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The text-based model's performance depends on the accuracy of the automatic speech recognition (ASR). This model is successful most of the time when the ASR can correctly detect the hateful words in the transcript."
  - [section] "We observe that for both the 'hate' and the 'non-hate' classes, the mean percentage of OOV words is almost 22%."
- Break condition: If ASR quality is too poor (high OOV, noise), text-based models become unreliable regardless of architecture.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Multi-modal fusion strategies (early vs late fusion, attention mechanisms)
  - Why needed here: The paper uses a trainable fusion layer to combine text, audio, and vision features; understanding fusion strategies is essential for replicating or improving this approach.
  - Quick check question: What are the key differences between early fusion (concatenation) and late fusion (weighted combination) in multi-modal learning?

- Concept: Transformer architectures for video (Vision Transformer) and text (BERT)
  - Why needed here: Both ViT and BERT are used as feature extractors; understanding their mechanisms helps in feature engineering and model design.
  - Quick check question: How does ViT's patch-based processing differ from traditional CNN approaches for video frames?

- Concept: Audio feature extraction (MFCC, VGG-based features)
  - Why needed here: Audio features are critical for capturing shouting, aggression, and other acoustic cues of hate; understanding feature engineering is necessary for effective audio modeling.
  - Quick check question: What acoustic properties does MFCC capture that might indicate aggressive or hateful speech?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Video input → Frame sampling (1 fps, 100 frames) → ViT feature extraction → LSTM → Fusion layer; Audio input → MFCC/AudioVGG → Dense layers → Fusion layer; Text input → BERT/HateXPlain/fastText → Dense layers → Fusion layer; Fusion layer → Classification head.
- Critical path: Data preprocessing → Feature extraction (ViT, MFCC, BERT) → Fusion → Classification.
- Design tradeoffs: Using ViT vs CNN for vision, MFCC vs VGG for audio, and transformer vs simpler models for text; each choice balances performance, computational cost, and data requirements.
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on hate targets not visually represented (Blacks, Jews) suggests vision model bias; high OOV rates indicate ASR quality issues affecting text model; class imbalance may require sampling or weighting strategies.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train individual modality models (BERT, ViT, MFCC) to establish baseline performance and identify modality-specific strengths.
  2. Implement early fusion (simple concatenation) and compare to late fusion (trainable neural network) to quantify fusion benefit.
  3. Perform ablation studies removing one modality at a time to measure contribution of each modality to overall performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do vision transformers like ViViT and Wav2Vec perform on hate video detection compared to the models used in this study?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that they plan to use other vision and speech transformers such as ViViT and Wav2Vec in future work, suggesting that these models may offer improved performance.
- Why unresolved: The authors did not implement or test these models in their current study.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing the performance of ViViT, Wav2Vec, and other vision/speech transformers with the current models on the HateMM dataset.

### Open Question 2

- Why unresolved: The authors did not determine the optimal video length for accurate detection or explore the relationship between video length and model performance in detail.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive analysis of model performance across various video lengths, identifying the optimal length for accurate detection.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the models developed in this study identify the specific sections of a video that contribute to its classification as hateful, and how effective is this approach compared to analyzing the entire video?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention their intention to build models that can identify the sections of a video that make it hateful, rather than analyzing the entire video.
- Why unresolved: The authors did not implement or evaluate such models in their current study.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing the performance of models that identify specific hateful sections of a video with models that analyze the entire video, as well as an assessment of the effectiveness of this approach in reducing the workload for human moderators.

## Limitations

- Dataset consists exclusively of videos from BitChute, which may limit external validity.
- ASR transcript quality shows high OOV rates (~22%), suggesting that text-based models may be operating with incomplete information.
- Vision models show bias toward certain hate targets (Blacks, Jews) that are more visually represented, indicating potential blind spots for other targets.

## Confidence

- Multi-modal fusion improves hate video detection accuracy by ~5.7% over best uni-modal model: High
- Vision transformer (ViT) features are most effective for detecting hateful videos among vision-based models: Medium
- Text-based models perform best when automatic speech recognition (ASR) transcript quality is high: Medium
