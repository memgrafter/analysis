# Robust Reinforcement Learning through Efficient Adversarial Herding

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07408
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07408
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Novel method using adversarial herds to improve robustness of RL policies against adversarial disturbances

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of training robust reinforcement learning policies that can withstand adversarial disturbances in the environment. The key insight is that using a group of multiple adversaries (an adversarial herd) is more efficient than a single adversary for approximating the inner optimization problem in robust RL. The method, called ROLAH (Robustness through OLd Adversary Herding), combines this herding approach with worst-k averaging to prevent over-pessimism while maintaining robustness. The authors provide theoretical analysis showing that adversarial herds can efficiently approximate the inner optimization problem and empirically demonstrate improved robustness on multiple MuJoCo environments.

## Method Summary
ROLAH improves robust RL by replacing the single adversary in adversarial training with an adversarial herd - a group of multiple adversaries. The method addresses two key issues: computational inefficiency of inner optimization and over-pessimism from worst-case optimization. Instead of solving the inner optimization via gradient descent, ROLAH uses a fixed set of adversaries and selects the worst-k performers for training. The agent's objective shifts from worst-case to average performance over the worst-k adversaries. Theoretical analysis shows that the number of adversaries needed grows linearly with 1/ϵ, and experiments validate improved robustness on Hopper, Walker2d, and Half-Cheetah environments.

## Key Results
- ROLAH consistently generates more robust policies compared to existing baselines across multiple MuJoCo environments
- Theoretical analysis proves adversarial herds can efficiently approximate the inner optimization problem with O(1/ϵ) adversaries
- Worst-k averaging prevents over-pessimism while maintaining robustness to plausible threats
- The method shows improved performance under varying mass/friction conditions and action noise

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: An adversarial herd can efficiently approximate the inner optimization problem compared to a single adversary.
- Mechanism: The inner optimization in robust RL is typically solved via first-order methods like gradient descent, which can get stuck in local optima. By using multiple adversaries, ROLAH replaces this with a linear-time search over a fixed set, avoiding gradient descent issues.
- Core assumption: The reward function R(θ, ϕ) is Lipschitz continuous in ϕ and the set of adversaries can be constructed to be an ϵ-packing or ϵ-net.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We first prove that adversarial herds can efficiently approximate the inner optimization problem."
  - [section] "To address these issues, we extend the two-player zero-sum game by employing an adversarial herd which involves a group of adversaries."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers focus on herding and adversarial training but do not directly address the efficiency claim.
- Break condition: If the reward landscape is highly non-convex and the adversary space is too large, even a herd may not provide sufficient coverage without an exponential number of adversaries.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using worst-k averaging instead of worst-case optimization prevents over-pessimism.
- Mechanism: The agent's objective shifts from optimizing worst-case performance to averaging over the worst k adversaries. This reduces focus on unlikely worst-case scenarios while maintaining robustness to plausible threats.
- Core assumption: The set of adversaries includes both likely and unlikely scenarios, and k is chosen appropriately to balance robustness and performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Then we address the second issue by replacing the worst-case performance in the inner optimization with the average performance over the worst-k adversaries."
  - [section] "We define the worst-k adversaries in a set of adversaries...The new objective is then defined as: max θ∈Θ min ϕ1,...,ϕm∈Φ 1/|Iθ, bΦ,k| Σ i∈Iθ, bΦ,k R(θ, ϕi)."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct support for the worst-k averaging mechanism from corpus papers.
- Break condition: If k is set too large, the method becomes equivalent to RAP and loses robustness benefits; if k is too small, the method reverts to RARL's over-pessimism.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The theoretical bound on the number of adversaries needed is linear in 1/ϵ.
- Mechanism: By constructing an adversarial herd that forms an ϵ-packing or ϵ-net, the approximation error is bounded by ϵ, and the required number of adversaries is O(1/ϵ) under certain conditions.
- Core assumption: The function class RΦ has finite radius under the L∞ norm and adversaries can be sampled or constructed to meet the packing/net requirements.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Theorem 1... If RbΦ is a maximal ϵ-packing then |RbΦ| ≥ ⌈ rmax/ϵ ⌉... the approximation error of ˆϕ on the inner optimization problem is upper bounded by ϵ, i.e., |R(θ, ϕ∗) − R(θ, ˆϕ)| ≤ ϵ."
  - [section] "Theorem 2... with probability 1 − δ, the approximation error of ˆϕ on the inner optimization problem is upper bounded by ϵ if m ≥ log(δ) log−1(1 − Lσ/Lϕ ϵ)."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence supporting the specific theoretical bound from corpus papers.
- Break condition: If the function class RΦ does not have finite radius or the sampling measure is not dense around optimal adversaries, the bound may not hold.

## Limitations
- Theoretical efficiency claims rely heavily on Lipschitz continuity assumptions that may not hold in complex environments
- Experimental validation is limited to simple MuJoCo locomotion tasks without testing on high-dimensional environments
- Theoretical bounds on adversary count are asymptotic and don't account for practical computational constraints
- The method requires careful tuning of the worst-k parameter to balance robustness and performance

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The core mechanism of using multiple adversaries instead of a single adversary is sound and theoretically justified
- **Medium confidence**: The worst-k averaging approach effectively balances robustness and performance, though empirical validation could be stronger
- **Low confidence**: The specific theoretical bounds on adversary count are tight and practically achievable in real-world scenarios
