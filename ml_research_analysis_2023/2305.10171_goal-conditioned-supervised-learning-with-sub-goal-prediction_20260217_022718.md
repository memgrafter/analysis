# Goal-Conditioned Supervised Learning with Sub-Goal Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.10171
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10171
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: TraIL improves GCSL success rates by predicting sub-goals instead of direct goals, particularly for longer trajectories

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces TraIL, a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning method that extends GCSL by learning to predict sub-goals in addition to actions. The key idea is that by replacing the original goal with a predicted sub-goal, GCSL can learn more accurate actions, especially for goals requiring longer trajectories. TraIL uses the same data as GCSL and adds two regularization terms to improve sub-goal prediction. The authors evaluate TraIL on several benchmark tasks and show that it outperforms GCSL in most cases, with significant improvements in success rates, particularly for longer trajectories.

## Method Summary
TraIL extends GCSL by adding a trajectory encoder that predicts intermediate states (sub-goals) along the path from current state to goal. The method uses a mixture density network (MDN) to represent multiple plausible trajectory modes and includes edge and self-consistency regularization terms to ensure smooth and consistent predictions. During action prediction, TraIL queries the sub-goal predictor to obtain an intermediate target, which is then used as the goal for the action predictor, effectively shortening the effective horizon and reducing bias toward short-horizon predictions.

## Key Results
- TraIL outperforms GCSL on success rates across multiple benchmark tasks
- Significant improvements observed for goals requiring longer trajectories
- MDN enables multimodal trajectory representation, handling diverse path options
- Regularization terms (edge loss, self-consistency) improve sub-goal prediction consistency

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Replacing the original goal with a predicted sub-goal shortens the effective horizon for GCSL, allowing it to predict actions with higher accuracy.
- Mechanism: TraIL predicts a state along the trajectory from current state to goal using a mixture density network. This predicted sub-goal becomes the new target for GCSL's action predictor, effectively reducing the remaining steps to the goal and avoiding the bias toward short-horizon predictions inherent in GCSL.
- Core assumption: The predicted sub-goal is close enough to the true trajectory that the shortened horizon improves action accuracy without introducing large errors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "replacing real goals in GCSL with predicted TraIL sub-goals allows the agent to reach a greater set of goal states using the exact same data as GCSL"
  - [section] "when predicting an action for data collection, we query πS with t = 0.5 and generate a sub-goal m and then replace g with m in GCSL action prediction a ∼ π(a|s,m)"
- Break condition: If the sub-goal prediction error is large relative to the state space, the shortened horizon may not compensate, leading to worse performance than vanilla GCSL.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The mixture density network (MDN) in TraIL can represent multiple plausible trajectory modes, improving generalization across different starting and goal states.
- Mechanism: The MDN outputs K Gaussian modes, each with its own mean and variance. During training, the loss is computed on the most likely mode for a given (s,g,t), and at test time the best mode is selected based on proximity to s and g.
- Core assumption: The true trajectory distribution can be well-approximated by a mixture of Gaussians, and the modes correspond to distinct path types (e.g., going around obstacles).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We thus use a model capable of representing several modalities – Mixture Density Network (MDN [3])"
  - [section] "Let K be the number of Gaussians, and d be the state dimension. The network predicts p ∈ ∆K, c1...cK ∈ Rd, and σ̂1...σ̂K ∈ Rd"
- Break condition: In domains where trajectories are not multimodal (e.g., simple straight-line motion), the MDN adds unnecessary complexity and may overfit.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The edge and self-consistency regularizations enforce that the trajectory encoder is smooth and consistent, which is critical for reliable sub-goal prediction.
- Mechanism: The edge loss ensures that at t=0 the encoder outputs the start state and at t=1 the goal state. The self-consistency loss ensures that overlapping sub-trajectories predict the same intermediate states, encouraging smooth interpolation.
- Core assumption: The MDP dynamics allow smooth interpolation between states, and the data contains sufficient coverage of the state space for these constraints to be meaningful.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Edge loss: For a consistent encoder we require that πS(s,g,0) = s and πS(s,g,1) = g"
  - [section] "Self-consistency loss: For a consistent encoder, let sm be a state in trajectory τ, all sub-trajectories of τ that contains m should predict it"
- Break condition: In discontinuous state spaces (e.g., images, categorical states) or highly stochastic dynamics, these smoothness assumptions break down.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GC-RL) where the agent receives a goal as input and must maximize success rate over a fixed horizon.
  - Why needed here: TraIL is built on top of GCSL, which is a method for GC-RL; understanding the formulation clarifies why sub-goal prediction is useful.
  - Quick check question: What is the objective function for goal-reaching in a goal-conditioned MDP?

- Concept: Supervised learning for policy optimization (as opposed to RL with value functions or policy gradients).
  - Why needed here: GCSL and TraIL both rely on maximizing log-likelihood of actions given states and goals, avoiding the instability of RL objectives.
  - Quick check question: How does the GCSL loss differ from a standard behavioral cloning loss?

- Concept: Mixture density networks (MDNs) for modeling multimodal output distributions.
  - Why needed here: TraIL uses an MDN to represent multiple plausible trajectory modes, which is essential for handling diverse path options in the state space.
  - Quick check question: What are the three outputs of an MDN and what do they represent?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: GCSL policy π(a|s,g) -> TraIL trajectory encoder πS(m|s,g,t) with MDN backbone -> Regularization modules (edge loss, self-consistency loss)

- Critical path:
  1. During data collection, store trajectories in replay buffer
  2. Train π on action prediction using hindsight goals
  3. Concurrently train πS on sub-goal prediction using the same data
  4. At test time, for a given (s,g), use πS to predict a sub-goal m, then use π to predict action a given (s,m)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - MDN vs. deterministic network: MDN allows multimodal predictions but is more complex and may overfit with limited data
  - Regularization strength: Too much regularization can over-constrain the model; too little may lead to inconsistent predictions
  - Sub-goal selection t: t=0.5 balances between shortening the horizon and maintaining accuracy; t closer to 1 reduces the benefit

- Failure signatures:
  - If TraIL underperforms GCSL, check whether the sub-goal predictions are accurate enough (e.g., by visualizing predicted vs. actual trajectories)
  - If training is unstable, inspect the MDN mixture weights for collapse (all weight on one component)
  - If regularization is too strong, the model may fail to fit the data (high training loss)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train GCSL and TraIL on a simple gridworld with known shortest paths; compare success rates and visualize predicted trajectories
  2. Vary the number of MDN mixture components (K=1,2,3) and observe impact on success rate and training stability
  3. Disable each regularization term separately to confirm their contribution to performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does TraIL's performance scale with increasing state space dimensionality, particularly in visual domains where Euclidean distance may not be meaningful?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that TraIL uses Euclidean distance-based regularization terms and discusses limitations in domains where Euclidean distance is not meaningful, such as images or categorical states.
- Why unresolved: The experiments are conducted in relatively low-dimensional state spaces (discrete grids, continuous 4D state spaces, 7-DOF robot arm). The paper does not provide empirical results for high-dimensional visual state spaces.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical evaluation of TraIL on visual goal-reaching tasks (e.g., image-based navigation) with ablation studies comparing different distance metrics or regularization approaches.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does TraIL's advantage over GCSL persist when the goal distribution is highly skewed or when goals require specific sequences of actions that are difficult to predict from sub-goals?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that TraIL improves performance for goals requiring longer trajectories, but does not investigate performance under skewed goal distributions or goals requiring specific action sequences.
- Why unresolved: The experimental evaluation uses uniformly sampled goals within defined regions. There is no investigation of performance under non-uniform goal distributions or goals requiring specific action patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing TraIL and GCSL under skewed goal distributions and with goals requiring specific action sequences, measuring success rates across different goal types.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is TraIL's performance to the choice of sub-goal selection strategy (e.g., different values of t in GetAction) and can this parameter be learned or adapted online?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses using t = 0.5 for sub-goal selection and mentions an "aggressive" variant t = max(0.5, i+1/T) as the episode progresses, but does not systematically explore alternative strategies or adaptive approaches.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses fixed sub-goal selection strategies without exploring the full parameter space or adaptive methods. The choice of t = 0.5 is motivated by dividing the trajectory, but its optimality is not established.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies varying the sub-goal selection parameter t, comparison of fixed vs. adaptive strategies, and evaluation of learned sub-goal selection policies.

## Limitations
- Performance depends on accurate sub-goal prediction; errors compound if sub-goals are far from true trajectories
- Scalability to high-dimensional visual state spaces remains unexplored
- Sensitivity to hyperparameters (MDN mixture count, regularization coefficients) may limit practical deployment

## Confidence

- **Core claim** (sub-goal prediction improves long-horizon performance): Medium confidence - supported by empirical evidence but theoretical justification could be more rigorous
- **MDN enables multimodal representation**: High confidence - architecture description is clear, though practical benefit is empirically demonstrated
- **Regularization terms improve performance**: Medium confidence - shown to help but individual effects not isolated through ablation

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study**: Remove each regularization term (edge loss, self-consistency) separately to quantify their individual contributions to performance gains.

2. **Error analysis**: Measure sub-goal prediction error versus action prediction error to verify that sub-goal prediction is indeed the bottleneck rather than a source of compounding errors.

3. **Horizon sensitivity**: Systematically vary the sub-goal selection parameter t (0.3, 0.5, 0.7) to determine the optimal trade-off between horizon reduction and prediction accuracy across different task difficulties.
