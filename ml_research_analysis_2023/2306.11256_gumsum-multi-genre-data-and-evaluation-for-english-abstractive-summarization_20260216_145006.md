# GUMSum: Multi-Genre Data and Evaluation for English Abstractive Summarization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.11256
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11256
- Reference count: 22
- This paper introduces GUMSum, a carefully crafted multi-genre dataset for evaluating English abstractive summarization.

## Executive Summary
GUMSum presents a novel multi-genre dataset designed for evaluating abstractive summarization in English. The dataset comprises one-sentence summaries for 213 documents spanning 12 different genres, created with strict guidelines to ensure substitutive potential, factuality, and faithfulness. The summaries are constrained to avoid hallucinations and remain true to the source text. The evaluation compares general-domain untuned approaches, a fine-tuned model, and a prompt-based approach against human performance across multiple genres.

## Method Summary
The GUMSum dataset was constructed by selecting documents from the GUM corpus across 12 genres, with careful attention to creating one-sentence summaries that meet strict quality criteria. Summary guidelines required that summaries be substitutive (could replace the original text for understanding purposes), factual, and faithful to the source. The evaluation methodology employed both automatic metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore, factuality scores) and human judgments to compare three approaches: GPT3.5-turbo, a fine-tuned PEGASUS model, and a baseline RAG model against human-generated summaries.

## Key Results
- GPT3 achieves impressive scores but still underperforms humans in abstractive summarization
- Summary quality varies significantly across different genres
- Human judgments reveal different types of errors in supervised, prompted, and human-generated summaries
- The dataset highlights the challenges of producing high-quality summaries that meet strict factuality and faithfulness criteria

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The dataset's effectiveness stems from its strict quality guidelines and multi-genre coverage, which create a challenging evaluation benchmark that reveals both strengths and weaknesses of summarization approaches. The one-sentence constraint forces models to capture essential information concisely, while the substitutive criterion ensures summaries serve as viable alternatives to the source text.

## Foundational Learning

1. **Abstractive Summarization**: Why needed - To create condensed versions of text that generate novel phrasings rather than copying; Quick check - Can the model generate coherent summaries that don't simply extract sentences?

2. **Factuality Metrics**: Why needed - To ensure generated summaries don't hallucinate or contradict source information; Quick check - Does the factuality score correctly identify hallucinations versus accurate information?

3. **Multi-Genre Evaluation**: Why needed - To assess model generalization across different writing styles and content types; Quick check - Does performance vary significantly across different document genres?

4. **ROUGE/BERTScore**: Why needed - To provide automatic evaluation metrics that correlate with human judgments; Quick check - Do these metrics align with human assessments of summary quality?

5. **Faithfulness**: Why needed - To ensure summaries accurately represent the source content without introducing errors; Quick check - Are summaries free from contradictions with the original text?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Source Text -> Summary Generation Model -> Summary Output -> Evaluation Metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore, Factuality) -> Human Judgment

**Critical Path**: The most critical evaluation path involves comparing model-generated summaries against human references using both automatic metrics and human judgment, with the human evaluation serving as the ground truth for quality assessment.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The one-sentence constraint ensures concise evaluation but may limit the assessment of longer-form summarization capabilities. The multi-genre approach provides breadth but reduces depth in any single domain.

**Failure Signatures**: Common failure modes include factual inconsistencies, loss of core information, and genre-specific comprehension issues. Models tend to struggle more with narrative and opinion genres compared to instructional or expository text.

## Limitations
- The evaluation methodology relies heavily on automatic metrics that may not fully capture semantic faithfulness or subtle hallucinations
- The one-sentence summary constraint limits generalizability to longer-form abstractive summarization tasks
- The sample size of 213 documents across 12 genres may not provide sufficient statistical power for definitive conclusions about genre-specific performance differences

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The dataset construction methodology, including the strict guidelines for substitutive summaries and the multi-genre composition, is well-documented and methodologically sound.

**Medium confidence**: The relative performance rankings between GPT3.5-turbo, fine-tuned models, and human annotators, as these are based on both automatic metrics and human judgments but may be influenced by the specific evaluation protocols.

**Medium confidence**: The observation that GPT3.5-turbo underperforms humans despite achieving high automatic scores, as this conclusion depends on the specific human evaluation criteria and sample selection.

## Next Checks

1. Conduct inter-annotator agreement studies on the human judgments to establish reliability of the quality assessments across different evaluators and genres.

2. Test additional state-of-the-art summarization models (e.g., contemporary LLMs, instruction-tuned variants) on GUMSum to determine if the performance gap between automated approaches and humans persists across a broader range of methods.

3. Perform ablation studies varying summary length constraints to assess whether the one-sentence restriction artificially limits the performance potential of both human and machine-generated summaries.
