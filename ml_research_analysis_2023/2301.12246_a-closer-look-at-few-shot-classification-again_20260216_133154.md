# A Closer Look at Few-shot Classification Again

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.12246
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12246
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Training and adaptation algorithms in few-shot classification are uncorrelated and can be optimized independently

## Executive Summary
This paper conducts a comprehensive empirical study of few-shot classification, challenging conventional wisdom about the relationship between training and adaptation algorithms. The authors systematically evaluate numerous combinations of training and adaptation methods across the Meta-Dataset benchmark, revealing that these two components are largely uncorrelated - meaning the ranking of one remains stable regardless of which algorithm is chosen for the other. This finding enables practitioners to optimize training and adaptation phases independently. The study also uncovers novel insights about scaling laws, the surprising effectiveness of vanilla fine-tuning, and the relationship between ImageNet performance and few-shot generalization.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate 12 training algorithms (5 meta-learning and 7 non-meta-learning) paired with 9 adaptation algorithms across 9 few-shot classification datasets from Meta-Dataset. They conduct systematic experiments to test whether training and adaptation algorithms are correlated by examining if their relative performance rankings remain stable when paired with different algorithms from the other category. The study also investigates scaling laws by varying the number of training classes versus samples per class, and examines the relationship between ImageNet performance and few-shot classification accuracy for different model families.

## Key Results
- Training and adaptation algorithms in few-shot classification are uncorrelated, allowing independent optimization
- Test error follows a power law with the number of training classes, not samples per class
- Vanilla fine-tuning outperforms specialized partial fine-tuning methods even with extremely limited data
- ImageNet performance poorly predicts few-shot accuracy for supervised models but well for self-supervised models

- **Component map**: Training algorithm -> Model architecture -> Adaptation algorithm -> Support set -> Classifier -> Query set evaluation
- **Critical path**: Train model using chosen training algorithm → Apply adaptation algorithm to support data → Classify query data → Aggregate results across tasks
- **Design tradeoffs**: Meta-learning vs. non-meta-learning training (complexity vs. performance), backbone vs. head adaptation (expressiveness vs. overfitting), hyperparameter search strategies

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal strategy for selecting training data that maximizes few-shot performance across diverse domains?
- Basis in paper: Increasing training data doesn't always improve performance on certain datasets
- Why unresolved: Paper identifies importance but doesn't provide specific data selection strategies
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing different data selection strategies on diverse benchmarks

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we design training objectives that generalize well to both few-shot classification and other vision tasks?
- Basis in paper: Supervised models overfit to ImageNet while self-supervised generalize better
- Why unresolved: Paper identifies discrepancy but doesn't provide concrete solutions
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of training objectives optimizing for few-shot generalization

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the fundamental differences in optimization dynamics between query-support matching algorithms and backbone adaptation methods?
- Basis in paper: Query-support matching scales poorly with shots while backbone adaptation scales better
- Why unresolved: Paper identifies scaling difference but doesn't explain underlying optimization principles
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis comparing optimization landscapes and convergence properties

## Limitations

- Partial order analysis relies on transitivity assumptions that may not hold for all algorithm pairs
- Scaling law claims are primarily based on ImageNet experiments and need validation on other domains
- ImageNet predictor analysis for self-supervised models needs verification across additional model families

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Empirical observations about vanilla fine-tuning effectiveness and stability of adaptation algorithm rankings
- **Medium Confidence**: Neural scaling law claims requiring further validation beyond ImageNet
- **Low Confidence**: Theoretical framework for uncorrelation based on partial orders
