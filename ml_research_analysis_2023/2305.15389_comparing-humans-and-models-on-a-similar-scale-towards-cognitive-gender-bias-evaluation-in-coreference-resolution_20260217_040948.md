# Comparing Humans and Models on a Similar Scale: Towards Cognitive Gender Bias Evaluation in Coreference Resolution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2305.15389
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15389
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Key outcome:** Human subjects were found more gender-biased on naturally-occurring sentences than on synthetic templates, while models exhibited greater bias on synthetic data. Both humans and models showed comparable levels of gender bias overall.

## Executive Summary
This work evaluates gender bias in coreference resolution models versus human behavior using two crowdsourcing experiments based on the dual-process theory of decision-making. The study finds that humans and models exhibit comparable levels of gender bias overall, with humans showing more bias on real-world data while models show more bias on synthetic data. This suggests that model biases reflect human decision-making under constrained settings rather than being mere annotation artifacts.

## Method Summary
The study uses three coreference gender bias datasets (WinoBias, Winogender, and BUG) to evaluate human versus model gender biases. Two crowdsourcing experiments were conducted: a self-paced reading experiment using the Maze task to measure fast, unconscious System 1 biases, and a constrained-time question answering experiment to measure slow, conscious System 2 biases. Human annotations were collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk and compared against SpanBERT and s2e model biases using accuracy metrics (∆QA for QA task, ∆MAZE for self-paced reading task).

## Key Results
- Humans make ~3% more gender-biased decisions than models on real-world data, while models are ~12% more biased than humans on synthetic data
- Both humans and models exhibit comparable overall levels of gender bias across all conditions
- The discrepancy between synthetic and real-world data bias patterns suggests models may reflect human decision-making shortcuts rather than data artifacts

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Models learn gender bias from training data via spurious correlations between pronouns and occupational gender norms
- **Mechanism:** Training data contains stereotypical associations (e.g., "nurse" → feminine, "doctor" → masculine) that models use as shortcuts
- **Core assumption:** These correlations reflect real-world societal biases rather than random artifacts
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "Spurious correlations were found to be an important factor explaining model performance in various NLP tasks"
  - Section: "Historical Bias definition: 'the already existing bias and socio-technical issues in the world'"
  - Corpus: Weak evidence from related papers on gender bias in vision language models

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Human decision-making under time constraints produces gender bias comparable to models
- **Mechanism:** Under constrained time, humans rely on System 1 heuristics that use gender stereotypes, similar to how models exploit statistical shortcuts
- **Core assumption:** Dual-process theory accurately models human cognitive processing in coreference resolution
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "We approach this question through the lens of the dual-process theory for human decision-making"
  - Section: "On real-world data humans make ~3% more gender-biased decisions compared to models"
  - Corpus: Weak evidence from related bias evaluation work

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Synthetic sentences produce more pronounced gender bias in models than real-world sentences
- **Mechanism:** Synthetic datasets create extreme stereotypical scenarios that models overfit to
- **Core assumption:** Synthetic datasets are designed to maximize gender bias scenarios
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section: "On real-world data humans make ~3% more gender-biased decisions compared to models, while on synthetic data models are ~12% more biased"
  - Section: "Models exhibit gender bias more than humans on synthetic sentences in the QA experiment"
  - Corpus: Moderate evidence from gender bias benchmarking studies

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Coreference gender bias datasets (WinoBias, Winogender, BUG) → preprocessing → experiment interfaces → human annotation (QA and MAZE) → model evaluation (SpanBERT, s2e) → comparative analysis (∆QA, ∆MAZE, CDF)

- **Critical path:** 1) Dataset selection and filtering for experiment compatibility, 2) Human annotation interface development and deployment, 3) Model evaluation on identical sentences, 4) Comparative analysis of human vs model biases

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Using synthetic vs real-world sentences: Synthetic provides controlled bias scenarios but may not reflect natural language; real-world provides ecological validity but less controlled bias measurement
  - Time constraints: Necessary for System 1 measurement but may introduce noise from comprehension difficulties
  - Self-paced reading vs traditional eye-tracking: MAZE provides scalability but may overestimate reading times

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High non-coreference error rates (>5%) indicating task confusion
  - No difference in bias between time-constrained and unlimited conditions
  - Models showing equal or less bias than humans across all conditions
  - Inconsistent results between synthetic and real-world datasets

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Run QA experiment on a small subset of WinoBias sentences with unlimited time to establish baseline human accuracy
  2. Implement MAZE experiment on the same WinoBias subset to compare response time patterns
  3. Evaluate SpanBERT model on the identical sentences to establish initial model bias baseline before full-scale experiments

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do human biases in coreference resolution compare to model biases when using pronouns beyond the three grammatical genders (feminine, masculine, neutral)?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper mentions that the datasets used are generally devoid of other types of pronouns, such as neopronouns
- **Why unresolved:** The study did not include diverse pronoun types, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other gender identities
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting experiments with datasets that include a variety of pronoun types and analyzing the biases exhibited by both humans and models

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do cultural differences affect the manifestation of gender bias in coreference resolution tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper acknowledges that the definitions for anti-stereotypical and pro-stereotypical are based on U.S.-centric societal norms
- **Why unresolved:** The study only considered U.S. societal norms, which may not reflect the gender stereotypes and biases in other cultures
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Performing similar experiments in different cultural contexts and comparing the results to identify cultural variations in gender bias

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the impact of using a more diverse group of human annotators on the observed gender biases in coreference resolution tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that the conclusions may apply only for the group of annotators used
- **Why unresolved:** The study used a specific group of annotators, which may not represent the broader population
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting the experiments with a more diverse group of annotators and analyzing whether the observed biases change with different demographic characteristics

## Limitations
- The dual-process theory framework may not fully capture the complexity of human decision-making in coreference resolution tasks
- The comparison relies heavily on synthetic datasets that may introduce artifacts not present in natural language
- Time-constrained experiments may confound gender bias with general comprehension difficulties, especially for non-native English speakers

## Confidence
- **Primary claim confidence:** Medium
- **Evidence quality:** Moderate - well-designed experiments but limited by dataset constraints and potential confounding factors
- **Methodology soundness:** Good - uses established dual-process theory framework and appropriate experimental controls
- **Reproducibility:** High - detailed methodology and publicly available datasets enable replication

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a controlled experiment varying time constraints systematically to isolate whether observed biases are truly gender-related versus general time-pressure effects
2. Test model performance on debiased training data to determine if biases persist without stereotypical correlations, distinguishing between learned patterns versus genuine decision-making shortcuts
3. Implement a third experimental condition using semi-synthetic data that maintains real-world context while controlling for stereotypical associations, to better understand the synthetic vs real-world discrepancy
