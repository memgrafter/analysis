# Integrating Psychometrics and Computing Perspectives on Bias and Fairness in Affective Computing: A Case Study of Automated Video Interviews

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.02629
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02629
- Reference count: 30
- One-line primary result: Automated video interview models show acceptable accuracy but gender bias in predictions for some personality traits

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a framework for understanding and measuring bias and fairness in affective computing machine learning pipelines by integrating psychometric perspectives with a noisy communication model. The framework maps each stage of the ML pipeline to potential sources of psychometric bias and fairness violations, providing a systematic approach to identifying where contamination or deficiency in information flow can differentially impact subgroups. The authors apply this framework to a case study of automated video interviews (AVIs) for employment screening, finding that while the ML model has acceptable accuracy, there is evidence of gender bias in predictions with adverse impact ratios below the legal threshold for some personality traits.

## Method Summary
The study uses multimodal data from video interviews including visual, audio, and behavioral features, along with ground truth personality and hireability scores collected from human annotators. A random forest model is trained with nested 5-fold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning. The framework evaluates bias and fairness using Spearman correlation, Cohen's d effect size, and adverse impact ratios. The method involves feature extraction and preprocessing, model training and validation, and comprehensive bias/fairness assessment throughout the ML development process.

## Key Results
- The ML model achieved acceptable accuracy in predicting personality traits and hireability from AVI data
- Adverse impact ratios below 0.8 (violating the "four-fifths rule") were found for some personality traits, indicating potential gender bias
- Cohen's d effect sizes revealed gender differences in model predictions for several traits, though most were within acceptable ranges
- The framework successfully identified multiple sources of bias throughout the AVI ML pipeline

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The noisy communication model explicitly maps each stage of the ML pipeline to potential sources of psychometric bias and fairness violations
- Mechanism: By representing data collection, annotation, feature extraction, modeling, and decision stages as noisy communication exchanges, the framework surfaces where contamination or deficiency in information flow can differentially impact subgroups
- Core assumption: All bias sources can be modeled as noise or individual differences in sender/receiver pairs, enabling systematic enumeration
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "expand on an interpersonal communication framework to elucidate how to identify sources of bias"
  - [section III.A] "we can utilize the noisy communication model from the previous section to represent the flow of information"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; corpus neighbors focus on fairness and bias but not this specific communication model mapping
- Break condition: If bias arises from structural or institutional factors that cannot be captured as communication channel noise or individual differences, the model will miss those sources

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Distinguishing psychometric bias (systematic measurement error) from fairness (value judgments about use) prevents conflation in ML system design
- Mechanism: By separating "sources of bias" from "manifestations of bias," the framework clarifies when fairness interventions (e.g., counter-factual fairness) address different concerns than bias mitigation (e.g., differential item functioning)
- Core assumption: Bias and fairness are conceptually distinct even when they co-occur in outcomes
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We provide a psychometric-grounded exposition of bias and fairness"
  - [section II.B] "Bias refers to any systematic error in a test score that differentially affects the performance of different groups"
  - [corpus] Weak; corpus mentions fairness but not the explicit separation from psychometric bias

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Applying US legal standards (e.g., "four-fifths rule") to ML predictions creates a hard constraint that guides engineering decisions
- Mechanism: Adverse impact ratios computed on predicted vs. ground truth labels serve as compliance checkpoints that force teams to address fairness before deployment
- Core assumption: Legal thresholds are valid proxies for ethical acceptability in high-stakes contexts
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "emphasize the need for AC researchers to assess bias and fairness throughout the ML development process"
  - [section V.B] "In the US, this violates the 'four-fifths rule' and would be considered prima facie evidence to support a legal discrimination claim"
  - [corpus] No evidence; corpus focuses on general fairness but not US-specific legal compliance

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Noisy communication models (Shannon-Weaver, SMCR)
  - Why needed here: Provide the structural vocabulary to decompose the ML pipeline into sender/receiver/message/channel elements
  - Quick check question: In the AVI case, who is the "sender" when ground truth labels are collected from human annotators?

- Concept: Psychometric definitions of bias (contamination vs. deficiency)
  - Why needed here: Distinguish systematic errors that differentially affect groups from overall measurement inaccuracy
  - Quick check question: If an ML model systematically underestimates personality scores for one gender, is this bias or just inaccuracy?

- Concept: Fairness metrics taxonomy (distributive, procedural, interactional)
  - Why needed here: Map stakeholder concerns (equity, explainability, justice) to measurable quantities in the ML pipeline
  - Quick check question: Which fairness type would require showing that decision thresholds are the same for all groups?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Ground Truth Stage → Human annotation + scoring/fusion
  - Data/Feature Stage → Feature extraction + feature engineering
  - Prediction Stage → ML model training + validation
  - Decision Stage → Decision function + compliance checks

- Critical path: Ground Truth → Features → Predictions → Decisions → Compliance Evaluation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fairness through unawareness vs. fairness through awareness (cannot satisfy both simultaneously in some contexts)
  - Accuracy vs. bias reduction (Pareto optimization may be needed)
  - Legal compliance (US four-fifths rule) vs. cultural equity definitions

- Failure signatures:
  - Adverse impact ratio < 0.8 → legal risk
  - Large gender differences in Spearman ρ → potential bias source
  - Restricted prediction range vs. ground truth → validity issue

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compute adverse impact ratio on ground truth labels only to establish baseline fairness
  2. Apply fairness through unawareness (remove gender features) and recompute bias metrics
  3. Test a counter-factual fairness mitigation: train separate models per gender and compare aggregate outcomes

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we effectively measure and mitigate bias in automated video interview systems while maintaining predictive accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the need for bias and fairness assessments in AVI systems and mentions some mitigation strategies like excluding gender-biased features or using fair representation learning
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies potential sources of bias and discusses some mitigation strategies, it does not provide a comprehensive framework for measuring and mitigating bias while maintaining predictive accuracy
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic evaluation of different bias mitigation strategies on AVI systems, measuring both bias reduction and predictive accuracy, would provide evidence for effective approaches

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do individual differences in communication styles and cultural backgrounds affect the performance of automated video interview systems?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the noisy communication model and how individual differences can introduce bias, but does not specifically address communication styles and cultural backgrounds
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide empirical evidence on how individual differences in communication styles and cultural backgrounds affect the performance of AVI systems
- What evidence would resolve it: A study comparing the performance of AVI systems across different communication styles and cultural backgrounds, controlling for other factors, would provide evidence on the impact of these individual differences

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we ensure that automated video interview systems are fair and unbiased across different demographic groups, including gender, race, and age?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the need for fairness in AVI systems and mentions some fairness metrics, but does not provide a comprehensive framework for ensuring fairness across different demographic groups
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies some fairness metrics, it does not provide a comprehensive framework for ensuring fairness across different demographic groups
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic evaluation of AVI systems across different demographic groups, using multiple fairness metrics, would provide evidence on the fairness of these systems

## Limitations

- The framework's applicability to contexts beyond automated video interviews remains uncertain
- The noisy communication model may not capture all forms of bias, particularly institutional or systemic factors outside the ML pipeline
- US legal standards (four-fifths rule) may not be valid proxies for ethical acceptability in global contexts

## Confidence

- High: The distinction between psychometric bias and fairness concepts, and their application to the AVI case study
- Medium: The noisy communication model's ability to comprehensively map bias sources across the ML pipeline
- Low: The generalizability of the framework to other affective computing applications and the sufficiency of US legal standards as a global fairness benchmark
