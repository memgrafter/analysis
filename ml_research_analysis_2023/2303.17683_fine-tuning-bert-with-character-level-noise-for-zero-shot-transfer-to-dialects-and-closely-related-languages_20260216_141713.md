# Fine-Tuning BERT with Character-Level Noise for Zero-Shot Transfer to Dialects and Closely-Related Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.17683
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17683
- Reference count: 4
- Key outcome: Character-level noise during fine-tuning improves zero-shot cross-lingual transfer for intent classification when lexical overlap is high and OOV tokens are short, with 100% stacked noise showing best results.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates inducing character-level noise during fine-tuning to enable zero-shot cross-lingual transfer to unseen dialects and closely-related languages. The authors fine-tune monolingual BERT models on three sentence-level classification tasks (intent classification, topic identification, and sentiment analysis) and introduce four types of character-level noise (insertion, deletion, replacement, and swapping) at various levels (25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) with two composition strategies (joint and stacked). Results show that character-level noise significantly improves zero-shot transfer performance for intent classification tasks, especially when the source-target language pair has high lexical overlap and shorter out-of-vocabulary tokens. The method achieved near-perfect performance for closely-related dialects (e.g., German to Swiss German) and substantial improvements for more distant languages (e.g., German to Italian). However, the approach was less effective for tasks requiring deeper semantic understanding (topic identification and sentiment analysis) and for language pairs with lower lexical overlap and longer out-of-vocabulary tokens.

## Method Summary
The study fine-tunes monolingual BERT models (German, Italian, Romanian, Spanish) on source language task data with character-level noise. Four noise types (insertion, deletion, replacement, swapping) are applied at levels from 25% to 100% using joint or stacked compositions. The method involves training with noise configurations including baseline (0% noise), joint composition at various noise levels, and stacked composition with separate copies for each noise type. Models are evaluated on target languages and source language, with five trials per configuration using 95% confidence intervals.

## Key Results
- Character-level noise significantly improves zero-shot transfer for intent classification tasks
- 100% stacked composition shows best average performance across language pairs
- Method achieves near-perfect transfer for closely-related dialects (German→Swiss German)
- Less effective for semantic tasks (topic identification, sentiment analysis) and distant language pairs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Character-level noise makes BERT more robust to minor orthographic variations between closely-related languages/dialects. By randomly inserting, deleting, replacing, or swapping characters during fine-tuning, the model learns to map semantically similar tokens with slightly different spellings (e.g., "sonnig" vs "sunnig") to the same semantic representation. This works when surface-level orthographic differences are more frequent than deep semantic differences.

### Mechanism 2
Stacked composition of fine-tuning data with different noise types improves generalization by exposing the model to multiple variations of the same semantic content. Including separate copies of the fine-tuning data, each with a different type of character-level noise, allows the model to build robustness to various orthographic variations that may appear in the target language.

### Mechanism 3
Higher levels of character-level noise (up to 100%) are more effective at promoting cross-lingual transfer than lower levels. Increasing the proportion of words that are noised during fine-tuning forces the model to rely less on exact token matches and more on semantic context, improving its ability to handle unseen orthographic variations.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Monolingual BERT model -> Character-level noise function -> Classification layer -> Task output
- Critical path: Fine-tune BERT on source language task data with character-level noise -> Evaluate on target languages without additional training
- Design tradeoffs: Higher noise levels and stacked composition improve performance but increase computational cost; zero-shot approach limits transfer scope compared to multilingual models
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on target languages with low lexical overlap and long OOV tokens; ineffective for tasks requiring deep semantic understanding
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune German BERT on German intent classification data with 100% character-level noise using stacked composition, then evaluate on Swiss German and South Tyrolean dialects
  2. Fine-tune Italian BERT on Italian intent classification data with 50% character-level noise using joint composition, then evaluate on Neapolitan dialect and German
  3. Fine-tune Romanian BERT on Romanian topic identification data with 75% character-level noise using stacked composition, then evaluate on Moldavian dialect

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do different types of character-level noise (insertion, deletion, replacement, swapping) individually contribute to cross-lingual transfer performance, and would a weighted combination of these noise types outperform the equal-proportion approach used in this study? The paper uses equal proportions of all noise types in joint composition and treats each type separately in stacked composition, but doesn't investigate individual effectiveness or test weighted combinations.

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal noise level for cross-lingual transfer across different language pairs, and how does this optimal level vary based on factors like lexical overlap, average OOV token length, and task complexity? While 100% noise showed best average performance for intent classification, the relationship between noise level and these factors wasn't systematically explored.

### Open Question 3
How would incorporating target language data in pre-training or fine-tuning stages affect the performance of character-level noise for cross-lingual transfer, and could this hybrid approach outperform strictly zero-shot methods? The study focuses exclusively on zero-shot transfer and doesn't explore how small amounts of target language data might enhance character-level noise techniques.

## Limitations
- Effectiveness highly dependent on lexical overlap and OOV token length
- Limited to zero-shot transfer, not comparing with hybrid approaches using target data
- Less effective for tasks requiring deep semantic understanding

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Character-level noise improves zero-shot transfer for intent classification when lexical overlap is high and OOV tokens are short, with consistent experimental results across multiple language pairs.

**Medium Confidence**: Stacked composition outperforms joint composition by approximately 4 points for German and 1 point for Italian intent classification, though the difference is modest and task-dependent.

**Low Confidence**: The assertion that higher noise levels universally lead to better performance ("the more, the better"), as this relationship wasn't monotonic across all configurations.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-task validation**: Apply the character-level noise approach to additional sentence-level tasks beyond the three studied to determine if the pattern of surface-level tasks benefiting more than semantic tasks holds across a broader range of NLP problems.

2. **Alternative noise types**: Experiment with different character-level noise operations (such as character substitution with similar-looking characters, or more sophisticated orthographic variations) to test whether the four noise types used are optimal or whether other noise patterns might yield better cross-lingual transfer performance.

3. **Tokenization sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary the underlying tokenizer (using different subword segmentation approaches or character-level models) to determine how sensitive the approach is to the specific tokenization scheme, and whether the lexical overlap metric meaningfully predicts performance across different tokenization strategies.
