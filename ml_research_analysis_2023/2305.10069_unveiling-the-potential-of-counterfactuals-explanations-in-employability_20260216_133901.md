# Unveiling the Potential of Counterfactuals Explanations in Employability

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10069
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10069
- Reference count: 40
- This paper demonstrates how counterfactual explanations outperform LIME and SHAP for identifying minimal feature changes in employability prediction tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the application of counterfactual explanations to employability decisions using real-world data from a Belgian employment agency. The authors demonstrate that counterfactuals can enhance decision support, comply with legal requirements, guide controlled changes, and provide novel analytical insights. Through systematic comparison with LIME and SHAP, they show counterfactuals are superior at identifying the smallest subset of relevant features needed to change a prediction. The work addresses the growing need for interpretable AI in employment contexts where decisions significantly impact people's lives.

## Method Summary
The authors use a regression model (CatBoost) trained on over 12 million job listings from the Belgian VDAB employment agency. They wrap this regression model in a binary classification framework by setting a threshold for "high job reach." The SEDC algorithm (greedy best-first search) generates counterfactual explanations by finding minimal feature changes that flip predictions. They compare SEDC's performance against LIME and SHAP using train/test splits (3/1) with 3-fold cross-validation for parameter tuning. The approach assumes binary skill features (present/absent) and optimizes for sparse counterfactuals that identify the smallest feature subsets for class change.

## Key Results
- SEDC outperforms LIME and SHAP in identifying minimal feature subsets needed to change employability predictions
- Aggregated counterfactuals reveal actionable skill insights that better match employer needs than top-requested skills
- Counterfactual explanations effectively enhance transparency and interpretability in AI-based hiring recommendations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Counterfactual explanations are superior to LIME and SHAP for identifying the smallest subset of relevant features for a class change in employability tasks.
- Mechanism: The SEDC algorithm directly optimizes for minimal feature changes that flip the prediction class, whereas LIME and SHAP rank feature importance but do not guarantee minimality for class change.
- Core assumption: The problem is binary (high job reach vs not) and the features are binary skills, making minimal subset search tractable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "counterfactuals are superior in identifying the smallest subset of relevant features for a class change"
  - [section] Table 1 shows SEDC achieves class change with fewer features than LIME/SHAP
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; corpus is unrelated to employability/counterfactual comparison
- Break Condition: If features are continuous or non-binary, the minimal subset search may become computationally infeasible or the notion of "minimal" may change.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Counterfactual explanations can enhance trust and transparency in AI-based hiring recommendations.
- Mechanism: By showing users which skills to acquire (or remove) to change the hiring recommendation, counterfactuals align with human intuitive reasoning ("if-only" explanations), making the AI decision process more comprehensible.
- Core assumption: Users prefer explanations that resemble their own causal reasoning and can act on the suggested changes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "counterfactual explanations are known to give simple, short, and comprehensible justifications"
  - [section] "counterfactual explanations can effectively address these problems in job recommendations due to their already-mentioned properties: they are easy to comprehend, operate at the instance level, can be applied to any prediction model, and permit customization"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; corpus is unrelated to trust/explainability in hiring
- Break Condition: If the model is unreliable or the counterfactual suggestions are unrealistic, providing explanations may actually reduce trust or mislead users.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Aggregating counterfactual explanations across many CVs reveals actionable insights about which skills most improve employability.
- Mechanism: Each counterfactual identifies missing skills that would flip a candidate's job reach prediction; aggregating these shows which skills are most frequently needed to improve outcomes.
- Core assumption: The aggregate frequency of suggested skill additions correlates with overall market demand and candidate skill gaps.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Figure 4 depicts the most cited competencies in numerous counterfactual explanations... this chart identifies which skills, if obtained, will contribute the most to changing the classification"
  - [section] Table 2 compares counterfactual-derived skill sets to top-requested skills by employers, showing counterfactuals find more effective sets
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; corpus is unrelated to skill aggregation analysis
- Break Condition: If the model is biased or the data is not representative, the aggregated counterfactuals may reflect model artifacts rather than true skill gaps.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data ingestion (VDAB job/skill dataset) → Preprocessing (binary skill columns, train/test split) → Model training (CatBoost regression) → Thresholding (define high job reach) → Counterfactual generation (SEDC) → Evaluation (sparsity, speed, comparison with LIME/SHAP)
- Critical path: Feature preprocessing → Model training → Counterfactual generation → Result aggregation and analysis
- Design tradeoffs: Using regression with threshold vs. direct classification (more flexible thresholds but requires extra step); SEDC for binary features (fast, minimal changes) vs. general-purpose methods (broader applicability but less sparse)
- Failure signatures: High counterfactual generation time (>90s per instance); low sparsity (many features changed); counterfactuals not leading to class change; model performance degradation after threshold adjustment
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run SEDC on a small sample (e.g., 100 CVs) and verify average feature changes are low and generation time is acceptable.
  2. Compare SEDC-generated counterfactuals to LIME/SHAP on the same sample to confirm SEDC finds smaller minimal sets for class change.
  3. Aggregate counterfactuals from a larger sample (e.g., 1000 CVs) and identify the top 10 most frequently suggested skills; compare with employer-requested skills.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different counterfactual generation methods (e.g., SEDC, FACE, ALIBI) perform across various use cases like bias detection, decision support, and analytics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that there is great variation in how counterfactuals can be generated and that multiple implementation strategies are described in literature. They suggest that future researchers could examine how these differences affect results for particular use cases.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on one counterfactual method (SEDC) and compares it to LIME and SHAP. It does not systematically compare multiple counterfactual generation methods across different use cases.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive benchmarking study comparing multiple counterfactual generation methods across various use cases using standardized datasets and evaluation metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we balance the trade-off between trustworthiness and critical thinking when providing counterfactual explanations to users?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss how increased trustworthiness from explanations can negatively affect decision interpretation, as justifications may induce users to place too much trust in models that are not wholly correct. They note that prudence and critical thinking may be essential when explanations are given.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies this as a challenge but does not provide concrete solutions for balancing trust with critical evaluation of model decisions.
- What evidence would resolve it: User studies comparing different explanation presentation methods that measure both trust levels and critical evaluation of recommendations, potentially exploring interface designs that encourage appropriate skepticism.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we effectively incorporate causal relationships and actionability constraints into counterfactual explanations for guidance applications?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss how counterfactuals must be actionable for guidance purposes and note the challenge of considering causal relationships between features. They mention existing methods like FACE and ALIBI but note that manual definition of changes can be exhaustive.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the importance of actionability and causal relationships, it does not provide a comprehensive solution for automatically incorporating these constraints into counterfactual generation.
- What evidence would resolve it: A method that successfully integrates causal inference techniques with counterfactual generation to produce truly actionable explanations, validated through user studies showing improved decision outcomes.

## Limitations

- The paper relies on a single dataset from a specific regional labor market, limiting generalizability to other employment contexts or countries.
- The binary nature of skills features may oversimplify real-world skill acquisition pathways and continuous skill levels.
- The comparison with LIME and SHAP is limited to feature subset minimality and does not assess other important dimensions like stability or user comprehension.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The mechanism that SEDC directly optimizes for minimal feature changes to flip class predictions, distinguishing it from LIME/SHAP's feature importance ranking, is well-supported by the described algorithm and experimental results.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that counterfactual explanations enhance trust and transparency is supported by theoretical arguments and the intuitive nature of counterfactuals, but lacks direct user studies or empirical evidence of improved decision-making or satisfaction.
- **Medium confidence**: The aggregated counterfactuals revealing actionable skill insights is plausible given the methodology, but the comparison to employer-requested skills is indirect and may conflate model artifacts with genuine market demand.
