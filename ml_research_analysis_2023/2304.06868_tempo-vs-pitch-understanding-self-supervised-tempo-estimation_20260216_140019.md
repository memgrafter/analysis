# Tempo vs. Pitch: understanding self-supervised tempo estimation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.06868
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06868
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Fourier tempograms outperform autocorrelation and hybrid tempograms for self-supervised tempo estimation, with better calibration results when trained on data distributions centered around lower tempi.

## Executive Summary
This paper adapts a self-supervised pitch estimation framework to tempo estimation using tempograms. The authors explore different tempogram formulations (Fourier, autocorrelation, hybrid) and tempo distributions to understand how these choices affect model performance. Through synthetic metronome experiments, they demonstrate that Fourier tempograms lead to more consistent calibration curves and that models trained on lower-tempo-centered distributions (around 70 bpm) perform better than those trained on higher-tempo distributions due to the logarithmic nature of the tempogram representation.

## Method Summary
The method adapts a self-supervised convolutional autoencoder framework from pitch to tempo estimation. The model takes 128-dimensional slices from tempograms (Fourier, autocorrelation, or hybrid) and learns to estimate relative tempo changes through comparing shifted slices. During training, two random slices are extracted from the same tempogram with different vertical shifts, and the model learns to predict the relative tempo difference. The loss function combines a relative tempo error term (Huber norm) and reconstruction loss, with the relative tempo loss weighted much higher. The model is calibrated using synthetic metronome data to map outputs to BPM values.

## Key Results
- Fourier tempograms show more consistent calibration results across different tempo distributions compared to autocorrelation and hybrid tempograms
- Models trained on data distributions centered around lower tempi (70 bpm) perform better than those trained on higher-tempo distributions (170 bpm)
- Higher-tempo-centered distributions cause model saturation, where the model assigns the same output to a wide range of unseen tempi
- The hybrid tempogram performs worse than both Fourier and autocorrelation tempograms

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The Fourier tempogram provides more consistent calibration curves because it better preserves the harmonic structure of tempo salience maps without introducing sub-harmonics. The Fourier tempogram analysis method tends to capture tempo periodicity in a way that aligns with the logarithmic perception of tempo changes, helping the model learn more stable mappings between tempo and output values.

### Mechanism 2
Models trained on data distributions centered around lower tempi (70 bpm) perform better because the logarithmic tempogram representation compresses higher tempo ranges, effectively reducing the usable tempo range for the model. Lower-tempo-centered distributions provide a broader effective range for the model to learn from.

### Mechanism 3
The self-supervised framework works by training the model to predict relative tempo changes through comparing shifted slices of the tempogram, which captures the intrinsic temporal structure of the music without requiring labeled data. The vertical shift in tempogram slices corresponds to artificial tempo changes that the model can learn to detect and quantify.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Tempogram computation → logarithmic preprocessing → slice extraction with shifts → encoder processing → relative tempo prediction → loss computation → weight update
- Critical path: Tempogram computation → logarithmic preprocessing → slice extraction with shifts → encoder processing → relative tempo prediction → loss computation → weight update
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fourier vs. autocorrelation tempograms: Fourier tempograms provide cleaner harmonic structure but may miss sub-harmonic information; autocorrelation tempograms capture more complete harmonic information but introduce more variability.
  - Data distribution choice: Lower-tempo-centered distributions provide better effective resolution in the logarithmic representation but may not cover the full range of musical tempi.
  - Loss function weighting: The relative tempo loss is weighted much higher than reconstruction loss, prioritizing tempo estimation over perfect tempogram reconstruction.
- Failure signatures:
  - Calibration curves with high variance indicate poor model performance and suggest issues with the tempogram representation or data distribution.
  - Saturated outputs for out-of-range tempi indicate that the model was trained on distributions that were too narrow or centered at inappropriate tempi.
  - Poor performance on real data compared to synthetic data suggests that the model is overfitting to the simplified synthetic data or that the tempogram representation is not capturing sufficient information from real music signals.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train the model with Fourier tempogram on synthetic data with log-normal distribution centered at 70 bpm, then evaluate calibration curves to establish a baseline for comparison.
  2. Train the model with autocorrelation tempogram on the same data distribution to compare calibration curves and identify the impact of tempogram representation.
  3. Train the model with Fourier tempogram on synthetic data with log-normal distribution centered at 170 bpm to test the hypothesis about lower-tempo distributions performing better and observe saturation effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the model performance change when trained on real-world tempo distributions that have multiple peaks and valleys, rather than the synthetic log-normal distributions tested? The authors note that their synthetic data experiments used log-normal distributions centered at different tempi, but real-world distributions like GTZAN have more complex structures.

### Open Question 2
Would incorporating sub-harmonic information from the autocorrelation tempogram improve model performance, or is the Fourier tempogram's harmonic-only representation truly optimal? The authors found the hybrid tempogram performed worse than Fourier alone, but don't explore why or whether specific sub-harmonic features could be beneficial.

### Open Question 3
How does the model's ability to generalize to unseen tempi depend on the tempo resolution (Q parameter) and range of shifts (k1, k2) used during training? The authors set Q=40 and shift range [11,18] based on informal testing, but don't systematically explore how these hyperparameters affect generalization.

### Open Question 4
Can the self-supervised framework be extended to handle tempo changes within a single piece, rather than assuming a constant tempo throughout? The authors note that the model processes tempogram slices and could theoretically handle varying tempi, but all experiments use constant-tempo synthetic data.

## Limitations
- Results are primarily based on synthetic metronome data, which may not generalize to real-world music with complex rhythmic patterns
- The study doesn't explore real-world tempo distributions or how they affect model performance
- Limited exploration of hyperparameter sensitivity, particularly for Q and shift range settings

## Confidence
- Fourier tempogram superiority: Medium
- Logarithmic representation limiting high-tempo performance: Medium
- Self-supervised framework effectiveness: Medium
