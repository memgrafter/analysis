# Scalable Causal Discovery with Score Matching

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.03382
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03382

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces DAS (Discovery At Scale), a novel approach for scalable causal discovery from observational data in non-linear additive Gaussian noise models. The method extends prior work by recovering the full causal graph from the second derivative of the log-likelihood, eliminating the need for an expensive pruning step. DAS leverages the score function and its Jacobian to identify edges and their directions efficiently, addressing the computational bottleneck in high-dimensional causal discovery.

The primary contribution is achieving competitive accuracy with state-of-the-art methods while significantly reducing computational time. The pruning-free approach is a key innovation that allows for principled and scalable causal discovery, making it feasible to apply causal inference to larger datasets. The method demonstrates strong performance on both synthetic and real-world datasets, significantly reducing the computational burden for high-dimensional causal discovery.

## Method Summary
DAS (Discovery At Scale) is a novel causal discovery algorithm that extends prior work by recovering the full causal graph from the second derivative of the log-likelihood. The method eliminates the expensive pruning step required by previous approaches by directly identifying edges and their directions using the score function and its Jacobian. This score matching approach enables efficient computation of the causal structure without iterative pruning, making it scalable to high-dimensional datasets.

The algorithm works by first computing the score function (gradient of log-likelihood) and then its Jacobian to identify causal relationships. By leveraging these second-order derivatives, DAS can determine both the presence and direction of edges in the causal graph simultaneously. This approach avoids the computational complexity of iterative pruning while maintaining accuracy, achieving a reduction from O(n³) to O(n²) complexity.

## Key Results
- DAS achieves competitive accuracy with state-of-the-art methods on synthetic data
- Runtime improvements exceed an order of magnitude compared to existing approaches
- Strong performance demonstrated on both synthetic and real-world datasets
- Successful elimination of pruning step while maintaining accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DAS works by leveraging the mathematical properties of score matching and the relationship between the score function's Jacobian and causal structure. In additive noise models, the causal structure induces specific patterns in the second derivatives of the log-likelihood. By computing these second derivatives directly, DAS can identify both edges and their directions without iterative refinement. The pruning-free approach is possible because the score matching formulation naturally separates true causal edges from spurious correlations through the structure of the second-order derivatives.

## Foundational Learning

**Score Function**: The gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to the data. Why needed: It captures the sensitivity of the model to data perturbations. Quick check: Verify that the score function is computed correctly for simple distributions.

**Jacobian of Score Function**: The matrix of second derivatives of the log-likelihood. Why needed: It contains information about the causal structure through its eigenvectors. Quick check: Ensure the Jacobian is positive semi-definite for Gaussian distributions.

**Additive Noise Models**: Causal models where each variable is a function of its parents plus independent noise. Why needed: They provide the theoretical foundation for causal discovery from observational data. Quick check: Confirm that the noise terms are indeed independent across variables.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Data -> Score Function Computation -> Jacobian Computation -> Eigenvalue Decomposition -> Edge Identification -> Direction Determination -> Full Causal Graph

**Critical Path**: The computation of the score function and its Jacobian forms the critical path, as these steps determine both the presence and direction of edges. The eigenvalue decomposition is also computationally intensive but can be parallelized.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The method trades some theoretical guarantees for computational efficiency by using second-order score matching instead of iterative pruning. This introduces potential sensitivity to noise in the score estimates but gains significant speed improvements.

**Failure Signatures**: 
- High variance in score estimates leading to incorrect edge identification
- Numerical instability in Jacobian computation for small sample sizes
- Poor performance when additive noise assumptions are violated
- Degraded accuracy for highly non-linear causal relationships
