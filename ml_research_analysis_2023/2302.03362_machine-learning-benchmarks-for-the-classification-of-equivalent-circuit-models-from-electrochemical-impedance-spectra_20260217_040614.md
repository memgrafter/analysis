# Machine Learning Benchmarks for the Classification of Equivalent Circuit Models from Electrochemical Impedance Spectra

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03362
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03362
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Gradient-boosted trees with tsfresh features achieve 0.51 weighted F1-score for ECM classification from EIS data.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of automatically classifying equivalent circuit models (ECMs) from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data, a task traditionally requiring expert knowledge. The authors benchmark several machine learning approaches including random forests, gradient-boosted trees with time-series features, and convolutional neural networks. The best-performing model uses tsfresh-extracted features with XGBoost, achieving a weighted F1-score of 0.51. The study reveals that similar ECMs are difficult to distinguish and highlights label uncertainty as a key challenge, while publishing the dataset and code for future research.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates three machine learning approaches on a dataset of 9,300 labeled EIS spectra: (1) Random forest using raw impedance data arranged in matrices, (2) XGBoost with time-series features automatically extracted by the tsfresh library from impedance magnitude and phase, and (3) CNN using grayscale Nyquist plot images. All models use an 80/20 train/test split and are evaluated using weighted and unweighted F1-scores and recall metrics. The tsfresh-XGBoost approach treats impedance spectra as multivariate time series, using log-transformed frequency as a proxy for time.

## Key Results
- XGBoost with tsfresh features achieves the best performance with 0.51 weighted F1-score on test data.
- Random forests using raw spectral data provide strong baseline performance.
- CNNs using Nyquist plot images show lower performance, likely due to loss of frequency and scale information.
- Models struggle particularly with distinguishing between L-R-nRCPE and nRCPE circuit groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Machine learning can classify EIS spectra into equivalent circuit models by extracting features from the impedance data.
- Mechanism: Time-series feature extraction (via tsfresh) transforms impedance magnitude and phase as a function of frequency into statistical descriptors that feed a gradient-boosted tree to learn decision boundaries between ECM classes.
- Core assumption: The number and shape of impedance peaks, and their frequency-dependent behavior, are distinctive enough to separate ECM classes.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states tsfresh-XGBoost is best-performing; section describes treating EIS as multivariate time series using log frequency.
- Break condition: If frequency range is too narrow or spectra too similar, extracted features lose discriminative power.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: CNNs can classify ECMs by learning visual patterns in Nyquist plots.
- Mechanism: Nyquist plots rendered as grayscale images; CNN learns spatial filters mapping pixel patterns to ECM classes.
- Core assumption: Visual similarity in Nyquist plots correlates with underlying ECM class; CNN can detect subtle structural differences.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions CNN with boolean Nyquist images; section describes simple network based on MNIST architecture.
- Break condition: If images lose scale or frequency information, CNN cannot distinguish classes differing mainly in magnitude or temporal order.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Random forests using raw impedance data can serve as strong baseline for ECM classification.
- Mechanism: Raw impedance values (real and imaginary parts) arranged into matrices; random forest learns to split on these values to assign ECM labels.
- Core assumption: Raw impedance values contain enough discriminative information to separate ECM classes without preprocessing.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions random forest using raw spectral data; section notes traditional methods don't perform well due to nonlinearity.
- Break condition: If raw spectra are too noisy or similar across ECMs, random forests will overfit or misclassify.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: EIS data -> Preprocessing (frequency interpolation to 30 log-spaced points) -> Feature extraction (tsfresh/RF matrix/CNN image) -> Model training (XGBoost/RF/CNN) -> Evaluation (weighted F1-score, confusion matrix, SHAP values)

- Critical path: 1) Load and preprocess EIS data, 2) Choose classification method (tsfresh-XGBoost recommended), 3) Train model on 80% of data, 4) Evaluate on held-out 20%, 5) Analyze confusion matrix and SHAP values for insights

- Design tradeoffs: Feature engineering increases interpretability but may lose raw spectral detail; CNN captures spatial patterns but loses frequency information unless explicitly encoded; Random Forest is robust but less accurate than XGBoost

- Failure signatures: High confusion between similar ECMs suggests feature insufficiency; low overall F1-score may indicate class overlap or label uncertainty; CNN performance plateau suggests Nyquist plot resolution or frequency encoding is inadequate

- First 3 experiments: 1) Train tsfresh-XGBoost on full training set; inspect confusion matrix, 2) Remove outlier spectra and retrain; compare SHAP feature rankings, 3) Try CNN with color-coded frequency in Nyquist plots; evaluate if accuracy improves

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can unsupervised machine learning approaches generate candidate ECMs without relying on human-labeled training data?
- Basis in paper: The paper notes the current supervised problem is limited to predicting nine ECM types due to bias in labeled data, suggesting unsupervised generation of candidate ECMs would be more useful.
- Why unresolved: Paper focused on supervised classification; unsupervised approaches were mentioned as future work but not implemented.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of unsupervised clustering or generative models that propose novel ECM structures from impedance data, compared against expert-defined models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can frequency information be effectively incorporated into CNN architectures for EIS classification?
- Basis in paper: Paper tested CNN with colored images encoding frequency information but found no significant improvement, noting this as open question.
- Why unresolved: Current CNN architecture cannot account for frequency information; uncertain whether CNNs can outperform other approaches even with more data and optimized architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful CNN models achieving better classification performance than current approaches by effectively incorporating frequency information through novel architectural designs.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can human bias in ECM labeling be quantified and corrected in machine learning models?
- Basis in paper: Paper identifies uncertainty and human bias in labels as key remaining challenge, noting different experts might choose different ECMs for same impedance spectrum.
- Why unresolved: Paper acknowledges this as challenge but doesn't provide solutions; no established method for quantifying or correcting this type of bias in the domain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Methods measuring label uncertainty or bias in training data demonstrating improved model performance when corrections are applied.

## Limitations

- Label uncertainty and human bias in the training data significantly impact model performance, particularly for similar ECMs.
- The CNN approach loses critical frequency and scale information when converting spectra to Nyquist images, limiting its effectiveness.
- The dataset size (9,300 spectra) may not capture full diversity of real-world EIS data, especially for rare or borderline cases.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Overall workflow and superiority of tsfresh-XGBoost over CNN and raw random forest are well-supported by experimental results.
- **Medium Confidence**: Claim that CNNs are less effective due to loss of scale and frequency information is plausible but not definitively proven.
- **Low Confidence**: Assertion that label uncertainty is main cause of confusion between certain ECM pairs is speculative; other factors may also contribute.

## Next Checks

1. **Label Quality Audit**: Manually inspect a random sample of spectra where the model is most uncertain or misclassifies, comparing model predictions to human labels to quantify label noise and bias.

2. **Cross-Dataset Generalization**: Evaluate the trained tsfresh-XGBoost model on an independent EIS dataset (if available) to assess robustness to different measurement conditions or battery chemistries.

3. **Feature Ablation Study**: Systematically remove or modify individual tsfresh features to identify which features are most critical for correct classification and whether certain features introduce bias.
