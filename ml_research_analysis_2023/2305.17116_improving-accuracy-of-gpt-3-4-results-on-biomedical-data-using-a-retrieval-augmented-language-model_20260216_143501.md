# Improving accuracy of GPT-3/4 results on biomedical data using a retrieval-augmented language model

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.17116
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17116
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Retrieval-augmented model outperformed GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Bing on biomedical accuracy and relevance for DLBCL questions

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated four large language models—GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bing's Prometheus, and a retrieval-augmented (RetA) model—on biomedical questions about diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). The RetA model, trained on 1,868 PubMed Central articles, achieved superior accuracy and relevance scores compared to the baseline models. While GPT-4 maintained the highest readability scores, the retrieval-augmented approach demonstrated fewer hallucinations and better domain-specific performance. The findings suggest that domain-specific training corpora can improve accuracy and relevance for biomedical applications.

## Method Summary
The study compared four LLMs—GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bing's Prometheus, and a retrieval-augmented model—on 19 questions about diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. The RetA model was trained on 1,868 PubMed Central articles and evaluated across three metrics: accuracy, relevance, and readability. Human assessors scored each model's responses using a structured rubric. The evaluation focused on comparing performance across these models while controlling for question difficulty and disease specificity.

## Key Results
- RetA model achieved highest accuracy (12/19 top scores, 47 total) and relevance (13/19, 50 total)
- GPT-4 scored highest in readability (17/19, 55 total) but lower accuracy (8/19, 43 total)
- Bing's Prometheus underperformed across all metrics
- RetA generated fewer hallucinations than GPT-3.5 and GPT-4

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The retrieval-augmented model demonstrates improved performance through access to domain-specific knowledge that grounds responses in verified biomedical literature. By retrieving relevant passages from a curated corpus of PubMed Central articles, the model can generate answers that align with established medical knowledge rather than relying solely on general training data. This approach reduces the likelihood of hallucinations by providing verifiable source material for each response.

## Foundational Learning
- **Domain-specific corpora**: Why needed - Biomedical language contains specialized terminology and context; Quick check - Verify corpus covers relevant subspecialties
- **Retrieval mechanisms**: Why needed - Enables grounding in source documents; Quick check - Test retrieval precision/recall on held-out questions
- **Hallucination metrics**: Why needed - Critical for evaluating reliability in medical applications; Quick check - Compare hallucination rates across models on same questions

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: PubMed Corpus -> Retrieval Engine -> RetA Model -> Response Generator
- **Critical path**: Question -> Retrieval -> Context Selection -> Answer Generation -> Evaluation
- **Design tradeoffs**: Domain specificity vs. generalization, retrieval latency vs. accuracy, corpus size vs. coverage
- **Failure signatures**: Poor retrieval precision, context misinterpretation, hallucination persistence despite retrieval
- **First experiments**: 1) Test retrieval accuracy on sample questions, 2) Evaluate hallucination rates on controlled prompts, 3) Compare performance across different corpus sizes

None identified in the provided material.

## Limitations
- Small question set (n=19) focused solely on one disease entity (DLBCL)
- Single disease focus limits generalizability to other biomedical domains
- Human scoring introduces subjective measurement uncertainty

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: RetA model achieved numerically higher accuracy and relevance scores compared to baseline LLMs on this specific dataset
- **Medium confidence**: Domain-specific training corpora reduce hallucinations relative to general-purpose models
- **Low confidence**: Claims about generalizability across biomedical domains or superiority over all other RAG approaches

## Next Checks
1. Test the RetA model on a larger, multi-disease biomedical question set (minimum 100 questions spanning 5+ disease areas) to assess generalizability
2. Compare hallucination rates using automated factuality checking against source documents rather than human scoring alone
3. Evaluate performance against other RAG architectures using identical biomedical corpora and evaluation criteria to isolate the effect of the retrieval mechanism itself
