# Asymptotically Unbiased Off-Policy Policy Evaluation when Reusing Old Data in Nonstationary Environments

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.11725
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11725
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: Introduces regression-assisted doubly robust estimator for OPE in nonstationary environments that is asymptotically unbiased and provides valid confidence intervals when reusing past data

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of off-policy policy evaluation (OPE) in nonstationary environments where reward functions change over time. The core problem is how to reuse past data without introducing large bias while maintaining valid confidence intervals. The authors propose the regression-assisted doubly robust (Reg) estimator, which incorporates past data through proxy values (past reward predictions) and applies a regression approach to reduce variance further. This method is asymptotically unbiased and unifies several existing OPE approaches through different choices of feature vectors and coefficients. The approach is validated on semi-synthetic YouTube data and real-world MovieLens data, showing superior performance compared to sliding-window variants of existing estimators.

## Method Summary
The regression-assisted doubly robust estimator combines importance sampling with regression techniques inspired by survey sampling methods. It uses past data by incorporating past reward predictions as auxiliary variables, then applies a regression framework to correct for nonstationarity. The estimator works by estimating coefficients that relate past reward predictions to current rewards, then using these coefficients to adjust the doubly robust estimate. This approach achieves asymptotic unbiasedness even when using data from different time periods with varying reward functions. The method also provides a consistent variance estimator that accounts for the estimation error in coefficients, enabling valid confidence interval construction.

## Key Results
- Reg estimator outperforms sliding-window variants of IS, WIS, and DM estimators in both estimating current policy values and predicting future values
- Reg provides tight and valid confidence intervals with proper coverage rates, while sliding-window estimators fail to maintain coverage even with small window sizes
- The approach is extended to finite-horizon reinforcement learning with similar improvements in performance
- Theoretical analysis proves asymptotic unbiasedness and provides a consistent variance estimator

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal window size B for different nonstationary environments and how does it depend on the rate of change?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the bias-variance tradeoff when choosing B, noting that sliding window estimators are sensitive to B while Reg is robust to B.
- Why unresolved: The experiments show Reg performs well across different B values, but the theoretical understanding of how B should be chosen based on environment characteristics is missing.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis deriving optimal B values as a function of the nonstationarity rate, or extensive empirical results across environments with varying rates of change.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the regression-assisted DR estimator perform when the context distribution is also nonstationary (not just rewards)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "We consider a stationary context distribution to present the paper succinctly, however, our methods described in the paper are applicable to the settings where the context distribution is also changing."
- Why unresolved: The theoretical analysis and experiments all assume stationary context distributions, leaving open how performance changes when this assumption is violated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results showing performance degradation or theoretical bounds showing robustness to nonstationary context distributions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the sample complexity required for the variance estimator to be consistent and for the asymptotic normality result to hold?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proves asymptotic unbiasedness and consistent variance estimation, but doesn't specify how large n needs to be for these results to be practically useful.
- Why unresolved: The proofs show these properties hold asymptotically but don't provide finite-sample guarantees or guidance on required sample sizes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Finite-sample concentration bounds for the variance estimator or empirical studies showing at what n values the asymptotic properties become practically relevant.

## Limitations
- Nonstationarity Assumptions: Theoretical guarantees rely on bounded nonstationarity rates and certain smoothness conditions; rapid or discontinuous changes could violate assumptions
- Model Specification Risk: Assumes linear relationships between past predictions and current rewards; poor feature selection could introduce significant bias
- Computational Overhead: Requires maintaining and processing past data, building prediction models, and computing regression coefficients, though costs aren't quantified

## Confidence
- Asymptotic Unbiasedness: High confidence - supported by formal proof in Section 5 with clear mathematical conditions
- Variance Estimator Consistency: High confidence - proven consistency with weighted residual technique, though empirical validation is limited
- Empirical Performance Superiority: Medium confidence - strong results on two datasets but limited to specific nonstationarity patterns
