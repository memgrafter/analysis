# Spiking Network Initialisation and Firing Rate Collapse

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.08879
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08879
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 32
- This paper addresses the problem of optimal weight initialization for spiking neural networks (SNNs), which is crucial for avoiding vanishing/exploding gradients and ensuring proper firing rates in deep networks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical problem of firing rate collapse in spiking neural networks caused by discrete-time simulation of continuous spiking dynamics. The authors identify that neurons can fail to spike even when they should according to continuous-time theory, due to the assumption that all input spikes arrive simultaneously. They propose theoretical corrections using random walk and Wiener process models to compute expected firing rates, and derive optimal weight initialization strategies that combine variance propagation techniques with these firing rate estimation methods. The approach is validated on feedforward SNNs, showing maintained firing rates and stable gradient variances across layers when using the corrections.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a comprehensive approach to SNN initialization that addresses both firing rate collapse and gradient flow stability. They model input spike integration using random walk theory for two symmetric weights, Wiener processes for many spikes with random weights, or random permutation methods for general cases. For gradient flow, they derive expressions for optimal weight variance based on the probability that surrogate gradients are non-zero, which depends on the membrane potential distribution before reset. The combined initialization strategy uses variance propagation from ANNs while computing expected firing rates through diffusion approximation, shot-noise analysis, or threshold integration methods, with corrections for discrete-time simulation effects.

## Key Results
- Identified firing rate collapse in discrete-time SNNs where neurons fail to spike despite continuous-time theory predicting spiking
- Proposed three solutions: random walk (two symmetric weights), Wiener process (many random weights), and random permutation methods
- Derived expressions for optimal weight variance in both forward and backward passes to maintain stable gradient flow
- Validated approach on feedforward SNNs, showing maintained firing rates and stable gradient variances across layers

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The firing rate collapse is caused by the discrete-time simulation assuming all input spikes arrive simultaneously, which fails to capture the stochastic ordering that can cause threshold crossing in continuous time.
- Mechanism: When all input spikes are integrated at once, the only information retained is the net difference of excitatory and inhibitory spikes. However, in continuous time, the order of spike arrivals matters - certain permutations can cause the membrane potential to cross the threshold even when the final integrated value is below threshold.
- Core assumption: The input spike times are independent uniform random variables within each timestep.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "where neurons fail to spike even when they should according to continuous-time theory"
  - [section 3.2] "simulating all input spikes as arriving at the same time is equivalent to constraining the order of spike arrival"
- Break condition: If spike times are not independent or not uniformly distributed within the timestep, the random walk/Wiener process approximations may not hold.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: By modeling the input spike integration as a random walk (for two symmetric weights) or Wiener process (for many spikes with random weights), we can compute the probability that threshold crossing occurs due to spike ordering, even when the final integrated value is below threshold.
- Mechanism: The random walk/Wiener process captures the stochastic evolution of the membrane potential during a timestep. Using results from random walk theory (reflection principle), we can compute the probability that the running maximum of this process exceeds the threshold, which corresponds to the probability of a spike occurring due to ordering effects.
- Core assumption: The number of input spikes per timestep is large enough for the random walk to be approximated by a Wiener process (for the general case).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.4.2] "With a slight abuse of notation we will refer to the random walk X⌊sn⌋ as Xn"
  - [section 3.5.1] "A standard result due to Donsker [26] states that given a sequence of independent identically distributed random variables ξ1,ξ 2,...,ξ n, with mean 0 and variance 1 the random walk: Wn(t) = 1/√n ∑ 1≤k≤⌊tn⌋ ξk, t ∈ [0, 1] converges in distribution to a Wiener process as n→∞"
- Break condition: If the number of input spikes per timestep is too small, the random walk/Wiener process approximation breaks down.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The gradient flow in SNNs can be stabilized by scaling the surrogate gradient function based on the membrane potential distribution before reset, ensuring the variance of gradients remains constant across layers.
- Mechanism: The optimal weight variance for gradient flow depends on the probability that the surrogate gradient is non-zero, which is determined by the membrane potential distribution before reset. By scaling the surrogate function appropriately, we can ensure that the variance of the spike gradients remains constant across layers, preventing vanishing or exploding gradients.
- Core assumption: The surrogate gradient function decreases fast enough so that if H′(v(l+1)i [t])≠0 at a given t it will be close to 0 at nearby steps.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3] "Since the surrogate gradient does not affect the computation of the forward firing rate it is safe to modify it without affecting the forward pass."
  - [section 4.2] "This result is an expression of the variance of the gradients as a function of the variance of the next layer."
- Break condition: If the surrogate gradient function is too wide or too slow to decay, the assumptions about gradient independence may not hold, leading to unstable gradient flow.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input layer (Poisson spike generators) -> Hidden layers (LIF neurons with discrete-time simulation) -> Output layer (LIF neurons with firing rate collapse correction) -> Loss function (Sum of membrane potentials) -> Optimizer (Surrogate gradient descent)

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize weights using the proposed method to ensure proper firing rates and gradient flow.
  2. Simulate network forward pass, applying firing rate collapse correction if necessary.
  3. Compute gradients using surrogate gradient descent.
  4. Update weights using the computed gradients.
  5. Repeat steps 2-4 until convergence.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Firing rate collapse correction: The random walk, Wiener process, and permutation methods each have different assumptions and computational costs. The choice depends on the specific network architecture and desired accuracy.
  - Surrogate gradient scaling: Scaling the surrogate function ensures gradient flow but may require careful tuning to avoid instability.

- Failure signatures:
  - Vanishing/exploding gradients: If the weight variance is not properly initialized or the surrogate gradient is not scaled correctly, the gradients may vanish or explode, preventing learning.
  - Firing rate collapse: If the firing rate collapse correction is not applied or the assumptions of the chosen method are violated, the firing rates may decrease as the network depth increases.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test the firing rate collapse correction on a simple network with homogeneous Poisson inputs and compare the empirical firing rates to the theoretical predictions.
  2. Verify the gradient flow by initializing a deep network with the proposed method and checking that the variance of the gradients remains constant across layers.
  3. Apply the initialization method to a network trained on a real dataset and compare the performance to networks initialized with standard methods.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the firing rate collapse correction impact training dynamics and final accuracy when applied during actual SNN training on real datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the correction adds stochasticity and could require more training steps, and they call for empirical validation on real data
- Why unresolved: The correction was only tested during initialization, not during actual training with real datasets
- What evidence would resolve it: Training experiments on standard vision or temporal datasets comparing networks with and without the correction, measuring convergence speed, final accuracy, and stability

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the maximum depth of SNNs for which the initialization methods can accurately predict the firing rate distribution in the final layer when using real-world inputs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that "there is still the need to develop a method to evaluate how many layers can we stack while somewhat accurately predicting the final rate when using real data instead of simple homogeneous Poisson spikes"
- Why unresolved: All theoretical analysis and experiments used simplified Poisson inputs, not realistic temporal or spatial patterns
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying network depth and input complexity, measuring prediction accuracy vs empirical firing rates in deep networks

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the computational overhead of the firing rate collapse correction methods (random walk, Wiener process, permutation) in practical SNN implementations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss computational complexity theoretically but note "there is still the need to empirically prove their viability on real data"
- Why unresolved: Only theoretical complexity analysis was provided, not empirical measurements of actual runtime overhead
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarks comparing training/inference time with and without corrections across different network sizes and architectures

### Open Question 4
- Question: How robust is the proposed initialization strategy to variations in the surrogate gradient function parameters (Bth) and what are the limits of the assumptions made in the gradient variance analysis?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that assumption 13 (narrow surrogate gradient) is "the most problematic one" and show that larger Bth values lead to worse variance propagation
- Why unresolved: The analysis relies on several assumptions that may not hold in practice, particularly regarding gradient independence and narrowness
- What evidence would resolve it: Sensitivity analysis varying surrogate function parameters, testing initialization performance across a range of gradient functions and widths

## Limitations
- The firing rate collapse correction methods (random walk, Wiener process, permutation) have different assumptions and computational costs that may limit their applicability to general network architectures
- The gradient flow analysis relies on assumptions about surrogate gradient narrowness and gradient independence that may not hold for all practical surrogate functions
- The proposed methods have primarily been validated on feedforward networks, with limited testing on more complex architectures
