# One-Step Distributional Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14421
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14421
- Reference count: 32
- One-line primary result: One-step distributional RL achieves contractive operators for both policy evaluation and control, providing stable and computationally efficient performance comparable to categorical distributional RL.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a one-step distributional reinforcement learning framework that simplifies full distributional approaches by considering only one-step dynamics. Unlike existing distributional methods that struggle with control tasks due to non-contractive operators, the authors show that their one-step approach yields contractive operators for both policy evaluation and control. They propose two algorithms based on Cramér projections and provide almost sure convergence guarantees without requiring the restrictive assumption of unique optimal policies. Experiments on tabular environments and Atari games demonstrate that the proposed method achieves competitive performance compared to categorical distributional RL, while being more stable and computationally efficient.

## Method Summary
The authors develop one-step distributional reinforcement learning by defining Bellman operators that average randomness after only the first transition step, rather than considering the full distribution of returns. They prove these operators are contractive in Wasserstein metrics for both policy evaluation and control, enabling stable learning without requiring unique optimal policies. The method uses Cramér projections to map arbitrary distributions onto a fixed categorical support while preserving the mean. Two algorithms are proposed: a tabular version for policy evaluation and control, and a deep learning variant (OS-C51) that extends the approach to complex environments like Atari games.

## Key Results
- One-step distributional operators Tπ and T are γ-contractions in Wp for 1 ≤ p ≤ ∞ for both policy evaluation and control
- Cramér projection preserves the mean of distributions, ensuring accurate return estimation
- The approach achieves competitive performance on Atari games (BeamRider, Breakout, Pong) compared to categorical distributional RL while being more stable
- Convergence analysis provided without requiring the restrictive assumption of unique optimal policies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: One-step distributional operators are contractive in Wasserstein metrics for both policy evaluation and control, unlike full distributional Bellman operators.
- Mechanism: By averaging the randomness after only the first transition step, the one-step operator reduces problem complexity and avoids non-contractive behavior seen in full distributional control operators.
- Core assumption: The transition kernel P and reward function r are known or can be sampled, and discount factor γ < 1 ensures contractivity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we show that our approach comes with a unified theory for both policy evaluation and control... provide an almost sure convergence analysis."
  - [section 3.2] Proposition 3.1 states one-step operators Tπ and T are γ-contractions in Wp for 1 ≤ p ≤ ∞.
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; claim is theoretically derived within paper.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The Cramér projection preserves the mean of the distribution, ensuring that the projected one-step operator approximates the true return distribution well.
- Mechanism: The Cramér projection ΠC has a mean-preserving property: for any discrete distribution q with support included in interval [z1, zK], then EZ∼ΠC(q)[Z] = EZ∼q[Z]. This ensures projected distribution maintains correct expected return.
- Core assumption: Support of projected distribution must contain true return values, i.e., z1 ≤ r(x,a,x′) + γV(x′) ≤ zK for all triplets (x,a,x′).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.2] Definition 2.3 states Cramér projection and mentions its mean-preserving property (Eq. 2).
  - [section 4.1] "The Cramér projection satisfies an important mean-preserving property: for any discrete distribution q with support included in the interval [z1,zK], then: EZ∼ΠC(q)[Z] = EZ∼q[Z]."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; claim is theoretically derived within paper.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The one-step approach avoids instability issues of full distributional control by not requiring uniqueness of optimal policy.
- Mechanism: By considering only one-step dynamics, the one-step operator's fixed point is simply projection of fixed point of unprojected operator, which is unique and doesn't depend on choice of greedy actions in set arg maxaQ*(x,a).
- Core assumption: One-step operators have unique fixed points, guaranteed by their contractivity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "our approach comes with a unified theory for both policy evaluation and control... without requiring the restrictive assumption of unique optimal policies."
  - [section 4.2] "the convergence analysis of Algorithm 1... does not require the uniqueness of the optimal policy in the case of control."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; claim is theoretically derived within paper.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  MDP environment (states X, actions A, transition kernel P, reward function r, discount factor γ) -> Distribution functions µ(x,a) ∈ Pb(R) for each state-action pair -> One-step distributional operators Tπ and T -> Cramér projection ΠC -> Categorical distributions p1(x,a), ..., pK(x,a) over support z1 < ... < zK -> Algorithms for policy evaluation and control (tabular and deep versions)

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize distribution functions or categorical probabilities
  2. Sample a transition (xt, at, rt, xt+1)
  3. Compute Q-values or categorical targets using one-step operator
  4. Update distribution functions or categorical probabilities using Cramér projection
  5. Repeat until convergence

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of categories K: More categories provide better approximation but increase computational cost
  - Support range [z1, zK]: Must contain true return values for accurate approximation
  - Stepsizes αt(x,a): Must satisfy Robbins-Monro conditions for convergence

- Failure signatures:
  - Divergence of value functions or distributions
  - Oscillations or instability in learning dynamics
  - Poor performance on evaluation tasks or control tasks

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run tabular one-step CDRL algorithm on simple MDP (e.g., two states, two actions) to verify convergence and stability.
  2. Compare performance of one-step CDRL and classic CDRL on Frozen Lake environment.
  3. Implement deep one-step CDRL algorithm (OS-C51) and evaluate on Atari games (e.g., Beamrider, Breakout, Pong).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How would convergence guarantees change if one-step distributional operators were applied to more than one step of environment's dynamics?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] Paper mentions future research could investigate "generalization of our method based on dynamics of several successive steps instead of single one."
  - Why unresolved: Authors only analyze one-step case and explicitly state this as open direction for future work.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis showing convergence rates and conditions for multi-step distributional operators, followed by empirical validation comparing one-step vs multi-step approaches.

- Question: What happens to convergence properties when using different projection methods beyond Cramér projections, such as Wasserstein projections?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] Paper uses Cramér projections throughout but mentions in related work that other projections like Wasserstein-1 have been used in distributional RL.
  - Why unresolved: Authors only analyze Cramér projection case and don't explore how other projection methods would affect theoretical guarantees.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Convergence proofs for one-step distributional operators with different projection methods, plus empirical comparisons showing performance differences.

- Question: How does performance of one-step distributional RL scale with very large state spaces where function approximation becomes necessary?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors mention implementing deep learning version (OS-C51) but only test on Atari games, not exploring scalability to truly massive state spaces.
  - Why unresolved: Theoretical analysis is for tabular cases, and while deep learning implementation provided, they don't analyze how well it scales or what happens to convergence guarantees with function approximation.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies on environments with millions of states, theoretical analysis of how function approximation affects convergence, and investigation of which approximation methods work best with one-step distributional RL.

## Limitations

- Theoretical guarantees apply primarily to tabular settings with small state spaces, with limited analysis of function approximation in large state spaces
- Assumes contractive transition kernels (Assumption 3.1), which may be restrictive in practice
- Claims about computational efficiency improvements lack systematic comparison of runtime or sample complexity
- Empirical validation relies on single implementation without ablation studies or broader benchmarking

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Theoretical contractivity proofs for one-step operators, mean-preserving property of Cramér projection, stability without unique optimal policies
- **Medium confidence**: Empirical performance claims against categorical distributional RL on Atari games (based on single implementation without ablation studies)
- **Low confidence**: Claims about computational efficiency improvements, as paper provides no systematic comparison of runtime or sample complexity

## Next Checks

1. **Contractivity verification**: Test one-step operators on MDPs where transition kernel P is not contractive to verify whether γ-contraction property still holds or breaks down as predicted.

2. **Support sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary support interval [z1, zK] relative to possible return values to quantify how projection error affects convergence and final performance.

3. **Computational overhead measurement**: Implement both one-step CDRL and standard CDRL with identical network architectures and measure wall-clock training time per step to verify claimed efficiency improvements.
