# Benchmarking Large Language Models for News Summarization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.13848
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13848
- Reference count: 12
- Key outcome: Instruction tuning, not model size, is the key to strong zero-shot summarization capability; existing benchmarks underestimate performance due to low-quality reference summaries

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks ten large language models (LLMs) on news summarization tasks using human evaluation. The study finds that instruction tuning, rather than model size, is the primary factor determining zero-shot summarization performance. Through comprehensive human evaluation on CNN/DM and XSUM datasets, the authors reveal that existing reference summaries are of low quality, leading to underestimates of both human and model performance. To address this, they collect high-quality summaries from freelance writers and demonstrate that the best LLM (Instruct Davinci) performs comparably to human writers in terms of faithfulness, coherence, and relevance, despite stylistic differences.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates ten LLMs including GPT-3 variants using zero-shot and five-shot prompting on CNN/DM and XSUM datasets. Human evaluation is conducted through Amazon Mechanical Turk, with three annotators rating summaries on faithfulness (binary), coherence, and relevance (1-5 Likert scale). The authors collect high-quality reference summaries from six freelance writers for 100 test examples from each dataset. Automatic metrics (Rouge-L, METEOR, BertScore, BARTScore, BLEURT) are computed and their correlation with human ratings is analyzed at the system level. A blinded pairwise comparison is conducted between the best LLM and freelance summaries with five additional annotators.

## Key Results
- Instruction-tuned models outperform larger non-instruction-tuned models in zero-shot summarization
- Existing reference summaries are of low quality, leading to underestimates of human and model performance
- Better quality reference summaries significantly improve correlation between automatic metrics and human judgment
- Instruct Davinci performs comparably to human writers on faithfulness, coherence, and relevance metrics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Instruction tuning is the key to strong zero-shot summarization capability, not model size.
- Mechanism: Instruction tuning adapts LLMs to follow task-specific instructions, enabling them to generate summaries without requiring demonstration examples or fine-tuning. This allows smaller models with instruction tuning to outperform much larger models without it.
- Core assumption: The ability to follow instructions is more important for summarization performance than the sheer number of model parameters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we find instruction tuning, and not model size, is the key to the LLM's zero-shot summarization capability."
  - [section 3.2] "Compared to the fine-tuned LMs (e.g. Pegasus), Instruct Davinci achieves higher coherence and relevance scores... In contrast to instruction tuning, we find scale to be less important."
  - [corpus] Found 25 related papers; average neighbor FMR=0.507. Top related titles focus on element-aware summarization, ChatGPT capabilities, and benchmarking, supporting the focus on instruction tuning.
- Break condition: If instruction tuning is applied to a model but it still fails to follow instructions or generate coherent summaries, the mechanism breaks down. This could happen if the instruction tuning data is of poor quality or if the model architecture is fundamentally incompatible with instruction following.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Low-quality reference summaries in current benchmarks lead to underestimates of human performance and lower few-shot and fine-tuning performance.
- Mechanism: When reference summaries are of poor quality, automatic metrics computed against them do not correlate well with human judgment. This leads to models that perform well on these metrics not necessarily performing well in terms of faithfulness, coherence, and relevance. Additionally, models trained on poor-quality data learn to replicate the low quality, further degrading performance.
- Core assumption: The quality of reference summaries directly impacts the effectiveness of both evaluation metrics and the performance of models trained on that data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "existing studies have been limited by low-quality references, leading to underestimates of human performance and lower few-shot and finetuning performance."
  - [section 3.2] "Reference summaries in current benchmarks are extremely low quality... most automatic summarization systems score better than the reference summaries across all three aspects."
  - [section 4.3] "The left plot shows results of computing Rouge-L with existing reference summaries from XSUM, which has negative correlation with human ratings."
- Break condition: If high-quality reference summaries are used and the correlation between automatic metrics and human judgment improves significantly, this mechanism is validated. Conversely, if the correlation remains poor even with high-quality references, other factors may be at play.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Better quality reference summaries improve the correlation between automatic metrics and human judgment.
- Mechanism: When reference summaries are of higher quality, automatic metrics that compare generated summaries to these references become more reliable indicators of summary quality. This is because the references now accurately represent the desired output, making the metrics more meaningful.
- Core assumption: Automatic metrics are only as good as the reference summaries they compare against.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "To address the quality issues of reference summaries... we collect better quality summaries from freelance writers."
  - [section 4.3] "On the right, we see the results of computing Rouge-L with the freelance writer summaries, which leads to a much more positive correlation."
  - [corpus] Related papers focus on element-aware summarization and expert-aligned evaluation, supporting the importance of reference quality.
- Break condition: If even with high-quality reference summaries, automatic metrics still do not correlate well with human judgment, it suggests that the metrics themselves are flawed or that human judgment is too subjective to be captured by automated means.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Zero-shot learning
  - Why needed here: The study evaluates LLMs in a zero-shot setting, where the model generates summaries without any demonstration examples or fine-tuning.
  - Quick check question: Can you explain the difference between zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning in the context of LLMs?

- Concept: Instruction tuning
  - Why needed here: The study identifies instruction tuning as the key factor in LLM summarization success, making it crucial to understand how it works.
  - Quick check question: How does instruction tuning differ from traditional fine-tuning, and why is it particularly effective for tasks like summarization?

- Concept: Automatic evaluation metrics
  - Why needed here: The study evaluates the correlation between automatic metrics (like ROUGE) and human judgment, highlighting the importance of understanding these metrics' strengths and limitations.
  - Quick check question: What are the key differences between ROUGE, METEOR, and BERTScore, and in what scenarios might each be most appropriate?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: LLMs (with varying sizes and pretraining methods) -> Human evaluators -> Automatic evaluation metrics
- Critical path: Generate summaries from LLMs -> Human evaluation on faithfulness, coherence, and relevance -> Compute automatic metrics -> Assess correlation with human judgment
- Design tradeoffs: The study trades off between the accuracy of human evaluation and the scalability of automatic metrics. Human evaluation is more accurate but time-consuming, while automatic metrics are faster but may not always align with human judgment, especially with low-quality reference summaries.
- Failure signatures: Poor correlation between automatic metrics and human judgment, inconsistent human ratings, or LLMs failing to follow instructions despite instruction tuning.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate the same set of summaries using both human evaluation and automatic metrics to assess their correlation.
  2. Compare the performance of instruction-tuned and non-instruction-tuned LLMs in both zero-shot and few-shot settings.
  3. Generate summaries using high-quality reference summaries and re-evaluate the correlation between automatic metrics and human judgment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does instruction tuning on higher-quality summarization data significantly improve the performance of fine-tuned language models compared to their current performance on low-quality training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that current fine-tuned models are trained on low-quality reference summaries, which may underestimate their true performance potential.
- Why unresolved: The paper only benchmarks LLMs on existing low-quality datasets and does not experimentally compare fine-tuned models trained on higher-quality data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a controlled experiment training fine-tuned models on high-quality summaries (like those collected from freelance writers) and comparing their performance to both zero-shot/few-shot LLMs and existing fine-tuned models on the same data.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different learning algorithms for instruction tuning (such as supervised learning vs learning from human feedback) affect the summarization performance of LLMs, and which approach is most effective?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that instruction tuning is key to LLM summarization success but notes that the exact training details are unknown and suggests that learning from human feedback might be particularly effective.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not experimentally compare different instruction tuning methodologies or analyze which specific aspects of the training process contribute most to improved summarization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematically training LLMs using different instruction tuning approaches (supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback, etc.) on the same high-quality summarization data and comparing their performance across multiple evaluation metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does multi-task learning during instruction tuning provide significant benefits for summarization performance, or is task-specific training more effective?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that Instruct Davinci was trained on a diverse distribution of inputs across multiple tasks and suggests that multi-task learning might be important, but does not empirically test this hypothesis.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not conduct controlled experiments comparing multi-task instruction tuning with single-task instruction tuning for summarization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training two versions of the same LLM - one with multi-task instruction tuning including summarization and other NLP tasks, and another with instruction tuning focused solely on summarization - then comparing their performance on news summarization benchmarks.

## Limitations

- The study does not provide detailed analysis of what constitutes "low quality" in reference summaries across different dimensions.
- The impact of prompt engineering on summarization performance is not fully explored, with different prompt formulations potentially affecting results significantly.
- The findings are based on CNN/DM and XSUM datasets, and the extent to which these results generalize to other domains remains unclear.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Instruction tuning is more important than model size for zero-shot summarization performance.
- **Medium Confidence**: Existing reference summaries underestimate both human and model performance.
- **Medium Confidence**: Automatic metrics correlate poorly with human judgment when reference summaries are low quality.

## Next Checks

1. **Prompt Variation Study**: Systematically vary prompt formulations across different instruction-tuned models to quantify the impact of prompt engineering on summarization performance, controlling for model size and instruction tuning presence.

2. **Reference Quality Analysis**: Conduct detailed error analysis of reference summaries to identify specific quality issues (factuality errors, coherence problems, relevance gaps) and correlate these with automatic metric scores.

3. **Cross-Dataset Validation**: Test the best-performing instruction-tuned model (Instruct Davinci) on additional summarization datasets from different domains to assess generalizability of the findings beyond news summarization.
