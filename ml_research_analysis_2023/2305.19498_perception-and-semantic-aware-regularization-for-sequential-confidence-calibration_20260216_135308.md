# Perception and Semantic Aware Regularization for Sequential Confidence Calibration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19498
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19498
- Reference count: 40
- The authors propose PSSR to address overconfidence in deep sequence recognition models by regularizing training using perceptually and semantically similar sequences as additional supervision.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates overconfidence in deep sequence recognition (DSR) models, showing it arises from visual similarity and semantic correlation between target and incorrect tokens. The authors propose Perception and Semantic aware Sequence Regularization (PSSR), which regularizes training using perceptually and semantically similar sequences as additional supervision. An adaptive calibration intensity module adjusts regularization based on sample difficulty. Experiments on scene text and speech recognition tasks demonstrate PSSR achieves state-of-the-art calibration performance, significantly improving expected calibration error (ECE) and other metrics compared to baseline methods.

## Method Summary
The PSSR method constructs a similarity sequence set containing perceptually similar sequences (using a semantic context-free model like CTC) and semantically similar sequences (using a language model like BCN). During training, the model's loss is augmented with weighted regularization terms from these similar sequences. An adaptive calibration intensity module based on sample difficulty (posterior probability of target sequence) adjusts regularization strength. The method is evaluated on scene text recognition datasets (Synth90K, SynthText, IIIT5K, SVT, IC03, IC13, IC15, SVTP, CUTE80) and Chinese benchmarks, as well as speech recognition dataset AISHELL-1.

## Key Results
- PSSR significantly improves expected calibration error (ECE) compared to baseline methods
- The method achieves state-of-the-art calibration performance on both scene text and speech recognition tasks
- Adaptive calibration intensity based on sample hardness further improves calibration effectiveness

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Perception overconfidence arises because DSR models overfit to visual similarity between tokens, leading to high-confidence predictions for visually similar but incorrect tokens.
- Mechanism: The model assigns high posterior probabilities to tokens that are visually similar to the ground truth (e.g., "l" predicted as "i") because training only uses the target token as supervision, ignoring perceptual similarity cues that could regularize predictions.
- Core assumption: The training data contains sufficient examples of visually similar token pairs so that confusion matrices reflect systematic visual similarity-driven overconfidence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "tokens/sequences with high perception and semantic correlations with the target ones contain more correlated and effective information and thus facilitate more effective regularization"
  - [section] "the ground-truth class is more likely to be confused with the classes with higher perception similarity" and confusion matrix data showing "0" confused with "o"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: Corpus neighbors do not mention perception-overconfidence mechanisms explicitly
- Break condition: If the visual similarity distribution in the dataset is uniform or if the model uses explicit perceptual distance regularization already, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Semantic overconfidence occurs because DSR models predict sequences that are semantically coherent with the context but incorrect, assigning them high confidence.
- Mechanism: Context-aware decoders (e.g., attention-based) generate predictions that are frequent in the training set and contextually valid, even if they deviate from the true sequence, leading to high-confidence semantically correlated mispredictions.
- Core assumption: The language model used to find semantically similar sequences accurately captures co-occurrence patterns in the training data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "word 'universiti' is semantically similar to word 'university' and thus it is also predicted to 'university'"
  - [section] "models tend to assign high probabilities to sequences that share a highly similar context to the target sequence and appear more frequently in training"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: Corpus neighbors do not discuss semantic-overconfidence directly
- Break condition: If the language model is poorly trained or the training set lacks diverse semantic contexts, the mechanism breaks.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Adaptive calibration intensity based on sample hardness prevents underconfidence on easy samples and overconfidence on hard samples by modulating regularization strength.
- Mechanism: The modulating factor function f(pi) scales regularization based on the posterior probability of the target sequence; harder samples (low pi) receive stronger smoothing toward perceptually/semantically similar sequences.
- Core assumption: The posterior probability of the target sequence correlates with actual sample difficulty.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "over-confidence degree varies across samples according to their difficulties"
  - [section] "we propose a modulating factor function f (pi) that is integrated into the regularization term to achieve adaptive calibration based on sample hardness"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: Corpus neighbors do not mention hardness-based adaptive calibration
- Break condition: If sample difficulty is poorly correlated with posterior probability or if the model cannot estimate pi reliably, the mechanism fails.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Label smoothing
  - Why needed here: Label smoothing redistributes probability mass from the target to other classes, reducing overconfidence by preventing the model from assigning full probability to the correct token.
  - Quick check question: What happens to the model's confidence distribution if you apply uniform label smoothing without considering perceptual or semantic similarity?

- Concept: Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss
  - Why needed here: CTC is a sequence recognition loss that handles alignment between input frames and output tokens without requiring explicit alignment, relevant for models like CRNN and TRBC.
  - Quick check question: How does CTC loss differ from cross-entropy loss in terms of handling unsegmented sequence data?

- Concept: Attention-based sequence decoding
  - Why needed here: Attention mechanisms allow the model to focus on relevant parts of the input sequence at each decoding step, critical for models like MASTER and ASTER that exploit both perception and semantic context.
  - Quick check question: Why might attention-based models be more prone to semantic overconfidence compared to CTC-based models?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input sequence -> Base DSR model -> Perception similarity mining -> Semantic correlation mining -> Adaptive calibration intensity -> Loss computation with regularization

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass through base DSR model
  2. Extract perception similarity sequences via semantic context-free model
  3. Extract semantic correlation sequences via language model
  4. Compute adaptive calibration intensity f(pi)
  5. Compute total loss Ltotal combining base loss and regularization
  6. Backward pass and parameter update

- Design tradeoffs:
  - More similar sequences improve calibration but increase computational cost
  - Higher adaptive calibration intensity improves calibration on hard samples but may hurt calibration on easy samples
  - Using a semantic context-free model for perception similarity is efficient but may miss subtle perceptual cues

- Failure signatures:
  - Calibration metrics (ECE, ACE, MCE) do not improve despite increased regularization
  - Model accuracy drops significantly after adding PSSR
  - Similar sequences mined are not perceptually or semantically similar to target sequences

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate that confusion matrices show higher frequencies for visually similar token pairs
  2. Test adaptive calibration intensity by varying εe and εh and measuring calibration performance
  3. Compare calibration performance when using only perception similarity sequences vs. only semantic correlation sequences

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the optimal ratio of perception similarity sequences to semantic correlation sequences vary across different types of sequence recognition tasks (e.g., text vs. speech recognition)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "As for TRBA, the calibration performance is better when the number of visually similar sequences is approximately equal to that of semantically similar sequences. However, the TRBC is better with the increase of visually similar sequences."
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides evidence that this ratio varies between different model architectures (attention-based vs. CTC-based), it does not explore how this optimal ratio changes across different task domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the ratio of perception to semantic sequences across multiple task domains (text, speech, sign language, etc.) while measuring calibration performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the computational overhead of the proposed PSSR method during both training and inference, and how does this impact its practical deployment in real-time applications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper introduces additional components including similar sequence mining and an adaptive calibration intensity module, but does not provide any computational complexity analysis or runtime measurements.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on calibration performance metrics but does not address the practical considerations of implementing this method in production systems.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed profiling of computational resources (time, memory) required during training and inference, along with comparisons to baseline methods under various hardware constraints.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed PSSR method perform when applied to multilingual or code-switching sequence recognition tasks where the language context can change within a single sequence?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper evaluates the method on English and Chinese benchmarks separately but does not explore scenarios where multiple languages or scripts are present within the same sequence.
- Why unresolved: The semantic correlation component relies on language models, and the effectiveness of this approach in multilingual settings remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on multilingual datasets (e.g., Europarl, Common Voice multilingual) and code-switching datasets (e.g., SEAME, Bilingual Chinese-English) measuring both calibration performance and recognition accuracy.

## Limitations
- Lack of ablation studies showing individual contributions of perception-aware regularization, semantic-aware regularization, and adaptive calibration intensity
- Implementation complexity requiring pre-trained semantic context-free and language models
- Limited evaluation to scene text and speech recognition tasks, not exploring other sequence recognition domains
- No discussion of computational costs or training time implications

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The core observation that DSR models suffer from overconfidence, particularly for perceptually and semantically similar tokens/sequences
- Medium Confidence: The effectiveness of PSSR in improving calibration metrics (ECE, ACE, MCE)
- Low Confidence: The claim that PSSR achieves state-of-the-art calibration performance

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation study validation**: Implement and evaluate each component of PSSR (perception-aware regularization, semantic-aware regularization, and adaptive calibration intensity) independently to determine their individual contributions to calibration improvement. This would clarify which mechanisms are most critical for the observed performance gains.

2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary εe, εh, and the global calibration intensity α across a range of values to understand their impact on calibration performance and identify robust settings that work across different datasets and model architectures.

3. **Cross-domain generalization test**: Apply PSSR to a different sequence recognition task (such as handwriting recognition or medical sequence labeling) to validate whether the perception and semantic overconfidence mechanisms generalize beyond scene text and speech recognition. This would test the broader applicability of the framework.
