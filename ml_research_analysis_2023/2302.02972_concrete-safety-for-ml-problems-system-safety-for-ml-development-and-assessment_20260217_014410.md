# Concrete Safety for ML Problems: System Safety for ML Development and Assessment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02972
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02972
- Reference count: 40
- This paper applies Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) to identify social and ethical risks in ML-driven systems through control structure modeling.

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates how Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) can be applied to identify social and ethical risks in machine learning systems. The authors apply STPA to two case studies: a Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP) risk scoring system and a criminal justice facial recognition system. Through systematic modeling of control structures and analysis of unsafe control actions, the method reveals mismatches between system goals and optimization targets, potential biases, and hazards that could lead to unacceptable losses for stakeholders.

The paper shows that STPA provides a repeatable framework for ML developers to proactively identify and mitigate social and ethical risks through actionable sociotechnical requirements traceable to stakeholder-defined losses. By focusing on control structures rather than just component failures, STPA captures how hazards emerge from the interaction between technical systems and their sociotechnical contexts, making it particularly suitable for complex ML systems where traditional component-based safety approaches may fall short.

## Method Summary
The paper applies STPA through a four-step process: (1) define the purpose of analysis including identifying stakeholders, system context, and unacceptable losses; (2) model the system's control structure by mapping control actions and feedback loops; (3) identify unsafe control actions (UCAs) using a modified STPA rubric examining missing, incorrect, mistimed, or wrong quantity control actions; and (4) examine loss scenarios to ensure mitigation of hazardous states. The analysis draws on publicly available documentation including operating manuals, legal requirements, training documents, and open source API documentation rather than direct system access.

## Key Results
- STPA revealed a critical mismatch between the PDMP system's stated goals (reducing opioid use disorder and preventing overdoses) and its optimization target (minimizing drug diversion), potentially leading to patient harm through unsafe treatment alternatives
- The facial recognition system analysis identified 32 unsafe control actions across development, modeling, and deployment phases, highlighting risks of wrongful warrant issuance and demographic disparities
- The method successfully generated actionable sociotechnical requirements traceable to stakeholder-defined losses, providing a framework for systematically identifying social and ethical risks in ML systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: STPA systematically identifies unsafe control actions (UCAs) by modeling system control structures and analyzing how control actions can lead to hazardous states.
- Mechanism: STPA models the sociotechnical system as a set of control loops, where each loop consists of a controller, a controlled process, control actions, and feedback. By applying a structured rubric to each control action, STPA identifies when actions are missing, incorrect, mistimed, or in wrong quantity, leading to hazards.
- Core assumption: Hazards emerge from unsafe control actions within the system's control structure, not just from component failures.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "STPA systematically identifies hazards and unsafe control actions (UCAs) by modeling system control structures and analyzing how control actions can lead to hazardous states."
  - [section] "STPA models sociotechnical systems in terms of control and control structures. In this paradigm, unwanted losses and hazardous system states are understood as the result of insufficient control within the system."
- Break condition: If the system control structure is not adequately modeled or if the STPA rubric is misapplied, the analysis may miss critical UCAs.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: STPA reveals mismatches between system goals and optimization targets by analyzing the system's control structure and identifying hazards that arise from such mismatches.
- Mechanism: By defining losses and hazards at the system level, STPA forces consideration of the overall system goal and optimization targets. Analysis of control loops and UCAs reveals when these targets are misaligned with stated goals, leading to unintended consequences.
- Core assumption: Optimization targets must be aligned with system goals to prevent unintended hazards.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "One important fact that our analysis highlighted was the gap between stated goals for the system (reducing Opioid Use Disorder and preventing overdoses) and the optimization target for the score (minimizing drug diversion)."
  - [section] "A frequent problem in ML systems is a mismatch between the objective function and the system goal."
- Break condition: If the system goal and optimization targets are not clearly defined or if the analysis does not consider the full sociotechnical context, the mismatch may go undetected.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: STPA provides actionable sociotechnical requirements traceable to stakeholder-defined losses by identifying UCAs and their causal paths to hazards.
- Mechanism: STPA's structured approach leads to a validated listing of UCAs linked to hazardous system states and stakeholder-defined losses. This traceability enables the development of specific control requirements to mitigate or eliminate hazards.
- Core assumption: Identifying the causal paths from UCAs to hazards enables the creation of effective control requirements.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The method provides a repeatable framework for ML developers to proactively identify and mitigate social and ethical risks through actionable sociotechnical requirements traceable to stakeholder-defined losses."
  - [section] "Once finalized, the UCA analysis table contains a validated listing of UCAs providing traceability to hazardous system states and stakeholder-defined unacceptable losses that can result."
- Break condition: If the causal paths from UCAs to hazards are not fully explored or if the requirements are not clearly linked to the losses, the analysis may not lead to effective mitigation strategies.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: STPA process flows as: Define Analysis Purpose -> Model Control Structure -> Identify UCAs -> Examine Loss Scenarios
- Critical path: The critical path is the sequence of steps from defining the analysis purpose to examining loss scenarios. Each step builds on the previous one, so delays in any step can impact the overall timeline.
- Design tradeoffs: STPA provides a solution-neutral approach, meaning it identifies the need for better control but does not prescribe specific solutions. This allows for flexibility in choosing the most effective mitigation strategies, but it also requires careful consideration of the tradeoffs between different options.
- Failure signatures: Common failure modes in STPA include: inadequate control structure modeling, misidentification of losses and hazards, incomplete analysis of UCAs and loss scenarios, and failure to develop effective control requirements.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does STPA handle the temporal aspect of ML system failures, particularly regarding concept drift and evolving social contexts over time?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] from the discussion of Goodhart's Law and system drift in Section 5.1, where the authors note that "the behavior and effectiveness of controls may change over time"
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions the need to consider temporal changes but doesn't provide concrete methods for incorporating time-varying factors into the STPA analysis framework.
- What evidence would resolve it: A case study demonstrating STPA applied to an ML system that has undergone significant concept drift, showing how the analysis framework adapts to temporal changes.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal frequency for retraining ML models in high-stakes systems, and how does this balance against the risks of stale models versus introducing new vulnerabilities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] from Table 8, where the authors note that "Too low, (not frequently enough), is hazardous for same reason as not providing" but also that "Too high(too frequently) is hazardous as it increases the opportunity for those in providing"

- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical data comparing system performance and failure rates across different retraining frequencies in real-world high-stakes ML deployments.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can STPA be effectively applied to transformer-based models like large language models, which have emergent behaviors that are difficult to predict from their training data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] from the conclusion, which states "Future work will further examine the applicability and generalizability of STPA for ML-driven social and ethical impact by investigating a case study involving a large language model (LLM) driven content moderation system for social media"
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this as an open question but doesn't provide a methodology for applying STPA to models with complex, emergent behaviors.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed application of STPA to an LLM system, showing how to model control structures and identify UCAs when the model's behavior cannot be fully specified a priori.

## Limitations

- The analysis relies on publicly available documentation rather than direct access to actual ML systems, limiting verification of control structure models and identified hazards.
- While STPA identifies unsafe control actions and potential hazards, the paper does not demonstrate how these findings translate into actual safety improvements or mitigation strategies in practice.
- The effectiveness of STPA depends on accurately identifying and defining losses from stakeholder perspectives, but the paper does not detail the process used to determine stakeholder concerns or resolve conflicts between different stakeholder interests.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The application of STPA methodology itself is well-established in safety engineering literature. The paper correctly applies the four-step STPA process to analyze sociotechnical systems, and the identification of unsafe control actions through control structure modeling follows the standard STPA framework.

**Medium Confidence**: The specific hazards and UCAs identified for both case studies are plausible given the system descriptions and context provided. However, without access to the actual systems or empirical validation of the identified risks, the completeness and accuracy of these findings remain uncertain.

**Low Confidence**: Claims about the relative effectiveness of STPA compared to other safety engineering approaches are not supported by empirical evidence in this paper. The assertion that STPA is "the most general and most recently developed hazard analysis technique" is stated without comparative validation.
