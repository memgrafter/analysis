# Autocorrelations Decay in Texts and Applicability Limits of Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06615
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06615
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Natural texts exhibit power-law autocorrelation decay across languages, while LLM-generated texts show exponential decay, suggesting limitations of autoregressive models for long-text analysis.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates autocorrelation decay patterns in natural and generated texts using distributional semantics. The authors demonstrate that literary works exhibit power-law decay in word autocorrelations across multiple languages, while texts generated by autoregressive language models (GPT-2, S4) show exponential decay. This fundamental difference suggests that autoregressive models, which operate under Markov assumptions, may have inherent limitations when applied to long-text analysis or generation tasks that require capturing long-range dependencies.

## Method Summary
The study uses distributional semantics models (GloVe and BOW embeddings) to map words to high-dimensional vectors. Autocorrelation functions are computed as cosine similarities between word vectors separated by distance τ. Power-law, exponential, and logarithmic decay models are fitted to the autocorrelation data, with goodness of fit measured by Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The analysis spans multiple literary works in five languages and compares natural texts with outputs from GPT-2 and S4 language models.

## Key Results
- Natural texts consistently show power-law autocorrelation decay with exponent α ≈ -0.25 across languages
- LLM-generated texts exhibit exponential decay patterns, indicating Markovian behavior
- Cross-linguistic consistency of power-law exponents suggests language-independent semantic structure
- MAPE analysis confirms power-law fits are significantly better for natural texts than exponential fits

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Autocorrelations in natural language texts follow power-law decay over long distances.
- Mechanism: Distributional semantic models map words to vectors where semantic similarity is preserved. Similar words across large text spans yield cosine similarities that decay slowly according to a power law, reflecting long-range correlations in language.
- Core assumption: Vector embeddings capture meaningful semantic similarity that correlates with long-range dependencies.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using distributional semantics we empirically demonstrate that autocorrelations of words in texts decay according to a power law."
  - [section 4.2, Q1] "How accurately can we say that autocorrelations in texts decay according to a power law?"
- Break condition: If embeddings fail to encode long-range dependencies, power-law behavior won't emerge.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Large autoregressive language models exhibit Markovian behavior, leading to exponential autocorrelation decay.
- Mechanism: Transformers and LSTMs use limited context windows, truncating long-range dependencies. This truncation manifests as faster (exponential) decay in autocorrelations for generated text.
- Core assumption: Model's context window and attention mechanism effectively limit dependency range.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We conclude that language models exhibiting Markov behavior, including large autoregressive language models, may have limitations when applied to long texts."
  - [section 3.2] "Large language models actually use only a limited context, often significantly less than the maximum context possible."
- Break condition: If models are modified to maintain longer-range dependencies, exponential decay pattern may change.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Power-law decay in natural texts is consistent across languages when measured with distributional semantics.
- Mechanism: Multilingual GloVe embeddings align word semantics across languages, so autocorrelation decay exponent remains stable for same text translated into multiple languages.
- Core assumption: Pretrained multilingual embeddings preserve semantic relationships across languages sufficiently to yield comparable autocorrelation behavior.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that distributional semantics typically provides coherent autocorrelations decay exponents for texts translated to multiple languages."
  - [section 5.3] "the parameters of power law, as well as the accuracy of the approximation are extremely consistent among languages for both embeddings."
- Break condition: If embeddings fail to capture language-independent semantics, exponents will diverge.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Text preprocessing → Word vector mapping (GloVe/BOW) → Autocorrelation computation → Power-law fitting → Comparison across languages/models
- Critical path: Preprocessing (clean text, tokenize) → Load embeddings → Compute centered vectors → Apply window averaging → Compute cosine autocorrelations → Fit power-law → Evaluate MAPE
- Design tradeoffs:
  - GloVe vs. BOW: GloVe preserves fine-grained semantic similarity; BOW smooths via averaging but requires large windows
  - Window size a: Larger a reduces noise but may blur local structure; smaller a preserves locality but may be noisier
  - Distance range τ: Must span at least one order of magnitude for reliable power-law fitting
- Failure signatures:
  - Negative autocorrelations at small lags → possible embedding or preprocessing issue
  - MAPE high for both power and exponential fits → poor model fit; check vector quality
  - Exponents vary widely across languages → possible embedding quality or tokenization inconsistency
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate autocorrelation computation for a single text (e.g., Don Quixote) using GloVe embeddings with a=1, τ∈[1,40000]; plot log-log and log-linear
  2. Compare power-law vs. exponential fits via MAPE for the same dataset; confirm power-law superiority
  3. Repeat experiment 1-2 for a multilingual corpus (same text in multiple languages) and compare exponents

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the autocorrelation decay patterns differ between texts generated by autoregressive models and those generated by other types of language models (e.g., non-autoregressive or latent variable models)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares autocorrelations in texts generated by GPT-2 (autoregressive) and S4 (structured state space), finding different decay patterns, but does not explore other model types.
- Why unresolved: The study only examines two specific models, leaving open whether the observed differences are characteristic of autoregressive models in general or specific to these implementations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing autocorrelations across a broader range of language model architectures, including non-autoregressive models, latent variable models, and hybrid approaches, would clarify whether autoregressive models consistently exhibit different decay patterns.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different sampling strategies (temperature, top-k, nucleus sampling, etc.) affect the autocorrelation decay patterns in generated texts?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that conditions like sampling approach may influence autocorrelations decay laws in generated texts, but does not systematically explore this relationship.
- Why unresolved: The study uses default generation parameters for both models without exploring how different sampling strategies impact the observed decay patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematically varying sampling parameters across multiple runs and measuring their effect on autocorrelation decay would reveal how generation strategies influence long-range dependencies in generated text.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the minimum sequence length required to reliably detect power law decay in autocorrelations using distributional semantics approaches?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper analyzes long literary works but does not investigate the relationship between text length and the reliability of detecting power law decay.
- Why unresolved: The study assumes sufficient length but does not quantify how sequence length affects the statistical reliability of identifying power law patterns versus other decay forms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting controlled experiments with texts of varying lengths and analyzing the statistical significance of power law fits across different sequence lengths would establish minimum requirements for reliable detection.

## Limitations
- Distributional semantics approach assumes pretrained embeddings adequately capture long-range dependencies in natural language
- Text preprocessing pipeline details (handling of footnotes, translator notes) are not fully specified
- Comparison assumes LLM-generated texts are comparable in length and complexity to literary corpus

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Empirical observation that autocorrelations in natural texts decay according to a power law, supported by consistent decay exponents across multiple languages and texts
- **Medium Confidence**: Claim that LLM-generated texts exhibit exponential autocorrelation decay, as this relies on the assumption that autoregressive models enforce Markovian behavior
- **Low Confidence**: Cross-linguistic consistency of power-law exponents, as this depends on the quality and alignment of multilingual embeddings
