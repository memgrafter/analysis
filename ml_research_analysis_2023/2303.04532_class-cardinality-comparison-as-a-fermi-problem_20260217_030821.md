# Class Cardinality Comparison as a Fermi Problem

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04532
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04532
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Proposed method achieves 83.7% accuracy on 4005 class pairs, outperforming direct signals and matching non-expert human performance

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of comparing the cardinalities of two classes using knowledge bases, search engines, and language models. The core innovation is using signals from root classes and their orthogonal subgroups (e.g., by country) to improve dominance estimation. Novel aggregation techniques combine these signals at both subgroup and source levels. Experiments show the approach achieves 83.7% accuracy across 4005 class pairs in 6 domains, outperforming direct signals and matching non-expert human performance.

## Method Summary
The method leverages signals from knowledge bases (Wikidata), search engines (Bing), and language models (GPT-3) to estimate class dominance. It extracts root class information and uses orthogonal subgroups (e.g., G20 countries) to gather more specific signals. The paper proposes aggregation methods at both subgroup and source levels, including ensemble techniques to combine evidence. The approach is evaluated on 4005 class pairs across 6 domains, demonstrating improved accuracy and reduced abstention rates compared to direct signal approaches.

## Key Results
- 83.7% accuracy on 4005 class pairs across 6 domains
- Outperforms direct signals from individual sources
- Matches non-expert human performance on dominance estimation
- Subgroup aggregation reduces abstention rates
- Ensemble methods over sources improve robustness

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by leveraging the structure of class hierarchies and subgroup information to obtain more specific and reliable signals. By combining evidence from multiple sources (KBs, SEs, LMs) and using aggregation techniques at both subgroup and source levels, the approach can overcome the limitations and biases inherent in individual information sources. The use of orthogonal subgroups (like countries) provides additional context that helps disambiguate and strengthen dominance signals.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does subgroup aggregation improve dominance estimation for non-G20 countries as well, or are G20 countries uniquely informative?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses G20 countries as the only subgroup set and does not test other geographic or demographic subgroups.
- Why unresolved: Limited to one subgroup type; no ablation on alternative subgrouping schemes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental comparison of dominance estimation accuracy using different subgroup sets (e.g., continents, population brackets, regional blocs) while holding other factors constant.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the aggregation methods perform on open-ended cardinality estimation (absolute counts), not just dominance comparison?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on dominance estimation; absolute cardinality is mentioned as a harder task but not evaluated.
- Why unresolved: No evaluation on numeric error metrics like mean absolute error or relative deviation from ground truth.
- What evidence would resolve it: Quantitative evaluation of cardinality estimates against true values using standard regression or estimation error metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive are the results to the choice of sources and their quality, e.g., if using different KBs or search engines?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Only Wikidata, Bing, and GPT-3 are used; no comparison with alternatives like Google, Freebase, or other LMs.
- Why unresolved: Single-source evaluations may not generalize across domains or platforms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Cross-source replication showing how accuracy and abstention rates vary with different KB, SE, and LM combinations.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can the ensemble methods scale to dominance estimation over more than two classes simultaneously?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] All experiments compare exactly two classes at a time; no multi-class dominance framework is presented.
- Why unresolved: Pairwise comparison does not address the combinatorial complexity of multi-class ranking or estimation.
- What evidence would resolve it: An extension of the aggregation and ensemble methods to multi-class dominance ranking with empirical validation.

## Limitations
- Reliance on six specific domains may limit generalizability to other domains or real-world applications
- Use of knowledge bases, search engines, and language models introduces potential biases and noise in signals
- Abstention rates, while reduced, still indicate some uncertainty in the model's predictions

## Confidence
- Major claims: Medium
  - The proposed method demonstrates improvements over direct signals and matches non-expert human performance
  - Relatively small dataset size and specific domain focus suggest caution in extrapolating results
  - Effectiveness of subgroup aggregation and ensemble methods is promising but needs further validation
