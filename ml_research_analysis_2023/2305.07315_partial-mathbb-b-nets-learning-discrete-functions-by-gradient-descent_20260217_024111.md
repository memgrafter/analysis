# $\partial\mathbb{B}$ nets: learning discrete functions by gradient descent

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07315
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07315
- Reference count: 15
- Primary result: Differentiable neural networks that learn discrete boolean-valued functions by gradient descent with comparable performance to standard methods while using 1-bit weights and producing interpretable logical functions.

## Executive Summary
∂B nets are differentiable neural networks designed to learn discrete boolean-valued functions. They consist of two semantically equivalent aspects: a differentiable soft-net with real weights, and a non-differentiable hard-net with boolean weights. The soft-net is trained by backpropagation, then the learned weights are 'hardened' to boolean values and bound to the hard-net, resulting in a learned discrete function with no accuracy loss. This approach achieves comparable performance to standard machine learning methods while producing compact models with interpretable logical functions.

## Method Summary
∂B nets work by first training a soft-net using standard backpropagation with gradient-rich activation functions that are hard-equivalent to discrete functions. The key innovation is the use of margin packing to construct activation functions that maintain gradients while preserving the semantics of boolean operations. After training, the real-valued weights are converted to boolean values through a hardening function (thresholding at 1/2), and these boolean weights are then bound to a hard-net that computes the exact discrete function. The method is demonstrated on classification tasks including binary Iris, noisy XOR, and MNIST datasets.

## Key Results
- ∂B nets achieve comparable classification accuracy to Tsetlin machines, neural networks, SVMs, naive Bayes, and logistic regression on standard benchmarks
- The hardening process preserves accuracy, unlike existing neural network binarization approaches
- Models are compact due to 1-bit weights and interpretable due to the logical nature of learned functions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Gradient-rich functions backpropagate error to all inputs, enabling effective training.
- Mechanism: ∂B nets use margin packing to construct activation functions that are both hard-equivalent and gradient-rich, unlike traditional differentiable relaxations.
- Core assumption: The average soft-bit value is a natural choice for packing a fraction of the margin without crossing the 1/2 threshold.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Preliminary experiments demonstrate that ∂B nets achieve comparable performance on standard machine learning problems yet are compact (due to 1-bit weights) and interpretable (due to the logical nature of the learnt functions)."
  - [section] "∂B nets must be composed of 'activation' functions that are hard-equivalent to discrete functions but also, where possible, gradient-rich."
- Break condition: If the average soft-bit value is not a good fraction of the margin, the hard-equivalence may be broken.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Hard-equivalence allows the soft-net and hard-net to have identical predictive performance.
- Mechanism: The hardening function converts soft-bits to hard-bits at the threshold 1/2, preserving the semantic meaning of the learned function.
- Core assumption: The hardening function is defined such that f(x) = 1 if x > 1/2, and 0 otherwise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "∂B nets have two semantically equivalent aspects: a differentiable soft-net, with real weights, and a non-differentiable hard-net, with boolean weights."
  - [section] "A function, f : [0, 1]^n → [0, 1]^m, is hard-equivalent to a discrete function, g : {1, 0}^n → {1, 0}^m, if harden(f(x)) = g(harden(x)) for all x ∈ { (x1,...,x n)|xi ∈ [0, 1]\{ 1/2}}."
- Break condition: If the hardening function is not defined correctly, the soft-net and hard-net may not be semantically equivalent.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The differentiable majority function allows ∂B nets to solve multi-class classification problems.
- Mechanism: The ∂Maj function sorts the input soft-bits and selects the median as a representative bit, which is then augmented using margin packing.
- Core assumption: The median soft-bit is representative of the boolean majority function.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We aim to construct a differentiable analogue of Maj. Maj for n bits in DNF form is a disjunction of (n choose k) conjunctive clauses of size k, where k = ⌈n/2⌉."
  - [section] "Observe that if the elements of x are in ascending order then, if any soft-bits are high, there exists a unique contiguous pair of indices (i,i + 1) where xi is low and xi+1 is high, where index i is a direct count of the number of soft-bits that are low in x."
- Break condition: If the sorting algorithm is not efficient or the median is not a good representative, the differentiable majority function may not work as intended.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of margin fraction in the augmented-bit function affect the gradient-rich property and learning performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper defines the augmented-bit function using the mean soft-bit value as the margin fraction, but notes that this is "a natural choice" without exploring alternatives.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the impact of different margin fractions on the gradient-rich property or learning performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of ∂B nets with different margin fractions (e.g., mean, median, or learned fractions) would provide insights into the optimal choice.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of ∂B nets scale with the number of boolean variables in the learned function?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the number of terms in the differentiable majority function grows exponentially with the number of variables, but does not provide empirical results for larger problems.
- Why unresolved: The paper only presents experiments on small-scale problems (e.g., Iris, noisy XOR, and MNIST) and does not investigate the scalability of ∂B nets to larger boolean functions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on problems with a large number of boolean variables (e.g., high-dimensional binary classification or logical reasoning tasks) would demonstrate the scalability of ∂B nets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of ∂B nets compare to other differentiable approaches for learning boolean functions, such as differentiable inductive logic programming or neural logic machines?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses related work on differentiable approaches for learning boolean functions but does not provide empirical comparisons with ∂B nets.
- Why unresolved: The paper only compares ∂B nets to standard machine learning approaches (e.g., Tsetlin machines, neural networks, SVM, and naive Bayes) but does not investigate their performance relative to other differentiable methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of ∂B nets to other differentiable approaches on the same benchmark problems would provide insights into their relative strengths and weaknesses.

## Limitations
- The empirical results showing comparable accuracy to baseline methods are promising but limited in scope and need more rigorous statistical validation.
- The interpretability claims are asserted but not demonstrated through concrete examples or case studies.
- The claims about comparable performance across datasets need more systematic verification with larger problem sizes.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The hard-equivalence mechanism between soft-net and hard-net is well-defined and theoretically sound. The architectural framework is clearly specified.

**Medium Confidence**: The empirical results showing comparable accuracy to baseline methods are promising but limited in scope. The claim that hardening introduces no accuracy loss needs more systematic verification.

**Low Confidence**: The interpretability benefits and the specific advantages of ∂B nets over existing binarization approaches lack sufficient empirical support.
