# Deep reinforcement learning applied to an assembly sequence planning problem with user preferences

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.06567
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06567
- Reference count: 30
- One-line primary result: DRL with parametric actions and two reward signals effectively optimizes assembly sequence planning while incorporating user preferences, with A2C and Rainbow outperforming DQN.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses assembly sequence planning (ASP) optimization by integrating user preferences and ergonomic considerations into deep reinforcement learning (DRL). The authors introduce parametric actions to improve training efficiency and sample efficiency, and employ two reward signals: one for user preferences addressing ergonomic and difficulty concerns, and another for total assembly time minimization. Four algorithms—tabular Q-Learning, A2C, DQN, and Rainbow—were tested on a toy airplane assembly task in both deterministic and stochastic settings. After 10,000 episodes, A2C and Rainbow achieved near-optimal assembly times (~64.3-64.5 time units) with low unwanted assembly sequence occurrences (31-39), while DQN underperformed due to lack of action restrictions. The results demonstrate the viability of DRL for ASP problems with human interaction, though sample efficiency remains a challenge.

## Method Summary
The study formulates assembly sequence planning as a Markov Decision Process and tests four RL algorithms on a toy airplane assembly task. Parametric actions are introduced to restrict the action space to feasible assembly tasks at each state, improving sample efficiency. Two reward signals are used: normalized assembly time and penalties for unwanted assembly sequences based on user preferences. The algorithms are evaluated over 10,000 episodes in both deterministic and stochastic settings, with task durations sampled from normal distributions (10% standard deviation) in the stochastic case. Performance is measured by mean assembly time and cumulative sum of unwanted assemblies.

## Key Results
- A2C and Rainbow achieved near-optimal assembly times (~64.3-64.5 time units) with low unwanted sequence occurrences (31-39) after 10,000 episodes.
- DQN underperformed due to lack of action restrictions, showing slower learning and higher unwanted sequences.
- Parametric actions improved sample efficiency for all algorithms except Rainbow, which was not implemented with this feature.
- Stochastic task durations improved robustness but increased variance in learning curves.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Parametric actions reduce the action space and improve sample efficiency by only allowing feasible assembly tasks at each state.
- Mechanism: The environment dynamically filters available actions based on task dependencies, ensuring the agent only considers valid next steps.
- Core assumption: Task dependencies are correctly encoded and enforced in the action space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The actions selected by the agent are directly correlated to the assembly tasks, i.e., in this case study the agent has a total of 8 different available tasks to select from. Nevertheless, due to task dependencies, in every state some actions are restricted."
  - [abstract] "The proposed approach introduces in the RL environment parametric actions to improve training time and sample efficiency..."
- Break condition: If task dependencies are incorrectly specified, the agent may select invalid actions or miss optimal sequences.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Two reward signals—assembly time minimization and user preference avoidance—enable both performance and ergonomic optimization.
- Mechanism: The reward function combines normalized time penalties with explicit penalties for unwanted sequences, guiding the agent toward fast and ergonomic solutions.
- Core assumption: User preferences can be accurately captured and encoded as reward penalties.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The reward function considers two main criteria, the total assembly time minimization, and the user's preferences... The resulting reward function is defined by..."
  - [abstract] "The user's preferences signal addresses the difficulties and non-ergonomic properties of the assembly faced by the human..."
- Break condition: If user preferences are poorly defined or inconsistent, the agent may learn suboptimal or counterintuitive behaviors.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Stochastic task durations better reflect real-world variability and improve robustness of learned policies.
- Mechanism: Task times are sampled from normal distributions with 10% standard deviation, introducing variability that forces the agent to adapt.
- Core assumption: The 10% variance is representative of real-world variability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "In the setting where the accumulated assembly duration is stochastic, the duration of each task is obtained from a normal distribution with mean equal to the deterministic task assembly time and with standard deviation equal to 10% of the deterministic task assembly time duration."
  - [abstract] "The user's preferences signal addresses the difficulties and non-ergonomic properties of the assembly faced by the human..."
- Break condition: If the variance is too high or too low, the agent may either fail to converge or learn overly conservative policies.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Environment: Simulates the toy airplane assembly with task dependencies and tool requirements
  - Agent: Implements RL algorithms (DQN, A2C, Rainbow, tabular Q-Learning)
  - Reward function: Combines normalized assembly time and user preference penalties
  - State representation: Binary vector indicating completed tasks and current tool

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize environment and agent
  2. For each episode:
     - Agent observes state
     - Agent selects action (parametric filtering applied)
     - Environment executes action, returns next state and reward
     - Transition stored in replay buffer (for DQN/Rainbow)
     - Agent updates policy/value function
  3. Evaluate performance metrics (assembly time, unwanted sequences)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Parametric actions improve sample efficiency but are not available for all algorithms (e.g., Rainbow)
  - Stochastic durations improve robustness but increase variance in learning curves
  - Two reward signals balance performance and ergonomics but may conflict

- Failure signatures:
  - Slow convergence or suboptimal performance: Check reward scaling, exploration strategy, or parametric action implementation
  - High variance in results: Verify stochastic settings or check for bugs in state transition logic
  - Unwanted sequences still frequent: Review user preference encoding or reward penalty magnitudes

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run DQN with parametric actions enabled to compare against non-parametric baseline
  2. Vary the standard deviation of stochastic task durations to study robustness
  3. Test alternative reward weighting between time and user preferences to find optimal balance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would the Rainbow algorithm perform if parametric actions were implemented, allowing for action restrictions based on task dependencies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that Rainbow was not used with parametric actions due to unavailability, and suggests this could lead to slower learning and higher variability.
- Why unresolved: The study did not test Rainbow with parametric actions, so its potential performance improvement remains unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative results showing Rainbow's performance with and without parametric actions on the same ASP task.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would the algorithms scale to more complex assembly tasks with increased number of components, tools, and dependencies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that Q-Learning is known to suffer from the "curse of dimensionality" and suggests that increasing assembly complexity would lead to worse performance compared to A2C and Rainbow.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested on a relatively simple toy airplane assembly; scalability to more complex scenarios was not empirically validated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Results from testing the same algorithms on progressively more complex assembly tasks with higher dimensionality.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal balance between the two reward signals (user preferences and assembly time) for different manufacturing contexts?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses a weighted reward function combining user preferences and assembly time, but does not explore the impact of varying these weights.
- Why unresolved: The study used fixed weights for the reward signals without exploring how different weightings might affect learning outcomes in different contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the weights of user preference and time-based rewards across different assembly scenarios to identify optimal configurations.

## Limitations

- Narrow experimental scope: Tested only on a toy airplane assembly with 11 components, limiting generalizability to complex industrial assemblies.
- Unspecified hyperparameters: Exact hyperparameters (e.g., learning rates, network architectures) used for each algorithm were not provided, hindering reproducibility.
- Assumption of 10% task duration variance: The 10% standard deviation assumption may not accurately reflect real-world variability across all manufacturing contexts.

## Confidence

- Parametric actions reducing sample complexity: High
- Two-reward signal approach balancing performance and ergonomics: Medium
- Stochastic robustness claims: Medium
