# HK-LegiCoST: Leveraging Non-Verbatim Transcripts for Speech Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2306.11252
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11252
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** 600+ hours of Cantonese-English speech translation corpus with non-verbatim transcripts, achieving competitive baseline results

## Executive Summary
HK-LegiCoST is a large-scale Cantonese-English speech translation corpus containing over 600 hours of parliamentary proceedings audio with corresponding non-verbatim transcripts and translations. The corpus addresses key challenges in speech translation research for languages with significant spoken-written form differences by providing naturally occurring vernacular Cantonese alongside formal written Chinese translations. The authors developed specialized alignment algorithms to handle long recordings and non-verbatim transcripts, achieving competitive baseline performance on both their corpus and the FLEURS benchmark.

## Method Summary
The authors created HK-LegiCoST by collecting video recordings of Hong Kong Legislative Council meetings, then applying a multi-stage processing pipeline. They first preprocessed the audio and text data, then used a sliding-window flexible alignment algorithm to match audio segments with non-verbatim transcripts. The alignment pipeline employed k2 toolkit for WFST-based decoding and VecAlign for cross-lingual sentence alignment. Post-filtering removed low-quality segments, and the final corpus was split into training, development, and test sets. Baseline speech recognition models were trained using a two-stage approach: first on CommonVoice Cantonese data, then fine-tuned on HK-LegiCoST.

## Key Results
- Baseline ASR models achieved competitive results on HK-LegiCoST and FLEURS benchmarks
- Fine-tuned model significantly outperformed FLEURS baseline in zero-shot setting
- Demonstrated the value of non-verbatim transcripts for speech translation in languages with spoken-written form differences

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Non-verbatim transcripts improve alignment accuracy for languages with spoken-written form differences
- **Mechanism:** The corpus creation pipeline uses a sliding-window flexible alignment algorithm that can skip irrelevant or non-spoken text segments, improving sentence-level alignment quality
- **Core assumption:** The difference between spoken and written forms is systematic enough that an alignment algorithm can learn to identify and skip non-matching text
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Such transcripts make the corpus suitable for speech translation research when there are significant differences between the spoken and written forms of the source language."
  - [section 3.3.3] "To overcome these challenges, we implemented a sliding-window flexible alignment algorithm, which enabled us to address both issues by efficiently breaking down long segments into smaller ones of manageable lengths while simultaneously filtering out non-spoken text from the transcript."
- **Break condition:** If the non-verbatim differences are too random or context-dependent, the alignment algorithm cannot reliably identify which text to skip

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Large corpus size enables competitive baseline models despite non-verbatim transcripts
- **Mechanism:** With 600+ hours of data, the ASR models can learn the mapping between spoken forms and their written transcriptions through exposure to many examples
- **Core assumption:** The statistical patterns in the spoken-written form differences are learnable given sufficient data
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Due to its large size, we are able to demonstrate competitive speech translation baselines on HK-LegiCoST"
  - [section 4.1] "the HK-LegiCoST baseline model achieved competitive results in the zero-shot setting, while the fine-tuned model significantly outperformed the FLEURS baseline"
- **Break condition:** If the spoken-written form differences are too complex or idiosyncratic, even large datasets may not provide sufficient coverage

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Domain-specific content enables better performance than general-purpose models
- **Mechanism:** The parliamentary proceedings domain provides consistent vocabulary and discourse patterns that help models learn specialized terminology and contextual dependencies
- **Core assumption:** The domain-specific patterns are consistent enough across the corpus to provide useful learning signals
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "mainly focusing on government policy-related inquiries and their corresponding responses, alongside discussions and debates on motions and resolutions"
  - [section 5.2] "Due to the nature of the council meetings, it is common for the translations of the transcripts to exhibit contextual dependencies at the document level"
- **Break condition:** If the domain shifts significantly or contains too much variation in topics, the consistency advantage diminishes

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Raw data ingestion → Video preprocessing → Text preprocessing → Bitext alignment → Audio-text alignment → Post-filtering → Corpus splits → ASR training pipeline (CommonVoice → HK-LegiCoST → FLEURS) → MT training pipeline (fairseq transformer → evaluation)

- **Critical path:** Audio-text sentence alignment is the bottleneck, as it involves complex decoding and filtering steps for long recordings

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Non-verbatim transcripts provide linguistic richness but complicate alignment
  - Sliding-window approach reduces memory requirements but may miss long-range dependencies
  - Character-level tokenization handles Chinese morphology but may lose word-level information

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High CER in alignment indicates systematic mismatches between speech and transcript
  - Low BLEU scores on test set suggest domain shift or insufficient model capacity
  - Memory errors during alignment suggest need for more aggressive segmentation

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Run alignment on a small subset with different sliding window sizes to find optimal parameters
  2. Train ASR model on CommonVoice subset to verify alignment pipeline works before full training
  3. Evaluate zero-shot performance on FLEURS to establish baseline before fine-tuning

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the non-verbatim nature of Cantonese transcripts specifically impact the performance of speech translation models compared to verbatim transcripts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly discusses non-verbatim transcripts as a key feature and challenge, mentioning differences between spoken and written forms, and noting that formalization occurs during transcription.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates competitive results and mentions the impact of non-verbatim transcripts, it doesn't provide a direct comparison between models trained on verbatim vs. non-verbatim data to quantify the specific performance impact.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled experiment comparing speech translation model performance using the same dataset with either verbatim or non-verbatim transcripts, measuring differences in accuracy, fluency, and other relevant metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the long-term impact of document-level contextual dependencies on speech translation quality, and how can models be designed to better capture these dependencies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly identifies long context dependency as a notable feature of the corpus, mentioning document-level contextual dependencies such as co-reference and named entities.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges these dependencies and speculates that models leveraging this information would perform better, it doesn't explore this systematically or evaluate different approaches to capturing context.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different model architectures or training strategies that incorporate document-level context (e.g., transformer models with extended context windows, hierarchical models) against baseline models on the HK-LegiCoST corpus.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How transferable are the techniques developed for handling non-verbatim transcripts and long recordings in Cantonese to other vernacular or dialectal languages with similar characteristics?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses challenges common to vernacular/dialectal speech (non-verbatim transcripts, long recordings) and mentions related work on other languages, but doesn't explicitly test cross-linguistic transferability.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on Cantonese specifically and doesn't validate whether the approaches would work for other languages with similar spoken-written form discrepancies or long meeting-style recordings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying the same corpus creation pipeline and baseline models to datasets from other vernacular/dialectal languages (e.g., Swiss German, Hokkien, or other Chinese dialects) and comparing results to existing benchmarks.

## Limitations

- The alignment pipeline may struggle with systematic mismatches between spoken and written forms in non-verbatim transcripts
- The parliamentary domain focus may limit generalizability to other Cantonese speech contexts
- Evaluation relies on human-verified alignments from only a small subset, potentially introducing sampling bias

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The competitive baseline results on both HK-LegiCoST and FLEURS benchmarks are well-supported by quantitative metrics (CER and BLEU scores). The methodology for corpus creation is thoroughly described and follows established practices in speech processing.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that non-verbatim transcripts are "suitable for speech translation research" when spoken-written differences are significant relies on assumptions about the systematic nature of these differences. While the alignment algorithm appears robust, the paper does not provide comprehensive error analysis showing how often alignment fails or the types of mismatches that cause failures.

**Medium confidence**: The assertion that the corpus enables "insights into vernacular and dialectal speech processing" is supported by the corpus content but lacks empirical validation through downstream task performance comparisons with other Cantonese resources.

**Low confidence**: The paper claims the corpus addresses "key challenges" in segmentation and alignment, but does not provide quantitative comparisons with alternative approaches or ablation studies showing the impact of different alignment strategies.
