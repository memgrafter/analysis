# Do Large Language Models Show Decision Heuristics Similar to Humans? A Case Study Using GPT-3.5

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.04400
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04400
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: ChatGPT-3.5 displays decision-making heuristics and biases similar to humans across four tested domains

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether GPT-3.5 exhibits decision-making heuristics and biases similar to humans. Using novel prompts, researchers tested ChatGPT's responses across four studies: anchoring heuristic, representativeness and availability heuristics, framing effects, and endowment effect. Results showed that ChatGPT consistently displayed these decision effects in similar patterns to human participants. For example, in the framing effect study, ChatGPT rated medical drug efficacy as 5 in positive frames and 4 in negative frames, mirroring human responses. The findings suggest that linguistic patterns alone may be sufficient to generate decision effects, even without the cognitive and affective processes typically thought to drive such effects in humans.

## Method Summary
The researchers created novel prompts for four decision-making heuristics and tested them on both ChatGPT-3.5 and human participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk. For each study, they conducted 30 trials with ChatGPT (using fresh chat sessions for each trial) and recruited 40-60 human participants per study. The prompts were carefully designed to test specific heuristics while avoiding potential contamination from training data. Responses were statistically analyzed and compared between the LLM and human subjects to assess the presence and magnitude of decision effects.

## Key Results
- ChatGPT showed consistent anchoring effects, with mean estimates of 20.83 for low anchors versus 105.97 for high anchors, mirroring human patterns
- In framing studies, ChatGPT rated medical drug efficacy as 5 in positive frames and 4 in negative frames across all 11 trials, closely matching human responses
- ChatGPT displayed the endowment effect, showing increased valuation of items it "owned" compared to identical items it didn't own

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LLMs display human-like heuristics without cognitive or affective processes.
- Mechanism: LLMs learn linguistic patterns from training data that encode statistical regularities associated with human decision-making biases, allowing them to reproduce these biases through pattern completion rather than through genuine cognitive processes.
- Core assumption: Language patterns alone can be sufficient to generate decision effects, even without the underlying cognitive/affective mechanisms typically thought to drive them.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The fact that an LLM – which lacks these processes – also shows such effects invites consideration of the possibility that language may play a role in generating these effects in humans."
  - [section] "rather, we propose that the decision-making-related properties of LLMs are profoundly relevant to anyone interested in human cognition, for they can begin to illuminate whether heuristics and biases are driven by cognitive and affective processes that are largely encapsulated from language (in which case an LLM would not show such effects) or whether they might, at least in part, driven by patterns inherent in language (in which case an LLM would show such effects)."
  - [corpus] Found 25 related papers with average neighbor FMR=0.496, suggesting moderate relevance of language patterns to decision heuristics in LLMs.
- Break condition: If linguistic patterns in training data don't capture the statistical regularities associated with human biases, or if RLHF explicitly removes these patterns.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Anchoring effect occurs through sensitivity to contextual numbers in prompts.
- Mechanism: When LLMs encounter numerical values in context, they treat these as statistically relevant information for prediction, leading to estimates that are influenced by these "anchors" even when they are arbitrary.
- Core assumption: LLMs treat numbers in context as predictive features rather than filtering out arbitrary numerical information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Mean estimates for the low anchor condition were 20.83 (95% CI: 13.92, 27.74) for ChatGPT and 22.5 (95% CI: 15.20, 29.80) for human participants. Mean estimates for the high anchor condition were 105.97 (95% CI: 87.40, 124.54) for ChatGPT and 80.50 (95% CI: 60.44, 100.56) for human participants."
  - [section] "The anchoring effect was visible in both ChatGPT and in human subjects. For ChatGPT, the low anchor condition was different from the high anchor condition t(58) = 8.78, p < 0.0001."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows moderate relatedness to language patterns affecting numerical reasoning in LLMs.
- Break condition: If LLMs are explicitly trained to ignore numerical context or if prompt engineering removes numerical sensitivity.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Framing effects emerge from learned associations between linguistic frames and outcome evaluations.
- Mechanism: LLMs learn from training data that positive and negative frames are statistically associated with different evaluations of identical information, leading them to reproduce these associations when generating responses.
- Core assumption: Training data contains consistent patterns where the same information framed positively receives different evaluations than when framed negatively.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "In all 11 trials in the positive frame, ChatGPT rated the efficacy of the medicine to be 5 (1-7 scale), and in all 11 trials in the negative frame, it rated the efficacy of the medicine to be 4."
  - [section] "Human participants followed a similar pattern. In the positive frame, they rated the efficacy of the medicine to be 5.2 (95% CI: 5.03-5.37) and in the negative frame, they rated the efficacy to be 4.6 (95% CI: 4.35-4.85)."
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence in corpus for framing effects specifically, though related language patterns exist.
- Break condition: If training data lacks consistent frame-outcome associations or if RLHF explicitly removes frame sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the cognitive and affective processes underlying human decision-making heuristics compare to the linguistic patterns driving similar effects in LLMs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that human heuristics are driven by cognitive and affective processes, while LLMs rely on linguistic patterns, and invites consideration of whether language plays a role in human heuristics.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a direct comparison or empirical evidence to determine the relative contribution of linguistic patterns versus cognitive/affective processes in human heuristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental studies comparing human decision-making under conditions that manipulate linguistic cues versus cognitive/affective states, and neuroimaging studies to identify the neural correlates of these processes during heuristic-driven decisions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent do linguistic patterns in LLMs influence the development of abstract concepts that drive decision effects, as opposed to simply learning regularities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the possibility that LLMs might implicitly learn to form abstract concepts that allow them to display decision effects, but this is not directly tested.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide evidence to distinguish between LLMs learning linguistic patterns versus developing abstract concepts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Studies comparing the decision-making of LLMs trained on different types of linguistic data (e.g., highly structured vs. unstructured) and experiments testing the transferability of learned heuristics to novel contexts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How generalizable are the findings of decision heuristics in GPT-3.5 to other LLMs and to different domains of decision-making?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that it focused on GPT-3.5 and used novel prompts, and calls for more systematic testing across other models and domains.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to one LLM and a specific set of decision effects, and the prompts used are novel and not broadly researched.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replication studies with other LLMs, testing across a wider range of decision effects and domains, and using established prompts from the decision-making literature.

## Limitations

- The use of novel prompts means results may not directly map to established psychological paradigms
- Findings are specific to ChatGPT-3.5 and may not generalize to other LLM architectures or more recent versions
- The role of RLHF in shaping these responses remains unclear - some effects might be artifacts of alignment training

## Confidence

- High confidence: ChatGPT shows consistent anchoring effects and framing effects similar to humans
- Medium confidence: The representativeness and endowment effects replicate human patterns, though with slightly more variability
- Low confidence: The mechanism explanation (language patterns vs. cognitive processes) remains speculative without direct experimental evidence
