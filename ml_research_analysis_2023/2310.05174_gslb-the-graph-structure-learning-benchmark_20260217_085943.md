# GSLB: The Graph Structure Learning Benchmark

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2310.05174
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05174
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: First comprehensive benchmark evaluating 16 state-of-the-art GSL algorithms across 20 diverse datasets

## Executive Summary
GSLB introduces the first comprehensive benchmark for Graph Structure Learning (GSL), integrating 16 state-of-the-art algorithms and 20 diverse datasets covering homogeneous/heterogeneous node-level and graph-level tasks. The benchmark systematically evaluates GSL algorithms from three dimensions: effectiveness, robustness, and complexity. Results show that GSL generally improves node-level task performance, especially on heterophilic graphs, with unsupervised GSL algorithms (e.g., SUBLIME) often achieving optimal results. The benchmark also reveals that GSL models demonstrate strong robustness in scenarios with few labels and against random noise and topology attacks, though they have significantly higher time and space complexity compared to GCN, limiting scalability.

## Method Summary
GSLB is a benchmark framework that evaluates 16 state-of-the-art graph structure learning algorithms across 20 diverse graph datasets. The framework assesses algorithms on node-level classification (homogeneous and heterogeneous), graph-level classification, and three evaluation dimensions: effectiveness (performance metrics like accuracy and F1 scores), robustness (against label scarcity, noise, and topology attacks), and complexity (time and space complexity analysis). The benchmark uses default hyperparameters for each algorithm and provides visualization tools for analyzing learned graph structures.

## Key Results
- GSL improves node-level task performance, especially on heterophilic graphs, with unsupervised algorithms like SUBLIME achieving optimal results
- GSL demonstrates strong robustness in scenarios with few labels and against random noise and topology attacks, with self-supervised methods showing particular resilience
- GSL algorithms have significantly higher time and space complexity (O(N^2)) compared to GCN, limiting scalability to large graphs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Graph structure learning improves node-level task performance especially on heterophilic graphs by learning new graph structures that increase homophily
- Mechanism: Traditional GNNs assume homophily, but heterophilic graphs violate this assumption. GSL learns new graph structures that connect nodes with similar labels, effectively increasing the homophily ratio and improving message passing
- Core assumption: The learned graph structure better reflects the true relationships between nodes than the original structure
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "GSL generally brings performance improvement for node-level tasks, especially on heterophilic graphs"
  - [section 4.2] "GSL can improve significantly on heterophily graph datasets by learning new graph structures based on downstream tasks and specific learning objectives, thus enhancing the homophily of the graph"
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers discuss structure learning but don't provide direct evidence for this specific mechanism

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Unsupervised GSL algorithms (e.g., SUBLIME) achieve optimal results by leveraging abundant unlabeled information through contrastive learning
- Mechanism: Labels alone are insufficient for learning optimal graph structures. Unsupervised GSL uses self-supervision to extract information from unlabeled data, creating richer representations that guide structure learning
- Core assumption: Unlabeled data contains valuable structural information that can improve graph learning
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "It learns graph structure through contrastive learning in an unsupervised manner. As mentioned in the recent literature [10], optimizing graph structures solely based on label information is insufficient. Leveraging a large and abundant amount of unlabeled information can enhance the performance of GSL."
  - [abstract] "unsupervised GSL algorithms (e.g., SUBLIME) often achieving optimal results"
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers mention self-supervision but don't provide direct evidence for this specific contrastive learning mechanism

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: GSL demonstrates strong robustness in scenarios with few labels and against random noise and topology attacks by learning denser structures with cleaner community boundaries
- Mechanism: GSL learns graph structures that are denser and have clearer community boundaries, which facilitates better propagation of supervision signals and makes the learned structure more resilient to noise and attacks
- Core assumption: The learned denser structures with clearer boundaries are more robust to various types of noise and attacks
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "GSL models demonstrate strong performance in scenarios with few labels and against random noise and topology attacks, with self-supervised methods showing particular resilience"
  - [section 4.5] "We have observed that GSL learns denser and more distinct graph structures, which facilitates the propagation of supervision signals"
  - [section 4.5] "We found that self-supervised graph structure modeling methods (e.g., STABLE and SUBLIME) show excellent performance on corrupted graph structure datasets"
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers discuss robustness but don't provide direct evidence for this specific mechanism of denser structures improving robustness

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and message passing
  - Why needed here: GSL works by optimizing the computation graph structure that GNNs use for message passing. Understanding how GNNs aggregate information from neighbors is fundamental to understanding how GSL improves performance
  - Quick check question: How do GNNs typically aggregate information from neighboring nodes in the original graph structure?

- Concept: Homophily vs heterophily in graphs
  - Why needed here: GSL's effectiveness on heterophilic graphs is a key finding. Understanding the difference between homophilic (connected nodes have similar labels) and heterophilic (connected nodes have different labels) graphs is crucial for understanding when GSL helps

  - Why needed here: Unsupervised GSL algorithms like SUBLIME use contrastive learning. Understanding how contrastive learning works and how it can be applied to graph structure learning is important for understanding these methods
  - Quick check question: How does contrastive learning typically work in the context of graph representation learning?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can GSL algorithms be made scalable to handle large-scale graphs while maintaining effectiveness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that most GSL algorithms have high time and space complexity (O(N^2)), making them challenging to deploy on large-scale graphs.
- Why unresolved: While some methods like IDGL-ANCH and Fast-GRCN attempt to reduce complexity, a general solution for scalable GSL remains an open problem.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and empirical validation of GSL algorithms with sub-quadratic complexity that maintain or improve performance on large graphs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between learned graph density and performance improvement in GSL?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper observes that learned structures are typically denser than original graphs, but visualization and property statistics suggest this doesn't necessarily increase homophily ratios.
- Why unresolved: The paper notes denser structures facilitate supervision signal propagation but doesn't establish a clear causal relationship between density and performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying graph density while controlling other factors to determine optimal density for different tasks and datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can unsupervised GSL methods be effectively combined with self-supervised learning to improve robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that self-supervised GSL methods (STABLE, SUBLIME) show excellent robustness against structure attacks and noise.
- Why unresolved: While promising results are shown, the paper doesn't explore combinations with other self-supervised techniques or provide theoretical understanding of why these methods work well.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of different self-supervised approaches integrated with GSL, along with analysis of the learned representations and their properties.

## Limitations

- GSL algorithms have significantly higher time and space complexity (O(N^2)) compared to GCN, limiting scalability to large graphs
- Performance gains on homophilic graphs (like Cora) are modest, suggesting effectiveness is context-dependent rather than universal
- Robustness claims are based on specific attack scenarios that may not capture all real-world adversarial conditions

## Confidence

- High confidence: GSL generally improves node-level task performance, especially on heterophilic graphs; GSL shows strong robustness in scenarios with few labels and against random noise and topology attacks; GSL algorithms have significantly higher time and space complexity compared to GCN
- Medium confidence: Unsupervised GSL algorithms (e.g., SUBLIME) achieve optimal results through contrastive learning; GSL improves performance by learning structures that increase homophily; The learned denser structures with clearer boundaries improve robustness
