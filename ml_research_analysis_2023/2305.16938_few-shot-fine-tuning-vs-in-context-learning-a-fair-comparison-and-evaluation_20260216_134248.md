# Few-shot Fine-tuning vs. In-context Learning: A Fair Comparison and Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16938
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16938
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Controlling for model size and number of examples reveals that fine-tuning can match or exceed in-context learning's out-of-domain performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical gap in comparing few-shot fine-tuning (FT) and in-context learning (ICL) for adapting pre-trained language models. Prior comparisons often used models of different sizes, leading to questions about whether observed differences in out-of-domain generalization were inherent to the methods or experimental artifacts. The authors conduct a fair comparison using OPT models ranging from 125M to 30B parameters, controlling for both model size and number of examples (2-128) on NLI and paraphrase identification tasks. Their results demonstrate that when experiments are properly controlled, fine-tuning can achieve similar or better in-domain and out-of-domain performance than ICL. The study also reveals high variance in both methods and identifies key factors affecting performance, including model selection strategy and training data quantity.

## Method Summary
The authors compare few-shot fine-tuning and in-context learning using OPT models of seven different sizes (125M to 30B parameters). For fine-tuning, they implement pattern-based fine-tuning (PBFT) with a minimal pattern and Yes/No verbalizer, training for 40 epochs with learning rate 1e-5 and warmup. In-context learning uses 16 examples with a gpt-3 pattern and Yes/No verbalizer. The comparison is conducted on MNLI and RTE for NLI, QQP for paraphrase identification, and HANS as an out-of-domain challenge dataset. Model selection is performed based on out-of-domain validation performance to evaluate generalization capabilities.

## Key Results
- When controlling for model size and example count, fine-tuning can match or exceed in-context learning's out-of-domain performance.
- Both methods show high variance and depend significantly on model size and number of examples.
- Model selection strategy critically impacts out-of-domain generalization, with OOD-based selection yielding better results than in-domain selection.
- Larger models benefit more from increased training data, with OOD performance improving as sample size grows.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Controlling for model size and number of examples reveals that fine-tuning can match or exceed in-context learning's out-of-domain performance.
- Mechanism: By ensuring experimental fairness—using identical model sizes, sample counts, and patterns—the study isolates the adaptation method's intrinsic capabilities from artifacts of mismatched conditions.
- Core assumption: Differences in prior OOD performance were due to experimental setup rather than fundamental method weaknesses.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Prior comparisons used models of different sizes, leading to questions about whether observed differences in out-of-domain (OOD) generalization are inherent to the methods or experimental artifacts."
  - [section] "Unfortunately, previous comparisons of the two approaches were done using models of different sizes. This raises the question of whether the observed weaker out-of-domain generalization of fine-tuned models is an inherent property of fine-tuning or a limitation of the experimental setup."
- Break condition: If model size or example count is not strictly controlled, observed differences in OOD generalization may reflect setup artifacts rather than method capability.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Model selection strategy significantly influences out-of-domain generalization in fine-tuning.
- Mechanism: Selecting checkpoints based on out-of-domain validation performance yields models that generalize better to challenge datasets than those selected on in-domain performance.
- Core assumption: The best in-domain checkpoint does not necessarily generalize well to out-of-domain data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also find that both approaches improve as we increase model size, and that, for the models and datasets we consider, FT often generalizes even better than ICL."
  - [section] "Our results show that when performing model selection according to in-domain performance, only the largest models achieve good OOD performance. On the other hand, when performing model selection according to OOD performance, smaller models can also generalize well."
- Break condition: If OOD validation data is unavailable or too small, in-domain selection may be the only feasible option, potentially sacrificing OOD performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Increasing training data size improves out-of-domain generalization for larger fine-tuned models.
- Mechanism: Larger models benefit more from additional training examples, leading to better OOD performance as sample size grows.
- Core assumption: The relationship between in-domain and OOD performance depends on both model size and training data quantity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also find that both approaches improve as we increase model size, and that, for the models and datasets we consider, FT often generalizes even better than ICL."
  - [section] "On the other hand, for larger models, OOD performance improves as the amount of training data increases."
- Break condition: If model size is too small, additional data may not improve OOD performance, consistent with prior work showing limited benefits for smaller models.

## Limitations
- Results may not generalize to other architectures (e.g., GPT, T5) or task types beyond NLI and paraphrase identification.
- High variance across runs suggests both methods remain sensitive to implementation details like pattern choice and data seed.
- Assumes availability of representative out-of-domain validation sets, which may not always be practical in real-world applications.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core finding that experimental fairness (controlling model size and example count) is essential for valid method comparison. This is well-supported by direct comparisons and clear discussion of prior methodological issues.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that fine-tuning can match or exceed ICL's OOD performance when models are comparably sized. While supported by the main experiments, the high variance and limited task diversity suggest caution in generalizing this conclusion.
- **Medium Confidence**: The assertion that larger models benefit more from increased training data for OOD generalization. This is observed in the experiments but relies on a relatively narrow range of model sizes and datasets.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture replication**: Repeat the controlled comparison using models from other families (e.g., GPT, T5) to test generalizability beyond OPT.
2. **Pattern and seed sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary pattern choices and random seeds to quantify their impact on performance variance and method ranking.
3. **OOD validation data availability study**: Test the impact of model selection strategy when OOD validation data is limited or unavailable, comparing in-domain vs. last-checkpoint selection under these constraints.
