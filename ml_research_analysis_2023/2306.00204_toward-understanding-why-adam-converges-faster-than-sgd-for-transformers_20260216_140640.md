# Toward Understanding Why Adam Converges Faster Than SGD for Transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00204
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00204
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Directional sharpness explains Adam's faster convergence through coordinate-wise gradient imbalance

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why Adam converges faster than SGD for transformer models by introducing the concept of directional sharpness - a measure of curvature along optimization update directions. The authors demonstrate that adaptive algorithms like Adam have significantly lower directional sharpness compared to SGD, primarily due to imbalanced gradient distributions across coordinates. To address this, they propose coordinate-wise clipping as a simple technique that reduces directional sharpness and improves convergence for various optimization algorithms. The work provides both theoretical analysis and empirical validation across multiple transformer architectures and tasks.

## Method Summary
The method involves computing directional sharpness using Hessian-vector products to measure the curvature of the loss landscape along optimization update directions. The authors analyze gradient distributions across coordinates to identify the source of sharpness differences between algorithms. Coordinate-wise clipping is implemented by removing a fraction of coordinates with largest gradients before computing updates. Experiments train transformer models (T5 for machine translation, GPT-Neo for language modeling) with various optimizers and their clipped variants, comparing convergence speed and directional sharpness metrics.

## Key Results
- SGD exhibits much higher directional sharpness than adaptive algorithms like Adam on transformers
- Only a small fraction of coordinates account for most of SGD's directional sharpness
- Coordinate-wise clipping significantly reduces directional sharpness and improves convergence for all tested algorithms
- Clipping thresholds differ by algorithm (10% for SGD, 50% for Adam/Adafactor)
- The sharpness reduction effect of adaptive coordinate-wise scaling is identified as the key reason for Adam's practical success

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Directional sharpness is a key indicator of optimization algorithm performance in local geometry.
- Mechanism: The update direction's curvature along the optimization path determines how large a step size can be taken before the loss increases, with lower directional sharpness allowing larger effective step sizes.
- Core assumption: The second-order Taylor expansion accurately models local loss behavior and the directional sharpness term dominates step size selection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We argue that the performance of optimization algorithms is closely related to the directional sharpness of the update steps"
  - [section] "We define the directional sharpness of a function f at x in the direction v ∈ Rd, ∥v∥2 = 1 as v⊤∇2f (x)v"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct discussion of directional sharpness in corpus neighbors
- Break condition: If the function landscape is highly non-quadratic or if higher-order terms dominate the local behavior.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adaptive algorithms like Adam have lower directional sharpness than SGD due to coordinate-wise gradient imbalance.
- Mechanism: A small fraction of coordinates contribute disproportionately to directional sharpness, and adaptive algorithms' coordinate-wise scaling mitigates this by normalizing these large gradients.
- Core assumption: The Hessian coordinates are positively correlated with gradient coordinates, creating concentrated sharpness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "SGD has much worse directional sharpness compared to adaptive algorithms" and "only a small fraction of the coordinates causes the bad sharpness"
  - [section] "We observe that only a small fraction of the coordinates account for most of SGD's directional sharpness"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors discuss Adam vs SGD but not coordinate-wise sharpness specifically
- Break condition: If gradient-Hessian correlation is negative or if coordinate-wise scaling is ineffective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Coordinate-wise clipping reduces directional sharpness by removing large gradient coordinates that contribute most to sharpness.
- Mechanism: Clipping thresholds based on gradient distribution remove the top-k% of gradient coordinates, which are the primary contributors to directional sharpness, allowing larger effective step sizes.
- Core assumption: The top coordinates by absolute value are also the primary contributors to directional sharpness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We propose to use coordinate-wise clipping as a solution to SGD and other optimization algorithms" and "clipping significantly reduces the worst-case directional sharpness"
  - [section] "We observe that the sharpness is also concentrated in the large coordinates in the gradient, and clipping those coordinates can significantly decrease directional sharpness"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct discussion of clipping in corpus neighbors
- Break condition: If clipping removes too many coordinates and significantly degrades gradient correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific properties of transformer architectures make them particularly susceptible to the high directional sharpness problem observed with SGD?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that the directional sharpness properties appear to be transformer-specific, as evidenced by the ResNet experiments showing different behavior.
- Why unresolved: While the paper observes that transformers exhibit this property while ResNets do not, it doesn't deeply analyze what architectural features of transformers cause this phenomenon. The authors suggest this warrants further theoretical analysis of transformer architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed comparative studies of gradient-Hessian correlations across different neural network architectures, focusing on transformer-specific features like self-attention mechanisms and layer normalization.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal clipping threshold strategy that balances the trade-off between gradient correlation and directional sharpness across different optimization algorithms and tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses different clipping fractions (10% for SGD/normalized SGD, 50% for Adam/Adafactor) but acknowledges that finding optimal thresholds requires further investigation.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses empirically determined thresholds rather than theoretically derived ones, and the optimal strategy likely depends on task, model architecture, and optimization algorithm in complex ways.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying clipping thresholds across multiple tasks and architectures, coupled with theoretical analysis of the gradient correlation-directional sharpness trade-off.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the directional sharpness metric relate to other convergence and generalization properties of neural network optimization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces directional sharpness as a proxy for optimization performance but doesn't fully explore its relationship to generalization, sharpness-aware minimization, or edge-of-stability phenomena.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates directional sharpness's utility for understanding optimization speed, it doesn't investigate whether this metric also predicts generalization performance or relates to other geometric properties of loss landscapes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies correlating directional sharpness with test error, sharpness-aware metrics, and stability properties across multiple training runs and architectures.

## Limitations

- The analysis focuses primarily on transformer architectures, with limited validation on other model types like ResNets
- The theoretical framework relies on second-order Taylor expansion assumptions that may not hold in highly non-convex regions
- Coordinate-wise clipping, while effective, requires careful threshold selection and may not generalize optimally across all tasks
