# Learning to Substitute Ingredients in Recipes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.07960
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07960
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Graph-based ingredient substitution model (GISMo) improves mean reciprocal rank by 14% over baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses ingredient substitution in recipes by introducing GISMo, a Graph-based Ingredient Substitution Module that combines recipe context with generic ingredient relational information from FlavorGraph. The authors demonstrate that leveraging graph neural networks to propagate ingredient embeddings through FlavorGraph significantly improves substitution ranking compared to language model baselines. GISMo is integrated into an image-to-recipe generation pipeline, enabling recipe personalization through user-specified ingredient substitutions while maintaining recipe coherence.

## Method Summary
The method involves a three-component model: an ingredient encoder using Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) layers to propagate embeddings through FlavorGraph, a context encoder that aggregates ingredient embeddings from the recipe, and an ingredient substitution decoder (MLP) that scores candidate substitutions. The model is trained using self-supervised contrastive loss with negative sampling on the Recipe1MSubs dataset. GISMo is then integrated into an inverse cooking pipeline where predicted ingredients are fed to GISMo along with a mask indicating which ingredient to substitute, before generating the final recipe.

## Key Results
- GISMo achieves 14% improvement in mean reciprocal rank over baseline models
- The combination of graph structure and contextual information performs better than either alone
- GISMo successfully integrates into an image-to-recipe generation pipeline for recipe personalization
- Quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed system

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The graph-based ingredient substitution outperforms language models by incorporating relational ingredient data from FlavorGraph.
- Mechanism: GISMo uses Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) layers to propagate and update ingredient embeddings through the FlavorGraph, allowing the model to capture generic ingredient interactions and improve substitution predictions.
- Core assumption: Relational ingredient data (e.g., co-occurrence, flavor molecules) provides useful signals for plausible substitutions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Graph-based Ingredient Substitution Module (GISMo), a novel model that leverages the context of a recipe as well as generic ingredient relational information encoded within a graph to rank plausible substitutions."
  - [section 3] "GISMo consists of three components: 1) an ingredient encoder, 2) a context encoder, and 3) an ingredient substitution decoder. The input graph models generic ingredient and food molecule-ingredient relations."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; the paper cites FlavorGraph but no external validation of graph structure's impact on substitution accuracy.
- Break condition: If FlavorGraph contains noisy or irrelevant edges, the GNN layers could propagate misleading signals and degrade substitution quality.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using both recipe context and generic ingredient relations improves substitution ranking over using either alone.
- Mechanism: GISMo combines context embeddings (from recipe ingredients) with ingredient embeddings updated by the GNN, then scores candidate substitutions via a multi-layer perceptron.
- Core assumption: The recipe context captures the specific use case, while the graph provides general knowledge; combining them yields better predictions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "GISMo surpasses the best performing baseline by a large margin in terms of mean reciprocal rank."
  - [section 5.3] "When considering both the graph structure and contextual information (IE + CE + ISD), the kind of contextual information used becomes less important, with all variants performing similarly."
  - [corpus] Weak; the paper does not provide ablation evidence that either context or graph alone is insufficient.
- Break condition: If the context and graph provide redundant information, the MLP could overfit or the model could suffer from feature correlation issues.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Decoupling ingredient substitution from recipe editing allows leveraging compositional language models for personalization.
- Mechanism: GISMo is trained separately on substitution pairs and then plugged into the inverse cooking pipeline; the recipe generator uses pretrained language models to adapt instructions to the modified ingredient set.
- Core assumption: Language models can generalize to new ingredient sets at inference time due to their compositional nature.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We modify the pipeline such that predicted ingredients are fed to GISMo together with a mask indicating the ingredient to be substituted, as specified by the user."
  - [section 4] "At inference time, the personalized inverse cooking pipeline operates out of training distribution."
  - [corpus] Weak; no quantitative evidence that compositionality alone is sufficient for high-quality recipe adaptation.
- Break condition: If the recipe generator cannot handle large ingredient changes, the pipeline could produce incoherent or nonsensical recipes.

- Contrastive Loss
  - Why needed here: GISMo is trained using a self-supervised contrastive loss to maximize the score for valid substitutions and minimize for invalid ones.
  - Quick check question: How does the loss function treat negative samples in this setup?

- Multi-layer Perceptron
  - Why needed here: The ISD module uses an MLP to score candidate substitutions based on concatenated embeddings.
  - Quick check question: Why is concatenation of source, target, and context embeddings fed into the MLP?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Ingredient Encoder (GIN on FlavorGraph) → Context Encoder (averaged ingredient embeddings + optional CLIP title embeddings) → Ingredient Substitution Decoder (MLP scoring) → Inverse Cooking Pipeline (image encoder → ingredient decoder → GISMo → recipe decoder)
- Critical path: Image → Ingredient Decoder → GISMo (Ingredient Encoder + Context Encoder + ISD) → Modified Ingredients → Recipe Decoder
- Design tradeoffs: Using GIN vs simpler embedding lookup trades model complexity for potential accuracy gains; adding title embeddings increases computation but may improve context.
- Failure signatures: Low IoU between generated and ground-truth ingredients; recipe collapse with generic instructions; random substitutions ignored by the recipe generator.

  3. Measure IoU and PPL differences between ground-truth, GISMo, and random substitutions in the personalized inverse cooking pipeline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How does GISMo's performance vary when substituting multiple ingredients simultaneously versus one at a time?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "We consider a single ingredient substitution" and "Although this setup works well in our experiments, the users might want to substitute multiple ingredients at once."
  - Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates single ingredient substitutions, leaving multi-ingredient substitution performance unexplored.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing GISMo's performance on single vs multiple ingredient substitutions using appropriate metrics like MRR and Hit@k.

- Question: How does the substitution directionality (source→target vs target→source) affect GISMo's performance and the resulting recipes?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses "Are substitutions directional?" and finds that swapping source and target ingredients during training decreases validation MRR by 0.64 on average.
  - Why unresolved: While the paper shows swapping decreases performance, it doesn't explore the underlying reasons or how this affects recipe quality in practical applications.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of how directional vs non-directional substitutions impact both model performance metrics and qualitative recipe generation quality.

- Question: How does GISMo's performance generalize across different cuisines beyond the US-centric Recipe1M dataset?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes "Our work is based on the Recipe1M dataset that was scraped from mostly US-centric food webpages" and discusses potential biases.
  - Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges potential bias but doesn't empirically test GISMo's performance on non-Western recipes or cuisines.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing GISMo's performance (MRR, Hit@k) when tested on recipes from diverse global cuisines or datasets.

## Limitations

- The paper's claims about GISMo's effectiveness are based primarily on MRR improvement over baselines, but lacks strong ablation evidence that graph structure itself is the critical differentiator
- External validation of FlavorGraph's edge weights and structure is limited, with no clear evidence that the graph contains the right type of relational information for substitution tasks
- While the personalized inverse cooking pipeline shows qualitative improvements, there's limited quantitative evidence that substitutions actually improve overall recipe quality beyond what the language model could achieve alone

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core mechanism of using GIN layers to update ingredient embeddings and score substitutions via an MLP is well-specified and reproducible. The training procedure with contrastive loss is clearly described.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that GISMo improves MRR by 14% over baselines is supported by the reported numbers, but the exact implementation details of FlavorGraph edge weights and preprocessing steps introduce uncertainty in faithful reproduction.

**Low Confidence**: The claim that combining graph structure with context is the primary driver of improvement, rather than model capacity or training procedure differences, lacks strong ablation evidence.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study replication**: Reproduce the ablation results showing that using only graph structure or only context performs worse than their combination, with statistical significance testing across multiple runs.

2. **Graph structure importance**: Create a variant of GISMo using a random graph with the same degree distribution as FlavorGraph to test whether the specific relational information matters beyond just providing a graph structure.

3. **Substitution quality evaluation**: Conduct a human evaluation comparing GISMo substitutions against those generated by a strong language model baseline, using metrics like plausibility, creativity, and adherence to dietary constraints.
