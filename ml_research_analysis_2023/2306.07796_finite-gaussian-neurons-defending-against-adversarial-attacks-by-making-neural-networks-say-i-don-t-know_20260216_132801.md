# Finite Gaussian Neurons: Defending against adversarial attacks by making neural networks say "I don't know"

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07796
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07796
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Finite Gaussian Neurons (FGNs) defend against FGSM attacks while maintaining accuracy on real data, but fail against stronger PGD and CW attacks

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Finite Gaussian Neurons (FGNs), a novel neuron architecture designed to defend against adversarial attacks on neural networks. FGNs combine classical artificial neurons with Gaussian components to restrict neuron activity to finite areas near training samples, preventing overconfident predictions on out-of-domain inputs. The key innovation is a method to convert existing neural networks to FGN architecture without retraining while preserving behavior on real data. Experimental results show FGNs maintain high accuracy and confidence on real MNIST images while having lower confidence on randomized and FGSM adversarial images compared to classical networks. However, FGNs fail to defend against stronger PGD and Carlini-Wagner attacks on more complex tasks.

## Method Summary
FGNs extend classical neurons by multiplying their linear output with a Gaussian function centered on training data, creating a gating mechanism that restricts activity to regions near observed data. During conversion, existing networks are transformed by preserving classical weights and initializing large variances so Gaussian components are near unity for training samples. A variance regularization term λσ² is added to the loss function during training, encouraging the network to minimize variances and shrink activity zones. This creates smaller hyperspheres that cover training data but exclude adversarial perturbations. The approach claims to maintain accuracy on clean data while reducing confidence on adversarial examples and random inputs.

## Key Results
- FGN networks maintain high accuracy and confidence on real MNIST images while showing lower confidence on randomized and FGSM adversarial images
- The conversion method preserves classical network behavior on real data without requiring retraining
- FGNs fail to defend against PGD and Carlini-Wagner attacks on SPEECHCOMMANDS task
- Variance regularization with λ=10^-10 shows differential behavior between clean and adversarial inputs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gaussian Gating to Limit Out-of-Domain Activity
FGNs prevent overconfident predictions on out-of-domain inputs by zeroing neuron activity far from training data. Each neuron multiplies its linear output by a Gaussian function centered on a training sample. Inputs far from the center (relative to variance σ) yield Gaussian values near zero, suppressing the neuron's contribution. Core assumption: High-dimensional input space regions without training samples should produce negligible neuron activity. Break condition: If variance σ becomes too large during training or initialization, the Gaussian gating becomes ineffective and FGN behaves like classical neurons.

### Mechanism 2: Simple Conversion Preserves Original Behavior
Existing networks can be converted to FGN architecture without retraining while maintaining identical behavior on real data. During conversion, classical neuron weights are preserved and variance σ is set large enough that the Gaussian component is near 1 for all training samples, making FGN output identical to the original neuron. Core assumption: A single variance value can be chosen to preserve behavior across all training samples for each neuron. Break condition: If training data distribution is highly non-uniform, a single variance may not preserve behavior across all samples.

### Mechanism 3: Variance Shrinkage Adds Adversarial Resistance
Training with variance regularization shrinks neuron activity zones, reducing confidence on adversarial examples while maintaining accuracy on real data. A regularization term λσ² in the loss function pressures the network to minimize variances during training. This creates smaller activity zones that cover training data but exclude adversarial perturbations. Core assumption: Adversarial examples lie outside the minimal hyperspheres needed to cover real training data. Break condition: If λ is too small, variances won't shrink enough; if too large, network may underfit and lose accuracy on real data.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Classical neuron output → Gaussian gating function → Product output → Variance regularization
- Critical path: Conversion → Training with variance regularization → Evaluation on clean vs adversarial data
- Design tradeoffs: Larger σ preserves accuracy but reduces defense; smaller σ increases defense but may hurt accuracy
- Failure signatures: High confidence on random inputs (variances too large), low accuracy on real data (variances too small), no improvement over classical networks (conversion not properly implemented)
- First 3 experiments: 1) Convert MNIST classifier to FGN and verify identical predictions on validation set, 2) Train FGN on MNIST with variance regularization and measure confidence on clean vs randomized inputs, 3) Test FGSM attack success rate on classical vs FGN networks with various λ values

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do the boundaries of the FGN model in high-dimensional input space enable generalization to unseen real images while rejecting random images? The paper discusses the behavior of FGNs in high-dimensional spaces and their ability to generalize to validation data while rejecting random images, but does not fully explain the mechanism behind this phenomenon.

### Open Question 2
What are the specific factors that contribute to the FGN model's resistance to FGSM attacks, and why does this resistance diminish against stronger attacks like Carlini-Wagner and PGD? The paper demonstrates that FGNs offer some protection against FGSM attacks but do not defend against stronger PGD attacks, suggesting a need to understand the underlying factors contributing to this differential resistance.

### Open Question 3
How does the FGN architecture compare to other non-linear neuronal architectures in terms of flexibility and effectiveness in defending against adversarial attacks? The paper highlights the flexibility of the FGN architecture and its potential in building neural networks that do not make false-positive predictions over random inputs, but does not compare it to other non-linear architectures.

## Limitations
- FGNs fail to defend against PGD and Carlini-Wagner attacks on complex tasks like SPEECHCOMMANDS
- Effectiveness depends heavily on choosing appropriate regularization coefficient λ, which may require dataset-specific tuning
- Single variance parameter may be insufficient for high-dimensional spaces with complex distributions

## Confidence

**High confidence**: FGN conversion preserves classical network behavior on real data when properly initialized with large variances.

**Medium confidence**: Variance regularization during training can reduce confidence on FGSM adversarial examples while maintaining accuracy on clean data.

**Low confidence**: FGN provides meaningful defense against state-of-the-art adversarial attacks beyond simple FGSM in complex, high-dimensional tasks.

## Next Checks
1. **Sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary the regularization coefficient λ across multiple orders of magnitude to determine optimal values for different datasets and attack types.

2. **Covariance extension**: Replace the single variance parameter with full covariance matrices to test whether more expressive Gaussian gating improves defense against PGD and CW attacks.

3. **High-dimensional scaling**: Evaluate FGN performance on CIFAR-10 or ImageNet against PGD attacks to assess whether the Gaussian gating mechanism remains effective in spaces with thousands of dimensions.
