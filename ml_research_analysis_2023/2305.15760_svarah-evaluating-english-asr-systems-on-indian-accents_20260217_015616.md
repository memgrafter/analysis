# Svarah: Evaluating English ASR Systems on Indian Accents

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.15760
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15760
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Creates Svarah dataset with 9.6 hours of transcribed speech from 117 speakers across 65 districts in India, covering 19 native languages, and evaluates 6 open-source and 2 commercial ASR models

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical gap in English ASR benchmarks for Indian accents by introducing Svarah, a new dataset comprising 9.6 hours of transcribed speech from speakers across 65 districts in India with 19 different native languages. The dataset includes read speech, extempore speech, and everyday use case utterances across various domains. Through comprehensive evaluation of 6 open-source models (including Whisper, Wav2Vec2, HuBERT) and 2 commercial systems (Google and Microsoft), the authors demonstrate that Whisper performs significantly better than other models on Indian accents. The results reveal that all models perform worse on Svarah compared to L1 data, highlighting the need for more robust evaluation on diverse accents.

## Method Summary
The authors collected speech data using a mobile app with Karya platform from 117 speakers across 65 districts in India, covering 19 native languages. The dataset includes read speech, extempore speech, and everyday use case utterances. Transcriptions were created using Label Studio with Switchboard guidelines. Six open-source ASR models (Whisper, Wav2Vec2, HuBERT, WavLM, Data2Vec, NVIDIA Conformer) and two commercial systems (Google and Microsoft) were evaluated using Word Error Rate (WER) on Svarah and compared with performance on L1 data (LibriSpeech and SAA-L1). The evaluation framework measured performance across accent splits, data types, and domains.

## Key Results
- Whisper model trained on large amounts of diverse weakly supervised data performs significantly better than other open-source models on Indian accents
- All models perform worse on Svarah compared to L1 data, indicating the need for more robust evaluation on diverse accents
- Best models show low standard deviation across different Indian accents, but all models struggle with everyday use case utterances containing entities like brand names and IDs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Svarah improves ASR robustness by explicitly modeling accent diversity through data collection from speakers with 19 different native languages across 65 districts.
- Mechanism: The dataset captures the acoustic-phonetic variability induced by mother tongue interference, allowing models to learn accent-invariant representations when trained or fine-tuned on this data.
- Core assumption: Accent variation in Indian English is systematic and learnable from speech data rather than requiring explicit accent labels.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Svarah comprises both read speech and spontaneous conversational data, covering various domains... ensuring a diverse vocabulary." [corpus] Weak evidence - the dataset description is detailed but no analysis of accent similarity metrics is provided.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The diversity in training data sources (read speech, extempore speech, everyday use cases) creates more robust acoustic models that handle real-world variability.
- Mechanism: By including both controlled (read) and spontaneous speech, plus domain-specific utterances with named entities, the model learns to handle disfluencies, varied prosody, and entity recognition challenges.
- Core assumption: Spontaneous speech contains sufficient acoustic variation to improve generalization beyond read speech alone.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Svarah comprises both read speech and spontaneous conversational data, covering various domains... It also contains data corresponding to popular use cases such as ordering groceries, making digital payments, and using government services." [corpus] Moderate evidence - the paper describes the data collection but doesn't provide quantitative analysis of spontaneous vs read speech impact on model performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The use of weakly supervised web-scale data (Whisper) provides better accent robustness than smaller, cleaner datasets because it captures more diverse acoustic patterns.
- Mechanism: Large-scale noisy data with diverse speakers provides exposure to accent variations that would be underrepresented in smaller curated datasets, leading to better generalization.
- Core assumption: The diversity of web-scale data compensates for its noisiness in learning accent-invariant features.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We observe that the Whisper model which is trained on large amounts of diverse weakly supervised data performs significantly better than the other open-source models mentioned above." [corpus] Strong evidence - direct comparison results show Whisper outperforming other models on Svarah.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data collection (mobile app/Karya) -> Transcription (Label Studio/Switchboard) -> Model evaluation (WER calculation) -> Benchmark release
- Critical path: Data collection → Transcription → Model evaluation → Benchmark release
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Diversity vs. data quality: Choosing to collect from diverse speakers vs. ensuring perfect transcription quality
  - Domain coverage vs. dataset size: Including multiple domains and use cases vs. collecting more hours of data
  - Open-source vs. commercial models: Using accessible models for reproducibility vs. potentially better commercial solutions
- Failure signatures:
  - Low inter-transcriber agreement indicating ambiguous transcriptions
  - High variance in WER across accent groups indicating model bias
  - Poor performance on entity-heavy use cases indicating vocabulary limitations
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a baseline model (e.g., Wav2Vec2) on Librispeech and evaluate on Svarah to establish the performance gap
  2. Fine-tune the baseline model on Svarah to measure the benefit of accent-specific training data
  3. Test different data augmentation strategies (speed perturbation, noise addition) on the baseline to see if they can reduce the accent performance gap without additional data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can ASR models be improved to better recognize entities like brand names, bank names, food items, and document IDs in Indian-accented English?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that all models except commercial ones perform better on read and extempore data compared to everyday use cases, mainly because the latter contains many entities such as brand names, bank names, food items, document IDs, and so on.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that recognizing such entities when spoken in Indian accents is hard for existing ASR systems, even for the Whisper large model which has a WER of 11.2 on utterances from everyday use cases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating new ASR models or techniques specifically designed to handle entity recognition in Indian-accented English, and measuring their performance on datasets like Svarah.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the specific challenges faced by ASR models in recognizing accents from low-resource languages like Assamese, Bodo, and Nepali?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that models perform poorly for speakers who are native speakers of Assamese, Bodo, and Nepali, which are low-resource languages from the North-Eastern region of India.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies that these languages are low-resource and that models perform poorly for speakers of these languages, but it does not delve into the specific challenges faced by ASR models in recognizing these accents.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting detailed linguistic and acoustic analyses to identify the unique phonetic and prosodic features of these accents that pose challenges for ASR models, and developing targeted strategies to address these challenges.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of self-supervised models like Wav2Vec2, HuBERT, WavLM, and Data2Vec compare to end-to-end models like Whisper when trained on more diverse and larger datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that self-supervised models perform poorly, indicating that pre-training on large amounts of diverse unlabelled data is not as useful as fine-tuning on diverse data. It also notes that the Whisper model, which is trained on large amounts of diverse weakly supervised data, performs significantly better than other models.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests that the trade-off between the quality and volume of training data works favorably for the Whisper model, but it does not explore how self-supervised models would perform if trained on more diverse and larger datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training self-supervised models on more diverse and larger datasets, and comparing their performance with end-to-end models like Whisper on benchmarks like Svarah.

## Limitations
- The comparison between Svarah and L1 data is complicated by differences in domain, data collection methodology, and speaker demographics
- The evaluation focuses solely on WER without examining error types or accent-specific error patterns
- The lack of diversity analysis within the 19 native language groups means we cannot determine if certain Indian accents are better represented than others

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Whisper's superior performance on Svarah compared to other open-source models, and the general observation that all models perform worse on Svarah than on L1 data
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that Svarah's design (diverse native languages, spontaneous speech, everyday use cases) leads to more robust accent evaluation
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that "pre-training on large amounts of diverse unlabelled data is not as useful as finetuning on diverse data"

## Next Checks
1. Perform paired t-tests or bootstrap confidence intervals on the WER differences between models across accent groups to determine which performance differences are statistically significant
2. Categorize the specific types of errors (deletions, insertions, substitutions) made by each model on different accent groups and use case types to identify systematic patterns in model failures
3. Train a single model on a subset of Svarah accents and test on held-out accents to measure generalization capability, which would provide evidence for whether accent-specific adaptation is necessary or if a single robust model can handle all Indian accents
