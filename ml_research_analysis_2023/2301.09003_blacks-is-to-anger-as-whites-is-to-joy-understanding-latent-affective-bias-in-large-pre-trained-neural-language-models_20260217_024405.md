# Blacks is to Anger as Whites is to Joy? Understanding Latent Affective Bias in Large Pre-trained Neural Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09003
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09003
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Large pre-trained language models exhibit biased emotion predictions for different social groups, with intensity-level bias being more prevalent than class-level bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates affective bias in large pre-trained language models (PLMs) for emotion detection across gender, race, and religion domains. The study fine-tunes four popular PLMs (BERT, GPT-2, XLNet, T5) on the SemEval-2018 emotion detection dataset and evaluates them using synthetic and non-synthetic corpora. The research reveals statistically significant intensity bias across all models, with T5 showing the highest bias. Religious domains exhibit more bias than gender or race domains. The findings demonstrate that biased emotion predictions often align with societal stereotypes, such as associating African Americans with anger or Muslims with fear, highlighting the need for fairness-aware emotion detection systems.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes pre-training corpora for affect imbalance, fine-tunes PLMs on emotion detection, and evaluates bias using both synthetic and non-synthetic corpora. Four PLMs are fine-tuned on SemEval-2018 EI-oc dataset for emotion detection. The evaluation uses three corpora: EEC, BITS (synthetic), and CSP (non-synthetic). Bias is measured using class-level demographic parity (DP) and intensity-level measures including average difference of prediction intensity scores (avg.Δ), p-value, and average confidence score (ACS). The analysis examines gender, race, and religion domains with specific target terms for each.

## Key Results
- Corpus-level analysis reveals imbalanced emotion associations with social groups across all domains
- Prediction-level analysis shows statistically significant intensity bias across all four PLMs, with T5 showing highest bias
- Religious domain exhibits more bias than gender or race domains
- Biased emotion predictions often align with societal stereotypes (e.g., African Americans with anger, Muslims with fear)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Corpus-level affect imbalance propagates to prediction-level affective bias in PLMs
- Mechanism: Pre-training corpora contain imbalanced co-occurrence of emotions with social group terms. When PLMs are trained on these corpora, they learn these skewed associations, which then influence emotion predictions for sentences containing social group terms.
- Core assumption: Large-scale pre-training corpora accurately reflect societal biases encoded in language.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3.2]: Co-occurrence statistics show anger associated more with non-binary/female genders, joy with European American race, and fear with Muslim religion.
  - [section 5.2]: Observations of how corpus-level imbalances are reflected in model predictions (e.g., anger prediction intensities higher for African American race).
- Break condition: If pre-training corpora are balanced or if fine-tuning sufficiently overrides learned biases.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Intensity-based evaluation measures are more sensitive to affective bias than class-based measures
- Mechanism: Class-based measures only detect bias when emotion class predictions differ, while intensity-based measures detect bias in the magnitude of emotion predictions even when classes remain the same. This makes intensity measures more sensitive to subtle biases.
- Core assumption: Emotion prediction models output probability distributions over emotions, not just single class labels.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2.2]: Description of intensity-based measures (avg. Δ, p-value, ACS) that compare prediction intensity scores between sentence pairs.
  - [section 5.2]: Results showing class-based DP measure often fails to detect bias while intensity-based measures do.
- Break condition: If models output deterministic class labels rather than probability distributions.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Synthetic evaluation corpora are less effective at detecting affective bias than non-synthetic corpora
- Mechanism: Synthetic corpora contain template-based sentences with explicit emotion terms that don't reflect real-world language use. Non-synthetic corpora contain naturally occurring sentences that better capture the complexity of affective bias in real-world contexts.
- Core assumption: Real-world language use is more complex and nuanced than template-based synthetic sentences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2.1]: Comparison of EEC and BITS (synthetic) versus CSP (non-synthetic) corpora.
  - [section 5.4]: Observation that CSP, the non-synthetic corpus, identified more instances of affective bias than synthetic corpora.
- Break condition: If synthetic corpora are carefully designed to match real-world language patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the most effective methods for mitigating affective bias in large PLMs, particularly for emotion detection tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "Our initial attempt to identify affective bias in textual emotion detection models that utilize large PLMs, opens up the vast future scope towards affective bias mitigation, which we believe, can be better achieved by adopting more convenient solutions that utilize constraints while fine-tuning the prediction system (i.e., in-processing) and post-processing, rather than retraining or fine-tuning the PLM based affect prediction systems with unbiased corpora which are expensive and cumbersome [25]."
- Why unresolved: The paper only identifies the existence of affective bias and suggests future directions for mitigation, but does not explore specific mitigation techniques or evaluate their effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different mitigation techniques (e.g., adversarial debiasing, counterfactual data augmentation, fairness constraints) on PLM-based emotion detection models, measuring their impact on reducing affective bias while maintaining model performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does affective bias in PLMs generalize across different emotion detection tasks and domains beyond the four basic emotions studied?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on four basic emotions (anger, fear, joy, sadness) and specific domains (gender, race, religion), but does not explore the full range of emotions or other potential domains where affective bias might manifest.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited in scope and does not provide insights into how affective bias might affect more nuanced emotions or other social dimensions not covered in the analysis.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extending the study to include a broader range of emotions (e.g., love, surprise, disgust) and social dimensions (e.g., age, nationality, political affiliation), using comprehensive evaluation corpora and bias metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent does affective bias in PLMs reflect and amplify societal stereotypes, and what are the potential real-world consequences?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how biased predictions reflect societal stereotypes, such as the "Angry Black" stereotype and Islamophobia, and mentions potential harmful real-world consequences.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides examples of stereotype reflection but does not quantify the extent to which these biases amplify existing societal prejudices or examine the specific impacts on affected communities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting impact studies with affected communities to assess the real-world effects of biased emotion detection, and analyzing the correlation between model biases and societal prejudice metrics over time.

## Limitations
- Study focuses on English-language PLMs and Western-centric social categories, limiting generalizability
- Limited exploration of the full range of emotions and social dimensions beyond gender, race, and religion
- Uncertainty about whether biases are learned from pre-training data or emerge during fine-tuning

## Confidence
- High: Intensity-level bias is statistically significant across all four PLMs
- High: Non-synthetic corpora detect more bias than synthetic ones
- Medium: Religious domain exhibits more bias than gender or race (based on small number of terms)
- Medium: Correlation between corpus-level imbalance and prediction-level bias (causation not established)
