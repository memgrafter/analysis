# Multitask Instruction-based Prompting for Fallacy Recognition

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09992
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09992
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Multitask instruction-based prompting improves fallacy recognition across heterogeneous datasets, outperforming single-dataset approaches by 16% average margin in macro F1 scores

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of fallacy recognition across diverse datasets with different formats, genres, and fallacy types. The authors propose a unified multitask instruction-based prompting approach using T5 models that treats each dataset as a separate task. By training on multiple datasets simultaneously with natural language instructions, the model learns generalizable patterns for recognizing 28 unique fallacies. The experimental results show significant improvements over both single-dataset models and strong few-shot/zero-shot baselines, with the approach demonstrating robustness across different model sizes and prompt formats.

## Method Summary
The method involves training T5 transformer models (large and 3B sizes) on five fallacy datasets using instruction-based prompts that convert fallacy types into natural language instructions. The approach treats each dataset as a separate task in a multitask learning framework, allowing the model to learn generic traits of fallacy recognition rather than dataset-specific patterns. Prompts include either fallacy definitions or just fallacy names, and the model generates the fallacy type as output. Training involves converting all 28 unique fallacy types across datasets into consistent instruction formats, with evaluation using accuracy and macro F1 scores on individual datasets.

## Key Results
- Multitask fine-tuned models outperform task-specific models trained on single datasets by an average margin of 16% in macro F1 scores
- The approach beats strong few-shot (GPT-3) and zero-shot (UnifiedQA) baselines by average margins of 25% and 40% respectively
- Larger model sizes (T5-3B) and including fallacy definitions in prompts provide consistent performance improvements across datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The unified multitask instruction-based prompting approach improves fallacy recognition across heterogeneous datasets by leveraging shared semantic structures across fallacy types.
- **Mechanism**: Training on multiple datasets with diverse fallacy types, input formats, and genres allows the model to learn generic traits of fallacy recognition rather than overfitting to dataset-specific patterns. Instruction-based prompts provide structured representation of each fallacy type consistently.
- **Core assumption**: Fallacies share enough common semantic and structural features across datasets that a single model can learn to recognize them effectively when presented with varied examples.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "instruction-based prompting in a multitask setup based on the T5 model improves the results against approaches built for a specific dataset"
  - [section 3] "when a model is able to learn to recognize fallacy types from multiple datasets, it is more likely able to learn generic traits of fallacy types rather than learning characteristics specific to a single dataset"
- **Break condition**: If semantic overlap between fallacy types across datasets is minimal, the model would struggle to learn generalizable patterns and performance would degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Larger model sizes (T5-3B vs T5-large) provide better performance on fallacy recognition, particularly for complex fallacy types requiring contextual understanding.
- **Mechanism**: Larger models have more parameters and better pretraining coverage, allowing them to capture more nuanced contextual information needed for detecting fallacies like Red Herring and Strawman that require understanding of diversion tactics.
- **Core assumption**: The increased capacity of larger models translates directly to better performance on the fallacy recognition task, especially for fallacies requiring complex reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2] "increasing the model size (from T5-large to T5-3B both trained on all prompts) improves the overall results (especially macro F1) on all datasets"
  - [section 4.2] "A model with more parameters is in principle better in capturing more information during pretraining, which could be more useful for such fallacies that require more information beyond the provided segment"
- **Break condition**: If the task requires specific domain knowledge not captured in pretraining, simply increasing model size may not yield proportional improvements.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Including fallacy definitions in prompts provides better performance than just listing fallacy names, particularly for closely related fallacy types.
- **Mechanism**: Definitions help disambiguate between similar fallacies by providing explicit criteria for classification, reducing confusion between related concepts like Thought-Terminating Cliches and Loaded Language.
- **Core assumption**: The additional semantic information in definitions is more useful for the model than just having the names, especially when fallacies are closely related.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2] "including the definitions (T5-3B-Def) rather than just fallacy names (T5-3B-List) has higher accuracy and macro F1 scores in 4 out of 5 datasets"
  - [section 4.2] "In particular, it seems that definitions are more useful for fallacies that are closely related to other fallacies in one scheme where the definition helps in further clarifying the difference between the two"
- **Break condition**: If the model has already learned sufficient distinctions between fallacies through training examples alone, the additional information in definitions may provide diminishing returns.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data preparation → Prompt construction (with/without definitions) → Model training (T5-large or T5-3B) → Evaluation on individual datasets → Error analysis and refinement
- **Critical path**: Data preparation → Prompt construction (with/without definitions) → Model training (T5-large or T5-3B) → Evaluation on individual datasets → Error analysis and refinement
- **Design tradeoffs**: The choice between including definitions vs. just fallacy names in prompts involves a tradeoff between providing more semantic information (definitions) and keeping prompts simpler and more general (names only). Similarly, choosing model size involves balancing computational resources against performance gains.
- **Failure signatures**: Poor performance on specific fallacy types (e.g., Red Herring, Strawman) may indicate insufficient contextual understanding. Low macro F1 scores across datasets suggest the model is struggling with the diversity of fallacy types. Failure to generalize to new datasets indicates overfitting to specific training datasets.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train T5-large on all five datasets using only List prompts (fallacy names only) and evaluate performance on each dataset to establish baseline performance
  2. Repeat training with Def prompts (including definitions) and compare performance to identify the impact of including semantic information
  3. Train T5-3B on all datasets with both prompt types and compare to T5-large results to quantify the benefit of increased model capacity

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

2. How would the model performance change if we incorporated external knowledge (e.g., knowledge graphs, fact databases) into the fallacy recognition task?
3. How does the performance of the model change when considering multi-label instances where a single text segment contains multiple fallacies?

## Limitations

- The approach excludes "No Fallacy" classes due to severe class imbalance issues, limiting evaluation of complete fallacy detection
- The model requires substantial computational resources, particularly for the best-performing T5-3B variant, limiting practical deployment
- Results are based on five specific datasets and may not generalize to completely novel domains or informal fallacy detection in everyday discourse

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The superiority of multitask instruction-based prompting over single-dataset approaches (average 16% improvement in macro F1) is well-supported by the experimental design and results across multiple datasets.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that larger models (T5-3B vs T5-large) provide better performance is supported but could be influenced by the specific datasets used and may not generalize to all fallacy types.
- **Medium confidence**: The advantage of including definitions versus just fallacy names in prompts is demonstrated but may be context-dependent and require further validation across different domains.

## Next Checks

1. Evaluate the trained model on a held-out dataset from a completely different domain (e.g., social media discourse or academic writing) to assess whether the learned patterns truly generalize beyond the five training datasets.

2. Conduct a controlled study comparing model performance against human annotators on the same fallacy detection tasks to establish whether the model's performance represents true understanding or pattern matching.

3. Systematically test different prompt formats (definitions, names, examples, or combinations) across all datasets to isolate which components of the instruction-based approach contribute most to performance improvements.
