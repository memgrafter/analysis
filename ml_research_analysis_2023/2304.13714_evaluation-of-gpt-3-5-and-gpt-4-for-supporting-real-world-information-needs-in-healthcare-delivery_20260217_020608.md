# Evaluation of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 for supporting real-world information needs in healthcare delivery

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.13714
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13714
- Reference count: 40
- No response was deemed harmful by majority vote; concordance with prior consultation reports was low (8/66 for GPT-3.5, 13/66 for GPT-4)

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 responses to clinical questions from an informatics consultation service. Twelve physicians assessed each response for potential harm and concordance with prior consultation reports. No responses were deemed harmful by majority vote, demonstrating basic safety. However, concordance with prior reports was low, with GPT-4 performing slightly better than GPT-3.5 but still failing to meet specific information needs in most cases. The study highlights the gap between model safety and clinical utility, emphasizing the need for better prompt engineering and domain-specific grounding.

## Method Summary
The study used 66 clinical questions from an informatics consultation service (2017-2019) and prior consultation reports containing researcher interpretations. Simple prompts were submitted via API to GPT-3.5 ("Act as an AI doctor") and GPT-4 ("You are a helpful assistant with medical expertise"). Twelve physicians independently evaluated each LLM response for potential harm ("Are there elements of this answer that are so incorrect as to cause patient harm?") and concordance ("The response from GPT-# is similar to the informatics consult and would lead me to the same action") using majority vote thresholds (>6/12).

## Key Results
- No LLM responses were deemed harmful by physician majority vote
- Concordance with prior consultation reports: 8/66 for GPT-3.5, 13/66 for GPT-4
- Many responses lacked clear majority agreement on concordance
- Both models produced safe but often non-specific answers to clinical questions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GPT-4 produces safer clinical responses than GPT-3.5
- Mechanism: GPT-4 has larger model capacity and refined training data, reducing hallucination rates
- Core assumption: Model scale and data quality directly influence clinical safety
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "For no questions did a majority of physicians deem either LLM response as harmful"
  - [section] "Agreement with a known answer improved from 8 for GPT-3.5 to 13 for GPT-4"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct safety comparison between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in neighbor papers
- Break condition: If larger model produces more hallucinations in unseen domains, safety gains reverse

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Physician concordance with LLM responses is low because models lack domain-specific grounding
- Mechanism: Without EHR integration, LLMs generate generic answers missing clinical nuance
- Core assumption: Clinical questions require local patient context to be actionable
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "They often do not meet the specific information need of a given question"
  - [section] "GPT-4 responses contained hallucinated references, and physicians were divided on what constitutes harm"
  - [corpus] Weak - no neighbor paper directly addresses EHR grounding
- Break condition: If prompt engineering or retrieval augmentation is added, concordance improves significantly

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Physician disagreement on "harm" highlights subjective thresholds in AI evaluation
- Mechanism: Clinical harm perception varies by specialty, experience, and risk tolerance
- Core assumption: Harm is not an objective binary but a spectrum judged differently by individuals
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Physicians were divided on what constitutes harm"
  - [section] "The difficulty of objectively measuring usefulness of LLM provided responses to answer bedside clinical queries underscore the need for more elaborate functional evaluations"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct evidence in neighbor papers about subjective harm assessment
- Break condition: If standardized harm rubric is adopted, inter-rater variability drops

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific prompt engineering techniques could improve concordance between LLM responses and known answers from informatics consultation reports?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - "Additional customization to reduce hallucinated references... may already be possible" and "Advanced prompt engineering and grounding of future LLMs in local EHR records could produce significantly better responses"
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies this as a key area for improvement but does not test specific prompt engineering approaches beyond simple role-assigning prompts
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative study testing various prompt engineering techniques (chain-of-thought prompting, few-shot examples, retrieval-augmented generation) against the same clinical questions and evaluation framework used in this study

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different definitions of "harm" affect physician assessments of LLM responses in clinical settings?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - "Physicians were divided on what constitutes harm" and "The discrepancy suggests that what constitutes potential for harm in a response is difficult to pin-down and likely varies by physician"
- Why unresolved: The study used a binary "Yes/No" assessment but found significant variation in physician judgments, suggesting the definition itself needs refinement
- What evidence would resolve it: Qualitative research to develop a standardized taxonomy of potential harms in LLM clinical responses, followed by validation studies measuring inter-rater reliability with different physician groups

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the clinical utility of LLM responses that are deemed safe but lack concordance with known answers?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - "The difficulty of objectively measuring usefulness of LLM provided responses to answer bedside clinical queries underscore the need for more elaborate functional evaluations"
- Why unresolved: The paper found responses were largely devoid of harm but had low concordance rates, yet couldn't determine if these responses still provided useful information to clinicians
- What evidence would resolve it: Randomized controlled trial where clinicians make actual clinical decisions based on LLM responses versus standard practice, measuring outcomes like decision-making time, diagnostic accuracy, and patient outcomes

## Limitations
- Study relies on physician judgment without standardized evaluation criteria
- Evaluation framework conflates safety assessment with clinical utility
- Simple prompts without retrieval augmentation or domain-specific fine-tuning may underestimate LLM capabilities

## Confidence
- High confidence: Neither GPT-3.5 nor GPT-4 produced responses deemed harmful by physician majority vote
- Medium confidence: Concordance improved from 8/66 to 13/66 between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, but absolute values remain low
- Low confidence: Interpretation that LLMs "often do not meet the specific information need" conflates lack of agreement with lack of utility

## Next Checks
1. Replicate with standardized harm criteria using a structured rubric defining specific types of harm
2. Test retrieval-augmented generation with the same clinical questions and evaluation framework
3. Evaluate temporal stability of physician agreement on harm and concordance with repeated exposure and training
