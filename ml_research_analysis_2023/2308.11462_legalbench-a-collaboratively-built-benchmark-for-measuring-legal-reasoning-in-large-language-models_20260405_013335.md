# LegalBench: A Collaboratively Built Benchmark for Measuring Legal Reasoning in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2308.11462
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A benchmark of 162 legally-grounded tasks reveals GPT-4 significantly outperforms other LLMs on most legal reasoning types, but all models struggle with generating sound legal explanations and performance is highly sensitive to prompt engineering.

## Executive Summary
LegalBench is a new benchmark designed to evaluate the legal reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). It was constructed through a collaborative process where legal professionals identified, designed, and curated 162 tasks across six core reasoning types aligned with the IRAC framework (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) and expanded to include interpretation and rhetorical understanding. Evaluating 20 LLMs, the study finds that model performance varies widely by reasoning type and that prompt engineering—specifically the selection of in-context demonstrations—can cause performance swings exceeding 20 points. While GPT-4 leads on most tasks, all models, including top performers, exhibit weaknesses in generating legally coherent explanations even when final answers are correct. The benchmark provides an open-source, standardized platform for the AI and legal communities to assess and track progress in legal AI.

## Method Summary
The benchmark was built via an interdisciplinary collaboration with legal domain experts who contributed or adapted datasets to create tasks measuring specific legal reasoning skills. The 162 final tasks are mapped to six reasoning categories (issue-spotting, rule-recall, rule-application, rule-conclusion, interpretation, rhetorical-understanding) and span 36 data sources, primarily U.S. law and English-language texts. For evaluation, models are tested in a few-shot setting using prompts that include 0-8 demonstrations selected from a provided training split. Performance is measured using task-appropriate metrics: balanced accuracy for classification, exact match/F1 for extraction, and a combination of correctness and analysis quality for open-ended generation tasks, with the latter requiring manual grading by legal experts. No model training is performed; the study focuses on inference-time evaluation.

## Key Results
*   **Model Performance Gap:** GPT-4 consistently outperforms other models, showing a large margin over GPT-3.5 (e.g., 82.9% vs 60.9% on issue-spotting; 82.2% vs 58.5% on rule-application).
*   **Prompt Sensitivity:** Few-shot performance is highly volatile, with different in-context demonstration selections causing over 20-point accuracy differences on some tasks.
*   **Explanation Weakness:** All models, including GPT-4, struggle to generate legally sound explanations for their answers, revealing a gap between correct conclusions and valid reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Interdisciplinary construction with legal domain experts ensures that tasks measure legally valid and practically relevant reasoning.
*   **Mechanism:** Legal professionals design and hand-craft tasks based on their mental models of useful skills and interesting reasoning forms. This grounds the benchmark in actual legal practice rather than generic NLP tasks.
*   **Core assumption:** Legal experts accurately identify what constitutes meaningful legal reasoning and can create tasks that test those skills.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "Because these subject matter experts took a leading role in construction, tasks either measure legal reasoning capabilities that are practically useful, or measure reasoning skills that lawyers find interesting."
    *   [section] Section 4.1 describes the collaborative process: legal professionals contributed datasets they believed captured useful applications or interesting reasoning.
    *   [corpus] The corpus of related work (e.g., LegalBench-RAG, LegalBench.PT) shows that LegalBench has inspired follow-on benchmarks; this indirect evidence suggests its construction approach is valued and adopted.
*   **Break condition:** If legal experts are unavailable or lack consensus, tasks may become skewed toward narrow or idiosyncratic interpretations of legal reasoning, reducing generalizability.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Mapping tasks to the IRAC framework creates a common vocabulary that bridges legal and AI communities, enabling targeted evaluation of specific reasoning types.
*   **Mechanism:** Legal reasoning is decomposed into issue-spotting, rule-recall, rule-application, rule-conclusion, interpretation, and rhetorical-understanding. This categorization allows performance to be interpreted in terms of discrete legal skills.
*   **Core assumption:** The IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) structure accurately reflects how lawyers conceptualize legal reasoning and can be extended to cover additional forms like interpretation.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "popular legal frameworks for describing legal reasoning—which distinguish between its many forms—correspond to LegalBench tasks, thus giving lawyers and LLM developers a common vocabulary."
    *   [section] Section 3.1 details the IRAC framework and shows how each LegalBench task fits into it; Section 5 groups results by these categories.
    *   [corpus] The corpus showing specialized extensions (e.g., LAR-ECHR for European Court reasoning) suggests the typology can be adapted beyond U.S. law, supporting its versatility.
*   **Break condition:** If the IRAC model fails to capture certain legal reasoning nuances (e.g., analogical reasoning from case law), then tasks will not evaluate those capabilities, leading to Blind spots.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** For few-shot LLMs, prompt engineering—especially the choice of in-context demonstrations—causes substantial performance variance on legal tasks, often exceeding 20 points.
*   **Mechanism:** LLMs are highly sensitive to the content and ordering of few-shot examples. Different demonstrations provide different implicit inductive biases, affecting how the model approaches the task. Legal tasks, with their specialized vocabulary, likely amplify this effect.
*   **Core assumption:** LLMs do not have robust, task-agnostic legal reasoning; instead, they rely heavily on surface patterns in the prompt.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "prompt engineering significantly impacts performance, with some tasks showing over 20-point variance from different demonstration selections."
    *   [section] Section 5.4 experimentally demonstrates this by varying in-context samples for 8 tasks; Figure 3 shows large performance swings.
    *   [corpus] The corpus includes work on structured prompting for rule application (e.g., "Explainable Rule Application via Structured Prompting"), indicating that prompt design is recognized as a key factor.
*   **Break condition:** If task instructions become sufficiently detailed or if models improve in few-shot stability, the marginal impact of demonstration selection may diminish, reducing variance.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Legal reasoning taxonomy (IRAC)**
    *   **Why needed here:** To select appropriate tasks, interpret results, and communicate capabilities in legal terms. A new engineer must understand the six categories (issue-spotting, rule-recall, rule-application, rule-conclusion, interpretation, rhetorical-understanding) to properly use the benchmark.
    *   **Quick check question:** What is the difference between rule-application and rule-conclusion in LegalBench, and why are they evaluated differently?

*   **Concept: Few-shot learning with large language models**
    *   **Why needed here:** LegalBench tasks are designed for few-shot evaluation via prompting. Engineers need to know how to construct effective prompts, choose in-context demonstrations, and anticipate sensitivity to these choices.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why might varying the in-context demonstrations for a legal classification task cause over 20-point differences in accuracy?

*   **Concept: Adaptation of existing legal datasets**
    *   **Why needed here:** Many LegalBench tasks are transformed from pre-existing corpora (e.g., CUAD, MAUD). Understanding the original dataset’s structure and the transformation process is essential for correct usage and for extending the benchmark.
    *   **Quick check question:** How would you convert a long contract with span-level annotations into a short-context binary classification task for an LLM?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Task definitions -> Data loaders -> Prompt templates -> Inference engine -> Evaluation module -> Results aggregation
*   **Critical path:**
    1.  Choose a task from the task registry.
    2.  Load its train/test splits and inspect examples.
    3.  Review the base prompt and optionally modify (keeping track for reproducibility).
    4.  Run inference on test set using the chosen model and prompt.
    5.  Evaluate outputs against labels (automatically or via manual grading).
    6.  Record metrics; optionally compare across models/prompts.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Task size vs. cost:** Small tasks (<100 samples) are cheap but may yield high-variance estimates; large tasks (>1000) are more reliable but costlier, especially for manual evaluation.
    *   **Prompt specificity:** Highly specific instructions may yield higher scores but reduce generalizability; generic prompts are more challenging but better reflect true capability.
    *   **Automatic vs. manual evaluation:** Automatic metrics (exact-match) are scalable but may miss nuanced reasoning errors; manual inspection is accurate but not scalable for large-scale runs.
    *   **Open-source vs. commercial models:** Open-source models offer transparency and customization but may lag in performance; commercial models are powerful but pose cost, privacy, and reproducibility issues.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Extreme prompt sensitivity:** Performance varies by >20 points across different demonstration selections (as reported in Section 5.4). This indicates the model is not robustly learning the task.
    *   **High error rate on rule-application explanations:** Even when conclusions are correct, explanations may lack analysis (Section 5.3.3). Manual grading often reveals superficial reasoning.
    *   **Domain mismatch:** Models trained on generic corpora may fail on tasks with specialized legal jargon (e.g., Abercrombie trademark categories) unless prompts provide definitions.
    *   **Data leakage concerns:** Some tasks (e.g., NY judicial ethics from 2022–2023) may overlap with training data of newer models, artificially inflating scores; check model release dates.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline replication:** Run the provided base prompts for all 20 models on a diverse subset of tasks (e.g., one from each reasoning type) to reproduce the main results in Table 2. This validates the setup and provides a performance baseline.
    2.  **Prompt sensitivity analysis:** For a single binary classification task (e.g., `cuad_audit_rights`), generate 5 prompts with different random in-context examples (maintaining class balance). Run GPT-3.5 and Llama-2-7B to quantify variance and identify whether sensitivity is model-dependent.
    3.  **Reasoning diagonality test:** Evaluate a strong model (e.g., GPT-4) on every task to compute the correlation between performance on different reasoning types (e.g., does high rule-recall imply high rule-application?). This tests whether the taxonomy reveals distinct capabilities.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can LegalBench be expanded to include non-English legal systems and jurisdictions beyond U.S. Federal law, and what methodological adjustments are needed to ensure cross-jurisdictional validity?
*   **Open Question 2:** Can LegalBench be extended to evaluate legal reasoning involving subjective or ambiguous legal standards—such as "reasonable care" or "fair use"—where answers are not objectively correct but rather graded on reasonableness?
*   **Open Question 3:** What architectural or training modifications (beyond scale) specifically improve model performance on challenging LegalBench tasks like rule-application explanation generation or multi-step IRAC reasoning?
*   **Open Question 4:** How can prompt engineering for legal tasks be standardized and automated to reduce sensitivity to in-context demonstration selection, which caused over-20-point performance variance?
*   **Open Question 5:** Can LegalBench tasks be adapted or extended to evaluate multi-step, integrated legal reasoning (full IRAC chains) on longer documents (e.g., entire cases or contracts), moving beyond isolated reasoning types?

## Limitations
*   **Exact prompts undisclosed:** Specific few-shot demonstrations used per model are not provided; replication is infeasible and scores may vary >20 points with different examples.
*   **Manual grading subjectivity:** Rule-application analysis scores depend on human evaluators; inter-rater reliability was not assessed.
*   **U.S. law bias:** Tasks predominantly cover U.S. legal domains; generalizability to other jurisdictions or legal systems is limited.
