# Backpack Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16765
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16765
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 28
- A 170M-parameter Backpack language model matches the loss of a 124M-parameter Transformer while offering interpretable sense vectors for controllable text generation and debiasing.

## Executive Summary
Backpack Language Models represent each word as a context-dependent, non-negative linear combination of multiple non-contextual sense vectors. This architecture balances modeling performance with interpretability and control, allowing for interpretable interventions on word aspects. The authors demonstrate that trained sense vectors specialize to encode different aspects of a word, and that interventions on these vectors can predictably change model behavior.

## Method Summary
Backpack models represent each word in a sequence as a weighted sum of multiple sense vectors, where the weights are computed by a contextualization network (e.g., Transformer). Each sense vector captures a different aspect of word meaning, and the contextualization weights select the relevant senses for each context. The model is trained on OpenWebText using a language modeling objective, and interventions on sense vectors enable controllable text generation and debiasing.

## Key Results
- A 170M-parameter Backpack language model matches the loss of a 124M-parameter Transformer on OpenWebText.
- Sense vectors outperform even a 6B-parameter Transformer LM's word embeddings on lexical similarity evaluations.
- Simple algorithms for controllable text generation and debiasing by intervening on sense vectors demonstrate predictable changes in model behavior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Sense vectors enable non-contextual interventions that apply globally across all contexts.
- Mechanism: Each word is represented as a context-dependent linear combination of non-contextual sense vectors. Because the contribution to predictions is log-linear, interventions on sense vectors predictably change model behavior regardless of context.
- Core assumption: The sense vectors specialize during training to encode distinct aspects of a word's meaning, and the contextualization weights correctly select the relevant senses for each context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We can interpret a sense vector by inspecting its (non-contextual, linear) projection onto the output space, and intervene on these interpretable hooks to change the model's behavior in predictable ways."
  - [section 2.1] "The log-linear contribution of senses to predictions then implies that the interventions on sense vectors we demonstrate in Section 6 apply identically (up to a non-negative scalar weight) regardless of context."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only mentions related work on interpretability and control, but no direct evidence for this mechanism.
- Break condition: If sense vectors do not specialize to encode distinct aspects, or if the contextualization weights do not correctly select senses for each context, interventions will not apply predictably across all contexts.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Backpacks can match the performance of Transformers with a smaller contextualization network by leveraging the expressiveness of sense vectors.
- Mechanism: The contextualization weights select a non-contextual sense vector for each word, and the weighted sum of these sense vectors is fed into the output layer. This allows the model to represent complex functions as a weighted sum of basis vectors.
- Core assumption: The underlying contextualization network (e.g. Transformer) is expressive enough to compute the appropriate weights for each sense vector, given the sequence context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We train a 170M-parameter Backpack language model on OpenWebText, matching the loss of a GPT-2 small (124M-parameter) Transformer."
  - [section 2.1] "By contrast, Backpack models may seem limited in expressivity: the representations oi are scalar-weighted sums of non-contextual vectors C(xj)ℓ. Contextual relationships between sequence elements can only be expressed through the weights α = A(x1:n). Nevertheless, our experiments show that an expressive contextualization weight network can represent complex functions by weighted sums of sense vectors."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only mentions related work on language modeling, but no direct evidence for this mechanism.
- Break condition: If the contextualization network is not expressive enough to compute the appropriate weights, or if the sense vectors are not diverse enough to capture the relevant features, Backpacks will not match the performance of Transformers.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Sense vectors encode rich lexical information that can be leveraged for interpretability and control.
- Mechanism: After training, sense vectors specialize to encode different aspects of a word's meaning, such as relatedness, verb objects, or proper noun associations. This allows for interpretable interventions and controllable text generation by modifying the sense vectors.
- Core assumption: The training objective (e.g. language modeling) and architecture encourage the sense vectors to specialize to distinct aspects of word meaning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We find that, after training, sense vectors specialize, each encoding a different aspect of a word."
  - [section 5.1] "Empirically, trained Backpack models associate specific sense vector indices with different roles for prediction. We interpret these roles by picking a sense ℓ of a word x, and projecting this sense onto the word embeddings: E⊤C(x)ℓ ∈ R|V|."
  - [section 5.2] "Sense 12 (the 'synonym' sense) performs well across datasets, matching or outperforming embeddings like GPT-2-1.5B and GPT-J-6B."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only mentions related work on interpretability and control, but no direct evidence for this mechanism.
- Break condition: If the training objective or architecture does not encourage sense vector specialization, or if the sense vectors do not encode meaningful aspects of word meaning, they will not be useful for interpretability or control.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Word embeddings -> Sense vectors (via shared feed-forward network) -> Contextualization weights (via Transformer + self-attention) -> Weighted sum of sense vectors -> Output layer (linear transformation to logits) -> Cross-entropy loss

- Critical path:
  1. Compute sense vectors for each word in the sequence
  2. Compute contextualization weights using the contextualization network
  3. Compute the Backpack representation as a weighted sum of sense vectors
  4. Compute logits over the vocabulary using the output layer
  5. Compute loss using cross-entropy
  6. Backpropagate gradients to update model parameters

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of sense vectors: More sense vectors allow for more specialization and interpretability, but increase model size and computation
  - Contextualization network: More expressive networks (e.g. deeper Transformers) can better select the relevant sense vectors, but increase computation and memory
  - Parameter tying: Tying the input and output embeddings reduces model size, but may limit the expressiveness of the sense vectors

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor language modeling performance: Sense vectors do not specialize to meaningful aspects of word meaning, or contextualization network does not select the relevant senses
  - Lack of interpretability: Sense vectors do not encode interpretable aspects of word meaning, or the projections onto the vocabulary are not meaningful
  - Uncontrollable behavior: Interventions on sense vectors do not have the intended effect on model predictions

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a small Backpack language model on a toy dataset (e.g. synthetic language or small corpus) and visualize the sense vectors to verify that they specialize to distinct aspects of word meaning.
  2. Evaluate the language modeling performance of the Backpack on a held-out set and compare to a baseline Transformer to verify that the Backpack can match the performance with a smaller contextualization network.
  3. Perform a simple intervention on a sense vector (e.g. amplify or suppress a particular sense) and verify that the effect on model predictions is as expected, to test the controllability of the Backpack.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Do Backpack language models scale effectively with increasing model size and training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "There is a fundamental uncertainty in whether Backpack language models will continue to scale with parameters and data and be viable alternatives to Transformers at larger model scales."
- Why unresolved: The authors were unable to scale Backpack models larger due to computational constraints and only tested up to 170M parameters.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training and evaluating significantly larger Backpack models (e.g., 1B+ parameters) on extensive datasets and comparing their performance to equivalent-sized Transformer models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How well do Backpack language models perform across multiple languages, especially those with rich morphological structures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions "We also do not verify that Backpack language models perform well across multiple languages" and raises concerns about modeling languages with richer morphological structure than English.
- Why unresolved: All experiments were conducted on English text using the GPT-2 tokenizer, which may not handle morphological complexity well.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating Backpack models on multilingual datasets and languages with complex morphology, comparing performance to Transformer models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Are Backpack language models as compute-efficient as Transformers when considering the entire training and inference pipeline?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "At a glance, no" regarding compute efficiency, noting that Backpacks have extra parameters and take longer to converge during training.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides some timing benchmarks, it doesn't fully analyze the end-to-end computational costs including training time, memory usage, and inference speed across different hardware configurations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive benchmarking of Backpack models against Transformers across various hardware setups, measuring total computational cost from training through deployment.

## Limitations
- Sense vector specialization quality is primarily demonstrated qualitatively through visual inspection rather than systematic evaluation
- Generalizability of interpretability across different domains and vocabulary is unclear
- Scalability beyond 170M parameters is uncertain due to computational constraints

## Confidence
- Language modeling performance (Medium): The claim that Backpacks match Transformers with fewer parameters is supported by the 170M-parameter experiment, but the comparison is limited to one relatively small Transformer baseline.
- Interpretability and sense specialization (Medium): The paper provides compelling examples of interpretable sense vectors and successful interventions, but these are primarily qualitative demonstrations on selected examples.
- Controllable generation (High): The topic control experiments show clear quantitative improvements in accuracy, and the debiasing results demonstrate measurable reduction in gender bias.

## Next Checks
1. Design a systematic evaluation that measures how consistently sense vectors encode specific aspects across the full vocabulary, involving clustering words by their sense vector representations and measuring purity of semantic categories.
2. Train Backpack models across a range of sizes (e.g., 125M, 350M, 760M parameters) and compare their language modeling performance to Transformers of equivalent size to reveal whether efficiency advantages scale with model capacity.
3. Evaluate the same trained Backpack model on out-of-domain text (e.g., scientific writing, legal documents, technical manuals) to assess whether sense vector specialization and interpretability generalize beyond the training domain.
