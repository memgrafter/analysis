# How many dimensions are required to find an adversarial example?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.14173
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14173
- Reference count: 40
- This paper studies how adversarial vulnerability depends on the dimension of the subspace in which perturbations are constrained.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the relationship between adversarial vulnerability and the dimensionality of constrained perturbation spaces. Through both theoretical analysis and empirical experiments on MNIST, CIFAR10, and ImageNet, the authors demonstrate that adversarial success rates for PGD attacks scale predictably with the ratio of subspace dimension to ambient dimension. The work suggests that adversarial examples are endemic to locally linear models in high-dimensional spaces, and proposes that adversarial risk can be mitigated by restricting the number of manipulable dimensions.

## Method Summary
The authors use Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks with ℓp norm constraints to generate adversarial examples within randomly sampled d-dimensional subspaces of the input space. They measure adversarial success rate (ASR) across varying perturbation budgets (ϵ) and subspace dimensions (dim(V)) for three datasets (MNIST, CIFAR10, ImageNet) and corresponding models (2-layer CNN, ResNet9, ResNet50). The experimental methodology includes dimension-aware step size tuning for the PGD algorithm, and the results are compared against theoretical predictions derived from a simple linear classification model.

## Key Results
- Adversarial success rates for PGD attacks scale with (dim(V)/dim(X))^(1/q) where 1/p + 1/q = 1 for p > 1
- Success rates across different subspace dimensions can be collapsed onto a single curve through appropriate reparameterization
- The scaling relationship can be derived from a simple linear model, suggesting adversarial examples are endemic to locally linear models in high-dimensional spaces

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Adversarial success rate depends on the effective dimensionality of the perturbation space through the factor (dim(V)/dim(X))^(1/q) where 1/p + 1/q = 1 for p > 1.
- **Mechanism:** When adversarial perturbations are constrained to a subspace V, the adversary can only exploit directions in that subspace. The effective "reach" of adversarial perturbations in the full input space scales with the fraction of dimensions available, raised to the power 1/q that depends on the p-norm constraint.
- **Core assumption:** The relationship holds when the subspace V is sampled randomly and the adversarial examples are generated using PGD attacks with appropriate step sizes that scale with dimension.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "we show that the adversarial success of standard PGD attacks with ℓp norm constraints behaves like a monotonically increasing function of ε(dim(V)/dimX)^(1/q)"
  - [section] "We find that the relationships of ϵ to ASR for different dim(V) are nearly identical up to scaling: more specifically, suppose that CV1 : R → [0,1] and CV2 : R → [0,1] map adversarial budget to ASR when adversarial examples are constrained to subspaces V1 and V2 respectively. We find that C1((dim(V1)/dim(X))^(1/q)ϵ) ≈ C2((dim(V2)/dim(X))^(1/q)ϵ)"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers focus on different aspects of adversarial examples (combinatorial bandits, hubness) but don't directly address the dimensionality-scaling relationship.
- **Break condition:** The relationship breaks down for p = 1 (ℓ1 norm) as shown in Appendix B.5, and when the subspace V is not randomly sampled but adversarially chosen to maximize attack success.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The scaling behavior can be derived from a simple linear classification model where the margin in the subspace V determines adversarial success.
- **Mechanism:** For a linear classifier f(x) = w^T x + b, the minimum perturbation needed to cross the decision boundary in subspace V scales as |w^T x + b| / |w^T U|^q where U is the isometry from R^d to V. The expected value of |w^T U|^q scales as (d/n)^(1/q) |w|^q when w is random.
- **Core assumption:** The model behaves locally linearly near data points, and the weights w have random-like structure where coordinate magnitudes are roughly comparable.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "This functional form can be easily derived from a simple toy linear model... Lemma 6.3... min_{δ∈V} {|δ|^p | f(x+δ) = 0} = |w^T x + b| / |w^T U|^q"
  - [section] "Using a 'point estimate' and replacing |w^T U|^q with its mean (d/n)^(1/q)|w|^q, one would simplify to P(|w^T x + b| ≤ ϵ(d/n)^(1/q)|w|^q)"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related work on linear models and adversarial examples doesn't explicitly derive this dimensionality scaling.
- **Break condition:** When the model is highly nonlinear or when the data distribution is not well-approximated by the linear model assumption near decision boundaries.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The effective adversarial budget scales with the step size of the optimization algorithm, which must be tuned based on the subspace dimension.
- **Mechanism:** For PGD attacks, the step size η must be chosen to ensure that the algorithm can reach the boundary of the constraint set. The analysis shows η should scale as ϵ√T for ℓ2 and involve Φ^(-1)(1-1/d) for ℓ∞, where the dependence on d affects the effective budget.
- **Core assumption:** The gradient directions are sufficiently random and the projection step doesn't dominate the optimization dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "To set the step size η, we can adopt the heuristic that the δ_t behave like a random walk... E[|δ|^2_2] = η^2 T... Since we want to ensure the left hand side is at least ϵ, we obtain the step size η = ϵ√T"
  - [section] "In the case where p = ∞, by our IID-ness assumptions and the fact that by definition ˜g_t = sign g_t, the individual coordinates ˜g_{t,j} for j = 1,...,d are IID samples from {±1}"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers on bandit algorithms and adversarial examples don't discuss the step size tuning for constrained subspaces.
- **Break condition:** When the gradient directions are highly correlated or when the projection step frequently activates, invalidating the random walk assumption.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does adversarial success scale when perturbations are constrained to non-linear or curved subspaces of the input space?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only considers linear subspaces spanned by standard basis vectors or random orthonormal vectors. The theoretical analysis assumes isometries U: R^d → V, which puts strong restrictions on V when p ≠ 2.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on linear subspaces and doesn't explore non-linear constraints or curved manifolds. The theoretical framework may not directly apply to curved subspaces.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring adversarial success with perturbations constrained to curved subspaces (e.g., spheres, ellipsoids) and theoretical analysis extending the linear subspace framework to curved spaces.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the scaling relationship between adversarial success, perturbation budget ε, and subspace dimension dim(V) change when using other adversarial attack methods beyond PGD?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on PGD attacks and mentions that other subspace-constrained adversarial example generators exist but aren't explored. The functional form C(ε(dim(V)/dim(X))^(1/q)) is derived for PGD.
- Why unresolved: The paper only experiments with PGD and doesn't test other attack methods like DeepFool, C&W, or gradient-free attacks. Different attack algorithms may have different scaling properties.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing adversarial success scaling across multiple attack methods (PGD, DeepFool, C&W, etc.) for the same subspace constraints.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal subspace dimension that maximizes the trade-off between adversarial robustness and model utility/performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that adversarial success decreases as dim(V)/dim(X) decreases, but doesn't explore the trade-off with model performance. The results suggest restricting dimensions can mitigate adversarial risk.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on measuring adversarial success but doesn't consider how restricting subspaces affects model accuracy or utility on clean data. There's likely a sweet spot where robustness is improved without significant performance degradation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring both adversarial success and clean accuracy as functions of subspace dimension, identifying the dimension that provides the best robustness-utility trade-off.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis relies on assumptions about local linearity that may not hold for complex deep networks
- The relationship breaks down for ℓ1 norm constraints, but the reasons are not fully explored
- Random subspace sampling may not reflect realistic attack scenarios where adversaries can choose informative subspaces

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The experimental methodology for measuring adversarial success rates is well-established and the results showing scaling behavior across different subspace dimensions appear robust. The observation that success rates can be collapsed onto a single curve through appropriate reparameterization is supported by clear empirical evidence.

**Medium Confidence:** The theoretical derivation connecting linear model behavior to the observed scaling law is sound for the simplified case, but the extension to deep networks relies on assumptions about local linearity that require further validation. The claim that adversarial examples are endemic to high-dimensional locally linear models is compelling but needs broader testing across different model architectures.

**Low Confidence:** The specific numerical predictions for step sizes in PGD attacks across different norms and dimensions are presented as heuristics rather than rigorously proven results, with limited empirical validation of their optimality.
