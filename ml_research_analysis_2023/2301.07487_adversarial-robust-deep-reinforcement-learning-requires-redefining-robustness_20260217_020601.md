# Adversarial Robust Deep Reinforcement Learning Requires Redefining Robustness

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.07487
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07487
- Reference count: 4
- Key outcome: Policy-independent high-sensitivity directions can cause greater performance degradation than adversarial directions at similar perceptual distances, and adversarial training may actually decrease robustness to these natural perturbations.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the conventional approach to adversarial robustness in deep reinforcement learning by demonstrating that policy-independent high-sensitivity directions intrinsic to the Markov Decision Process can be more effective at degrading policy performance than traditional adversarial perturbations. The authors show that these natural, policy-agnostic directions can cause significant performance drops while maintaining perceptual similarity, suggesting that current adversarial training methods may be targeting the wrong threat model. Surprisingly, state-of-the-art adversarial training methods like SA-DDQN and RADIAL result in policies that are less robust to these policy-independent directions compared to simple vanilla training.

## Method Summary
The authors train deep RL policies (DQN with Double Q-learning) on Atari games from the Arcade Learning Environment, then generate two types of perturbations: policy-dependent adversarial directions using Carlini & Wagner formulation and policy-independent high-sensitivity directions using natural transformations like brightness/contrast changes, rotation, shifting, blurring, DCT artifacts, and perspective transforms. They evaluate policy performance degradation and perceptual similarity using LPIPS metric, comparing vanilla trained policies against those trained with SA-DDQN and RADIAL adversarial training methods.

## Key Results
- Policy-independent high-sensitivity directions cause greater performance degradation than policy-dependent adversarial directions at similar perceptual similarity distances
- State-of-the-art adversarial training methods (SA-DDQN, RADIAL) surprisingly result in policies less robust to policy-independent high-sensitivity directions compared to vanilla training
- Policy-independent high-sensitivity directions cover a broader frequency spectrum than adversarial directions, potentially explaining their effectiveness

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Policy-independent high-sensitivity directions intrinsic to the MDP can cause greater performance degradation than policy-dependent adversarial directions at similar perceptual similarity distances.
- Mechanism: The policy-independent directions exploit natural variations in the state space (e.g., brightness, contrast, geometric transformations) that are semantically meaningful but cause large shifts in the learned value function, while adversarial directions are optimized to find worst-case perturbations but may not fully capture these intrinsic sensitivities.
- Core assumption: The perceptual similarity metric (LPIPS) effectively measures human-perceived differences between states, and the deep RL policy's decision boundary is highly sensitive to certain low-level transformations of the state representation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "These high sensitivity directions do not lie only along particular worst-case directions, but rather are more abundant in the deep neural policy landscape and can be found via more natural means in a black-box setting."
  - [section] "We compare policy-independent high-sensitivity high-sensitivity directions with the state-of-the-art adversarial directions based on ℓp-norm changes, and we show that policy-independent high-sensitivity directions intrinsic to the MDP are competitive in degrading the performance of the deep reinforcement learning policies with lower perceptual similarity distance."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: neighboring papers focus on adversarial robustness but don't directly compare policy-independent vs policy-dependent directions.
- Break condition: If LPIPS does not correlate well with human perception or if the policy is trained on data augmentation that explicitly includes these transformations, the effect may diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adversarial training methods like SA-DDQN and RADIAL can make policies less robust to policy-independent high-sensitivity directions compared to vanilla training.
- Mechanism: Adversarial training optimizes for robustness against ℓp-norm bounded perturbations, which primarily affect high-frequency components. This optimization creates a trade-off where the policy becomes more vulnerable to low-frequency or semantically meaningful transformations that are not well-represented in the ℓp-norm ball.
- Core assumption: There is an inherent trade-off in the policy's robustness across different frequency bands of perturbations, and adversarial training's focus on high-frequency robustness comes at the expense of low-frequency robustness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "State-of-the-art adversarial training methods like SA-DDQN and RADIAL surprisingly result in policies that are less robust to policy-independent high-sensitivity directions compared to simple vanilla training."
  - [section] "Intriguingly, as these parameters for high-sensitivity directions are varied Figure 2 demonstrates that simple vanilla trained deep reinforcement learning policies are more robust compared to state-of-the-art adversarially trained ones."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: neighboring papers discuss adversarial training but don't report this specific trade-off with policy-independent directions.
- Break condition: If the adversarial training includes a diverse set of perturbation types that cover both high and low frequency bands, or if the policy architecture is inherently robust across frequency bands.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Policy-independent high-sensitivity directions cover a broader portion of the frequency spectrum compared to policy-dependent adversarial directions.
- Mechanism: Policy-independent directions include both low-frequency changes (brightness, contrast) and high-frequency changes (blurring, DCT artifacts), while adversarial directions are typically optimized for high-frequency perturbations within an ℓp-norm constraint. This broader spectral coverage makes them more effective at degrading performance.
- Core assumption: The effectiveness of a perturbation direction depends on its coverage of the frequency spectrum, and the deep RL policy's value function is sensitive to changes across multiple frequency bands.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The frequency spectrum analysis shows that policy-independent high-sensitivity directions cover a broader portion of the spectrum compared to policy-dependent adversarial directions."
  - [section] "Figure 4 shows the power spectral density of the base state compared to states that diverge from base states along the high-sensitivity direction... demonstrates that each policy-independent high-sensitivity direction occupies a different particular band in the frequency domain."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: neighboring papers focus on adversarial attacks but don't analyze the frequency spectrum of policy-independent directions.
- Break condition: If the policy is trained with data augmentation that explicitly covers the same frequency spectrum as the policy-independent directions, or if the policy architecture is designed to be invariant to these transformations.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: The paper uses LPIPS to measure the perceptual distance between base states and perturbed states, which is crucial for evaluating the imperceptibility of different types of perturbations.
  - Quick check question: How does LPIPS differ from traditional ℓp-norms in measuring perceptual similarity, and why is this distinction important for evaluating robustness in deep RL?

- Concept: Frequency domain analysis in image processing
  - Why needed here: The paper analyzes the frequency spectrum of different perturbation types to understand their coverage across different frequency bands and their effectiveness at degrading policy performance.
  - Quick check question: What are the key differences between low-frequency and high-frequency perturbations in terms of their effects on image appearance and neural network activations?

  - Why needed here: The paper compares the robustness of vanilla trained policies with state-of-the-art adversarially trained policies, requiring an understanding of how adversarial training works and its limitations.
  - Quick check question: What is the core principle behind adversarial training, and how does it attempt to improve robustness to worst-case perturbations?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Base deep RL policy (e.g., DQN with Double Q-learning) -> Perceptual similarity metric (LPIPS) -> Policy-independent high-sensitivity direction generators (brightness/contrast, rotation, shifting, blurring, DCT artifacts, perspective transform) -> Policy-dependent adversarial direction generators (Carlini & Wagner formulation) -> Evaluation framework for measuring performance degradation and perceptual similarity -> Adversarial training implementations (SA-DDQN, RADIAL)

- Critical path:
  1. Train a base deep RL policy on the target environment
  2. Generate both policy-dependent and policy-independent perturbations for a set of states
  3. Evaluate the performance degradation caused by each perturbation type
  4. Measure the perceptual similarity between base and perturbed states
  5. Compare the effectiveness of different perturbation types at degrading performance
  6. Test the robustness of vanilla vs adversarially trained policies to policy-independent directions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computational cost of generating perturbations vs number of evaluation samples
  - Choice of perceptual similarity metric (LPIPS vs alternatives)
  - Balance between policy-dependent and policy-independent perturbation types
  - Trade-off between comprehensive frequency coverage and practical implementation

- Failure signatures:
  - If LPIPS does not correlate well with human perception, the imperceptibility claims may be invalid
  - If the policy is trained with extensive data augmentation, the effectiveness of policy-independent directions may be reduced
  - If the adversarial training includes a diverse set of perturbation types, the vulnerability of adversarially trained policies may not be observed

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a basic DQN agent on a simple Atari game (e.g., Pong) and verify it can learn to play
  2. Implement the LPIPS perceptual similarity metric and verify it produces reasonable similarity scores for different image transformations
  3. Implement a simple policy-independent perturbation (e.g., brightness change) and verify it can degrade the policy's performance while maintaining perceptual similarity

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we develop a more comprehensive notion of robustness in deep RL that goes beyond ℓp-norm bounded perturbations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates that adversarial training focusing on ℓp-norm bounded perturbations can make policies more vulnerable to other natural types of imperceptible high-sensitivity directions.
- Why unresolved: Current robustness definitions in deep RL are heavily based on ℓp-norm bounded adversarial perturbations, which may not capture the full range of potential vulnerabilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating robustness metrics that account for a broader spectrum of perturbations, including those inherent to the MDP, and demonstrating improved policy performance across diverse perturbation types.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the fundamental trade-offs between adversarial training and vanilla training in terms of policy robustness to different types of perturbations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that vanilla trained policies are more robust to certain policy-independent high-sensitivity directions compared to state-of-the-art adversarially trained policies.
- Why unresolved: The relationship between adversarial training objectives and overall policy robustness to various perturbation types is not well understood.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing the robustness of vanilla and adversarially trained policies to a wide range of perturbation types, and analyzing the frequency spectrum effects of different training methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do policy-independent high-sensitivity directions innate to the MDP relate to the frequency spectrum of observations, and how can this knowledge be used to improve policy robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper analyzes the frequency spectrum of both policy-dependent and policy-independent high-sensitivity directions, showing that the latter cover a broader portion of the spectrum.
- Why unresolved: The connection between frequency domain characteristics of perturbations and policy robustness is not fully explored or utilized in current deep RL approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing training methods that explicitly consider the frequency spectrum of perturbations, and demonstrating improved robustness to a diverse set of high-sensitivity directions across different frequency bands.

## Limitations

- Limited evaluation across only 5 Atari games raises questions about generalization to other RL environments
- The focus on image-based observations may not translate to non-visual state representations
- The specific adversarial training methods (SA-DDQN, RADIAL) may not represent the full space of possible adversarial training approaches

## Confidence

- Medium confidence: The core claims about policy-independent high-sensitivity directions being more effective than adversarial directions have moderate support from the presented experiments but require further validation across different RL algorithms and environments.
- Medium confidence: The surprising finding that adversarial training can decrease robustness to policy-independent directions is supported by the experiments but needs verification across different training procedures and perturbation types.
