# Sketch Less Face Image Retrieval: A New Challenge

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05576
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05576
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Introduces Sketch Less Face Image Retrieval (SLFIR) task, achieves higher early-stage retrieval accuracy with fewer sketch strokes than classical methods

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Sketch Less Face Image Retrieval (SLFIR), a novel task where the goal is to retrieve target face photos using partial sketches with as few strokes as possible. The authors develop a method to generate synthetic sketch drawing process data from complete sketches and propose a two-stage baseline approach. The first stage uses a triplet network to learn joint embeddings for complete sketches and photos, while the second stage employs a Bi-LSTM to optimize embeddings for incomplete sketches. Experiments demonstrate that this method outperforms classical approaches in early retrieval scenarios, enabling efficient human-machine interaction for applications like sketch teaching and criminal investigation.

## Method Summary
The method involves two main stages: First, a triplet network with InceptionV3 backbone, soft spatial attention, and linear mapping layers learns joint embeddings between complete sketches and corresponding photos using triplet loss. Second, a Bi-LSTM module processes the stroke-by-stroke sequence of incomplete sketches to optimize their embeddings while keeping the base model parameters fixed. The sketch drawing process generation method uses Canny edge detection to extract structural lines from complete sketches and incrementally repaints them to create synthetic stroke sequences for training.

## Key Results
- Outperforms classical approaches in early retrieval stages with fewer strokes
- Achieves higher accuracy using partial sketches compared to complete sketch-based methods
- Demonstrates effectiveness of two-stage approach combining triplet network and Bi-LSTM optimization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The Bi-LSTM sequence modeling compensates for incomplete sketch strokes by capturing temporal correlations in the drawing process. The method treats incremental strokes as a temporal sequence, using Bi-LSTM to integrate forward and backward contextual information, allowing inference of missing visual features from later or earlier strokes. This works under the assumption that stroke order contains recoverable structural information about the full sketch, even when incomplete.

### Mechanism 2
Triplet loss with positive sketch-photo pairs and negative photo samples forces the model to embed complete sketches and their corresponding photos close together in feature space. The base network is trained with triplet loss where the anchor is a complete sketch, the positive is the matching photo, and the negative is a non-matching photo. This encourages discriminative embeddings that generalize to partial sketches after Bi-LSTM fine-tuning, assuming the embedding space learned on complete sketches can be refined for incomplete sketches without catastrophic forgetting.

### Mechanism 3
The sketch drawing process generation method produces plausible stroke sequences that simulate real human drawing, enabling supervised training on partial sketches. The method extracts structural lines using a CNN, detects edges with Canny, and incrementally paints these edges to create stroke-by-stroke sequences. This synthetic sequence trains the Bi-LSTM to map incomplete sketches to correct photo embeddings, assuming the generated stroke sequence approximates natural human drawing enough for generalizable representations.

## Foundational Learning

- **Cross-domain embedding learning (sketch ↔ photo)**: Why needed - Face sketches and photos are from different domains; a model must learn to map them into a shared embedding space for direct similarity comparison. Quick check - What loss function ensures that a sketch embedding is closer to its matching photo than to any non-matching photo?
- **Sequence modeling with Bi-LSTM**: Why needed - Incomplete sketches are processed stroke-by-stroke; the model must capture context from both past and future strokes to infer the full structure. Quick check - Why is a bidirectional LSTM preferred over a unidirectional one for this task?
- **Triplet loss formulation**: Why needed - The method uses triplets of (sketch, matching photo, non-matching photo) to enforce relative distance constraints in embedding space. Quick check - How does the margin parameter in triplet loss affect retrieval performance?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Stroke sequence → Bi-LSTM → embedding → distance to photo embeddings → ranking
- **Critical path**: Stroke sequence → Bi-LSTM → embedding → distance to photo embeddings → ranking
- **Design tradeoffs**: Using pre-trained CNN (InceptionV3) speeds convergence but may limit adaptation to sketch-specific features; generating synthetic stroke sequences avoids costly human data collection but risks domain mismatch; Bi-LSTM increases model capacity and context capture but adds training complexity and potential overfitting on small datasets
- **Failure signatures**: Early strokes yield poor retrieval → stroke sequence lacks informative content or Bi-LSTM fails to recover structure; performance drops with higher feature dimensions → overfitting or insufficient training data; inconsistent retrieval across different painters → model overfits to a specific drawing style
- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Train base triplet network on complete sketches only; evaluate retrieval m@A and m@B to establish baseline; 2) Add synthetic stroke sequences; train Bi-LSTM to optimize embeddings; compare early retrieval performance against baseline; 3) Vary feature embedding dimensions (8, 16, 32, 64, 128) and triplet margin; analyze trade-offs between retrieval accuracy and computational cost

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How can the performance of SLFIR be improved for sketches at early stages where the diversity of painting processes is high? The authors note that diversity in painting process at early stages is one of the main reasons for decreased performance on their dataset D3, but do not provide a solution for addressing this diversity.

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal feature embedding dimension for different stages of the sketch drawing process in SLFIR? The paper shows that different feature embedding dimensions have varying effects on sketches at different percentages of completion, but does not determine an optimal dimension for each stage.

### Open Question 3
How can the cross-domain gap between early incomplete sketches and target face photos be effectively narrowed in SLFIR? While the paper proposes a two-stage method to optimize embeddings for incomplete sketches, it does not fully address how to bridge the cross-domain gap for early-stage sketches.

## Limitations

- The method relies heavily on synthetic stroke sequences generated from complete sketches, which may not fully capture human drawing variability
- No extensive validation on real-world incomplete sketches drawn by multiple users, leaving uncertainty about performance on genuine partial inputs
- Bi-LSTM module architecture details (number of layers, hidden dimensions) are not fully specified, making precise reproduction challenging

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism of using triplet loss for cross-domain embedding learning is well-established and directly supported by the described training procedure
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of the Bi-LSTM in optimizing incomplete sketch embeddings is supported by experimental results but lacks ablation studies to isolate its contribution
- **Low Confidence**: The generalizability of the sketch drawing process generation method to diverse drawing styles and the quality of synthetic stroke sequences are not thoroughly evaluated
