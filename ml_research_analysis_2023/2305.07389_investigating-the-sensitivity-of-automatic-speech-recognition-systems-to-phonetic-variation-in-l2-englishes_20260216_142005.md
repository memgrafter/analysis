# Investigating the Sensitivity of Automatic Speech Recognition Systems to Phonetic Variation in L2 Englishes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07389
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07389
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: ASR errors are consistent across speakers of the same L1 and align with human annotator judgments

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems perform on non-native English accents by analyzing phoneme-level recognition errors. Using a pretrained wav2vec 2.0 model and the L2 Arctic corpus, the authors align ASR transcripts with prompt texts to identify systematic mispronunciations. Results show that ASR errors are consistent across speakers of the same L1 and align with human annotator judgments. Misrecognitions often involve phoneme substitutions, with rates varying by L1 (e.g., Hindi /OY/ at 57.5% ASR vs. 95.0% human). This analysis aids in improving ASR robustness by targeting underrepresented pronunciations in training data.

## Method Summary
The study uses a pretrained wav2vec 2.0 ASR model fine-tuned on LibriSpeech, combined with the L2 Arctic Corpus containing 24 non-native speakers across 6 L1s. Prompt texts and ASR transcripts are converted to ARPAbet phoneme sequences using the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary and a grapheme-to-phoneme tool. A weighted edit-distance alignment algorithm generates speaker-level confusion matrices from phoneme sequences. K-means clustering groups speakers in 1600-dimensional space, and t-SNE visualizes the high-dimensional speaker vectors. The ASR recognition rates and common substitutes are compared with human annotator data to identify systematic error patterns across L1 groups.

## Key Results
- ASR errors are systematic and consistent across speakers with similar L1 backgrounds
- Phoneme substitution errors from ASR align with human annotator judgments
- Misrecognition rates vary significantly by L1, with some phonemes (e.g., Hindi /OY/) showing large gaps between ASR and human performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Phoneme-level misrecognition is systematic across speakers with the same L1
- Mechanism: Wav2vec 2.0, trained primarily on North American English, fails to map non-standard L2 phoneme realizations to canonical forms, leading to consistent substitution patterns
- Core assumption: Wav2vec 2.0's training data did not contain sufficient L2 English phoneme variants
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "phoneme-level misrecognitions and contribute to higher WERs"
  - [section] "It is demonstrated that the behaviour of the ASR is systematic and consistent across speakers with similar spoken varieties"
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus neighbors show high FMR scores, suggesting similarity in topic but not direct evidence of L2 performance
- Break condition: If L2 phoneme variants are well represented in training data or if speaker clustering shows no L1-based patterns

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Error patterns from ASR output align with human annotator judgments
- Mechanism: Both ASR and human annotators identify the same phoneme substitution targets, validating ASR as a diagnostic tool for phonetic variation
- Core assumption: Human annotators accurately represent native-like phoneme production as "correct"
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "phoneme substitution errors are typically in agreement with human annotators"
  - [section] "the ASR output captures the same judgements as made by a human listener"
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus evidence shows topic similarity but no direct link to annotation agreement
- Break condition: If ASR substitutions systematically diverge from human annotations or if ASR misrecognizes phonemes unrelated to L2 variation

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Targeted data collection for underrepresented phoneme variants can improve ASR performance
- Mechanism: Identifying specific L2 phoneme realizations that cause errors allows focused fine-tuning, reducing WER for speakers with similar L1 backgrounds
- Core assumption: Adding training examples of problematic phoneme variants improves ASR generalization to unseen but related variants
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "By identifying problematic productions specific weaknesses can be addressed by sourcing such realisations for training and fine-tuning thus making the system more robust"
  - [section] "more instances of retroflex [ú] in the training and fine-tuning data would improve the performance of the ASR for that speaker"
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus does not provide evidence for targeted data collection success
- Break condition: If fine-tuning on identified variants does not improve performance or if errors are not consistent across L1 groups

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: CMU Pronouncing Dictionary -> Grapheme-to-phoneme tool -> Wav2vec 2.0 ASR model -> Weighted edit-distance alignment -> Confusion matrices -> K-means clustering -> t-SNE visualization

- Critical path:
  1. Load ASR model and convert prompt/ASR text to phoneme sequences
  2. Align phoneme sequences using weighted edit-distance
  3. Build speaker-level confusion matrices from alignment results
  4. Reshape confusion matrices into high-dimensional vectors
  5. Apply K-means clustering and visualize with t-SNE
  6. Compare ASR error rates with human annotator judgments

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using CMU dictionary pronunciations as "ground truth" assumes these represent expected ASR behavior rather than absolute correctness
  - Weighted edit-distance requires a precomputed similarity matrix, which may not capture all L2-specific substitutions
  - K-means assumes spherical clusters, which may not perfectly reflect L1-based speaker groupings

- Failure signatures:
  - ASR error patterns show no L1-based clustering (suggests model robustness or insufficient variation)
  - High divergence between ASR and human annotator substitution rates (suggests ASR sensitivity to variation annotators overlook)
  - Confusion matrices dominated by insertions/deletions rather than substitutions (suggests model sensitivity to prosody or segmentation)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate clustering with different numbers of K-means clusters to test robustness of L1 grouping
  2. Compare ASR error patterns with a phonetically aware baseline model to isolate wav2vec 2.0-specific weaknesses
  3. Conduct targeted fine-tuning on a subset of L2 phoneme variants and measure WER reduction across speakers with the same L1

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the ASR system's performance on specific phoneme substitutions compare to human annotators across different L1 groups, and what factors contribute to the discrepancies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares ASR recognition rates and common substitutes with human annotator judgments for various phonemes across L1 groups (e.g., Hindi /OY/ at 57.5% ASR vs. 95.0% human).
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies discrepancies but does not deeply explore the underlying reasons for why ASR and human annotators differ in their judgments of phoneme substitutions.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the acoustic and perceptual features that lead to differences between ASR and human annotator judgments, including a study of how specific pronunciation features (e.g., vowel tenseness, consonant aspiration) are perceived differently by machines versus humans.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific types of pronunciation variants are most challenging for the ASR system, and how can targeted data collection address these weaknesses?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights that specific phonetic realizations (e.g., Hindi /OY/ realized as /AY/) lead to higher misrecognition rates and suggests that targeted data collection could improve ASR performance.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies problematic variants, it does not specify the exact nature of these variants or provide a clear methodology for identifying and collecting the most impactful data for fine-tuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study that identifies the most frequent and impactful pronunciation variants causing ASR errors, along with a pilot study showing improved ASR performance after targeted data collection and fine-tuning.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How generalizable are the findings of systematic ASR errors across different L1 groups to other non-native English varieties not included in the study?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates systematic ASR errors for six L1 groups (Arabic, Hindi, Korean, Mandarin, Spanish, Vietnamese) but does not explore whether these findings apply to other L1 groups or more diverse accents.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to a specific corpus and set of L1 groups, leaving open the question of whether similar patterns of ASR errors would be observed in other non-native English varieties.
- What evidence would resolve it: An expanded study that includes a broader range of L1 groups and non-native English varieties, comparing the patterns of ASR errors to determine if the findings are consistent across different speaker populations.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies heavily on CMU dictionary pronunciations as "ground truth," which may not accurately represent the full range of acceptable L2 English realizations.
- The weighted edit-distance alignment mechanism's effectiveness depends on the accuracy of the phoneme similarity matrix, which is referenced but not detailed in the paper.
- The paper does not provide evidence that targeted fine-tuning on identified variants actually improves ASR performance, only that it could theoretically help.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The systematic nature of ASR errors across L1 groups is well-supported by the clustering analysis and comparison with human annotations. The mechanism linking wav2vec 2.0's training bias to L2 performance is logically sound.
- **Medium confidence**: The alignment between ASR and human annotator judgments is demonstrated but depends on the quality of human annotations as reference standards. The proposed fine-tuning approach is theoretically justified but lacks empirical validation.
- **Low confidence**: The specific phoneme similarity matrix weights and grapheme-to-phoneme tool implementation details are not fully specified, making exact replication challenging.
