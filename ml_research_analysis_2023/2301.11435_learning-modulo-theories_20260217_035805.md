# Learning Modulo Theories

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.11435
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11435
- Reference count: 25
- One-line primary result: SMTLayer achieves better performance with less training data by incorporating SMT solvers into neural networks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SMTLayer, a method for incorporating SMT solvers into neural networks to learn tasks that involve symbolic reasoning. Unlike prior approaches that approximate solver behavior with differentiable relaxations, SMTLayer uses the solver's output (MaxSMT solutions or unsatisfiable cores) to construct gradients for backpropagation. The method is validated across four tasks - MNIST addition, visual algebra, liar's puzzle, and visual Sudoku - demonstrating superior performance with less training data compared to conventional networks and prior approaches like SATNet and Scallop.

## Method Summary
SMTLayer integrates SMT solvers into DNNs by using the solver during training to check the consistency of network outputs with symbolic constraints. During the forward pass, the network produces Boolean ground terms that are fed to the SMT solver to produce symbolic outputs. For the backward pass, SMTLayer uses information from the solver (either MaxSMT solutions or unsatisfiable cores) to compute gradients without approximating the solver itself. The approach leverages the decomposability of learning problems into perceptual grounding and symbolic prediction components, allowing the network to learn interpretable representations that are robust to certain types of covariate shift.

## Key Results
- SMTLayer requires less training data than conventional networks and prior approaches while achieving higher accuracy
- The method demonstrates superior performance on MNIST addition, visual algebra, liar's puzzle, and visual Sudoku tasks
- Learned representations are consistent with symbolic knowledge, making them interpretable and robust to covariate shift that doesn't affect perceptual data distribution
- Outperforms SATNet and Scallop baselines on all tested tasks while using fewer training samples

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SMTLayer provides gradients that are more informative than zero-gradient approximations used in prior solver layers
- Mechanism: Instead of approximating solver behavior with differentiable relaxations, SMTLayer uses the solver's output (MaxSMT solutions or unsat cores) to identify inconsistent inputs and construct counterfactual gradients
- Core assumption: The solver can efficiently identify either weighted MaxSMT solutions or minimal unsat cores for the constraint problem
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Notably, the solver need not be differentiable"
  - [section 4.2] "they use information provided by the solver in its computation of the forward pass to identify which components of the input may have contributed to higher loss"
- Break condition: If the solver cannot efficiently compute MaxSMT solutions or unsat cores, the backward pass cannot provide useful gradients

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Learning problems can be decomposed into perceptual grounding and symbolic prediction components
- Mechanism: The network learns to map raw inputs to Boolean ground terms that satisfy symbolic constraints, then the solver handles the logical inference
- Core assumption: There exists a decomposition where the grounding function f(x) can be approximated by the neural network and the symbolic component φ can be solved efficiently
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3] "A learning problem defined in terms of a distribution D and hypothesis class H is decomposable if members of H can be decomposed into functions that are responsible for grounding and prediction"
  - [section 5] "Our approach is able to perform well in data-impoverished settings where the training sample is insufficient to fully specify the symbolic component"
- Break condition: If the problem cannot be decomposed this way or the grounding function is too complex for the network to learn

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Models learn interpretable representations that generalize across covariate shift in the symbolic component
- Mechanism: When the network learns the correct ground term representation, it produces consistent outputs across different distributions of how ground terms are composed
- Core assumption: The learned grounding function produces the correct Boolean representation of inputs, making the symbolic constraints always satisfied
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5] "the model's representation will be inherently interpretable, because it will coincide with the provided symbolic domain knowledge"
  - [section 5] "the resulting model is naturally robust to covariate shift that does not affect the distribution of perceptual data"
- Break condition: If the network fails to learn the correct grounding representation, the model loses interpretability and robustness

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Lower network layers → Boolean ground terms → SMT solver → symbolic output
- Critical path: Forward pass: lower network layers → Boolean ground terms → SMT solver → symbolic output. Backward pass: loss gradient → SMTLayer gradient construction using solver information → lower network layers
- Design tradeoffs: Using a non-differentiable solver avoids the need for solver approximations but requires efficient solver implementations. The approach trades computational cost (solver invocations) for better gradient information and interpretability
- Failure signatures: Training stalls with zero gradients, poor performance on test data despite good training accuracy, or inability to generalize when symbolic knowledge changes. These suggest either solver inefficiency or failure to learn correct ground term representations
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement SMTLayer with a simple constraint (e.g., AND operation) on synthetic data to verify gradient flow and basic functionality
  2. Test MNIST addition with full training data to verify the approach works on established benchmarks
  3. Test with limited training data (e.g., 10% of MNIST pairs) to verify the robustness advantage over conventional approaches

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of SMTLayer change when the grounding function f is not efficiently computable, such as in cases where it corresponds to a natural process or poorly understood data generation aspect?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that the grounding function f may not be efficiently computable, as it may correspond to a natural process or an aspect of data generation that is not well understood
- Why unresolved: The empirical evaluation focuses on cases where the grounding function can be learned or approximated, and does not explore scenarios where f is fundamentally non-computable
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments demonstrating the performance of SMTLayer on datasets where the grounding function is deliberately made non-computable or extremely difficult to approximate, compared to conventional methods

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of using Bφ_core versus Bφ_max on the convergence rate and final accuracy of SMTLayer, and under what conditions does one outperform the other?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that Bφ_core and Bφ_max may lead to different training dynamics, with Bφ_core potentially having more in common with block coordinate descent than gradient descent, and does not provide a detailed exploration of this
- Why unresolved: The empirical results show that both methods perform similarly on the tested datasets, but the paper does not provide a theoretical analysis or extensive experiments to determine when one method is superior
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive empirical study comparing Bφ_core and Bφ_max across a wide range of problems, analyzing convergence rates, final accuracy, and the characteristics of problems where one method consistently outperforms the other

### Open Question 3
- Question: How robust is SMTLayer to covariate shift that affects both the perceptual data and the symbolic composition, and what strategies can be employed to mitigate potential performance degradation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the robustness of SMTLayer to certain types of covariate shift that do not affect the distribution of perceptual data but do affect the statistics of their composition. However, it also mentions that the network may learn training biases when the full instance is provided, rather than individual digits
- Why unresolved: The paper only touches on this issue in the context of visual algebra configuration 2 and does not provide a comprehensive analysis of SMTLayer's robustness to more general forms of covariate shift
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating SMTLayer's performance on datasets with various types of covariate shift affecting both perceptual data and symbolic composition, along with proposed strategies (e.g., data augmentation, regularization techniques) and their effectiveness in mitigating performance degradation

## Limitations

- The paper lacks detailed implementation specifics for the SMTLayer's forward and backward passes, which could impact reproducibility
- Computational overhead of solver invocations is not thoroughly discussed, potentially limiting practical applications
- Exact hyperparameters and optimization settings for each task are not specified, affecting the ability to reproduce results

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The paper clearly articulates the motivation for using SMT solvers and the overall architecture of SMTLayer. The concept of leveraging non-differentiable solvers to provide informative gradients is well-explained
- Medium Confidence: The experimental results demonstrate improved performance on the tested tasks, but the lack of detailed implementation information makes it challenging to fully assess the robustness of the approach
- Low Confidence: The paper does not adequately address the computational overhead of using SMT solvers in each forward pass, which could be a significant limitation in practical applications
