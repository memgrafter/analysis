# On Minimizing the Impact of Dataset Shifts on Actionable Explanations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.06716
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06716
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Explanation stability during model retraining is influenced by model curvature, weight decay, and dataset shift magnitude; higher weight decay and lower curvature improve stability.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the stability of feature attributions when machine learning models are retrained due to dataset shifts. Explanation instability can undermine trust and usability in real-world applications. The authors provide both theoretical analysis and empirical validation, showing that model curvature, weight decay, and dataset shift magnitude are key factors influencing explanation stability. They offer actionable recommendations for ML practitioners to improve explanation stability during model retraining.

## Method Summary
The authors train medium-sized neural networks (5 layers, 50 nodes each) on three tabular datasets (WHO, HELOC, Adult). They generate synthetic dataset shifts by adding Gaussian noise and also use a real-world temporal split. Models are retrained from scratch or fine-tuned on shifted data. Explanation stability is measured using ℓ2 distance for gradients and top-K feature attribution stability metrics (SA, CDC, SSA) across methods like LIME, SHAP, SmoothGrad, and saliency. Sensitivity analysis is performed on hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, epochs, and weight decay.

## Key Results
- Higher weight decay and lower model curvature improve gradient stability.
- SmoothGrad consistently outperforms other methods in explanation stability.
- Retraining from scratch is less stable than fine-tuning.
- Training hyperparameters like learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs also impact stability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Explanation stability improves when model curvature is reduced via smooth activation functions.
- Mechanism: Lower curvature means the gradient of the loss with respect to inputs changes less as model parameters shift during retraining. This makes the resulting feature attributions more consistent.
- Core assumption: The relationship between second-order smoothness and gradient stability holds in realistic, non-linear, non-convex settings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "model curvature... are key factors that determine the extent of explanation (in)stability"
  - [section] Theorem 2: "the smoother the model, the smaller are its second derivatives, and thus its gradient-input Lipschitz"
  - [corpus] Weak – neighbors focus on fairness and concept drift rather than curvature.
- Break condition: If the loss landscape becomes highly non-smooth or if second-order terms dominate due to large shifts, the bound no longer applies.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Weight decay stabilizes explanations by constraining parameter movement during retraining.
- Mechanism: Larger weight decay pulls the optimal solution toward the origin, reducing the norm of parameters. This limits the distance between solutions on slightly shifted data, which in turn limits the change in gradients and feature attributions.
- Core assumption: The model is trained to convergence on both the original and shifted datasets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "weight decay parameters during training... are key factors influencing the extent of explanation (in)stability"
  - [section] Theorem 1: "It depends inversely on the weight decay parameter γ, and directly on the norm of weights ∥θ∥2"
  - [corpus] Weak – no direct neighbor discussion of weight decay effects.
- Break condition: If dataset shift is too large, even strong weight decay may not prevent large parameter changes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The magnitude of dataset shift controls how much retraining affects explanation stability.
- Mechanism: Larger shifts change the loss landscape more, which increases parameter movement and consequently gradient changes. This directly translates to less stable explanations.
- Core assumption: Dataset shift is measured accurately and its effect on the loss surface is monotonic.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the magnitude of the dataset shift are key factors that determine the extent of explanation (in)stability"
  - [section] "larger dataset shift... the larger can be the parameter shift, and explanation shift"
  - [corpus] Weak – neighbors do not quantify or bound dataset shift effects on explanations.
- Break condition: If the shift is so large that the model must change significantly to maintain accuracy, stability is sacrificed.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Lipschitz continuity of model outputs
  - Why needed here: Ensures small input perturbations lead to small output changes, forming the basis for bounding explanation shifts.
  - Quick check question: If a model has Lipschitz constant L, what is the maximum change in output for an input change of ε?

- Concept: Weight decay as regularization
  - Why needed here: Controls the magnitude of parameters and limits the distance between solutions on shifted data.
  - Quick check question: How does increasing the weight decay coefficient affect the norm of the optimal parameters?

- Concept: Second-order derivatives and curvature
  - Why needed here: Curvature controls how sensitive gradients are to parameter changes, directly affecting explanation stability.
  - Quick check question: What is the effect of a smoother activation function on the second derivative of the loss?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Dataset -> Model (5-layer FC, 50 nodes) -> Explanation method (LIME, SHAP, SmoothGrad, saliency) -> Stability metrics (ℓ2 distance, SA, CDC, SSA)
- Critical path:
  1. Train base model on original dataset.
  2. Generate shifted dataset.
  3. Retrain or fine-tune to obtain new model.
  4. Compute feature attributions for both models on same test set.
  5. Measure stability using chosen metrics.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fine-tuning vs. retraining: Fine-tuning gives more stable parameters but retraining better reflects real-world updates.
  - Smooth activation (softplus) vs. ReLU: Smooth activations improve stability but may reduce final accuracy.
  - Large weight decay vs. low weight decay: Large weight decay improves stability but can harm training accuracy.
- Failure signatures:
  - High ℓ2 gradient distance coupled with low top-k consistency indicates unstable explanations.
  - Large variance across trials suggests sensitivity to random seeds or optimization paths.
  - Low accuracy on shifted data suggests the model is changing too much, making stability less relevant.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train two models with different weight decay values; compare explanation stability after synthetic shift.
  2. Compare ReLU vs. softplus activation in terms of gradient stability under the same shift.
  3. Measure how stability degrades as the magnitude of synthetic noise increases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do explanation stability findings generalize to non-differentiable model classes like decision trees and random forests?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper conducts preliminary experiments on tree-based models (decision trees, random forests, XGBoost) and notes mixed results, but calls for more research to generalize findings.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical analysis in the paper focuses on differentiable models (neural networks), and the experiments with tree-based models are preliminary with inconsistent results across explanation methods and dataset shifts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensive experiments on a variety of tree-based models with different explanation methods under various real-world dataset shifts, comparing to differentiable models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different types of real-world dataset shifts (geographic, temporal, data corrections) impact explanation stability compared to synthetic shifts?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses synthetic Gaussian noise and one temporal shift dataset, but acknowledges the need to evaluate other real-world shifts.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates synthetic shifts and one temporal shift, which may not capture the full complexity of real-world dataset shifts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing explanation stability across multiple real-world dataset shifts (geographic, temporal, data corrections) on various datasets and model types.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, epochs) on explanation stability across different explanation techniques?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper conducts sensitivity analysis on learning rate, learning rate decay, batch size, and base model epochs, showing they impact gradient stability.
- Why unresolved: The experiments focus on gradient-based metrics, but different explanation techniques may respond differently to these hyperparameters.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring explanation stability (SA, CDC, SSA) for various explanation techniques (LIME, SHAP, SmoothGrad) while varying training hyperparameters.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can novel explanation techniques be developed that inherently preserve stability under dataset shifts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper concludes by suggesting future work explore developing explanation techniques that preserve stability despite model and data changes.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates existing explanation techniques and observes their stability properties, but does not attempt to design new techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and empirical validation of new explanation techniques specifically designed to maintain stability under dataset shifts, compared to existing methods.

## Limitations

- Theoretical analysis assumes strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity, which may not hold for deep, non-convex models under large dataset shifts.
- Empirical validation is limited to three tabular datasets; generalizability to other domains (images, text, etc.) is unclear.
- The paper does not explore the effect of different base model architectures (CNNs, transformers) on explanation stability.

## Confidence

- Theoretical claims: Medium (assumes strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity, which may not hold for deep, non-convex models)
- Real-world applicability: Low (limited to three tabular datasets)
- Neural network generalizability: Medium (does not explore effect of different base model architectures)

## Next Checks

1. Test explanation stability under large, non-Gaussian dataset shifts (e.g., covariate shift or label shift) to verify the robustness of the findings.
2. Validate the theoretical bounds for non-convex models using higher-order gradient information or empirical curvature measures.
3. Extend the experiments to image and text datasets with models like CNNs and transformers to assess cross-domain generalizability.
