# Using Kaldi for Automatic Speech Recognition of Conversational Austrian German

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.06475
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06475
- Reference count: 0
- Best WER for conversational Austrian German: 48.5%

## Executive Summary
This paper presents ASR experiments for conversational Austrian German, a low-resource language variety. The authors improve a Kaldi-based ASR system by incorporating a knowledge-based pronunciation lexicon while exploring data-based methods to restrict pronunciation variants. For read speech, they achieve a best WER of 0.4%. For conversational speech, they achieve a best average WER of 48.5%. The authors find that using their best pronunciation lexicon yields similarly high performance as increasing the size of the language model data by 360% to 760%. Their findings indicate that knowledge-based approaches are a successful and efficient method for low-resource ASR scenarios.

## Method Summary
The study uses Kaldi-based DNN-HMM systems with GMM-HMM initialization for Austrian German ASR. Four pronunciation lexicons were created: standard (no variants), allPVs (all variants from 26 phonological rules), usedPVs (variants occurring in forced alignments), and likelyPVs (variants with occurrence probability >0.65). The models were trained on GRASS corpus (14.3h conversational, 4.6h read Austrian German) with additional German corpora for LM training. Cross-validation with p=2 speakers was used to evaluate performance across different conversation pairs.

## Key Results
- Best WER for read Austrian German: 0.4%
- Best average WER for conversational Austrian German: 48.5%
- Using likelyPVs lexicon performs similarly to increasing LM data by 360-760%
- High variation in performance across conversation pairs (5-6% standard deviation)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Knowledge-based pronunciation lexicons reduce WER by encoding dialect-specific phonological rules without requiring large LM data.
- Mechanism: The lexicon incorporates 26 phonological rules and manually added variants that capture Austrian German pronunciation phenomena (e.g., devoicing of fricatives, syllable deletion), improving alignment between spoken and hypothesized forms.
- Core assumption: The set of manually crafted rules and variants is sufficient to cover the bulk of pronunciation variation in conversational Austrian German.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we improve a Kaldi-based ASR system by incorporating a (large) knowledge-based pronunciation lexicon"
  - [section] "We created four different pronunciation lexicons... allPVs... contained on average 5.57−6.18 variants per word"
- Break condition: If pronunciation variation in Austrian German is broader than captured by the 26 rules, or if new dialects emerge not covered by the lexicon, WER will plateau or degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Restricting pronunciation variants to only likely occurrences improves WER more than including all possible variants.
- Mechanism: By filtering the full set of variants to those observed in forced alignments (p > 0.65), the system avoids confusion from rare or incorrect variants that degrade acoustic modeling.
- Core assumption: Variants with higher frequency in actual speech are more likely to be correct, and low-frequency variants add noise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We created a lexicon containing only those variants which showed a high frequency of occurrence in the forced alignment"
  - [section] "likelyPVs... had an average of 1.16−1.26 variants per word"
- Break condition: If speech data is too sparse to reliably estimate variant frequencies, or if rare variants are systematically important, the filtering may remove useful pronunciations.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Speaker-pair cross-validation reveals conversation-specific performance gaps that pooled training/test splits hide.
- Mechanism: By training and testing on individual conversation pairs, the system exposes how robust the ASR is to speaker-specific and dialectal variation rather than masking it with averaged metrics.
- Core assumption: Each conversation pair represents a distinct enough speaking style to warrant separate evaluation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We performed leave-p-out cross-validation with respect to GRASS CS (with p = 2 speakers of the same conversation)"
  - [section] "We observed high variation in performance from conversation to conversation (i.e., approx. 5−6 % standard deviation)"
- Break condition: If conversation pairs are not truly independent (e.g., shared topic or speaker background), cross-validation may overestimate variability.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: To generate pronunciation variants that reflect Austrian German reductions (schwa deletion, devoicing) which are not captured by generic G2P tools.
  - Quick check question: What happens to the phone set when rule R1 (devoicing alveolar fricatives) is applied to /z/?

  - Why needed here: To identify which pronunciation variants actually occur in spontaneous speech, enabling construction of the "likelyPVs" lexicon.

  - Why needed here: To assess ASR robustness across diverse speaker pairs and avoid optimistic bias from pooled metrics.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input: Raw conversational Austrian German audio (GRASS CS)
  Preprocess: MFCC extraction, CMVN, forced alignment
  AM: GMM-HMM → DNN-HMM (TDNN, 13 layers, 512 dims)
  Lexicon: standard, allPVs, usedPVs, likelyPVs
  LM: SRILM trigram, optionally augmented with AGS + Wikipedia + Europarl
  Output: WER per conversation pair

- Critical path:
  1. Extract MFCCs from audio
  2. Train GMM-HMM with canonical lexicon
  3. Generate forced alignments
  4. Build likelyPVs lexicon from alignments
  5. Train DNN-HMM with likelyPVs
  6. Train LM with corpus + optional augmentation
  7. Evaluate WER per conversation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger pronunciation lexicons increase coverage but add confusion; filtering to likely variants trades recall for precision.
  - Adding LM data improves language modeling but increases training time and storage; knowledge-based lexicons offer a cheaper alternative.
  - Cross-validation gives robust variability estimates but requires more training runs.

- Failure signatures:
  - WER spikes for specific conversation pairs → lexicon missing speaker-specific variants.
  - High variance across conversations despite similar WER averages → lack of robustness to speaker/dialect differences.
  - Degraded performance when adding LM data → possible overfitting or domain mismatch.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train baseline GMM-HMM with standard lexicon on GRASS CS, evaluate per conversation.
  2. Apply rule R1 to generate devoiced variants, retrain AM, compare WER per conversation.
  3. Generate likelyPVs from forced alignments, retrain AM, compare WER per conversation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the integration of pronunciation variants impact ASR performance for conversational speech in Austrian German?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors found that using a lexicon with likely pronunciation variants (likelyPVs) improved performance by approximately 1.5% compared to the standard lexicon, suggesting that pronunciation variation modeling is crucial for ASR accuracy.
- Why unresolved: The study focused on a limited set of pronunciation rules and variants. The impact of more extensive or different pronunciation variants on ASR performance remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with a broader set of pronunciation variants and rules, and comparing their impact on ASR performance, would provide insights into the optimal level of pronunciation variation modeling.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the primary factors contributing to the lack of robustness in ASR systems for conversational Austrian German?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors observed high variation in WER across different conversations, suggesting that factors such as pronunciation variation, speech rate, lexical choice, and turn-taking behavior may contribute to the lack of robustness.
- Why unresolved: The study did not perform a detailed analysis of the specific factors affecting ASR robustness for conversational speech. Understanding these factors is crucial for improving system performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing the correlation between WER and specific conversational speech features, such as pronunciation variation, speech rate, and lexical choice, would help identify the primary factors affecting robustness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the size of the language model data affect the performance of ASR systems for conversational Austrian German?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors found that increasing the size of the language model data by approximately 360% to 760% had a similar impact on improving WER as using the best pronunciation lexicon. This suggests that data size is a critical factor for ASR performance.
- Why unresolved: The study did not explore the impact of varying language model data sizes beyond the tested range. Understanding the relationship between data size and performance is essential for optimizing ASR systems.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different sizes of language model data and analyzing their impact on WER would provide insights into the optimal data size for improving ASR performance.

## Limitations
- Small conversational dataset (14.3 hours total) may not capture full Austrian German variation
- Knowledge-based lexicon relies on 26 manually crafted rules whose sufficiency is uncertain
- Filtering threshold of p > 0.65 for likely pronunciation variants was chosen empirically without systematic exploration
- Single language modeling approach (trigram) and evaluation only on GRASS corpus limits generalizability

## Confidence

**Confidence labels:**
- High confidence: The general finding that knowledge-based pronunciation lexicons improve ASR performance for low-resource dialects
- Medium confidence: The specific claim that using likelyPVs lexicon performs similarly to 360-760% more LM data
- Low confidence: The precise WER values (48.5% average) due to the small test set size per conversation pair
