# The language of sounds unheard: Exploring musical timbre semantics of large language models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.07830
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07830
- Reference count: 7
- ChatGPT's semantic ratings for musical instruments partially correlate with human ratings along established perceptual dimensions like brightness and pitch height, with comparable internal variability to human raters.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models can generate semantic profiles for musical instrument sounds without direct auditory input. Using ChatGPT, researchers collected ratings on 20 semantic scales for various instrument-register combinations and compared them to human ratings. The results show partial correlation with human judgments, robust agreement along key psychophysical dimensions, and surprisingly comparable internal variability between the chatbot and human raters. Factor analysis revealed shared dimensionality but different spatial configuration of latent semantic spaces.

## Method Summary
The study collected human ratings from 540 participants who evaluated notes from eight Western orchestral instruments across three registers on 20 semantic scales. ChatGPT was prompted to rate the same instrument-register combinations in separate conversations, with 50 ratings collected per combination. Statistical analyses included Pearson correlation coefficients between ChatGPT and human ratings, inter-rater correlations to assess consistency, and exploratory factor analysis to compare latent semantic representations. The analysis focused on identifying patterns of agreement and differences in semantic organization between human and machine ratings.

## Key Results
- ChatGPT's ratings partially correlated with human ratings while showing robust agreement on brightness (bright-dark) and pitch height (deep-high) dimensions
- Internal variability of ChatGPT responses matched the magnitude of human inter-rater variability
- Factor analysis revealed same dimensionality but different spatial configuration of latent semantic spaces between human and chatbot ratings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LLMs can partially align with human perceptual semantics for sound when semantic dimensions are anchored in well-established psychophysical constructs like brightness and pitch height.
- Mechanism: LLMs trained on large corpora capture crossmodal associations and linguistic patterns that map onto stable auditory perceptual dimensions, allowing partial semantic alignment even without direct auditory input.
- Core assumption: Crossmodal linguistic associations in training data are sufficient to recover core perceptual dimensions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ChatGPT generated semantic profiles that only partially correlated with human ratings, yet showed robust agreement along well-known psychophysical dimensions of musical sounds such as brightness (bright-dark) and pitch height (deep-high)."
  - [section] "It is worth mentioning that these two scales are related to two of the most salient perceptual dimensions of musical sounds, namely pitch height (deep–high) and brightness ( bright–dark)."
- Break condition: If training data lacks sufficient crossmodal linguistic grounding for specific semantic dimensions, alignment will degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Internal variability of LLM responses to identical prompts can be comparable in magnitude to human inter-rater variability.
- Mechanism: Stochastic sampling in LLM generation introduces response variance that mirrors the natural inconsistency observed in human raters when evaluating subjective perceptual dimensions.
- Core assumption: LLM temperature-based sampling introduces variability patterns similar to human judgment variance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Unexpectedly, the chatbot showed degrees of internal variability that were comparable in magnitude to that of human ratings."
  - [section] "Calculating the median IRC across scales suggested highest consistency between responses generated by CGPT with higher levels of IRC of the ratings of the chatbot (across different conversations) compared to human ratings (across different participants)."
- Break condition: If sampling temperature is set too low or too high, the variability pattern will no longer match human inter-rater variability.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Factor analysis reveals that LLM and human semantic spaces share dimensionality but differ in spatial configuration.
- Mechanism: While both LLM and human responses reduce to similar latent dimensional structures, the mapping of specific semantic scales to these factors differs, indicating different underlying representations.
- Core assumption: Both humans and LLMs organize semantic judgments into similar low-dimensional latent spaces despite different acquisition mechanisms.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Exploratory factor analysis suggested the same dimensionality but different spatial configuration of a latent factor space between the chatbot and human ratings."
  - [section] "Parallel analysis supported a three-factor solution for both human and machine ratings. The factors cumulatively accounted for 82% and 70% of data variance in the human and CGPT ratings, respectively."
- Break condition: If the underlying semantic structure of the LLM's training data differs fundamentally from human experience, the shared dimensionality assumption fails.

  - Low correlations across all scales: Poor prompt design or insufficient crossmodal associations in training data
  - Uniform responses across all conversations: Temperature set too low, failing to capture natural variability

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do variations in the "temperature" parameter of GPT models affect the consistency of their semantic ratings compared to human raters?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the online public CGPT interface uses a temperature of 1, while GPT3 uses 0.7, and suggests this could affect consistency.
- Why unresolved: The study did not systematically vary the temperature parameter to assess its impact on consistency.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with multiple temperature settings and comparing the consistency of ratings to human data would clarify this effect.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the knowledge cutoff of LLMs (e.g., Q4 2021 for GPT3) influence their ability to replicate human perceptual tasks compared to more recent studies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that human data from Reymore et al. (2023) was not available to CGPT, unlike earlier studies used by Marjieh et al. (2023).
- Why unresolved: The study does not explore how access to or lack of specific datasets might influence LLM performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing LLMs with tasks involving data from different time periods could reveal how knowledge cutoffs impact performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can LLMs achieve higher consistency with human ratings if prompted individually for each data point rather than providing multiple ratings in one response?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests that collecting one data point per prompt might better fit the human experiment design.
- Why unresolved: The study used a single prompt for multiple ratings, which may not align with how humans provide ratings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing consistency metrics between single-prompt and multi-prompt approaches would determine the optimal method.

## Limitations
- Results may not generalize beyond the specific set of 20 semantic scales and eight Western orchestral instruments used in the study
- The specific version of ChatGPT (based on GPT-3) used may no longer be available, limiting reproducibility
- Factor analysis revealed differences in spatial configuration between human and LLM semantic spaces, suggesting different underlying representations

## Confidence
- High confidence: ChatGPT's ratings partially correlate with human ratings along well-known psychophysical dimensions (brightness and pitch height)
- Medium confidence: LLM internal variability is comparable to human inter-rater variability
- Low confidence: Generalizability of findings to other semantic scales, instruments, or LLM architectures

## Next Checks
1. Test the semantic rating task with different instrument sets (e.g., non-Western instruments or electronic sounds) to assess whether the observed correlations and factor structures generalize beyond the original experimental materials

2. Implement a controlled temperature sweep (e.g., 0.0 to 1.0) to systematically map how sampling temperature affects LLM response variability and compare it directly to human inter-rater variability across the same range

3. Conduct cross-modal grounding experiments where semantic ratings are elicited from both text descriptions and audio samples of the same instruments to better understand how LLM responses differ when auditory input is or is not available
