# WangLab at MEDIQA-Chat 2023: Clinical Note Generation from Doctor-Patient Conversations using Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.02220
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02220
- Reference count: 21
- Fine-tuned pre-trained language models and few-shot in-context learning with large language models both achieved high performance on automatic clinical note generation from doctor-patient conversations, ranking second and first, respectively, among all submissions to the MEDIQA-Chat 2023 shared task.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents two approaches for generating clinical notes from doctor-patient conversations: fine-tuning pre-trained language models and few-shot in-context learning with large language models. The fine-tuning approach uses LED models initialized from BART and fine-tuned on PubMed summarization data, while the ICL approach leverages GPT-4 with natural language instructions and similarity-based in-context examples. Both methods achieved strong performance on the MEDIQA-Chat 2023 shared task, with the ICL approach ranking first and generating notes preferred about as often as human-written notes in expert evaluation.

## Method Summary
The paper explores two complementary approaches for clinical note generation. The fine-tuning approach uses the LED model (BART-based) fine-tuned on PubMed summarization data and then adapted to the specific task of converting doctor-patient conversations into structured clinical notes. The few-shot in-context learning approach uses GPT-4 with carefully selected examples that demonstrate the expected structure and style of clinical notes. In-context examples are selected based on semantic similarity to the input dialogue using the Instructor text encoder. Both approaches are evaluated on automatic metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore, BLEURT) and human evaluation by expert physicians.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned pre-trained language models achieved strong performance, ranking second among all submissions
- Few-shot in-context learning with GPT-4 achieved state-of-the-art performance, ranking first among all submissions
- GPT-4-generated notes were preferred about as often as human-written notes in expert human evaluation
- Similarity-based selection of in-context examples improved performance by 4 or more points on average

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning pre-trained language models achieves strong performance by adapting general language understanding to the specific domain of clinical notes.
- Mechanism: The LED model, initialized from BART which was pre-trained on general domain text, is fine-tuned on PubMed summarization data to align it with medical text characteristics before being further adapted to the clinical note generation task.
- Core assumption: The knowledge gained from general language pre-training and medical domain fine-tuning transfers effectively to the specific task of converting doctor-patient conversations into structured clinical notes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Fine-tuned pre-trained language models and few-shot in-context learning with large language models both achieved high performance"
  - [section]: "Performance further improves by initializing the model with a checkpoint fine-tuned on the PubMed summarization dataset (LEDLARGE-PubMed). This is likely because (1) Dialogue2Note resembles a summarization task, and (2) text from PubMed is more similar to clinical text than is the general domain text used to pre-train LED."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus doesn't contain specific evidence about PubMed fine-tuning effectiveness
- Break condition: If the medical domain fine-tuning fails to capture the specific terminology, structure, or style of clinical documentation, or if the conversation-to-note mapping is too complex for the fine-tuned model to learn effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: In-context learning with large language models achieves state-of-the-art performance by leveraging the model's inherent understanding of task structure and style through carefully selected examples.
- Mechanism: GPT-4 is provided with natural language instructions and in-context examples that demonstrate the expected structure, style, and content of clinical notes, allowing it to generate appropriate outputs without parameter updates.
- Core assumption: GPT-4 has sufficient pre-existing knowledge of clinical documentation conventions and can generalize from a small number of in-context examples to produce high-quality notes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "notes generated via the in-context learning approach with GPT-4 were preferred about as often as human-written notes"
  - [section]: "We find that selecting in-context examples based on the similarity of dialogues has a strong positive impact, typically improving average score by 4 or more"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus doesn't contain specific evidence about in-context learning effectiveness for this task
- Break condition: If GPT-4 lacks sufficient understanding of clinical documentation conventions, or if the in-context examples fail to adequately represent the task structure and style, leading to poor generalization.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Selecting in-context examples based on dialogue similarity improves performance by providing the most relevant examples for each specific input.
- Mechanism: The Instructor text encoder embeds both training dialogues and the input dialogue, ranking training examples by cosine similarity to select the most semantically relevant in-context examples for each generation task.
- Core assumption: Similar doctor-patient conversations require similar clinical note structures and content, making dialogue similarity a good proxy for example relevance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We rank train examples based on their similarity to test dialogue using Instructor"
  - [section]: "First, selecting in-context examples based on the similarity of dialogues has a strong positive impact, typically improving average score by 4 or more"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus doesn't contain specific evidence about dialogue similarity selection effectiveness
- Break condition: If dialogue similarity doesn't correlate with note structure or content similarity, or if the Instructor embeddings fail to capture the relevant semantic features for this task.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input: Doctor-patient dialogue (text)
  - Fine-tuning pipeline: LED model with PubMed fine-tuning + task-specific fine-tuning
  - ICL pipeline: GPT-4 with natural language instructions + similarity-based in-context examples
  - Output: Clinical note with multiple sections
  - Evaluation: ROUGE, BERTScore, BLEURT, human evaluation

- Critical path:
  1. Input dialogue processing and tokenization
  2. For fine-tuning: encode with LED, decode with beam search
  3. For ICL: embed dialogues with Instructor, select top-k examples, construct prompt, generate with GPT-4
  4. Post-process output (parse sections, fuzzy matching for invalid headers)
  5. Evaluate against ground truth

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fine-tuning vs. ICL: Fine-tuning offers more control and data privacy but requires computational resources and may not generalize as well; ICL is more flexible and requires no parameter updates but raises data privacy concerns and may be sensitive to prompt engineering
  - Example selection: Using similar dialogues improves performance but adds computational overhead; random selection is simpler but less effective
  - Model size: Larger models (LEDLARGE vs LEDBASE) improve performance but require more resources; smaller models are more efficient but less capable

- Failure signatures:
  - Fine-tuning: Invalid section headers, missing critical information, incorrect medical terminology, failure to capture conversation nuances
  - ICL: Hallucinations, irrelevant information, failure to follow instructions, sensitivity to prompt formatting, inability to handle out-of-distribution conversations

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare fine-tuned LEDBASE vs LEDLARGE on the validation set to measure the impact of model scaling
  2. Test ICL with GPT-4 using 0, 1, 2, and 3 in-context examples to determine optimal example count
  3. Compare random vs. similarity-based example selection for ICL to validate the effectiveness of the similarity ranking approach

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal number of in-context examples to use with GPT-4 for clinical note generation from doctor-patient conversations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "increasing the number of in-context examples had a marginal effect on performance."
- Why unresolved: While the paper found that using more in-context examples had little impact, it did not explore a wide range of example counts to determine the true optimal number.
- What evidence would resolve it: Additional experiments varying the number of in-context examples (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 5, 10) and comparing the generated note quality would help identify the optimal number.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different LLM architectures (e.g., GPT-3.5, Claude, etc.) compare to GPT-4 for clinical note generation from doctor-patient conversations using in-context learning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only explored using GPT-4 for in-context learning and did not compare its performance to other LLMs.
- Why unresolved: The paper's findings are specific to GPT-4 and may not generalize to other LLM architectures, which could potentially perform better or worse.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments using different LLM architectures (e.g., GPT-3.5, Claude, etc.) for in-context learning and comparing their performance on the clinical note generation task would provide insights into the generalizability of the approach.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different strategies for selecting in-context examples (e.g., random selection, semantic similarity, topic modeling) compare for clinical note generation from doctor-patient conversations using in-context learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explored using semantic similarity to select in-context examples and found it to be effective, but did not compare it to other selection strategies.
- Why unresolved: While semantic similarity was found to be effective, it is unclear if it is the optimal strategy for selecting in-context examples or if other strategies could perform better.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments using different in-context example selection strategies (e.g., random selection, semantic similarity, topic modeling, etc.) and comparing their impact on the quality of generated clinical notes would help identify the most effective strategy.

## Limitations
- Limited dataset size (67 training examples) raises concerns about generalizability to real-world clinical settings
- Human evaluation was conducted by only two physicians, which may introduce bias and limit reliability
- The paper doesn't address potential hallucinations or factual errors that could arise from large language models generating clinical notes
- Privacy implications of using GPT-4 for clinical documentation are not discussed

## Confidence
- High confidence: The effectiveness of fine-tuning pre-trained language models for clinical note generation is well-supported by the empirical results showing strong automatic metric scores and second-place ranking.
- Medium confidence: The claim that GPT-4's in-context learning approach produces notes "preferred about as often as human-written notes" is based on limited human evaluation with only two physicians, which may not be representative of broader clinical practice.
- Low confidence: The assertion that dialogue similarity selection is a universally effective approach for in-context learning lacks sufficient evidence, as the paper only reports improvements in average score without providing statistical significance testing or ablation studies.

## Next Checks
1. **Statistical validation of human evaluation results**: Conduct significance testing on the human evaluation preferences to determine if GPT-4's performance is truly comparable to human-written notes, using a larger pool of clinicians and more rigorous evaluation protocols.

2. **Error analysis and hallucination detection**: Perform systematic analysis of model-generated notes to identify and quantify hallucinations, factual errors, or clinically significant omissions that could impact patient care if deployed in practice.

3. **Generalization testing on out-of-distribution conversations**: Test both fine-tuned and ICL approaches on doctor-patient conversations from different medical specialties, conversation lengths, or clinical contexts to assess the robustness of the approaches beyond the specific dataset used in the study.
