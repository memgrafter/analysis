# The Role of Heuristics and Biases During Complex Choices with an AI Teammate

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05969
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05969
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: Participants over-rely on AI helpers, especially under loss frames, leading to worse outcomes due to reduced exploration effort.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how cognitive biases, specifically framing and anchoring effects, impact human decision-making when working with an AI teammate on complex choice tasks. Participants completed dial-tuning tasks both alone and with an AI helper, with treatments varying between gain/loss framing and the presence/absence of explicit anchors. The results show that participants performed worse when working with the AI, particularly in the loss frame, due to reduced search effort and over-reliance on the AI. Those in the gain frame maintained performance despite reduced effort, while those with explicit anchors adjusted their effort less when working with the AI but did not improve outcomes. The study highlights the importance of accounting for cognitive biases in human-AI team design and suggests that people may over-rely on AI helpers, especially when operating under loss frames.

## Method Summary
The study used a 2x2 factorial design crossing gain/loss framing with anchoring conditions. 400 participants were recruited via Prolific Academic and completed four dial-tuning tasks (two solo, two with AI helper) on procedurally generated landscapes with 1 or 4 peaks. The AI helper implemented a stochastic simulated annealing algorithm. Performance was measured by adjusted scores, search duration (number of dial settings), and explore/exploit trade-off (fraction of explorations). Statistical analysis tested for main effects and interactions of framing and anchoring on choice outcomes and effort levels.

## Key Results
- Participants performed worse when working with the AI, particularly in the loss frame, due to reduced search effort and over-reliance on the AI.
- Loss frame participants reduced their effort significantly more than gain frame participants when working with the AI.
- Participants with explicit anchors adjusted their effort downwards less than those without, but this did not improve outcomes as the AI did not consistently reach anchor values.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Participants over-rely on the AI helper due to framing-induced effort reduction.
- Mechanism: Loss framing causes participants to reduce their exploration effort more than gain framing, leading to worse outcomes with the AI. The AI is stochastic, so its performance varies, but participants assume it compensates for their reduced effort.
- Core assumption: The AI helper is not always optimal and participants do not understand its limitations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The evidence suggests that some participants, particularly those in a loss frame, put too much faith in the AI helper and experienced worse choice outcomes by doing so."
  - [section] "loss frame participants adjusted their effort downwards (M = −19.964, SD = 39.838) significantly more than gain frame participants (M = −6.088, SD = 24.808; t(318.21) = −4.319, p < 0.001) when they started working with the AI. This is the inverse of the anchoring effect: participants with explicit anchors adjusted their effort downwards (M = 6.449, SD = 29.426) significantly less than those without (M = 19.060, SD = 36.341; t(382.16) = 3.804, p < 0.001)."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; no direct citations on framing effects in AI teaming.
- Break condition: If participants are explicitly trained on AI helper limitations or if the AI helper becomes deterministic and consistently optimal.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Anchoring reduces adaptive effort adjustment when working with the AI.
- Mechanism: Participants with explicit anchors (knowledge of best possible value) reduce their effort less when the AI joins, but this does not improve outcomes because the AI does not consistently reach the anchor value.
- Core assumption: The AI helper's stochastic nature means it does not always achieve the best possible value, so reducing effort based on an anchor is not adaptive.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "participants with explicit anchors adjusted their effort downwards (M = 6.449, SD = 29.426) significantly less than those without (M = 19.060, SD = 36.341; t(382.16) = 3.804, p < 0.001). ... This was not correlated with a significantly lower adjusted score during the AI tasks for the no-anchor participants (Mdif f erence = −0.090; t(200) = −1.730, p = 0.090). It was, however, correlated with a significantly lower adjusted score for the anchor participants (Mdif f erence = −0.121; t(195) = −2.539, p = 0.012)."
  - [abstract] "The biases are predictive of choice outcomes and highlight opportunities to develop AI helpers that can improve outcomes by accounting for these biases."
  - [corpus] No direct citations; weak evidence for anchoring in AI teaming.
- Break condition: If the AI helper is deterministic and always achieves near-optimal values, or if participants are trained to adjust effort based on AI performance rather than anchors.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Reduced exploration in later tasks is due to expectation of AI compensation.
- Mechanism: Participants expect the AI to compensate for their reduced effort in later tasks, leading to less exploration and worse outcomes, especially in loss framing.
- Core assumption: Participants form expectations about AI helper performance based on initial interactions and adjust their effort accordingly.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The only significant drop was between their second solo effort and the first team effort (Mdif f erence = −5.108; t(396) = 5.003, p < 0.001). ... This points to participants expecting that the AI would reduce the effort they needed to invest in the task to perform well."
  - [abstract] "The evidence suggests that some participants, particularly those in a loss frame, put too much faith in the AI helper and experienced worse choice outcomes by doing so."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; no direct citations on expectation formation in AI teaming.
- Break condition: If participants are explicitly informed about the AI's stochastic nature or if the AI's performance is consistently communicated.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Experiment interface -> AI helper (stochastic simulated annealing) -> Data collection (choices, effort, strategy) -> Analysis pipeline (adjusted scores, statistical tests)

- Critical path:
  1. Participant completes solo tasks (two landscapes, one and four peaks).
  2. Participant works with AI helper on two more landscapes.
  3. Data is collected and adjusted scores are calculated.
  4. Statistical analysis tests for main effects and interactions of framing and anchoring.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Simplicity of AI helper vs. realism: A simple stochastic algorithm was chosen to ensure rich interactions and reduce variance, but this may not reflect real-world AI helpers.
  - Abstract task vs. ecological validity: The dial-tuning task is abstract and generalizable, but may not fully capture the complexity of real-world decision-making.
  - Fixed number of settings vs. exhaustive search: Limiting the number of settings prevents exhaustive search but may not fully explore the landscape.

- Failure signatures:
  - No significant difference in performance between solo and team tasks: May indicate the AI helper is not challenging enough or participants are not adjusting their effort.
  - No effect of framing or anchoring: May indicate the manipulations are not strong enough or the task does not capture the biases.
  - High variance in performance: May indicate the stochastic AI helper is too unpredictable or the task is too difficult.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run a pilot with a small sample to check for ceiling/floor effects and adjust task difficulty.
  2. Test the effect of framing alone by removing the anchoring manipulation to isolate its impact.
  3. Test the effect of anchoring alone by removing the framing manipulation to isolate its impact.

- Why unresolved: The paper does not delve into the specific cognitive processes or biases that lead to this over-reliance.

## Limitations

- The study's findings may not generalize to real-world AI assistants, as the simulated annealing AI is simplified compared to modern AI systems.
- The abstract nature of the dial-tuning task may not fully capture the complexity of real-world decision-making.
- The study does not provide specific design principles or strategies for AI helpers to account for cognitive biases.

## Confidence

- Framing effects on effort reduction: Medium
- Anchoring effects on adaptive behavior: Medium
- Generalizability to real-world AI helpers: Low
- Causal mechanisms of over-reliance: Medium

## Next Checks

1. Conduct a follow-up experiment with a more realistic AI helper (e.g., reinforcement learning-based) to test if the framing and anchoring effects persist with different AI architectures.

3. Replicate the study with a different complex choice task (e.g., resource allocation or route planning) to assess the generalizability of the findings across domains.
