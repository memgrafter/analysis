# Distinguishing ChatGPT(-3.5, -4)-generated and human-written papers through Japanese stylometric analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.05534
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05534
- Authors: [Authors not provided]
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: ChatGPT-generated and human-written Japanese texts can be distinguished with 100% accuracy using stylometric features, particularly function word rates

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the distinguishability of ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) -generated from human-written Japanese texts using stylometric analysis. The authors compared distributions of academic papers with GPT-generated texts based on the same paper titles, analyzing four stylometric features: bigrams of parts-of-speech, bigram of postpositional particle words, positioning of commas, and rate of function words. Random Forest classifiers achieved perfect performance (100% accuracy, recall, precision, F1) in distinguishing AI-generated from human-written texts, with function word rate being the most discriminative feature.

## Method Summary
The study collected 72 Japanese academic papers by single authors and generated 144 corresponding texts using ChatGPT (72 GPT-3.5, 72 GPT-4) based on the same paper titles. Four stylometric features were extracted: bigrams of parts-of-speech, bigrams of postpositional particle words, comma positioning, and function word rates. Random Forest classifiers with leave-one-out cross-validation were trained to distinguish between AI-generated and human-written texts. Multi-dimensional scaling was used to visualize the distributions of these features.

## Key Results
- Random Forest classifiers achieved 100% accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 score in distinguishing ChatGPT-generated from human-written Japanese texts
- Function word rate was the most discriminative feature, achieving 98.1% accuracy on its own
- Despite GPT-4 having more parameters than GPT-3.5, both models' text distributions overlapped similarly and remained distinct from human distributions
- Multi-dimensional scaling revealed clear separation between AI-generated and human text clusters for all four feature types

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Function word rate is the most discriminative stylometric feature between AI and human texts.
- Mechanism: Function words are used unconsciously and reflect deeper linguistic patterns that differ statistically between AI and human authors.
- Core assumption: Function word usage is less dependent on topic and more on inherent writing style.
- Evidence anchors: RF classifier achieved 98.1% accuracy using function word rates; function word rate denotes percentage of no-meaning words excluding noun, verb, and adjective.
- Break condition: If AI models are fine-tuned to mimic human function word distributions, this feature's discriminative power may degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Increasing model parameters (GPT-4 vs GPT-3.5) does not bring AI text distributions closer to human distributions.
- Mechanism: Despite GPT-4 having approximately 100 trillion parameters versus GPT-3.5's 175 billion, stylometric distributions remain distinct.
- Core assumption: Stylometric features reflect deeper linguistic processing differences that scale differently than parameter count.
- Evidence anchors: GPT-4 distributions were not closer to human distributions compared with GPT-3.5.
- Break condition: If future AI models incorporate explicit stylistic conditioning, parameter scaling might eventually close the gap.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Random Forest classifiers outperform transformer-based models for Japanese stylometric authorship classification.
- Mechanism: Random Forest handles high-dimensional feature spaces well, provides interpretable feature importance scores, and avoids overfitting better than transformer models for this specific task.
- Core assumption: Traditional ML methods with carefully engineered features can outperform end-to-end neural approaches when feature engineering captures domain-specific patterns effectively.
- Evidence anchors: RF achieved best scores among several machine learning techniques and provides understandable "importance" of variables.
- Break condition: If feature sets become less informative or if transformer models incorporate explicit stylistic features, the advantage may shift.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) for visualizing high-dimensional stylometric distributions
  - Why needed here: MDS reduces complex feature spaces to 2D visualizations that reveal separation patterns between AI and human text clusters
  - Quick check question: What distance metric did the authors use in their MDS analysis to measure similarity between text distributions?

- Concept: Jensen-Shannon divergence as a symmetric distance measure for probability distributions
  - Why needed here: JS divergence provides a mathematically sound way to compare stylometric feature distributions between different text classes
  - Quick check question: Why might JS divergence be preferred over KL divergence for comparing stylometric feature distributions?

- Concept: Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) for small sample sizes
  - Why needed here: With only 72 samples per class, LOOCV maximizes training data usage while providing unbiased performance estimates
  - Quick check question: How does LOOCV differ from k-fold cross-validation in terms of computational cost and variance of the performance estimate?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do ChatGPT-generated texts in languages other than Japanese compare to human-written texts using stylometric analysis?
- Basis in paper: The authors note their study only considered Japanese language and that GPT-4's performance in Japanese was ranked 16th in the MMLU benchmark.
- Why unresolved: The study was limited to Japanese texts and did not compare results across different languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct similar stylometric analyses on ChatGPT-generated and human-written texts in multiple languages, comparing distributions and classification performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Would the classification performance between AI-generated and human-written texts change with texts longer than 1,000 characters?
- Basis in paper: The authors controlled text length to approximately 1,000 characters and anticipated similar performance with longer texts but noted this as a limitation.
- Why unresolved: The study only analyzed texts of approximately 1,000 characters and did not explore the impact of text length on classification performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Perform the same classification analysis on texts of varying lengths, especially those longer than 1,000 characters, to determine if performance changes.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do stylometric features of texts generated by other AI systems (e.g., Google's Bard, Microsoft's Bing) compare to those generated by ChatGPT?
- Basis in paper: The authors mention that other text-generated AI systems exist and suggest that different AI may generate different distributions of stylometric texts.
- Why unresolved: The study focused solely on ChatGPT-generated texts and did not analyze texts from other AI systems.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct stylometric analyses on texts generated by various AI systems and compare their distributions and classification performance with human-written texts.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the flexibility of text types (e.g., diaries, blogs, social media posts) affect the ability to distinguish AI-generated from human-written texts using stylometric features?
- Basis in paper: The authors note that academic papers have lower flexibility and suggest that materials with higher degrees of freedom should be used in future studies.
- Why unresolved: The study used academic papers as samples, which have strict writing rules, and did not explore more flexible text types.
- What evidence would resolve it: Perform stylometric analyses on various types of texts with different levels of flexibility and compare the ability to distinguish AI-generated from human-written texts.

## Limitations
- Small sample size (72 human texts, 144 AI texts) may limit generalizability
- Focus exclusively on academic psychology papers, not representing other Japanese writing domains
- ChatGPT generation performed in January 2023, newer model versions may have evolved beyond these detection boundaries

## Confidence
**High Confidence**: RF classifier's perfect performance metrics and clear MDS visualization showing distinct clusters for AI vs human texts. Function word rates are confirmed as discriminative features.

**Medium Confidence**: Claim that GPT-4 does not produce distributions closer to human texts than GPT-3.5 lacks direct comparative evidence in the corpus.

**Low Confidence**: Generalization to other Japanese text domains beyond academic psychology papers and assumption that detection boundaries will remain stable as AI models evolve.

## Next Checks
1. **Temporal Robustness Test**: Generate new ChatGPT texts using the same prompts with current GPT-4 models (post-2023 updates) and compare feature distributions to the original study's data to assess detection boundary stability.

2. **Cross-Domain Validation**: Apply the same stylometric analysis and RF classifier to Japanese texts from different domains (fiction, news articles, technical documentation) to test generalizability beyond academic psychology papers.

3. **Feature Importance Verification**: Perform ablation studies by systematically removing individual feature types from the RF classifier to confirm that reported feature importances (particularly for function word rates) remain consistent and that the classifier doesn't rely on spurious correlations.
