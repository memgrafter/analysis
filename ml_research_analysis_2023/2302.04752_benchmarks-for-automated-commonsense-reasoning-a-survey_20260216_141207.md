# Benchmarks for Automated Commonsense Reasoning: A Survey

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.04752
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04752
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Survey of 139 benchmarks reveals significant quality flaws and coverage gaps in commonsense reasoning evaluation

## Executive Summary
This paper surveys 139 benchmarks for automated commonsense reasoning, revealing widespread quality issues including incorrect answers, unnatural language, and encyclopedic rather than commonsense knowledge. The analysis shows that only a handful of benchmarks adequately test complex reasoning or integrate multiple modalities. Major gaps remain in testing temporal and spatial reasoning, and many benchmarks suffer from training-set contamination issues. The paper recommends investing in high-quality benchmark construction, focusing on foundational commonsense understanding, and developing ways to exclude benchmarks from training data.

## Method Summary
The survey systematically extracted and analyzed 139 benchmarks across multiple modalities (text, image, video, simulated) and construction methods (expert, crowd, synthetic). Quality assessment involved identifying flaws such as incorrect answers, unnatural language, and reliance on encyclopedic knowledge. Coverage analysis examined which aspects of commonsense reasoning were tested, with particular attention to gaps in temporal and spatial reasoning. The methodology also considered the impact of training-set contamination on benchmark validity.

## Key Results
- Only a handful of benchmarks adequately test complex reasoning or integrate multiple modalities
- Many benchmarks contain significant flaws including incorrect answers and unnatural language
- Temporal and spatial reasoning remain largely untested in existing benchmarks
- Large language models may have seen benchmark examples during pre-training, reducing discriminative power

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Survey compiles 139 benchmarks to reveal quality gaps and coverage holes in commonsense reasoning evaluation
- Mechanism: Systematic enumeration across modalities and methods surfaces heterogeneous flaws
- Core assumption: Existing benchmarks are representative enough that flaws observed in samples generalize to the whole corpus
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Many important aspects of commonsense reasoning, such as temporal and spatial reasoning, remain untested"
  - [section 4.1] "Even in well-regarded benchmarks, a significant fraction of the examples may be substantially flawed"
  - [corpus] Weak: "Found 25 related papers" with very low citation counts; indicates sparse follow-up literature
- Break condition: If surveyed benchmarks are heavily self-selected or methodologically biased, generalization fails

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Benchmarks designed for other tasks can still reveal commonsense reasoning limits when reused
- Mechanism: Adaptation from pre-existing datasets introduces both breadth and unintended confounders
- Core assumption: Downstream reuse preserves enough semantic structure to test the intended commonsense reasoning dimension
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6] "Extraction from pre-existing collection... can make earlier flaws irrelevant, or it can preserve them, or exacerbate them"
  - [section 7] Describes benchmarks adapted from sources like MS-COCO, ActionNet, etc.
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct corpus evidence for successful reuse; only generic "related papers" listing
- Break condition: If semantic drift during adaptation is large, the benchmark no longer measures the intended reasoning skill

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Large language models trained on web text include many benchmark examples, reducing their discriminative power
- Mechanism: Pre-training exposure to benchmark texts means models can answer by recall rather than reasoning
- Core assumption: Benchmark examples are sufficiently prominent in web corpora to be captured during pre-training
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.4] "any test example that has ever been published on the open web or in social media... must be considered as possibly part of its training set"
  - [section 9] ChatGPT examples show recall of well-known schemas but failure on novel ones
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct evidence of pre-training overlap; only speculative claim
- Break condition: If benchmarks are truly novel or heavily filtered from training data, this effect disappears

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Commonsense reasoning vs. common knowledge vs. encyclopedic knowledge
  - Why needed here: Benchmark quality depends on correctly categorizing knowledge types to avoid unfair tests
  - Quick check question: Is "The Earth orbits the Sun" commonsense or encyclopedic knowledge? (Answer: Encyclopedic)

- Concept: Artifact detection in AI benchmarks
  - Why needed here: Many benchmarks contain shortcuts or biases that allow solving without the intended reasoning
  - Quick check question: In SNLI/MultiNLI, negation words correlated with contradictions—what kind of artifact is this? (Answer: Lexical cue bias)

- Concept: Distributional similarity in benchmark construction
  - Why needed here: Large datasets assume noise averages out; small clean sets assume quality dominates
  - Quick check question: If a test set has 10% noise, how much accuracy separation is needed to be statistically confident? (Answer: ~0.3% for 100k examples)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we design commonsense benchmarks that test temporal reasoning across different modalities (text, images, video) rather than focusing on a single modality?
- Basis in paper: Inferred - The paper discusses the need for benchmarks to cover the whole range of knowledge and forms of reasoning involved in common sense, and mentions that common sense interacts with a wide range of tasks in all modalities
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the need for multimodal temporal reasoning benchmarks, it does not provide specific design guidelines or examples of how to create such benchmarks
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of new multimodal temporal reasoning benchmarks that successfully measure performance across text, images, and video

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between benchmark size and quality for measuring commonsense reasoning abilities, and how does this balance change as AI technology advances?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper extensively discusses the trade-off between size and quality in benchmarks
- Why unresolved: The paper presents arguments for both large noisy and small clean datasets but doesn't provide empirical evidence to determine the optimal balance
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the effectiveness of different sized benchmarks (varying in both size and quality) in measuring commonsense reasoning abilities across different AI systems and over time

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we develop evaluation methods for large language models that go beyond multiple choice and true/false questions to assess deeper understanding of commonsense concepts?
- Basis in paper: Inferred - The paper discusses the limitations of current benchmark formats and the need for benchmarks that test richer reasoning
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the limitations of current evaluation methods, it doesn't propose specific alternative methods
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of new evaluation methods that can assess deeper commonsense understanding in large language models

## Limitations
- The survey's comprehensive claim of 139 benchmarks relies heavily on literature review and may suffer from selection bias
- The methodology for identifying and quantifying flaws across all 139 benchmarks is not fully specified
- Analysis of training-set contamination remains speculative without direct evidence of overlap between benchmarks and pre-training corpora

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The identification of general quality issues in commonsense reasoning benchmarks is well-supported by multiple examples
- **Medium confidence**: Claims about specific coverage gaps are reasonable but may be incomplete given the survey's methodology limitations
- **Low confidence**: The assertion that large language models have seen benchmark examples during pre-training lacks direct empirical evidence

## Next Checks
