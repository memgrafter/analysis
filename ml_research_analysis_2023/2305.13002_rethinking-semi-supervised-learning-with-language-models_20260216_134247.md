# Rethinking Semi-supervised Learning with Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.13002
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13002
- Reference count: 40
- This paper shows that task-adaptive pre-training (TAPT) outperforms or matches five state-of-the-art self-training approaches in semi-supervised learning, particularly with limited data or domain shifts.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the prevailing assumption that sophisticated self-training approaches are necessary for effective semi-supervised learning with language models. Through systematic experimentation across five NLP tasks, the authors demonstrate that simple task-adaptive pre-training (TAPT) - continuing pre-training on task-specific unlabelled data - consistently outperforms or matches state-of-the-art self-training methods. TAPT proves particularly robust in low-resource settings and when domain shifts exist between labelled and unlabelled data. The results suggest that leveraging unsupervised pre-training objectives offers a simpler, more stable alternative to complex pseudo-labeling approaches, especially when data is scarce or domain mismatch is present.

## Method Summary
The paper compares task-adaptive pre-training (TAPT) with five self-training approaches (VAT, FixMatch, DASH, FlexMatch, AdaMatch) in semi-supervised learning settings across five text classification tasks. TAPT continues MLM pre-training of RoBERTa-base on task-specific unlabelled data before fine-tuning on labelled data. The self-training methods use teacher models to generate pseudo-labels for unlabelled data, incorporating various augmentation and consistency regularization strategies. Experiments vary both labelled and unlabelled data sizes (from 100 to 50,000 samples) and include domain adaptation scenarios where labelled and unlabelled data come from different distributions.

## Key Results
- TAPT consistently outperforms or matches the best self-training method across all five tasks
- TAPT shows greater improvements in semi-supervised settings than fully-supervised settings, especially with limited labelled data
- TAPT demonstrates superior robustness to domain shifts, often benefiting from source domain data while self-training methods fail
- Self-training approaches are vulnerable to small amounts of either labelled or unlabelled data, sometimes performing worse than fully-supervised baselines

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
TAPT improves performance more in semi-supervised learning than in fully-supervised settings by leveraging task-specific unlabelled data to adapt the language model to the target domain before fine-tuning. This provides richer task-level representation learning. The core assumption is that task-specific unlabelled data contains useful patterns that align with the labelled data distribution. Evidence shows TAPT gains more improvement compared to supervised baselines when using fewer labelled samples and tends to bring greater improvements in SSL than in fully-supervised settings.

### Mechanism 2
TAPT is more robust than self-training approaches across varying amounts of labelled and unlabelled data because it directly optimizes the language model on unlabelled data without relying on potentially noisy pseudo-labels, avoiding confirmation bias. The core assumption is that the language model can learn useful task representations from unlabelled data without supervision. Evidence shows TAPT performs more stable across different labelled and unlabelled data sizes than ST approaches, which are vulnerable to small amounts of either labelled or unlabelled data.

### Mechanism 3
TAPT benefits from domain shifts in unlabelled data while self-training approaches are negatively affected because TAPT can adapt to the target domain through continued pre-training, while ST approaches accumulate errors from domain-mismatched pseudo-labels. The core assumption is that target domain unlabelled data is more informative than source domain unlabelled data. Evidence shows TAPT even benefits from training on the source domain and demonstrates robust performance even with domain shifts, on par with its own SSL performance without domain shifts.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-training -> Task-specific continued pre-training (TAPT) or Teacher model training (ST) -> Fine-tuning/Student model training
- Critical path: For TAPT: pre-training → task-specific continued pre-training → fine-tuning. For ST: pre-training → teacher model training → pseudo-label generation → student model training
- Design tradeoffs: TAPT trades computational cost (continued pre-training) for robustness and improved performance, while ST trades complexity (multiple training steps) for potentially higher performance with adequate labelled data
- Failure signatures: TAPT may fail if unlabelled data is noisy or irrelevant. ST may fail if labelled data is insufficient or if domain shifts exist between labelled and unlabelled data
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare TAPT and ST performance on a small labelled/unlabelled dataset to observe initial trends
  2. Vary the amount of unlabelled data for TAPT and ST to assess their data efficiency
  3. Introduce domain shifts between labelled and unlabelled data to evaluate robustness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does TAPT scale similarly to ST in terms of performance gains as unlabelled data size increases, or does it plateau earlier? The study shows TAPT improves with more unlabelled data but doesn't explore the scaling law compared to ST. This remains unresolved because the study focuses on comparing TAPT and ST under varying data sizes but does not investigate whether TAPT's performance follows a similar scaling trend as ST when unlabelled data grows.

### Open Question 2
Can TAPT's effectiveness be improved by combining it with data augmentation or consistency regularization techniques used in ST? While TAPT and ST are compared, the potential synergies between TAPT's pre-training and ST's regularization methods are unexplored. This would require experiments training ST models initialized from TAPT checkpoints and/or applying TAPT with data augmentation or consistency losses.

### Open Question 3
How does TAPT perform in low-resource semi-supervised settings beyond text classification, such as sequence labeling or generation tasks? The authors note that their experiments are limited to text classification and suggest future work in other NLP tasks. This remains unresolved because the study focuses exclusively on classification benchmarks, leaving applicability to other tasks unclear.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to five English text classification tasks, limiting generalizability to other NLP domains or languages
- The specific thresholds where ST methods fail versus TAPT succeeds are not precisely quantified
- The computational cost of TAPT's continued pre-training is not explicitly compared to ST methods
- Domain adaptation experiments show TAPT benefits from source domain data, but the mechanisms and boundaries of this advantage are not fully explored

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: TAPT's consistent performance advantage over ST methods when labelled data is limited (100-500 samples)
- **Medium Confidence**: TAPT's superiority in domain-shifted scenarios
- **Low Confidence**: The claim that TAPT "tends to bring greater improvements in SSL than in fully-supervised settings"

## Next Checks
1. Systematically measure the exact amounts of labelled and unlabelled data at which each ST method fails while TAPT remains effective, across all five tasks
2. Conduct ablation studies removing domain-specific features from unlabelled data to verify whether TAPT's domain adaptation benefits stem from learning domain-specific patterns versus general language understanding
3. Compare wall-clock training time and computational resources required for TAPT versus ST methods across different data regimes to assess practical tradeoffs beyond accuracy
