# Analyzing Impact of Socio-Economic Factors on COVID-19 Mortality Prediction Using SHAP Value

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.00517
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00517
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: SHAP value analysis revealed that household income, employment status, education level, and age significantly impact COVID-19 mortality prediction models

## Executive Summary
This study applied multiple machine learning algorithms to a dataset of 20,878 COVID-19 patients to understand the association of socio-economic characteristics with mortality. Using SHAP value analysis, the authors identified that household income (annual and disposable), employment status, education level, and age significantly impacted model predictions. The results highlight socio-economic factors as important indicators for assessing COVID-19 mortality risk and provide insights for clinical decision support and public health policy.

## Method Summary
The study used Random Forest and XGBoost models trained on a COVID-19 patient dataset with socio-economic features. Data preprocessing involved OrdinalEncoder for categorical features. SHAP values were computed to analyze both global feature importance and local prediction contributions. The analysis included SHAP summary plots, feature importance rankings, and partial dependence plots to understand the relationship between socio-economic factors and mortality predictions.

## Key Results
- Household income, employment status, education level, and age were identified as significant predictors of COVID-19 mortality
- Local and global SHAP interpretation methods showed low education and low employment were strongly associated with higher mortality risk
- Higher income and better employment status correlated with lower mortality risk across both Random Forest and XGBoost models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SHAP values can reliably rank socio-economic feature importance in XGBoost and Random Forest models for COVID-19 mortality prediction.
- Mechanism: SHAP values aggregate marginal contributions of features across all possible feature subsets, producing a consistent feature importance ranking that reflects the model's global behavior.
- Core assumption: The model's predictions are based on a stable relationship between socio-economic features and mortality risk.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using SHAP value analysis, the authors identified that household income (annual and disposable), employment status, education level, and age significantly impacted model predictions."
  - [section] "Shapley score is a concept recently taken from cooperative game theory and applied to machine learning."
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers discuss SHAP but do not directly validate its use for socio-economic mortality prediction.
- Break condition: If the underlying data contains significant bias or unmeasured confounding variables, SHAP rankings may misrepresent true causal relationships.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Local SHAP explanations can reveal feature contributions for individual patients that differ from global rankings.
- Mechanism: By computing SHAP values for each data point, the model can highlight how specific feature values drive a particular prediction, capturing heterogeneity in feature effects.
- Core assumption: Individual predictions are influenced by feature interactions and non-linear relationships not captured by global importance alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Local and global interpretation methods revealed that low education and low employment were strongly associated with higher mortality risk."
  - [section] "We also observe several individual patient data, which gives us insight into how the feature values impact the prediction for that data point."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct evidence in related papers about local vs global SHAP interpretation for socio-economic factors.
- Break condition: If the model's decision boundary is highly irregular or if the sample size is too small, local explanations may be unstable or misleading.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Partial dependence plots (PDPs) can reveal the shape and interaction effects of socio-economic features on mortality predictions.
- Mechanism: PDPs average the SHAP values across the dataset while varying one feature, showing whether the relationship is linear, monotonic, or more complex.
- Core assumption: The feature interactions and marginal effects are consistent enough across the dataset to produce meaningful averages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Finally, we analyze multiple partial dependence plots of features with a higher effect on prediction to understand the trend of its impact on a model's prediction and the feature's interaction with other features."
  - [section] "In SHAP partial dependence plot, individual feature values are plotted on the X-axis, and corresponding Shapley values are plotted on the Y-axis."
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers mention SHAP and model interpretability but do not discuss partial dependence plots for socio-economic variables.
- Break condition: If strong feature interactions exist that are not captured by averaging, PDPs may mask important local effects.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> Feature encoding (OrdinalEncoder) -> Train/test split -> Model training (XGBoost & Random Forest) -> SHAP value computation -> Global and local interpretation -> Partial dependence plots
- Critical path:
  1. Load and clean socio-economic dataset
  2. Encode categorical features
  3. Train both XGBoost and Random Forest models
  4. Compute SHAP values for both models
  5. Generate feature importance and summary plots
  6. Create partial dependence plots for key features
  7. Analyze individual patient SHAP plots for local insights
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a balanced dataset (44% mortality) improves model stability but may not reflect real-world prevalence.
  - Ordinal encoding of socio-economic features may impose artificial ordering; one-hot encoding could be considered.
  - SHAP computation can be computationally expensive for large datasets; sampling may be necessary.
- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in SHAP values across similar instances may indicate model instability.
  - Inconsistent feature rankings between XGBoost and Random Forest may suggest data or model issues.
  - Partial dependence plots that are flat or highly irregular may indicate poor model fit or strong interactions.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train both models and compare baseline performance metrics (AUC, accuracy).
  2. Compute and compare global SHAP feature importance rankings between models.
  3. Generate SHAP summary plots and identify any discrepancies between local and global interpretations.

## Limitations
- Dataset represents only 2020 COVID-19 patients, potentially missing temporal variations in socio-economic impacts
- Balanced dataset (44% mortality) may not reflect real-world prevalence
- Use of OrdinalEncoder for socio-economic variables may impose artificial relationships

## Confidence
- High confidence: Technical implementation of SHAP-based feature interpretation follows established practices in explainable AI
- Medium confidence: Generalizability limited by temporal scope and balanced dataset representation
- Low confidence: Causal interpretations between socio-economic factors and mortality risk
