# Mapping Brains with Language Models: A Survey

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2306.05126
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05126
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Analysis of 30 studies mapping brain activity to language models found pairwise matching accuracy may only capture shallow processing characteristics.

## Executive Summary
This survey analyzes 30 studies that map brain activity to language models using 8 different evaluation metrics. The analysis reveals that pairwise matching accuracy, the most commonly used metric, is less conservative and may only capture shallow processing characteristics rather than deep structural similarities. While some studies show positive correlations between model quality and brain alignment, the overall evidence for meaningful brain-language model alignment remains ambiguous. The authors recommend using more conservative metrics like precision@k and analogy scores for future research to better assess true brain-language model alignment.

## Method Summary
The survey examines 30 studies using neuroimaging datasets (fMRI/MEG) paired with language model representations. Linear regression models are trained to predict brain responses from language model embeddings, and 8 different evaluation metrics are compared across studies. The analysis focuses on identifying which metrics are more conservative and better capture structural similarities versus shallow processing characteristics.

## Key Results
- Pairwise matching accuracy is less conservative and may only capture shallow processing characteristics
- Larger and better language models generally align better with neural response measurements
- The authors recommend using more conservative metrics like precision@k for future research

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Pairwise matching accuracy is less conservative and captures only shallow processing characteristics in brain-language model alignment.
- **Mechanism:** The metric measures binary classification accuracy on balanced datasets with random baseline of 0.5. It can achieve high scores (0.7-0.8) even when brain signals only capture binary distinctions like word length, not deeper semantic processing.
- **Core assumption:** If neural responses track only simple features (e.g., word length), pairwise matching accuracy will still be relatively high (~0.75) because the model performs perfectly on half the data and randomly on the other half.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "pairwise matching accuracy, the most commonly used metric, is less conservative and may only capture shallow processing characteristics"
  - [section] "Pairwise Matching Accuracy is a relatively permissible performance metric... Our model will do perfectly (1.0) on half of the data, and exhibit random performance (0.5) on the other half"
  - [corpus] Found 25 related papers (using 8). Average neighbor FMR=0.536. Weak corpus evidence for deeper processing claims.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Larger and better language models align better with neural response measurements.
- **Mechanism:** As language models improve in quality and size, their representations become more predictive of neural activity patterns, suggesting convergence between computational and biological language processing.
- **Core assumption:** Model quality (measured by performance on language tasks) and size (number of parameters) positively correlate with alignment to brain activity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "correlations with model size and quality provide grounds for cautious optimism"
  - [section] "several studies show that larger (and better) language models align better with neural response measurements... Such correlations with model quality and size are positive, making the results reported above more credible"
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence - no direct citations supporting this mechanism found in neighbors.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Precision@k is more conservative than pairwise matching accuracy and better captures structural similarities.
- **Mechanism:** Precision@k requires exact retrieval of correct items from a candidate set, making it less permissive than pairwise matching. For k=1, random baseline is 1/n (where n is candidate set size), much lower than 0.5 for pairwise matching.
- **Core assumption:** If a model has perfect precision@1, it implies perfect isomorphism between brain and language model representations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The authors suggest using more conservative metrics like precision@k... for future research to better assess true brain-language model alignment"
  - [section] "We have argued that precision@k is more conservative than most other metrics... perfect precision@1 and perfect RSA scores"
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence - only one neighbor paper mentions precision metrics, not in brain-language context.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Data layer (fMRI/MEG datasets) -> Model layer (language models) -> Mapping layer (linear regression models) -> Evaluation layer (8 metrics)

- **Critical path:** Data collection → Feature extraction from language models → Linear mapping to/from brain representations → Evaluation using appropriate metrics → Comparison across studies

- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Using linear vs. non-linear mapping models (linear preferred for interpretability and biological plausibility)
  - Choosing conservative vs. permissive evaluation metrics (precision@k preferred over pairwise matching accuracy)
  - Focusing on fMRI vs. MEG vs. combining both (fMRI for spatial resolution, MEG for temporal resolution)

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High pairwise matching accuracy (>0.7) with low Pearson correlation suggests shallow processing characteristics
  - Poor performance across all metrics indicates fundamental misalignment between brain activity and language model representations
  - Inconsistent results across different datasets suggest methodological issues or dataset-specific effects

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate pairwise matching accuracy on a small fMRI dataset to establish baseline performance
  2. Implement precision@k evaluation on the same dataset to compare conservatism of metrics
  3. Train linear regression models to predict brain activity from language model representations and evaluate with multiple metrics to assess alignment quality

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Do more conservative metrics like precision@k and analogy scores reveal deeper structural similarities between language models and brain activity, beyond what can be explained by shallow processing characteristics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest using more conservative metrics like precision@k and analogy scores for future research to better assess true brain-language model alignment.
- Why unresolved: Current studies primarily use pairwise matching accuracy and Pearson correlation, which may only capture shallow processing characteristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Studies using precision@k and analogy scores showing alignment between language models and brain activity that correlates with model quality/size and cannot be explained by shallow processing.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific linguistic or cognitive phenomena drive the alignment between language models and brain activity?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors discuss the possibility that alignment may be driven by syntactic or semantic factors, or deeper similarities between model objectives and predictive processing in human brains.
- Why unresolved: Past studies have relied on linear mapping models which are more interpretable but may not capture all relevant factors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Studies using more sophisticated models that can decompose representations into linguistically meaningful units, combined with interpretability methods to identify the specific factors driving alignment.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different types of language models (e.g. static vs. contextual, recurrent vs. transformer) and their training objectives impact alignment with brain activity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that contextual models and larger models tend to align better, and that fine-tuning on specific tasks can improve alignment.
- Why unresolved: There is a wide variety of models and training objectives used in past studies, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Large-scale studies systematically comparing different model architectures and training objectives on the same brain activity datasets, using consistent evaluation metrics.

## Limitations
- Heterogeneity of neuroimaging methodologies across the 30 surveyed studies may confound metric comparisons
- Linear mapping models may oversimplify complex nonlinear neural representations
- Evidence for deep structural similarities between brains and language models remains ambiguous

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The characterization of pairwise matching accuracy as less conservative and potentially capturing only shallow processing characteristics
- **Medium Confidence**: The suggestion that precision@k and analogy scores should be used for future research
- **Low Confidence**: The overall evidence for deep structural similarities between brains and language models
