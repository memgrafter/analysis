# Performance Bounds for Policy-Based Average Reward Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2302.01450
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01450
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** First non-trivial performance bounds for policy-based RL algorithms in average-reward setting

## Executive Summary
This paper provides the first non-trivial performance bounds for policy-based reinforcement learning algorithms in the average-reward setting. The authors derive finite-time error bounds for approximate policy iteration under the assumption that every policy induces an irreducible Markov chain. The key innovation is converting the error bound from scaling with the square of the horizon (as in discounted-reward analysis) to scaling linearly with horizon, making it meaningful for average-reward problems. The results are applicable to policy-based RL algorithms by plugging in specific policy evaluation and improvement error bounds.

## Method Summary
The method involves transforming the MDP using the Schweitzer transformation to ensure every policy induces an irreducible Markov chain, then applying approximate policy iteration with policy evaluation and improvement steps. The analysis uses span contraction properties and ergodic matrix products to bound the error between optimal and approximate value functions, showing that asymptotic error goes to zero as policy evaluation and improvement errors decrease.

## Key Results
- Derives finite-time error bounds for approximate policy iteration in average-reward setting
- Converts error scaling from O(1/horizon²) to O(1/horizon), making bounds meaningful for infinite horizon
- Shows asymptotic error goes to zero as policy evaluation (δ) and improvement (ε) errors decrease
- Provides framework applicable to policy-based RL algorithms by plugging in specific error bounds

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The Schweitzer transformation enables bounded error analysis by ensuring every policy induces an irreducible Markov chain.
- **Mechanism:** The transformation adds self-loop probability κ to every state under any policy, making the Markov chain aperiodic and irreducible. This allows the product of transition matrices to contract, which is essential for bounding the error between optimal and approximate value functions.
- **Core assumption:** The transformed MDP maintains the same optimal average reward as the original (up to scaling by (1-κ)).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "every policy induces an irreducible Markov chain"
  - [section] "The aperiodicity transformation described is sufficient to ensure that such a contraction exists"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct citations, but this is a classical result from Schweitzer 1971
- **Break condition:** If κ is too small, mixing times increase and the convergence constant R grows, degrading practical performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The span contraction property, while insufficient alone, combines with ergodic matrix products to bound the error.
- **Mechanism:** For irreducible chains, products of transition matrices converge to positive matrices. This allows bounding the difference between optimal and approximate value functions using the span contraction factor (1-γ).
- **Core assumption:** The Markov chain induced by any policy is irreducible and aperiodic after transformation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "Lemma 3.4. There exists a finite natural number R such that for any sequence of policies..."
  - [section] "However, there is a very specific form of contraction that exists for such problems"
  - [corpus] Weak - the span contraction property is well-established in MDP theory
- **Break condition:** If the MDP structure prevents irreducibility even after transformation, the contraction constant γ approaches zero and bounds become vacuous.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Converting from horizon-squared to linear scaling makes the bounds meaningful for average-reward problems.
- **Mechanism:** The key insight is that in average-reward settings, the error bound scales as O(1/horizon) rather than O(1/horizon²) as in discounted-reward analysis. This makes the bound finite and meaningful as horizon → ∞.
- **Core assumption:** The relationship between discounted and average reward formulations in the limit α→1.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "converting the error bound from scaling with the square of the horizon (as in discounted-reward analysis) to scaling linearly with horizon"
  - [section] "Thus, in the limit as the horizon increases to infinity, we have essentially reduced the dependence of the error from the square of the length of the horizon to linear in the length of the horizon"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct citations, but this follows from the mathematical derivation
- **Break condition:** If the MDP structure violates the irreducibility assumption, the linear scaling may not hold.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  - MDP transformation module (Schweitzer transformation) -> Policy evaluation component (e.g., TD learning) -> Policy improvement component (e.g., greedy or softmax updates) -> Error tracking and bound computation -> Iteration control logic

- **Critical path:**
  1. Transform MDP to ensure irreducibility
  2. Initialize value function
  3. Policy evaluation (approximate)
  4. Policy improvement
  5. Error bound computation
  6. Check convergence criteria

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Larger κ in transformation → better contraction but slower mixing
  - More aggressive policy improvement → larger ǫ but fewer iterations
  - Function approximation accuracy → affects δ bound quality
  - Sample efficiency vs. computational cost in TD learning

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Error bounds not converging → check irreducibility assumption
  - Policy improvement making no progress → verify ǫ bound tightness
  - Value function estimates diverging → check δ bound validity
  - Mixing times too long → reconsider κ value

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Implement the Schweitzer transformation and verify irreducibility on a simple MDP
  2. Test policy evaluation with TD learning and measure δ bounds empirically
  3. Run complete approximate policy iteration and compare theoretical vs. empirical error bounds

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the precise conditions under which the aperiodicity transformation (Schweitzer transformation) maintains near-optimal performance while ensuring irreducibility?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions "for most MDPs of interest, this small modification will satisfy our assumption with a small O(ε) loss in performance" but does not quantify this bound or specify which MDP classes are affected.
- Why unresolved: The authors state that the transformation works for "most MDPs of interest" but don't provide rigorous conditions or examples of MDPs where the performance loss might be significant.
- What evidence would resolve it: Formal proofs of performance bounds for specific MDP classes (e.g., certain gridworlds, robotic control problems) showing the exact trade-off between ε and reward loss, or counterexamples demonstrating MDPs where the transformation causes substantial performance degradation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the finite-time bounds scale with the mixing time of the underlying Markov chain, and what are the implications for algorithms with non-uniform state visitation?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The proof relies on a constant γ derived from the minimum transition probability, but the paper doesn't explore how performance bounds depend on the full spectrum of mixing times across different policies.
- Why unresolved: The analysis assumes a uniform lower bound γ on all transition probabilities, but real-world MDPs often have states with very different mixing characteristics, which could affect convergence rates.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing convergence rates across MDPs with varying mixing time distributions, or theoretical extensions of the bounds that explicitly incorporate state-dependent mixing times.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the performance bounds extend to continuous state and action spaces, and what function approximation methods preserve the contraction properties?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions TD learning with linear function approximation in Section 4.2, but doesn't provide bounds for general function approximation architectures or continuous spaces.
- Why unresolved: The current analysis is limited to finite MDPs, and extending to continuous spaces would require different mathematical tools to maintain the key contraction properties.
- What evidence would resolve it: Convergence proofs for specific function approximation methods (e.g., neural networks, kernel methods) in average-reward settings, or counterexamples showing where the contraction properties fail in continuous spaces.

## Limitations
- Relies on Schweitzer transformation which may cause performance loss in some MDPs
- Analysis assumes finite MDPs with discrete state and action spaces
- Bounds depend on problem-specific parameters (κ, γ, R) that are not precisely quantified
- Requires irreducibility assumption that may not hold for all MDP structures

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The mathematical framework for converting horizon-squared to linear scaling is sound and well-established in MDP theory.
- **Medium Confidence**: The span contraction analysis is valid but relies on abstract existence proofs rather than concrete bounds for specific algorithms.
- **Low Confidence**: The practical applicability of these bounds depends heavily on problem-specific parameters (κ, γ, R) that are not empirically validated.
