# BayBFed: Bayesian Backdoor Defense for Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09508
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09508
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves 100% TPR and TNR across five datasets while maintaining benign model accuracy, outperforming seven state-of-the-art defenses.

## Executive Summary
BayBFed presents a novel defense against backdoor attacks in federated learning by leveraging probabilistic modeling and clustering. The method computes a probabilistic measure over client updates using Hierarchical Beta-Bernoulli processes and employs a clustering algorithm based on Chinese Restaurant Process and Jensen-Divergence to detect and filter malicious updates. Evaluated across five diverse datasets against multiple attack strategies, BayBFed achieves perfect detection rates while maintaining benign model accuracy, outperforming existing defenses.

## Method Summary
BayBFed introduces a Bayesian non-parametric framework that computes probabilistic measures over client updates using Hierarchical Beta-Bernoulli processes. It then applies a detection algorithm (CRP-Jensen) that uses Jensen-Divergence to compare client update distributions against cluster distributions. The method integrates cosine angular distance and L2-norm metrics to enhance detection robustness. The defense filters detected malicious updates before aggregation, maintaining the global model's performance on benign tasks.

## Key Results
- Achieved 100% True Positive Rate (TPR) and 100% True Negative Rate (TNR) across all five datasets
- Maintained benign model accuracy while defending against multiple backdoor attack strategies
- Outperformed seven state-of-the-art defenses including Krum, FoolsGold, Auror, and FLAME

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: BayBFed can detect malicious updates by leveraging probabilistic measures that encapsulate all adjustments made during local training, making it robust against diverse attack strategies.
- Mechanism: BayBFed computes a probabilistic measure over client updates using Hierarchical Beta-Bernoulli processes. This measure is then used in a detection algorithm based on Chinese Restaurant Process and Jensen-Divergence to efficiently identify and filter malicious updates.
- Core assumption: The probabilistic measure accurately captures the variations in client updates, and the Jensen-Divergence between the client's update distribution and existing clusters' distributions is significantly different for malicious and benign updates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "BayBFed computes a probabilistic measure over the clients' updates to keep track of any adjustments made in the updates, and uses a novel detection algorithm that can leverage this probabilistic measure to efficiently detect and filter out malicious updates."
  - [section]: "The main idea is to utilize a probabilistic measure to determine the distribution of the incoming local client updates. Additionally, in each FL round, we compute the distribution of existing groups that were assigned client updates (clusters) or a new group (client updates can get assigned to a new group). Then, we compute the (Jensen) divergence of these two distributions to detect malicious updates and compute the selected client's fit to an existing or a new cluster."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: BayBFed is robust against adaptive attacks by integrating the effect of cosine angular distance and L2-norm into the client's model updates and error computation.
- Mechanism: BayBFed updates each client's local model using the cosine angular distance between the local model and the global model. The L2-norm is used to compute the measurement error due to the new client's weight getting assigned to a specific cluster. These metrics are integrated into the detection module to effectively eliminate all malicious updates.
- Core assumption: Even though an adversary can manipulate the cosine angular distance and L2-norm, there is still a correlation between the two that differs for malicious and benign weights.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "First, we update each client's local model using the cosine angular distance (cos(Wt_i, Gt-1)) between the local model and the global model. The intuition for doing this is to integrate the effect of cos(Wt_i, Gt-1) into the weights. The reason is that even though an adversary can manipulate the cosine angular distance, the poisoned weights have to differ (slightly) from the benign weights."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: BayBFed is effective against backdoor attacks even when multiple backdoors are injected simultaneously by different malicious clients.
- Mechanism: BayBFed uses a clustering algorithm (CRP-Jensen) that can handle an infinite number of clusters, allowing it to effectively group and identify updates from different malicious clients, even if they are injecting different backdoors.
- Core assumption: The clustering algorithm can effectively differentiate between updates from different malicious clients, even if they are injecting different backdoors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "BayBFed utilizes two Bayesian Non-Parametric extensions: (i) a Hierarchical Beta-Bernoulli process to draw a probabilistic measure given the clients' updates, and (ii) an adaptation of the Chinese Restaurant Process (CRP), referred by us as CRP-Jensen, which leverages this probabilistic measure to detect and filter out malicious updates."

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Posterior Computation Module -> Detection Module (CRP-Jensen) -> Filtering and Aggregation

- Critical path:
  - Local client trains model on private data and sends update to global server
  - Global server computes probabilistic measure over client updates using Hierarchical Beta-Bernoulli processes
  - Detection module (CRP-Jensen) uses the probabilistic measure to detect and filter malicious updates
  - Remaining benign updates are aggregated to update the global model

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computational complexity vs. detection accuracy: Using more complex probabilistic models and clustering algorithms may improve detection accuracy but increase computational complexity
  - Robustness vs. adaptability: The defense mechanism needs to be robust against various attack strategies but also adaptable to different data distributions and client behaviors

- Failure signatures:
  - High false positive rate: The detection mechanism is incorrectly identifying benign updates as malicious
  - High false negative rate: The detection mechanism is failing to identify malicious updates
  - Degradation in global model performance: The defense mechanism is negatively impacting the performance of the global model on benign tasks

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test the detection mechanism on a simple dataset (e.g., MNIST) with a single malicious client injecting a backdoor. Measure the true positive rate and true negative rate
  2. Test the detection mechanism on a more complex dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10) with multiple malicious clients injecting different backdoors. Measure the true positive rate, true negative rate, and the impact on global model performance
  3. Test the detection mechanism against an adaptive attacker who is aware of the defense mechanism and tries to evade detection. Measure the true positive rate, true negative rate, and the impact on global model performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does BayBFed's performance scale with extremely large numbers of clients (e.g., 10,000+)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper tests with up to 60 clients for IoT datasets and 30 clients for image datasets, but doesn't explore extreme scaling scenarios
- Why unresolved: The computational complexity of Bayesian Non-Parametric modeling and clustering algorithms may become prohibitive at very large scales, potentially requiring approximations or distributed implementations
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments showing runtime, memory usage, and detection accuracy as client count scales to 10,000+ would clarify scalability limits and potential bottlenecks

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the theoretical guarantee on the convergence rate of BayBFed's Hierarchical Beta-Bernoulli Process posterior updates?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper describes the posterior computation mechanism but doesn't provide theoretical analysis of convergence rates or sample complexity
- Why unresolved: Without convergence guarantees, it's unclear how many training rounds are needed for the probabilistic measure to stabilize and provide reliable detection
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis proving convergence bounds (e.g., in terms of KL divergence between posterior and true distribution) would establish the efficiency and reliability of the approach

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is BayBFed to the choice of initial hyperparameters (c, H) for the Beta Process?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions choosing initial values (c=5, H=N(µp,σp)) but doesn't systematically study sensitivity to these choices
- Why unresolved: Poor initial choices could lead to slow convergence or suboptimal clustering, especially in the early rounds of training
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying initial concentration parameters and base measures across multiple orders of magnitude would reveal the robustness of the method to initialization

## Limitations

- The method relies heavily on Bayesian non-parametric assumptions that may not hold in all federated learning scenarios
- Computational overhead of computing Jensen-Divergence for each update against all clusters could become prohibitive with large numbers of clients
- The impressive 100% detection rates raise questions about potential overfitting to specific attack patterns

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the theoretical framework and mathematical foundations of the Bayesian approach
- **Medium confidence** in the empirical claims, particularly the 100% detection rates
- **Low confidence** in the scalability claims due to lack of computational complexity analysis
