# Quantile and moment neural networks for learning functionals of distributions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.11060
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11060
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: New neural network architectures for distribution functionals outperform existing methods on both univariate and bivariate cases

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces novel neural network architectures for approximating functions of probability distributions in the Wasserstein space. The key innovation is the use of quantiles and moments as feature representations, supported by universal approximation theorems. The networks are evaluated on both univariate and bivariate distributions, demonstrating superior performance compared to existing bin and cylinder networks, particularly in the bivariate case where moment networks excel.

## Method Summary
The methodology involves sampling probability distributions on compact sets using a bin representation with exponential law and uniform perturbations. Features are extracted as quantiles, moments, or combinations thereof, which are then fed into neural networks with two hidden layers (20 neurons each, ReLU activation). The networks are trained using stochastic gradient descent (Adam optimizer, learning rate 5×10⁻³) to minimize mean squared error between predicted and true functional values over M distributions per batch.

## Key Results
- Moment networks outperform other approaches in bivariate cases
- Combining quantile and moment features yields best results for univariate distributions
- Quantile of moments network achieves superior performance across all univariate test cases
- The methodology enables solving PDEs in Wasserstein space for multivariate cases

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Quantile networks provide effective feature representations for univariate distributions by capturing distributional shape information.
- Mechanism: Quantile networks use ordered quantiles as input features, which encode the cumulative distribution function's shape. These quantiles serve as natural breakpoints that capture the distribution's structure, allowing the neural network to learn complex functional relationships with fewer parameters than moment-based approaches.
- Core assumption: The distribution's shape information is sufficiently captured by a finite set of quantiles, and these quantiles are informative for the target functional.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The first one, limited to one-dimensional distributions, uses the quantile of the distribution as key features"
  - [section 3.1] "For a distribution µ, we note Fµ its cumulative distribution function and we note Qµ the quantile function"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations to quantile network methods
- Break condition: When the target functional depends primarily on moments rather than distribution shape, or when higher-dimensional distributions require more complex feature representations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Moment networks provide universal approximation capabilities for distribution functionals.
- Mechanism: Moment networks use statistical moments as features, which through the Hausdorff moment problem are determinate for compact support distributions. This determinacy ensures that moments uniquely characterize the distribution, allowing the network to approximate any continuous functional of the distribution.
- Core assumption: The distribution has compact support, ensuring the moment problem is determinate and moments uniquely characterize the distribution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The second ones uses the moments of the distribution as key features and leads to the moment network scheme"
  - [section 3.2] "In our case, where the support is compact, this problem is known as the Haussdorf moment problem and it is determinate"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations to moment network approximation theory
- Break condition: When distributions have unbounded support or when the target functional is highly sensitive to tail behavior not captured by low-order moments.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Combining quantile and moment features leverages complementary information for improved approximation.
- Mechanism: By concatenating quantile and moment features, the network captures both distributional shape (through quantiles) and statistical properties (through moments). This hybrid approach addresses cases where neither quantile nor moment features alone provide optimal performance, particularly for functionals that depend on both types of information.
- Core assumption: The target functional benefits from information captured by both quantile and moment representations, and the additional feature dimension doesn't lead to overfitting or computational inefficiency.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "By mixing the quantile and moment features in other new networks, we develop schemes that outperform existing networks"
  - [section 4.1] "Since the best network depends on the case, we can develop new networks based on moments and quantiles"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations to hybrid quantile-moment network approaches
- Break condition: When the target functional is primarily dependent on either quantiles or moments alone, making the additional features redundant.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Distribution sampling -> Feature extraction (quantiles/moments/both) -> Neural network (2 hidden layers, 20 neurons) -> Output layer -> Loss computation -> Backpropagation -> Parameter update
- Critical path: Distribution sampling → Feature extraction → Neural network forward pass → Loss computation → Backpropagation → Parameter update
- Design tradeoffs: Quantile networks excel for shape-dependent functionals but struggle with moment-dependent ones; moment networks provide universal approximation but may require more features; hybrid approaches balance these tradeoffs at the cost of increased complexity.
- Failure signatures: High MSE on test distributions despite good training performance suggests overfitting or insufficient feature representation; poor convergence indicates inappropriate learning rate or network architecture; bias in specific functional types suggests feature extraction limitations.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a basic quantile network for a simple quantile-dependent functional (e.g., V(µ) = Qµ(0.7)) and verify convergence.
  2. Implement a moment network for a moment-dependent functional (e.g., V(µ) = EX[X] + EX[X²]) and compare with the quantile network.
  3. Create a hybrid network combining both features and test on a mixed functional (e.g., V(µ) = Qµ(0.9) + EX[X²]) to observe performance improvements.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the theoretical justification for using a fixed number of quantiles (K=200) across all univariate test cases, and how does this choice affect approximation quality for distributions with different tail behaviors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "A number of quantiles equal to 200 seems to be sufficient to obtain a good accuracy" but provides no theoretical justification for this specific choice.
- Why unresolved: The paper only empirically demonstrates this works well for their test cases without proving why this specific number is optimal or how it relates to the properties of the target functions.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis showing how the choice of K relates to the smoothness and tail behavior of the target function V, or an empirical study varying K across a wider range of test functions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why does the moment network consistently outperform other networks in bivariate cases while requiring fewer features (K=7) compared to the univariate quantile of moments network (KM=5, KQ=200)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "In all cases, the moment network gives the best results" for bivariate distributions but doesn't explain this phenomenon.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide theoretical explanation for why moments are more effective in higher dimensions or why fewer features are needed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of the relationship between dimensionality and feature effectiveness, or theoretical results showing why moments capture more information in higher dimensions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the proposed networks scale to distributions with dimension higher than two, and what modifications would be needed to maintain performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only tests univariate and bivariate cases, with the authors stating "Using this moment scheme, the resolution of the PDE in Wasserstein space becomes possible in the multivariate case" but not demonstrating this.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explore cases beyond bivariate distributions, leaving open questions about scalability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results on distributions with d>2 dimensions, or theoretical analysis of how the network architectures would need to be modified for higher dimensions.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the relationship between the convergence rate of the stochastic gradient descent method and the choice of features (quantiles vs moments vs mixed features) for different classes of distribution functions V?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper compares MSE convergence for different networks but doesn't analyze how the convergence rate depends on the properties of V or the choice of features.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides empirical convergence curves but no theoretical analysis of convergence rates or their dependence on network architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical bounds on convergence rates as a function of network architecture and target function properties, or extensive empirical studies across a wider class of functions.
