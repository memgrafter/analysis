# Decomposing the Enigma: Subgoal-based Demonstration Learning for Formal Theorem Proving

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16366
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16366
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: 45.5% proof pass rate on miniF2F, 5× sampling efficiency improvement

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of formal theorem proving by introducing a subgoal-based demonstration learning framework that leverages large language models (LLMs). The approach decomposes complex proofs into smaller, manageable subgoals and uses diffusion models to optimally organize demonstration examples within LLM context limits. By combining insights from subgoal learning theory and recent advances in diffusion models, the framework achieves a 6.6% absolute improvement in proof success rate over state-of-the-art methods, demonstrating both effectiveness and improved sampling efficiency.

## Method Summary
The framework operates through two main components: subgoal-based proof generation and demonstration organization. First, informal proofs are transformed into formal proofs structured around subgoals that are reachable from both initial and final proof states. These subgoals are iteratively refined through self-play between ChatGPT and an automated theorem prover (Sledgehammer). Second, a diffusion model is trained to organize demonstration examples by simultaneously solving the subset selection and ordering problems, treating demonstration organization as a graph-based optimization task. During inference, the organized demonstrations guide ChatGPT to generate formal proof sketches that are then verified by the automated prover.

## Key Results
- Achieved 45.5% proof pass rate on miniF2F benchmark, a 6.6% absolute improvement over state-of-the-art
- Demonstrated 5× improvement in sampling efficiency compared to previous methods
- Ablation studies show both subgoal-based proof transformation and demonstration organization contribute to performance gains
- Proved 61 out of 488 problems in the miniF2F test set

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Subgoal-based proof decomposition improves LLM guidance quality in theorem proving
- Mechanism: Breaking complex proofs into smaller subgoals that are strictly aligned with automated prover states makes the problem more tractable for LLMs and improves learning efficiency
- Core assumption: Subgoals that are reachable from both initial and final states provide better guidance than informal proofs
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose the construction of distinct subgoals for each demonstration example and refine these subgoals in accordance with the pertinent theories of subgoal learning"
  - [section 2.1] "we seek to obtain a valid sequence of subgoals which satisfies the condition that each subgoal in this sequence should be reachable from both the initial state (i.e., the statement) and the final state (i.e., the passing state of the proof)"
- Break condition: If subgoals become too granular and lose contextual relevance, or if the refinement process introduces significant noise

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Diffusion models enable optimal demonstration example selection and ordering simultaneously
- Mechanism: Diffusion models progressively denoise latent variables representing demonstration organization to find the optimal subset and sequence within LLM context constraints
- Core assumption: The demonstration organization problem can be formulated as a diffusion process on a graph where nodes represent examples
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we build upon recent advances in diffusion models to predict the optimal organization, simultaneously addressing two intricate issues that persist within the domain of demonstration organization: subset selection and order determination"
  - [section 2.2] "we further formulate this problem into a diffusion process on the graph"
- Break condition: If the diffusion model overfits to training data or struggles with examples significantly different from training distribution

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Iterative refinement of subgoals through self-play improves consistency and LLM learnability
- Mechanism: Random selection of demonstration examples provides in-context learning for LLM to generate subgoals, which are then verified by automated prover and refined iteratively
- Core assumption: Iterative interaction between LLM and automated prover progressively improves subgoal quality
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "we iteratively refine the subgoal proof, in the spirit of self-play in reinforcement learning [39], making calls to both the LLM and the off-the-shelf automated theorem prover"
  - [algorithm 1] Formal description of iterative refinement process
- Break condition: If verification fails repeatedly or refinement cycles don't converge

  - Subgoal-based proof generator (with iterative refinement)
  - Demonstration organization module (diffusion model)
  - LLM integration layer (ChatGPT)
  - Automated theorem prover interface (Sledgehammer)
  - Training data collection pipeline

- Critical path:
  1. Generate subgoal-based proofs from informal proofs
  2. Collect successful demonstration organizations
  3. Train diffusion model on organization data
  4. During inference: organize demonstrations → generate formal sketch → verify with prover

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Trade accuracy vs. computational cost in diffusion model sampling
  - Balance subgoal granularity vs. LLM context length constraints
  - Choose between random organization vs. diffusion model organization

- Failure signatures:
  - Low pass rates suggest subgoal refinement issues or poor demonstration selection
  - High computational cost indicates inefficient diffusion model sampling
  - Inconsistent results across runs suggest randomness in organization process

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation study: Compare with and without subgoal-based proof transformation
  2. Sampling efficiency test: Measure pass rate vs. number of LLM calls
  3. Organization quality test: Compare random vs. diffusion model organization methods

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the impact of subgoal refinement iterations on proof success rate?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions setting the number of refinement iterations to 15 but doesn't explore how this number affects performance.
- Why unresolved: The authors fixed this parameter without investigating its optimal value or sensitivity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the number of refinement iterations (e.g., 5, 10, 15, 20) to identify the optimal number and understand diminishing returns.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the subgoal-based proof framework perform on undergraduate-level mathematics problems?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention this as a limitation, noting they only evaluated on the miniF2F dataset which focuses on high-school level problems.
- Why unresolved: The framework was only tested on simpler problems, and its scalability to more complex mathematical domains remains unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the framework on undergraduate-level theorem proving benchmarks to measure performance degradation and identify new challenges.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the computational overhead of the diffusion model-based demonstration reorganization compared to simpler methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that diffusion models are "computationally demanding" but doesn't provide quantitative comparisons.
- Why unresolved: While the paper mentions this limitation, it doesn't measure or compare the actual computational costs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarking the time and resource usage of the diffusion model approach against simpler in-context learning methods (e.g., random selection, top-k) across different problem sets.

## Limitations
- Limited to high-school level mathematics problems on the miniF2F benchmark, not tested on more complex undergraduate-level problems
- Significant computational overhead from iterative refinement process and diffusion model sampling, with unclear practical deployment costs
- Implementation details for subgoal refinement process and diffusion model architecture are not fully specified, impacting reproducibility

## Confidence

- **Medium confidence**: The 5× sampling efficiency improvement (based on comparison with a single baseline, limited ablation studies)
