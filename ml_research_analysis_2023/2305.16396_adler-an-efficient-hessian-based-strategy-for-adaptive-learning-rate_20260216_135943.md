# ADLER -- An efficient Hessian-based strategy for adaptive learning rate

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16396
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16396
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: ADLER achieves 0.399±0.026 accuracy on CIFAR100, outperforming grid search SGD (0.304±0.056)

## Executive Summary
ADLER introduces a hyperparameter-free adaptive learning rate strategy for SGD optimization that computes per-minibatch optimal learning rates using a novel positive semi-definite Hessian approximation. The method leverages second-order curvature information without requiring full Hessian computation by using Jacobian-vector products and per-example Hessian calculations. Experiments show ADLER performs comparably to grid search over SGD learning rates while requiring only twice the computation of a single SGD run. On CIFAR100, ADLER outperforms both grid search and Gauss-Newton approximation, though it shows overfitting effects due to lack of regularization.

## Method Summary
ADLER computes adaptive learning rates using a positive semi-definite approximation of the Hessian that combines the Jacobian of the network output with the Hessian of the loss function. The method calculates Hessian-vector products and per-example Hessian values on minibatches, then uses an exponentially weighted average of log learning rates to reduce variance in estimates. The PSD property ensures descent directions and enables conjugate gradient methods. The algorithm requires no hyperparameter tuning beyond standard deep learning parameters, though it does depend on the exponential averaging parameter β.

## Key Results
- ADLER achieves 0.399±0.026 accuracy on CIFAR100, significantly outperforming grid search SGD (0.304±0.056)
- The method requires only twice the computation of a single SGD run while matching grid search performance
- ADLER's Hessian approximation provides more accurate local geometry information than Gauss-Newton, better capturing the optimization landscape
- The method is particularly effective at optimization but shows overfitting effects due to lack of regularization

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The Hessian approximation QS(θ) = ∇FS(θ)^T ∇^2LS(FS(θ))∇FS(θ) provides more accurate local geometry information than Gauss-Newton.
- Mechanism: The proposed approximation captures second-order curvature information by combining the Jacobian of the network output with the Hessian of the loss function, while Gauss-Newton uses only the outer product of gradients. - Core assumption: The term ∇L(F(θ)) · ∇^2F(θ) is negligible compared to the first term in typical deep learning scenarios.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The exponentially weighted average of learning rates reduces variance in minibatch estimates.
- Mechanism: By averaging log learning rates across minibatches with discount factor β, the method smooths out high-frequency noise while maintaining responsiveness to local geometry changes.
- Core assumption: Gradient steps become smaller as training progresses, making local curvature more stable across minibatches.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "A difficulty in a direct application of the above formula is the high variance of the obtained estimates for the learning rate, especially problematic in later optimization phases; we thus consider an exponentially weighted average between different mini-batches approximations of the learning rate."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The PSD property of the Hessian approximation ensures descent directions and enables conjugate gradient methods.
- Mechanism: Since ∇^2L(F(θ)) is PSD due to loss convexity, the entire approximation QS(θ) remains PSD, guaranteeing that the computed learning rate produces a descent direction.
- Core assumption: The loss function L is convex in its parameters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Moreover, the approximation is PSD 7 as ∇^2L(F(θ)) is PSD because of the convexity of L."
  - [section]: "It is important that the approximation is PSD as this ensures that the taken direction is a descent direction, and enables the usage of Conjugate Gradient Descent [5] on the objective."

- Break condition: The approximation loses PSD property if the loss function is non-convex or if the Jacobian computation is unstable.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Forward pass → Jacobian-vector product → Loss Hessian → Learning rate computation → Parameter update
- Critical path: Forward pass → Jacobian-vector product → Loss Hessian → Learning rate computation → Parameter update
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Memory vs accuracy: Computing full Jacobians vs using Jacobian-vector products
  - Variance vs responsiveness: Choice of β in exponential averaging
  - Computational cost: Computing Hessians of the loss vs using approximations
- Failure signatures:
  - Learning rates becoming NaN or extremely large: Hessian regularization parameter ε too small
  - Poor performance despite correct implementation: β too close to 1 or too small
  - Numerical instability: Jacobian computations failing due to vanishing/exploding gradients

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the proposed Hessian approximation perform on deeper networks (e.g., ResNet50/101) compared to shallower architectures?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses custom CNN and Vision Transformer architectures, but does not test standard deep networks like ResNet50/101 which are more commonly used in practice.
- Why unresolved: The authors note their networks are "less performant than typical ResNet18/50" and are trained without batch normalization and weight decay on non-augmented datasets. This suggests the method hasn't been validated on standard deep architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing ADLER's performance on standard deep architectures like ResNet50/101, ResNet-1000, or ViT-Large on common benchmarks (ImageNet, COCO) would clarify whether the method scales to deeper networks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the β parameter in the exponential moving average be automatically tuned during training rather than using a fixed value of 0.99?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "Ideally, we would like to have different βk → 1−" and suggest β=0.99 as a good fixed value, explicitly stating they "leave β-autotuning to future research."
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge that early in optimization earlier estimates aren't very informative due to large steps, while near the end values of β≃1 are optimal, but don't implement dynamic β adjustment.
- What evidence would resolve it: A variant of ADLER with an adaptive β that changes during training (e.g., starts lower and increases toward 1) would show whether automatic tuning improves performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would incorporating a trust-region bound help prevent the large learning rate steps that cause the local approximation to break down on easier problems like CIFAR10?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state "on easier problems the local geometry appears flatter and thus the algorithm takes greater steps; these steps may sometimes be so big that the local approximation no longer holds" and "look forward to integrating a trust-region bound [8] in future extensions of the method."
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies this as a limitation but does not implement or test trust-region bounds as a solution.
- What evidence would resolve it: An ADLER variant with trust-region constraints that demonstrates improved stability and performance on easy datasets like CIFAR10 would show whether this addresses the identified limitation.

## Limitations

- The method shows overfitting effects due to lack of regularization, suggesting it may require careful calibration of batch sizes or additional regularization mechanisms.
- Performance on datasets beyond CIFAR10 and CIFAR100, or with different architectures like RNNs or GANs, remains unknown.
- The claim of being "hyperparameter-free" is questionable given the implicit dependence on the exponential averaging parameter β and Hessian regularization parameter ε.

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The core mathematical derivation of the PSD Hessian approximation and its descent guarantees are sound and well-supported by the theoretical framework presented. The experimental results showing ADLER's superiority over grid search on CIFAR100 are reproducible and well-documented.

**Medium Confidence:** The claim that ADLER provides "more accurate local geometry information than the Gauss-Newton method" is supported by experiments but lacks comprehensive ablation studies comparing the two methods across different network depths and loss landscapes.

**Low Confidence:** The assertion that ADLER is truly "hyperparameter-free" is questionable given the presence of β in the exponential averaging and the implicit dependence on the Hessian regularization parameter ε, both of which affect performance significantly.
