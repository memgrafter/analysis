# Weighted Sampling for Masked Language Modeling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.14225
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14225
- Reference count: 0
- The standard random masking strategy in MLM causes the pre-trained language models (PLMs) to be biased towards high-frequency tokens.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the frequency bias problem in masked language modeling, where standard random masking disproportionately masks high-frequency tokens, leading to poor representation learning for rare tokens. The authors propose two weighted sampling strategies - Frequency Weighted Sampling and Dynamic Weighted Sampling - that adjust token masking probabilities based on either token frequency or training loss. When applied to BERT, these strategies (collectively called WSBERT) significantly improve sentence embeddings on semantic textual similarity tasks and enhance transfer learning capability on the GLUE benchmark.

## Method Summary
The authors propose two weighted sampling strategies to address frequency bias in masked language modeling. Frequency Weighted Sampling uses token frequency as a proxy for informativeness, masking rare tokens more often by setting sampling weights as the inverse of token frequency raised to a power α. Dynamic Weighted Sampling uses token prediction loss as the informativeness signal, computing sampling weights from the exponential of negative loss divided by a temperature τ. Both strategies continue pre-training BERT-base-uncased on WikiText for 10 epochs with learning rate 5e-5, using a masking ratio of 15%.

## Key Results
- WSBERT significantly improves sentence embeddings on the STS benchmark over BERT
- Combining WSBERT with calibration methods and prompt learning further improves sentence embeddings
- Weighted sampling also improves transfer learning capability of BERT on the GLUE benchmark
- WSBERT produces more isotropic token embeddings with reduced frequency bias

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Weighted sampling reduces frequency bias by increasing masking probability for rare tokens and decreasing it for common tokens.
- Mechanism: The sampling weight for each token is inversely proportional to its frequency (raised to power α), so rare tokens have higher sampling weights and are masked more often during pre-training.
- Core assumption: Token frequency is a reliable proxy for token informativeness, with rare tokens being more informative than common ones.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The standard random masking strategy in MLM causes the pre-trained language models (PLMs) to be biased towards high-frequency tokens. Representation learning of rare tokens is poor and PLMs have limited performance on downstream tasks."
  - [section 2.2.1] "A natural statistical signal characterizing the informativeness of a token w is its frequency freq(w) in the pre-training corpus."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only 2 related papers found, neither directly supports frequency-based sampling.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Dynamic weighted sampling adapts masking probabilities based on model's current learning state, improving balance between common and rare token representations.
- Mechanism: Sampling weights are computed from token prediction loss during training - tokens with high loss (poorly learned) get higher weights and are masked more frequently.
- Core assumption: Prediction loss is a good indicator of how well a token is learned, and adjusting sampling based on loss will improve overall representation quality.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We propose two simple and effective Weighted Sampling strategies for masking tokens based on the token frequency and training loss."
  - [section 2.2.2] "We hypothesize that the signal of informativeness of a token w may also be derived from how poorly a masked language model predicts it."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only 2 related papers found, neither directly supports loss-based sampling.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Weighted sampling creates more isotropic token embeddings by reducing concentration of common tokens and dispersion of rare tokens in embedding space.
- Mechanism: By masking rare tokens more frequently, WSBERT learns better representations for them, bringing them closer to the origin and making the overall embedding space less sparse.
- Core assumption: The concentration of common tokens and dispersion of rare tokens is problematic, and reducing this will improve downstream performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We further analyze and provide insights into how WSBERT improves token embeddings."
  - [section 3.3] "WSBERT has more concentrated token distributions and smaller frequency bias in token embeddings compared to BERT and BERT-CP"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only 2 related papers found, neither directly supports embedding space analysis.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Tokenizer -> Weighted Sampling Module -> BERT Backbone -> Prediction Head
- Critical path: During training, for each batch: compute sampling weights → calculate masking probabilities → apply masking → forward pass through BERT → compute prediction loss → update weight dictionary (for dynamic sampling)
- Design tradeoffs: Weighted sampling improves rare token representation at the cost of increased training time (20 hours vs 11 hours for 10 epochs). The choice between frequency-based and dynamic sampling involves a tradeoff between simplicity and adaptivity.
- Failure signatures: If weighted sampling fails, you might see: no improvement in STS scores compared to BERT, degradation in GLUE performance, or the model training becoming unstable (loss exploding or vanishing)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run WSBERT with frequency-weighted sampling on a small subset of WikiText and verify that rare tokens are masked more frequently than in standard BERT.
  2. Compare the nearest neighbor distributions of rare vs common tokens between BERT and WSBERT using the Euclidean distance metric described in section 3.3.
  3. Fine-tune both BERT and WSBERT on a single GLUE task (e.g., SST-2) with identical hyperparameters and compare performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but several important questions arise from the work:

1. How does the effectiveness of Weighted Sampling vary across different pre-training datasets (e.g., Wikipedia vs. BookCorpus vs. newer large-scale datasets)?
2. What is the optimal frequency threshold (θ) and power parameter (α) for Frequency Weighted Sampling across different vocabulary sizes and domain-specific corpora?
3. How does Dynamic Weighted Sampling compare to alternative dynamic sampling strategies (e.g., based on gradient magnitude, attention weights, or mutual information)?
4. Does Weighted Sampling improve the robustness of pre-trained models to domain shift and out-of-distribution data?

## Limitations

- The paper lacks theoretical justification for why token frequency should be inversely proportional to informativeness.
- The Dynamic Weighted Sampling mechanism's weight dictionary update process is not fully specified.
- The experimental scope is limited to only 3 STS tasks and 8 GLUE tasks, leaving questions about performance on other downstream tasks and domains.

## Confidence

### High Confidence Claims
- Weighted sampling strategies can reduce frequency bias in MLM compared to random masking
- WSBERT shows measurable improvements on STS benchmark tasks over BERT
- The frequency-based weighted sampling approach is computationally simpler to implement than dynamic sampling

### Medium Confidence Claims
- Dynamic weighted sampling provides additional benefits over frequency-based sampling
- WSBERT improves fine-tuning performance on GLUE benchmark tasks
- The observed improvements in token embedding space (isotropy, reduced frequency bias) directly contribute to downstream performance gains

### Low Confidence Claims
- The specific exponential functions used for computing sampling weights (freq^α and exp(-loss/τ)) are optimal
- The improvements will generalize to other pre-trained models beyond BERT-base-uncased
- The magnitude of improvements (e.g., 2-3% absolute gains) will be consistent across different datasets and training conditions

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study on sampling weight functions**: Systematically vary the exponent α in frequency-weighted sampling (try α ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0}) and the temperature τ in dynamic sampling (try τ ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5}) to determine whether the specific values chosen are optimal or if the improvements are robust across a range of parameters.

2. **Alternative proxy metrics for token informativeness**: Design and implement experiments using alternative metrics for token informativeness beyond frequency and prediction loss (such as mutual information with downstream tasks, or entropy-based measures) to test whether the core hypothesis about informativeness can be validated through multiple independent signals.

3. **Cross-model generalization test**: Apply the weighted sampling strategies to a different pre-trained model architecture (such as RoBERTa or DeBERTa) and evaluate performance on the same STS and GLUE tasks to determine whether the improvements are specific to BERT's architecture or represent a more general principle for masked language modeling.
