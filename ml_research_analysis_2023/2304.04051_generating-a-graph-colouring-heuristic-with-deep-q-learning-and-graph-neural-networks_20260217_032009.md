# Generating a Graph Colouring Heuristic with Deep Q-Learning and Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.04051
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04051
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: RL-based heuristic (ReLCol) competitive with, and sometimes better than, established greedy graph coloring algorithms like DSATUR, Largest First, and Smallest Last

## Executive Summary
This paper presents ReLCol, a deep reinforcement learning approach for generating a greedy graph coloring heuristic. The method combines deep Q-learning with a graph neural network to learn a policy that selects vertices for coloring in a way that minimizes the total number of colors used. A key innovation is the use of a complete graph representation within the GNN, which allows the model to capture global vertex relationships beyond immediate neighbors. The approach is evaluated on standard benchmark graphs and demonstrates competitive performance against established heuristics, including superior results on graph classes where traditional methods like DSATUR are known to underperform.

## Method Summary
ReLCol employs deep Q-learning with a graph neural network to learn a vertex selection policy for greedy graph coloring. The state is represented as a complete graph, enabling full pairwise connectivity information to flow through the GNN. The training dataset consists of 1000 synthetic graphs (15-50 vertices) across 7 types. The model uses epsilon-greedy exploration with decaying epsilon (0.9 to 0.01 over 25000 episodes), Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001), and batch size 64. Two deterministic shortcuts are used: random selection of the first vertex and immediate coloring of isolated vertices. The learned policy is evaluated on the COLOR02 benchmark dataset and compared against existing greedy algorithms.

## Key Results
- ReLCol achieves competitive performance with established heuristics like Largest First, Smallest Last, and DSATUR on standard benchmark graphs
- The complete graph representation significantly improves performance compared to standard GNN representations
- ReLCol outperforms DSATUR specifically on Spinrad graphs, a class where DSATUR is known to perform poorly
- The learned heuristic generalizes across diverse graph topologies despite being trained on synthetic data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Using a complete graph representation in the GNN allows the policy to capture global vertex relationships beyond local edges. In the standard GNN, message passing is restricted to immediate neighbors. By representing the state as a complete graph, every vertex can attend to all others, enabling the model to learn dependencies that span the entire graph. Core assumption: The full pairwise connectivity structure is necessary for identifying optimal greedy choices. Evidence: The paper shows improved performance with complete graph representation versus standard GNN, though no neighboring papers discuss this specific parameterisation.

### Mechanism 2
Deep Q-learning with an epsilon-greedy policy can learn a vertex selection heuristic that generalizes across diverse graph topologies. The agent builds a Q-network that estimates the value of selecting each uncolored vertex at each step, balancing exploration and exploitation to learn a policy that minimizes the total number of colors. Core assumption: The MDP formulation captures the essential sequential decision process of greedy coloring, and the learned policy can transfer across graph instances. Evidence: The paper demonstrates competitive performance across varied graph families, though related papers don't detail Q-learning + GNN integration specifics.

### Mechanism 3
Specialized rules for first vertex selection and immediate coloring of isolated vertices preserve optimality while reducing search space. By selecting the first vertex at random (any choice preserves optimality) and immediately coloring isolated vertices (which do not affect final color count), the agent focuses learning on more critical decisions. Core assumption: These deterministic shortcuts do not reduce the solution space in a way that harms optimality. Evidence: The paper cites propositions supporting these rules, though no neighboring papers mention these specific rules.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 2
Can the complete graph representation used in ReLCol be effectively applied to other reinforcement learning tasks with graph-structured data? Basis: The paper shows the complete graph representation significantly improves ReLCol's performance and suggests investigating its effect in other RL tasks. Why unresolved: The paper only demonstrates this benefit for graph coloring and doesn't explore other applications. What would resolve: Experiments showing improved RL performance using complete graph representation on other graph-structured tasks.

### Open Question 3
What specific mechanisms does ReLCol employ that differ from DSATUR in selecting vertices for coloring, leading to better performance on certain graph classes? Basis: The paper mentions ReLCol outperforms DSATUR on Spinrad graphs, indicating a different approach for vertex selection. Why unresolved: While demonstrating performance differences, the paper doesn't provide detailed analysis of the mechanisms behind ReLCol's strategy. What would resolve: Detailed analysis of ReLCol's decision-making process compared with DSATUR's strategy.

## Limitations
- Computational overhead of complete graph representation may limit scalability to larger instances
- Training on synthetic graphs (15-50 vertices) raises questions about performance on larger, real-world instances
- Theoretical guarantees for deterministic shortcuts depend on specific graph properties that may not hold universally

## Confidence

**High confidence**: Experimental results showing ReLCol's competitive performance against established heuristics on benchmark datasets, particularly improvement over DSATUR on Spinrad graphs where DSATUR is known to underperform.

**Medium confidence**: Mechanism by which complete graph representation improves policy learning, as evidence is primarily empirical without detailed ablation studies.

**Low confidence**: Generalizability of learned policy to graph sizes substantially larger than training set, given exclusive training on graphs with 15-50 vertices.
