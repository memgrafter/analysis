# An Investigation of Evaluation Metrics for Automated Medical Note Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17364
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17364
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 20
- Key outcome: Ensemble metrics, especially MIST-Comb1 and MIST-Comb2, achieve highest correlations with human judgments across different datasets and evaluation criteria for automated clinical note generation.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies evaluation metrics for automated clinical note generation, focusing on correlation with human assessments of factual correctness, hallucinations, and omissions. The authors introduce new task-specific metrics based on knowledge graph embeddings, fine-tuned models, and customized model-based metrics. Manual annotations were performed on seven datasets to create reference scores. Experiments show that ensemble metrics, especially MIST-Comb1 and MIST-Comb2, achieve the highest correlations with human judgments across different datasets and evaluation criteria. The study highlights the importance of domain-specific metrics and suggests that language-model based metrics and ensembles can outperform traditional N-gram based measures like ROUGE in this task.

## Method Summary
The study compares 21 automatic metrics including ROUGE variants, BERTScore, BLEURT, BARTScore, and proposed task-specific metrics (knowledge-graph embedding-based, fine-tuned, customized, and ensemble metrics). Seven manually annotated datasets of automatically generated clinical notes were used, including MTS-DIALOG, MEDIQA-RRS, and CONSULT-FACTS, with a total of 400-3,397 summary-note pairs. Reference scores (factual precision, recall, F1, hallucination rate, omission rate) were calculated from manual annotations. The evaluation focused on measuring Pearson correlation between automatic metrics and reference scores for factual precision, recall, F1, hallucination rate, and omission rate. The aggregate score combines factual correctness, hallucination, and omission.

## Key Results
- Ensemble metrics (MIST-Comb1 and MIST-Comb2) achieve highest correlations with human judgments across different datasets and evaluation criteria
- Language-model based metrics and metric ensembles can outperform SOTA N-gram based measures such as ROUGE when reference summaries are not biased
- Fine-tuned evaluation metrics on domain-specific data improve correlation with human judgments in clinical note generation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Ensemble metrics outperform individual metrics in correlating with human judgments of clinical note quality.
- Mechanism: By combining multiple metrics with different strengths (e.g., knowledge graph embeddings, lexical overlap, and language model similarity), ensemble metrics can capture a broader range of evaluation criteria and reduce the bias of any single metric.
- Core assumption: Different metrics capture different aspects of note quality, and their combination provides a more comprehensive evaluation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "However, the results highlight one stable subset of metrics as the most correlated with human judgments with a relevant aggregation of different evaluation criteria."
  - [section]: "Our experiments also show that language-model based metrics and metric ensembles can outperform SOTA N-gram based measures such as ROUGE when reference summaries are not biased."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; no direct corpus support for ensemble performance.
- Break condition: If the ensemble weights are not properly normalized or if the constituent metrics are highly correlated, the ensemble may not provide additional value over the best individual metric.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning evaluation metrics on domain-specific data improves their correlation with human judgments in clinical note generation.
- Mechanism: By training evaluation metrics on a large corpus of clinical notes with quality scores derived from expert annotations, the metrics learn to recognize domain-specific terminology and evaluation criteria relevant to clinical note generation.
- Core assumption: The domain-specific training data and quality scores accurately represent the aspects of clinical note quality that matter to human experts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Our second approach relies on fine-tuning model-based metrics on relevant large medical collections of family medicine and orthopaedic notes."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; no direct corpus support for the effectiveness of fine-tuning on clinical data.
- Break condition: If the fine-tuning data is not representative of the target domain or if the quality scores are not reliable, the fine-tuned metrics may not generalize well to new data.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Assigning higher weights to medical terms in evaluation metrics improves their correlation with human judgments of clinical note quality.
- Mechanism: By increasing the weight of medical terms in the evaluation process, the metrics prioritize the correct use of medical terminology, which is a key aspect of clinical note quality.
- Core assumption: The correct use of medical terminology is a critical aspect of clinical note quality and is weighted appropriately by human experts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "These medical weighted metrics will allow us to examine whether words with a medical meaning can be more indicative for sentence similarity than common words for the task of automated medical note generation."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; no direct corpus support for the effectiveness of medical term weighting.
- Break condition: If the weighting scheme over-emphasizes medical terminology at the expense of other important aspects of note quality, such as factual correctness or coherence, the metrics may not correlate well with human judgments.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do ensemble metrics perform when additional evaluation criteria beyond factual correctness, hallucination, and omission rates are considered?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "the solution to the problem is likely to be domain- and task-specific" and suggests more results are needed to validate findings.
- Why unresolved: The study focused on three main evaluation criteria but didn't explore how ensemble metrics would perform with additional criteria like fluency, coherence, or readability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing ensemble metrics against a broader set of evaluation criteria on multiple clinical note generation datasets, including both expert and non-expert human assessments.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would the performance of the proposed medical weighted evaluation metrics change if different weighting schemes for medical terms were used?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "After experimenting with different values in the [0.1, 1.5] range, we found that the best α value was 1.0 for the weight policy."
- Why unresolved: The study only tested a limited range of weight values and didn't explore more extreme or nuanced weighting schemes that might better capture the importance of medical terminology in clinical note generation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic testing of the medical weighted metrics with a wider range of weight values and more complex weighting schemes (e.g., term-specific weights based on clinical importance).

### Open Question 3
- Question: How would the performance of the proposed metrics change if they were trained on a more diverse set of clinical note types beyond family medicine and orthopedics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The ClinicalBLEURT metric was fine-tuned on "a total of 6,367 family medicine and orthopaedic encounters."
- Why unresolved: The fine-tuning process was limited to two medical specialties, which may not capture the full diversity of clinical note types and medical terminology used across different specialties.
- What evidence would resolve it: Fine-tuning the ClinicalBLEURT metric and other domain-adapted metrics on a more diverse set of clinical note types from various medical specialties, then comparing their performance on the same evaluation tasks.

## Limitations

- The correlation analysis relies on a relatively small number of manually annotated samples (400-3,397 summary-note pairs across seven datasets), which may limit the generalizability of the findings.
- The paper reports that ensemble metrics like MIST-Comb1 and MIST-Comb2 achieve the highest correlations with human judgments, but the evidence supporting this claim is primarily based on aggregate correlation scores rather than detailed ablation studies.
- The paper does not provide a thorough analysis of the variance in metric performance across different datasets, making it difficult to assess the stability of the proposed metrics in real-world scenarios.

## Confidence

- Fine-tuning evaluation metrics on domain-specific data improves correlation: Low
