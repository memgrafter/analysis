# Accelerating Large Language Model Decoding with Speculative Sampling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.01318
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01318
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: 2-2.5x decoding speedup for Chinchilla 70B using speculative sampling without compromising sample quality

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces speculative sampling, a novel algorithm for accelerating transformer decoding by enabling the generation of multiple tokens per transformer call. The key insight is that parallel scoring of short continuations generated by a faster draft model has similar latency to sampling a single token from the larger target model. Combined with a modified rejection sampling scheme that preserves the target model's distribution, this approach achieves 2-2.5x speedup for large language models like Chinchilla without requiring any modifications to the model architecture itself.

## Method Summary
Speculative sampling accelerates decoding by having a smaller, faster draft model generate sequences of K tokens, which are then scored in parallel by the larger target model. A modified rejection sampling scheme accepts or rejects these draft tokens based on the ratio of target to draft probabilities, with rejected tokens being resampled from the difference distribution. The authors train a 4B parameter draft model optimized for sampling latency on the same dataset as the 70B parameter Chinchilla target model. The method is evaluated on XSum summarization and HumanEval code generation tasks, demonstrating significant speedups while maintaining sample quality.

## Key Results
- Achieved 2-2.5x decoding speedup for Chinchilla 70B in distributed TPU v4 setup
- Maintained sample quality with no degradation in XSum ROUGE-2 or HumanEval pass@1 metrics
- Demonstrated domain-dependent acceptance rates (higher for code than text generation)
- Showed that speedup plateaus or degrades with lookahead parameter K > 3 for XSum task

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Speculative sampling accelerates decoding by generating multiple tokens per transformer call.
- Mechanism: The draft model generates a sequence of tokens in parallel, and the target model scores this sequence. A modified rejection sampling scheme accepts or rejects draft tokens based on the ratio of target to draft probabilities.
- Core assumption: The latency of parallel scoring of short continuations is comparable to that of sampling a single token from the larger target model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The algorithm relies on the observation that the latency of parallel scoring of short continuations, generated by a faster but less powerful draft model, is comparable to that of sampling a single token from the larger target model."
  - [section] "We first make the observation that computing the logits of a short continuation of 𝐾 tokens in parallel has a very similar latency to that of sampling a single token."
- Break condition: If the parallel scoring latency significantly exceeds the single token sampling latency, the speedup advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The modified rejection sampling scheme preserves the distribution of the target model.
- Mechanism: Draft tokens are accepted with a probability based on the ratio of target to draft probabilities. If rejected, a new token is sampled from the difference of the target and draft distributions.
- Core assumption: The rejection sampling process converges to the target distribution within hardware numerics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This is combined with a novel modified rejection sampling scheme which preserves the distribution of the target model within hardware numerics."
  - [section] "By applying this sequentially, we recover the distribution of the target model for the accepted tokens (see proof in Theorem 1) within hardware numerics."
- Break condition: If the hardware numerics introduce significant bias or if the rejection sampling process fails to converge.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using a smaller draft model trained on the same data as the target model yields high acceptance rates.
- Mechanism: The draft model, being smaller and faster, can generate tokens quickly. Since it's trained on the same data, there's a high likelihood of agreement with the target model, leading to high acceptance rates.
- Core assumption: There are "easier" tokens to predict for which smaller models may be sufficient, leading to high agreement between draft and target models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Therefore we may simply use a smaller version of the target language model as the draft and obtain high acceptance rates."
  - [section] "We train a 4 billion parameter draft model optimised for sampling latency on 16 TPU v4s... This model was trained with the same tokeniser and dataset as Chinchilla."
- Break condition: If the draft model's predictions diverge significantly from the target model, leading to low acceptance rates and negating the speedup benefit.

  - Quick check question: In standard rejection sampling, what is the acceptance probability for a sample x from a proposal distribution q(x) when trying to sample from a target distribution p(x)?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Draft Model -> Parallel Scoring -> Rejection Sampling Module -> Resampling Module -> Output
- Critical path:
  1. Draft model generates a sequence of tokens.
  2. Target model scores the draft sequence in parallel.
  3. Rejection sampling module accepts or rejects draft tokens.
  4. If a token is rejected, the resampling module generates a new token.
  5. The accepted tokens are output as the final sequence.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model size vs. speed: Smaller draft models are faster but may have lower acceptance rates.
  - Lookahead length (K) vs. efficiency: Larger K can lead to more tokens generated per loop but may also increase variance and reduce acceptance rates.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low acceptance rates: Indicates the draft model is not well-aligned with the target model.
  - High variance in generation time: May be due to a large K or imbalanced acceptance rates across different sequence segments.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a basic version of speculative sampling with a simple draft model and target model.
  2. Measure the acceptance rate and speedup compared to auto-regressive sampling.
  3. Experiment with different values of K to find the optimal lookahead length for the given models and task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal lookahead parameter K for different domains and model sizes to maximize decoding speed while minimizing variance in latency?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses trade-offs between increasing K and loop time, noting that speedup plateaus or degrades with larger K (e.g., XSum optimal at K=3).
- Why unresolved: The paper provides empirical results for specific tasks but does not establish a general formula or methodology for determining optimal K across varying domains and model sizes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive experiments varying K across diverse domains (code generation, summarization, dialogue) and model sizes, with analysis of speedup vs. latency variance trade-offs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does speculative sampling perform when the draft model is trained using sequence-level distillation or other techniques beyond using a smaller version of the target model?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions sequence-level distillation as an alternative approach but focuses on using a smaller target model variant due to practicality.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not empirically compare performance between different draft model training strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarking speculative sampling with draft models trained via sequence-level distillation, parallel generation heads, and other techniques against the simpler approach used in the paper.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can speculative sampling be effectively combined with other acceleration techniques like quantization and multi-query attention to achieve multiplicative speedups?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that speculative sampling can be used alongside techniques like quantization and multi-query attention.
- Why unresolved: While compatibility is claimed, the paper does not provide empirical results demonstrating combined speedups.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring the cumulative speedup when applying speculative sampling together with quantization, multi-query attention, and other acceleration methods, compared to applying them individually.

## Limitations

- Hardware dependency: Speedup claims are specific to TPU v4 architecture and may not generalize to other hardware
- Limited domain evaluation: Performance across diverse domains and tokenizers is not extensively characterized
- Sample quality validation: Quality preservation claims rely on limited metrics without human evaluation or long-range coherence analysis

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core algorithmic insight (parallel scoring of short continuations) and the general framework of speculative sampling are well-founded. The mathematical proof of distribution preservation under ideal conditions is sound.

**Medium Confidence**: The empirical speedup measurements on TPU v4 are convincing for the specific hardware setup, but the generalizability to other hardware and the robustness across diverse domains require further validation.

**Low Confidence**: The claims about maintaining sample quality "without compromising" are supported by limited metrics (XSum ROUGE-2 and HumanEval pass@1). The paper doesn't address potential subtle quality degradation in longer generations or more complex domains.
