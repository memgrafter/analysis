# A Review of the Role of Causality in Developing Trustworthy AI Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.06975
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06975
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: This review comprehensively surveys how causality-based methods address trustworthy AI aspects: interpretability, fairness, robustness, privacy, safety, and accountability.

## Executive Summary
This survey comprehensively examines how causal reasoning and modeling enhance AI trustworthiness across six key dimensions: interpretability, fairness, robustness, privacy, safety, and accountability. The review demonstrates that causality enables AI systems to understand cause-effect relationships, improving generalization to unseen data, reducing unfair outcomes, and increasing interpretability. It highlights causality's significant applications in healthcare for disease analysis, drug discovery, and diagnosis, while providing extensive resources including datasets and tools for causal machine learning. Despite significant progress, the survey emphasizes that many important facets remain unexplored, presenting numerous opportunities for future research in applying causality to trustworthy AI.

## Method Summary
This paper surveys existing research on causality-based methods for trustworthy AI, categorizing approaches by the specific trustworthy aspect they address (interpretability, fairness, robustness, privacy, safety, and accountability). It provides an overview of causal methods developed to improve AI trustworthiness and motivates future research on causality-based solutions. The paper also surveys causality applications in healthcare and provides a list of datasets and tools. However, it does not provide detailed step-by-step implementation guides or code for reproducing specific results.

## Key Results
- Causal modeling improves interpretability by aligning model explanations with true causal structure through SCMs
- Causality mitigates fairness issues by distinguishing direct from indirect effects of sensitive attributes via path-specific counterfactual fairness
- Causal invariance improves robustness by focusing on features that remain stable across environments through invariant risk minimization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Causal modeling improves interpretability by aligning model explanations with true causal structure.
- Mechanism: Structural causal models (SCMs) explicitly encode cause-effect relationships, enabling post-hoc methods to trace feature contributions along causal paths rather than spurious correlations.
- Core assumption: The underlying data generating process can be approximated by an SCM, and the learned causal graph reflects true dependencies.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "causal reasoning and modeling enable AI systems to understand cause-effect relationships, improving generalization to unseen data, reducing unfair outcomes, and increasing interpretability." [section 3] "To ensure that the model's explanation communicates the true reasons behind a prediction, the explanation itself should preferably be causal." [corpus] Weak: No direct causal interpretability benchmarks in cited papers; relies on general interpretability literature.
- Break condition: If the SCM is misspecified or cannot be learned from available data, explanations may reflect spurious associations rather than true causal effects.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Causality mitigates fairness issues by distinguishing direct from indirect effects of sensitive attributes.
- Mechanism: Path-specific counterfactual fairness isolates unfair causal pathways (e.g., direct discrimination) while preserving legitimate indirect effects, enabling targeted fairness interventions.
- Core assumption: Sensitive attribute effects can be decomposed into fair and unfair causal paths, and these paths are identifiable from observational data.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "causal reasoning and modeling enable AI systems... reducing unfair outcomes." [section 4.1.1] "Path specific counterfactual fairness... attempts to remove the causal effects of sensitive attributes on the outcome along only unfair causal paths." [corpus] Weak: Fairness benchmarks often rely on synthetic or semi-synthetic datasets; real-world validation of path-specific fairness is limited.
- Break condition: When causal paths are not identifiable due to unmeasured confounders, path-specific fairness cannot be reliably enforced.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Causal invariance improves robustness by focusing on features that remain stable across environments.
- Mechanism: Invariant risk minimization (IRM) learns representations that are predictive across multiple environments, effectively filtering out spurious correlations that vary between domains.
- Core assumption: The causal structure of the problem is invariant across environments, even if the distribution of non-causal features shifts.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "causal reasoning and modeling enable AI systems... improving generalization to unseen data." [section 5.3.1] "One vital insight for causal ML is the connection between the causal relevance of a feature and its invariance across environments." [corpus] Moderate: OOD generalization benchmarks exist (e.g., DomainBed), but causal approaches are not yet standard in robustness evaluations.
- Break condition: If the causal structure itself changes between environments (e.g., policy shifts), invariance-based robustness will fail.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: SCMs provide the mathematical framework for encoding and reasoning about cause-effect relationships in AI systems.
  - Quick check question: What are the three components of an SCM, and how do they differ from standard probabilistic graphical models?

  - Why needed here: Counterfactuals allow evaluation of "what if" scenarios, essential for fairness audits and causal explanations.
  - Quick check question: How does a counterfactual query differ from an interventional query in causal inference?

- Concept: Causal discovery
  - Why needed here: Many applications assume access to a causal graph, but in practice these must often be learned from data.
  - Quick check question: What are the key assumptions required for reliable causal discovery from observational data?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> Feature engineering, causal graph construction or discovery -> Causal modeling -> SCM specification, potential outcomes framework, or hybrid approach -> Model training -> Causal regularization, invariant representation learning, or fairness constraints -> Post-processing -> Causal explanations, counterfactual interventions, or impact assessments -> Evaluation -> Causal fairness metrics, OOD robustness tests, or causal interpretability benchmarks
- Critical path: Data → Causal graph → Causal-aware model → Post-hoc causal analysis → Trustworthiness validation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Expressiveness vs. identifiability: Richer causal models may be harder to identify from data
  - Computational cost: Causal discovery and inference can be expensive, especially for large graphs
  - Domain knowledge vs. automation: Expert-provided causal graphs improve reliability but reduce scalability
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor OOD performance despite causal training: Likely violation of invariance assumptions
  - Fairness metrics improve but real-world bias persists: Possible unmeasured confounding or misspecified causal paths
  - Explanations seem plausible but don't match human reasoning: SCM may not capture the right causal structure

  1. Train a standard model and a causal counterpart (e.g., with IRM) on ColoredMNIST; compare OOD performance.
  2. Implement path-specific counterfactual fairness on Adult dataset; evaluate direct vs. indirect discrimination.
  3. Use causal mediation analysis on a pre-trained NLP model to identify bias sources; compare with SHAP explanations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Open Question 1: How can we develop standardized procedures for evaluating causal interpretability methods, particularly in high-stakes domains like healthcare?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] "However, the underlying processes that lead to biased predictions and, in turn, the best approach to counteract these biases is still not well understood, and methods need to be developed in that direction. We observe structural causal models to be generated and used for many problems but still lack standardized procedures for evaluation. For example, an assessment of the extent to which the provided explanations help humans to causally understand a model's output would be helpful."
  - Why unresolved: Current interpretability methods lack standardized evaluation criteria, particularly for assessing how well explanations help humans understand causal relationships.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of standardized evaluation metrics for causal interpretability methods, ideally validated through human studies in high-stakes domains.

- Open Question 2: What are the most effective approaches for extending causality-based fairness solutions to non-classification tasks like community detection, word embedding, or machine translation?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] "Finally, all research done in the field of causal fairness is connected to classification tasks [301], it will be interesting to expand it to achieve causal fairness in community detection, word embedding, named entity recognition, representation learning, semantic role labeling, language models, and machine translation [140]."
  - Why unresolved: Current causality-based fairness solutions are primarily focused on classification tasks, leaving other important AI applications unaddressed.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of causal fairness approaches for non-classification tasks, demonstrating effectiveness across different AI applications.

- Open Question 3: How can we design causal machine learning methods that effectively handle non-IID data in real-world settings, particularly for federated learning scenarios?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] "Most of the causality-based fairness solutions discussed above rely on the assumption that the underlying data is independent and identically distributed (IID). However, real-world use cases include non-IID data, therefore, another future direction could be to design causality-based decision support systems which relax the assumption of non-IID data and provide non-discriminatory predictions."
  - Why unresolved: Current causality-based methods assume IID data, which is often violated in real-world scenarios, especially in federated learning.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of causal methods that explicitly handle non-IID data distributions while maintaining fairness and other trustworthy AI properties.

## Limitations

- The paper does not provide detailed step-by-step implementation guides for the discussed causal methods
- Many causal fairness and interpretability methods lack standardized evaluation criteria and real-world validation
- Current causality-based methods primarily focus on classification tasks, with limited extension to other AI applications

## Confidence

This review demonstrates **high confidence** in the core claim that causal methods can enhance AI trustworthiness through interpretability, fairness, and robustness improvements. The mechanisms linking causality to these outcomes are well-established in the literature, though empirical validation remains limited in some areas. **Medium confidence** applies to claims about privacy, safety, and accountability, as these dimensions have fewer dedicated causal approaches and limited real-world deployment evidence. **Low confidence** surrounds scalability claims, as most causal methods are demonstrated on small-to-medium datasets and their performance at industrial scale remains unproven.
