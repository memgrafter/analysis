# Enhancing Cross-lingual Natural Language Inference by Soft Prompting with Multilingual Verbalizer

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12761
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12761

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SoftMV, a soft prompt learning framework with a multilingual verbalizer for cross-lingual natural language inference. The method constructs cloze-style questions with soft prompts and generates augmented multilingual questions via code-switched substitution. It uses a multilingual verbalizer to align representations across languages with consistency regularization. Experiments on XNLI show that SoftMV significantly outperforms previous methods under both few-shot and full-shot cross-lingual transfer settings, achieving 3.5% higher accuracy on average compared to state-of-the-art models.

## Method Summary
SoftMV is a soft prompt learning framework with a multilingual verbalizer for cross-lingual natural language inference. It constructs cloze-style questions with soft prompts and generates augmented multilingual questions via code-switched substitution. The framework uses a multilingual verbalizer to align representations across languages with consistency regularization. The model is trained on English data and evaluated on 14 target languages in few-shot and full-shot settings.

## Key Results
- SoftMV significantly outperforms previous methods under both few-shot and full-shot cross-lingual transfer settings on XNLI
- Achieves 3.5% higher accuracy on average compared to state-of-the-art models
- Demonstrates effectiveness of soft prompts for cross-lingual transfer without manual template design

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Soft prompts in embedding space enable better cross-lingual transfer than discrete prompts
- Mechanism: Trainable soft prompts initialized from multilingual vocabulary embeddings can be directly adapted to target languages without manual template design, allowing the model to learn language-agnostic representations
- Core assumption: The embedding space of the multilingual model captures cross-lingual semantic relationships that can be leveraged by soft prompts
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Soft prompts perform prompting directly in the embedding space of the model and can be easily adapted to any language without human-designed templates"
  - [section] "Soft prompts are tuned in the continuous space and initialized with the average value of embeddings of the PLM's multilingual vocabulary"
- Break condition: If the multilingual embedding space does not capture sufficient cross-lingual semantic relationships, or if the soft prompts become too specialized to the source language during training

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Code-switched augmentation creates effective cross-lingual views for consistency regularization
- Mechanism: Randomly replacing words in English sentences with translations from bilingual dictionaries generates augmented samples that serve as cross-lingual views, which are then used with KL divergence loss to align representations across languages
- Core assumption: The code-switched samples preserve semantic meaning while introducing controlled language variation that helps the model learn robust cross-lingual representations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we leverage bilingual dictionaries to generate an augmented multilingual question for the original question"
  - [section] "we apply the code-switched substitution strategy to generate multilingual questions which can be regarded as cross-lingual views for the English questions"
- Break condition: If the code-switched substitutions significantly alter meaning or if the bilingual dictionaries are incomplete, leading to semantic drift between original and augmented samples

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multilingual verbalizer reduces classification space gap between languages
- Mechanism: Using language-specific verbalizers translated from the English verbalizer ensures that the classification probability distributions are aligned across languages, reducing the gap when calculating masked token predictions
- Core assumption: Translations of the verbalizer terms preserve the semantic relationships needed for consistent classification across languages
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The multilingual verbalizer comprises a set of verbalizers for different languages"
  - [section] "The non-English verbalizers are translated from English using bilingual dictionaries"
- Break condition: If translations of verbalizer terms do not preserve semantic equivalence across languages, leading to misaligned classification spaces

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input -> Soft Prompt Layer -> Code-Switched Augmentation -> Multilingual Verbalizer -> Cross-lingual Model -> Consistency Regularization -> Output

- Critical path:
  1. Construct cloze-style questions with soft prompts
  2. Generate augmented multilingual questions via code-switched substitution
  3. Calculate answer probability distributions using multilingual verbalizer
  4. Apply consistency regularization via KL divergence loss
  5. Optimize combined cross-entropy and consistency objectives

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Soft prompts vs. discrete prompts: Soft prompts offer flexibility but may require more training data to converge
  - Code-switched augmentation rate: Higher rates may introduce more noise but could improve generalization
  - Consistency loss weight: Balancing between classification accuracy and representation alignment

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on low-resource languages: May indicate insufficient cross-lingual alignment or inadequate bilingual dictionary coverage
  - Overfitting on few-shot settings: Could suggest the soft prompts are too specialized to the source language
  - Large gap between original and augmented distributions: May indicate semantic drift in code-switched samples

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation study removing consistency loss to quantify its impact on cross-lingual transfer
  2. Test different code-switched substitution rates to find optimal augmentation strategy
  3. Compare soft prompts of varying lengths to determine optimal expressivity for few-shot learning

## Limitations
- The evaluation framework relies heavily on the quality and coverage of bilingual dictionaries for code-switched augmentation, which is not fully specified in the paper
- The method's performance in extremely low-resource settings (e.g., 16-shot scenarios) may be limited by the soft prompts becoming too specialized to the source language during fine-tuning
- The consistency regularization mechanism assumes that semantic meaning is preserved through code-switched substitution, but this assumption has not been empirically validated across all language pairs in XNLI

## Confidence
- **High confidence** in the core claim that soft prompts outperform discrete prompts for cross-lingual NLI, supported by direct comparison with mBERT-based methods and consistent improvements across all 14 target languages
- **Medium confidence** in the claim that code-switched augmentation improves cross-lingual transfer, as the mechanism is well-motivated but the optimal substitution rate and dictionary quality are not fully explored
- **Medium confidence** in the effectiveness of the multilingual verbalizer for alignment, as the translation quality from English verbalizers and its impact on classification performance across languages requires further investigation

## Next Checks
1. **Semantic drift analysis**: Measure semantic similarity between original English questions and code-switched augmented questions using established metrics (e.g., SBERT similarity scores) to quantify potential meaning alteration during augmentation
2. **Cross-lingual generalization stress test**: Evaluate performance on truly low-resource languages (e.g., Swahili, Urdu) with minimal parallel data to identify the practical limits of the soft prompting approach
3. **Verbalizer alignment validation**: Conduct human evaluation of translated verbalizer terms across all languages to verify semantic equivalence and identify potential misalignments that could affect classification performance
