# Low-resource Bilingual Dialect Lexicon Induction with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.09957
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09957
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Induced Bavarian lexicon (15k pairs) achieves 65% precision; Alemannic lexicon (68k pairs) achieves 40% precision for high-frequency words

## Executive Summary
This work presents a pipeline for inducing bilingual lexicons between German and two of its dialects, Bavarian and Alemannic. The approach combines bitext mining with word alignment using pre-trained language models. The pipeline is evaluated intrinsically via manual annotation of semantic similarity for 1,500 sentence pairs and 1,000 word pairs. Results show that bitext mining achieves human-annotated semantic similarity scores of 4-5 for 85% of Bavarian and 60% of Alemannic sentence pairs. Bilingual lexicon induction obtains precision of 65% for Bavarian and 40% for Alemannic on high-frequency words, with higher precision (90%+) for low- and mid-frequency words.

## Method Summary
The method involves extracting parallel sentences from Wikipedia using interlingual links and sentence similarity models (LaBSE with cutoff 0.7), performing word alignment on parallel sentences using awesome-align with MBERT or GBERT backbone, and filtering word pairs by alignment probability threshold (0.8). The induced lexicons are evaluated via manual annotation for semantic similarity and dictionary-based lookup, with evaluation stratified by word frequency quartiles. The pipeline combines bitext mining and word alignment to create bilingual lexicons without requiring parallel corpora or existing dictionaries.

## Key Results
- Bitext mining achieves human-annotated semantic similarity scores of 4-5 for 85% of Bavarian and 60% of Alemannic sentence pairs
- Bilingual lexicon induction obtains precision of 65% for Bavarian and 40% for Alemannic on high-frequency words
- Induced lexicons contain 15k and 68k word pairs for Bavarian and Alemannic respectively

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Cross-lingual LLMs can capture semantic similarity between sentences and words across German and its dialects through sentence-level contrastive objectives that align representations across languages, enabling cosine similarity-based retrieval. The core assumption is that dialect and standard German share enough lexical and semantic overlap for embeddings to align. Evidence shows bitext mining achieves human-annotated semantic similarity scores of 4-5 for 85% of Bavarian and 60% of Alemannic sentence pairs. The break condition occurs when dialect and standard language diverge too much in orthography or syntax, causing alignment performance to drop sharply.

### Mechanism 2
Word alignment via awesome-align can induce bilingual lexicons by leveraging surface-level similarity and contextual embeddings to compute token similarity in parallel sentences, then selecting the most probable word pair. The core assumption is that word pairs with high alignment probability correspond to correct translations. Evidence shows correlation coefficient between normalized edit distance and average alignment probability makes -0.4/-0.38 and -0.49/-0.56. The break condition is high orthographic variation or lack of standardization causing many false negatives or false positives.

### Mechanism 3
Frequency-stratified sampling improves evaluation coverage for low-frequency words by splitting words into quartiles by frequency and sampling equally from each to ensure low-frequency words are represented. The core assumption is that BLI performance varies systematically by word frequency. Evidence shows higher frequency source words generally resulting in more accurate translations. The break condition is if frequency distribution is highly skewed, quartile splits may leave some bins too small for reliable evaluation.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Cosine similarity as a measure of semantic similarity in embedding space.
  - Why needed here: Core to both bitext mining and word alignment steps.
  - Quick check question: If two sentences have cosine similarity 0.9, are they always semantically identical? (No—high similarity doesn't guarantee equivalence.)

- Concept: Edit distance as a surface-level feature for alignment probability.
  - Why needed here: Alignment models use it as a feature to weight similarity.
  - Quick check question: Can two words with high edit distance still be translations? (Yes, if they are morphologically different but semantically equivalent.)

- Concept: Stratified sampling for balanced evaluation.
  - Why needed here: To avoid bias toward high-frequency words in low-resource dialects.
  - Quick check question: If you sample uniformly from all words, what's the risk? (Underrepresenting low-frequency words, which are harder to align.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data extraction (Wikipedia2corpus → SoMaJo tokenizer) -> Bitext mining (LaBSE embeddings + cosine similarity + threshold 0.7) -> Word alignment (awesome-align with MBERT/GBERT backbone) -> Evaluation (human annotation for semantic similarity + frequency-stratified sampling)
- Critical path: Bitext mining → word alignment → evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Using pre-trained models saves time but may underperform on dialects if not exposed to them; one-to-one alignment simplifies output but loses many-to-one mappings; human annotation ensures quality but limits scale
- Failure signatures: Low precision on high-frequency words → too much noise from prepositions/auxiliaries; high orthographic variance → low alignment scores despite semantic similarity; skewed similarity distributions → threshold tuning issues
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run bitext mining with MBERT-[CLS] vs LaBSE and compare precision@K
  2. Vary awesome-align backbone (MBERT vs GBERT) and threshold, plot F1 curves
  3. Test whether frequency-stratified sampling changes downstream task performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does the two-stage pipeline of bitext mining followed by word alignment produce higher quality bilingual lexicons than direct word alignment methods like MUSE? While the paper compares their two-stage pipeline to MUSE embeddings as a baseline, finding that their pipeline produces more literal synonyms and identifies more correct translations (860/774 vs 48/74 word pairs), the study only examines Bavarian and Alemannic dialects. A systematic comparison across multiple language pairs using consistent evaluation metrics and datasets would resolve this.

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal cutoff threshold for alignment probability in bilingual lexicon induction across different frequency bins of words? The paper uses a fixed cutoff of 0.8 for MBERT alignment probability, but notes that the optimal threshold varies by model and may differ for different frequency bins. Experiments testing multiple cutoff thresholds separately for each frequency quartile and dialect pair, measuring precision, recall, and F1 scores, would determine optimal thresholds.

### Open Question 3
How much does the lack of dialect-specific linguistic tools (stemmers, lemmatizers, phonemizers) impact the quality and coverage of induced bilingual lexicons? The paper notes that the induced lexicons suffer from word form repetition due to multiple dialect spellings, and that the authors were unable to merge different forms of the same word due to the lack of dialect stemmers, lemmatizers, or phonemizers. Experiments applying different normalization strategies to the induced lexicons and measuring changes in precision, recall, and vocabulary coverage would resolve this.

## Limitations

- Reliance on pre-trained cross-lingual models trained primarily on standardized languages may not fully capture dialectal nuances
- One-to-one alignment constraint of awesome-align potentially misses many-to-one mappings common in dialects
- Orthographic non-standardization in dialects creates additional alignment challenges

## Confidence

- High confidence: Bitext mining achieves measurable semantic similarity scores for sentence pairs (85% Bavarian, 60% Alemannic at scores 4-5)
- Medium confidence: Word alignment precision rates (65% Bavarian, 40% Alemannic for high-frequency words)
- Medium confidence: Frequency-stratified sampling approach improves evaluation coverage
