# Catastrophic Forgetting in the Context of Model Updates

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10181
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10181
- Reference count: 5
- The paper evaluates strategies to mitigate catastrophic forgetting during sequential model updates, finding that data rehearsal with 50% past data mixing outperforms regularization methods and model averaging.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates methods to prevent catastrophic forgetting when updating deep learning models on sequentially arriving data. The study compares model averaging, L2 regularization, Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), and data rehearsal approaches using a malware detection task with 12 monthly datasets. Data rehearsal, particularly when mixing 50% past data without fixing epoch size, delivers the best average AUC across all time periods. Combining data rehearsal with EWC yields the strongest results, nearly matching models retrained from scratch while remaining far more computationally efficient.

## Method Summary
The study trains a PE malware detection model with five large fully connected layers on 12 monthly tasks, each containing approximately 4 million samples. After training a base model on the first 5 months, sequential fine-tuning is performed on months 6-9 using different mitigation strategies: model averaging, L2 regularization, EWC regularization, and data rehearsal at various mixing ratios (10%, 25%, 50%, 75%). The final models are evaluated on holdout months 10-12, with performance measured by average AUC across all 12 months, plus separate averages for base, update, and future months.

## Key Results
- Data rehearsal with 50% past data mixing without fixed epoch size achieved the best average AUC across all time periods
- Model averaging performed significantly worse than sequential fine-tuning
- L2 regularization reduced forgetting but also hindered learning on new data
- EWC provided modest improvements but was less effective for large models due to approximation errors
- Combining data rehearsal with EWC yielded the strongest results, nearly matching full retraining from scratch

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Data rehearsal prevents catastrophic forgetting by interleaving past samples with new data during fine-tuning, keeping older task representations active.
- Mechanism: During each fine-tuning epoch, the model sees a mix of new task data and randomly sampled past data. This regular exposure maintains the learned decision boundaries for previous tasks.
- Core assumption: Past data distribution is accessible and remains representative of older tasks; mixing ratio can be tuned to balance new learning with retention.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "mix in a small amount of past data into the new data during fine-tuning: also known as 'data rehearsal'"
  - [section] "Simply put: you mix in data from past tasks while training on new ones. It works really well..."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct citations; relies on internal experiment data.
- Break condition: If past data is not available, corrupted, or distribution shifts drastically, rehearsal cannot prevent forgetting.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) protects important parameters from shifting by penalizing updates based on estimated importance.
- Mechanism: EWC estimates parameter importance using the Fisher information matrix from past tasks, adding a regularization term to the loss that scales with parameter importance.
- Core assumption: Fisher information accurately reflects parameter importance; second-order derivatives are tractable and reliable for large models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "even better when combined with methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)"
  - [section] "EWC is similar to L2 regularization, but each parameter's movement penalty is scaled by the movement squared...multiplied by EWC's estimate of parameter importance to past tasks"
  - [corpus] Weak - no corpus citations; internal theoretical derivation only.
- Break condition: High model complexity increases approximation error in Taylor series estimates; negative Fisher values break the regularization.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: L2 regularization reduces forgetting by limiting overall parameter movement during fine-tuning.
- Mechanism: Adds a penalty term proportional to the squared difference between current and previous parameter values, discouraging large shifts that erase old knowledge.
- Core assumption: Parameter stability correlates with task retention; all parameters contribute equally to past performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "L2 regularization reduced forgetting but also hindered learning"
  - [section] "Simply training multiple models per time period and averaging the results seems like a potentially reasonable approach... performed much worse than a sequentially fine-tuned model"
  - [corpus] Weak - no corpus citations; relies on reported experimental outcomes.
- Break condition: Over-regularization prevents adaptation to new data; under-regularization fails to prevent forgetting.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Catastrophic forgetting in neural networks
  - Why needed here: Understanding why sequential fine-tuning degrades performance on older data is central to evaluating mitigation strategies.
  - Quick check question: What happens to a model's accuracy on past data after fine-tuning only on new data?

- Concept: Fine-tuning vs. full retraining
  - Why needed here: The paper contrasts cheap but forgetting-prone fine-tuning with expensive but stable full retraining as baselines.
  - Quick check question: Why is full retraining from scratch more computationally expensive than fine-tuning an existing model?

- Concept: Regularization in deep learning
  - Why needed here: Both L2 and EWC are regularization techniques applied during fine-tuning to control parameter changes.
  - Quick check question: How does adding an L2 penalty to the loss function affect parameter updates during training?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the proposed methods scale when applied to models with significantly more parameters, and what approximations can be made to reduce computational overhead?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that EWC becomes less effective for large models due to approximation errors, suggesting scalability is a concern.
- Why unresolved: The experiments focused on a specific model size and did not explore how different model scales affect method performance or computational feasibility.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying model size and parameter count, along with profiling of computational cost and approximation accuracy for each method.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal trade-off between the proportion of past data used in rehearsal and the increase in computational cost, and how does this vary across different data distributions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that 50% rehearsal without fixed epoch size yielded the best results but increased computational cost, suggesting a need to optimize this balance.
- Why unresolved: The experiments tested a limited set of rehearsal ratios and did not explore adaptive or dynamic approaches to balancing cost and performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing a wider range of rehearsal ratios, adaptive rehearsal strategies, and performance across diverse data distributions with varying computational budgets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the effectiveness of data rehearsal be maintained when only a non-iid subsample of past data is available, and what sampling strategies minimize performance degradation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that rehearsal requires access to past data or an iid subsample, but does not explore the impact of non-iid sampling.
- Why unresolved: The experiments assumed access to iid past data, leaving open the question of how non-iid sampling affects performance and what strategies can mitigate this.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing rehearsal performance using iid versus non-iid subsamples, and testing different sampling strategies (e.g., stratified, weighted) to preserve task distribution.

## Limitations
- The effectiveness of data rehearsal depends heavily on past data availability and quality
- EWC's performance degrades with model complexity due to approximation errors in Taylor series estimation
- The study only evaluates one specific task (PE malware detection) and architecture

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Data rehearsal with 50% past data mixing consistently outperforms other mitigation strategies across all time periods
- **Medium confidence**: The superiority of combined data rehearsal + EWC over individual methods may not generalize to all model architectures or task types
- **Low confidence**: The failure of model averaging and the specific effectiveness of L2 regularization parameters are based on limited experimental sweeps

## Next Checks
1. **Distribution Shift Test**: Simulate past data distribution drift and measure how each mitigation strategy's performance degrades
2. **Model Complexity Scaling**: Apply the same methods to progressively larger models to quantify EWC approximation error growth
3. **Alternative Task Evaluation**: Replicate the experiments on a different sequential learning task to test generalizability beyond malware detection
