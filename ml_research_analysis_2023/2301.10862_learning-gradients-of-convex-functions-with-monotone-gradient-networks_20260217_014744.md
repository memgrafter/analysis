# Learning Gradients of Convex Functions with Monotone Gradient Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.10862
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10862
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Proposes C-MGN and M-MGN architectures that directly learn convex function gradients with PSD Jacobians, achieving 10 dB lower MSE than ICNN with <10% parameters

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces two neural network architectures, C-MGN and M-MGN, designed to directly learn the gradients of convex functions without first learning the underlying convex function or its Hessian. The key innovation is ensuring the network's Jacobian is positive semidefinite by construction, guaranteeing the learned function is a monotone gradient of a convex function. Experiments demonstrate these networks are simpler to train, learn monotone gradient fields more accurately, and use significantly fewer parameters than state-of-the-art methods like ICNN and ICGN.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes two architectures: C-MGN (Cascaded Monotone Gradient Network) and M-MGN (Modular Monotone Gradient Network). Both ensure the Jacobian is positive semidefinite by design through weight-tying constraints and Gram matrix constructions. C-MGN uses cascaded layers with shared weights and a final layer with separate weights, while M-MGN employs multiple parallel subnetworks weighted by convex, non-negative scalar functions. The architectures avoid the training difficulties of ICNNs by using standard activation functions and structural constraints rather than requiring positive weights throughout.

## Key Results
- C-MGN achieved 10 dB lower mean squared error than ICNN on gradient field estimation using less than 10% the parameters
- Both architectures achieved lower negative log-likelihood and transport cost than competing methods on optimal transport problems
- Successfully learned mappings for color domain adaptation in autonomous driving images, producing results comparable to convex relaxation baseline

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The Jacobian of the network is guaranteed to be positive semidefinite by construction, ensuring the learned function is a monotone gradient of a convex function.
- Mechanism: The C-MGN architecture enforces PSD Jacobian through weight-tying: W^T * D * W + V^T * V where D is a diagonal matrix of non-negative activation derivatives.
- Core assumption: The activation functions used (e.g., ReLU, tanh, sigmoid) are monotonically increasing, ensuring their derivatives are non-negative.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The key idea is to ensure the network's Jacobian is positive semidefinite by design, guaranteeing it is a monotone gradient of a convex function."
  - [section] "If σ(·) is a monotonically increasing, element-wise function then each entry of σ′(·)≥ 0. It then follows that the Jacobian can be written as a Gram matrix W⊤D⊤DW, where D=(∑L−1ℓ=0∏L−1ℓ diag[σ′(zℓ)])1/2."
- Break condition: Using activation functions that are not monotonically increasing (e.g., cosine) would violate the non-negativity requirement.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The M-MGN architecture uses multiple parallel subnetworks whose outputs are weighted by convex, non-negative scalar functions.
- Mechanism: Each subnetwork's output is scaled by sk(zk) where sk is convex and non-negative, and σk = ∇sk. The sum of PSD matrices (from V^T*V, sk*Wk^T*Jσk*Wk, and Gram matrices) remains PSD.
- Core assumption: The scalar functions sk(x) are convex and non-negative, and their gradients σk(x) are element-wise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "C-MGN and M-MGN, two monotone gradient neural network architectures for directly learning the gradients of convex functions."
  - [section] "If, for each k, sk(x) is a convex, nonnegative scalar-valued function and σk(x) = ∇sk(x), then ∀x, JM-MGN(x)⪰ 0."
- Break condition: If sk(x) is not convex or becomes negative for some inputs, the PSD property of the Jacobian may be lost.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The network architectures avoid the training difficulties of ICNNs by using weight-tying and standard activation functions.
- Mechanism: Instead of requiring all weights to be positive (as in ICNNs), C-MGN and M-MGN use standard weight matrices and enforce PSD Jacobian through structural constraints, making training easier.
- Core assumption: Standard neural network training techniques (SGD, Adam) work effectively on these constrained architectures.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Compared to state of the art methods, our networks are easier to train, learn monotone gradient fields more accurately, and use significantly fewer parameters."
  - [section] "ICNNs are infamously hard to train [5] as they require most of the weights to be positive and only permit convex, monotonically increasing non-linearity functions."
- Break condition: If the structural constraints introduce new optimization difficulties or if the training process fails to converge.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - C-MGN: x -> W -> σ -> W -> σ -> ... -> W -> σ -> V -> output
  - M-MGN: x -> {Wk, bk, σk, sk} (K parallel modules) -> weighted sum -> output
  - Common: Input x, output gradient estimate, Jacobian PSD guarantee

- Critical path:
  1. Define network architecture (C-MGN or M-MGN)
  2. Implement forward pass with weight-tying constraints
  3. Compute loss (e.g., MSE against true gradient or NLL for OT)
  4. Compute Jacobian of network with respect to input
  5. Verify PSD property (optional, for debugging)
  6. Backpropagate and update weights
  7. Repeat until convergence

- Design tradeoffs:
  - C-MGN: Simpler architecture, potentially harder to train deep networks due to weight sharing
  - M-MGN: More flexible with parallel modules, but requires defining appropriate convex scalar functions sk
  - Both: Must choose activation functions that are monotonically increasing

- Failure signatures:
  - Non-PSD Jacobian (should not happen if implementation is correct)
  - Training instability or divergence (may indicate learning rate issues or poor initialization)
  - Poor generalization despite low training loss (may indicate overfitting or insufficient capacity)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Gradient field estimation: Train on the 2D function f(x) = x₁⁴ + x₂² + x₁x₂² + 3x₂⁴ - x₂³ with MSE loss, compare against ICNN/ICGN
  2. Gaussian optimal transport: Transform 2D Gaussian to standard Gaussian, measure NLL and transport cost
  3. Color domain adaptation: Map daytime to sunset images using single target image, compare against convex relaxation baseline

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of C-MGN and M-MGN scale with problem dimensionality compared to ICNN and ICGN methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that ICGNs are "not feasible for high-dimensional problems" and states that C-MGN and M-MGN are "suitable for high-dimensional problems" but doesn't provide systematic scalability experiments.
- Why unresolved: The paper only shows results for d=2 and d=16 dimensions, which is insufficient to characterize performance trends at scale.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the networks on problems with increasing dimensionality (e.g., d=2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) showing MSE, NLL, and parameter efficiency trends would clarify scalability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between the number of modules K in M-MGN and the expressiveness of the learned gradient field?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper states that K "can be varied as desired or tuned based on the problem setting" but provides no theoretical analysis of how K affects representational capacity.
- Why unresolved: The modular design suggests K should impact expressiveness, but without theoretical bounds or empirical studies varying K systematically, the relationship remains unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical proofs bounding the approximation error as a function of K, combined with empirical studies varying K while measuring performance on fixed problems, would establish this relationship.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do C-MGN and M-MGN perform on non-Gaussian optimal transport problems compared to existing methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper only evaluates on Gaussian distributions, noting that "the Wasserstein distance gives a closed form solution for the optimal cost" which makes these cases tractable but limited.
- Why unresolved: Real-world optimal transport problems rarely involve Gaussian distributions, yet the paper provides no evidence about performance on more complex distributions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing C-MGN and M-MGN to baselines on non-Gaussian distributions (e.g., mixtures, heavy-tailed, or multimodal distributions) would demonstrate practical applicability.

## Limitations
- The restriction to monotonically increasing activation functions limits architectural flexibility
- Computational overhead of Jacobian computation during training
- Need for careful weight-tying implementation to maintain PSD guarantee

## Confidence
