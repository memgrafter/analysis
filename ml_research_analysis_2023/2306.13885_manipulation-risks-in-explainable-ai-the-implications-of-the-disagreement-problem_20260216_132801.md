# Manipulation Risks in Explainable AI: The Implications of the Disagreement Problem

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.13885
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13885
- Reference count: 16
- One-line primary result: The paper identifies how explanation providers can exploit the disagreement problem in XAI to manipulate explanations for their benefit.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores a critical issue in Explainable AI (XAI) known as the disagreement problem, where multiple valid explanations can be generated for the same AI decision. The authors demonstrate how explanation providers could exploit this problem through three main strategies: changing explanation techniques, altering parameters or implementation, and exploiting non-deterministic components. They identify key objectives for such manipulation including mitigating liability, implementing beliefs, and increasing profit. The paper emphasizes the urgent need for regulation and transparency in XAI to prevent misuse and ensure ethical development and deployment of AI systems.

## Method Summary
This paper presents a conceptual framework rather than a technical method. It synthesizes existing knowledge about post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP, LIME, counterfactuals) and the Rashomon effect to construct a theoretical analysis of how explanation providers could manipulate AI explanations. The authors use hypothetical scenarios and examples from various domains (finance, healthcare, employment) to illustrate potential manipulation strategies and their implications. The approach is primarily analytical and normative, proposing mitigation strategies including regulatory frameworks and third-party audits.

## Key Results
- Multiple valid explanations exist for the same AI decision due to the inherent disagreement problem in post-hoc explanation methods
- Explanation providers can manipulate explanations through technique selection, parameter tuning, and exploitation of non-deterministic algorithms
- Three primary manipulation objectives: mitigating liability, implementing beliefs, and increasing profit
- Proposed solutions include government regulation, standardized tools, and independent audits for XAI systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Different explanation techniques and parameter settings can generate divergent explanations for the same AI decision.
- Mechanism: The disagreement problem arises because post-hoc explanation methods like SHAP, LIME, and counterfactuals rely on different assumptions and heuristics to approximate feature importance or decision changes, leading to multiple valid but differing explanations.
- Core assumption: There is no single "true" explanation for a black-box model's decision, so variation in explanations is inherent and exploitable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the disagreement problem, where multiple explanations are possible for the same AI decision or prediction."
  - [section] "The diversity of multiple counterfactual explanations, generated by the same counterfactual algorithm is also known as the Rashomon effect."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; neighbor papers discuss ambiguity and disagreement but not manipulation.
- Break condition: If a regulatory framework mandates a single, standardized explanation method and parameter set, the exploit window narrows significantly.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Explanation providers can manipulate explanations by selecting favorable outcomes from non-deterministic algorithms.
- Mechanism: Some explanation methods (e.g., LIME, DICE) introduce randomness or return multiple explanations; providers can rerun or cherry-pick until a desired explanation appears, all while remaining technically correct.
- Core assumption: Users and auditors do not track or verify the reproducibility of explanations, allowing providers to exploit stochastic outputs.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "certain explanation algorithms are not designed in a deterministic way and may return different explanations across runs."
  - [section] "the randomness introduced during the sampling and perturbation process can lead to variations in the generated explanations for each execution."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence in neighbors; must infer from cited literature.
- Break condition: If explanation generation is made deterministic or if audit logs of algorithm runs are required, manipulation becomes detectable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Providers can shift blame or hide unfairness by selecting explanations that deflect responsibility.
- Mechanism: In adversarial contexts, providers choose explanations that attribute model errors or bias to external factors, user behavior, or unavoidable model uncertainty, thereby avoiding liability.
- Core assumption: Explanation recipients lack the technical expertise or context to challenge the plausibility of the chosen explanation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Explanation providers can also take advantage of the plurality of explanations to shift blame or evade responsibility for controversial or erroneous decisions."
  - [section] "They could selectively present an explanation that attributes the fault to external factors or human error."
  - [corpus] No neighbor evidence; relies on paper's own scenario construction.
- Break condition: If third-party audits compare multiple explanations or if users can request alternative explanations, the manipulation becomes transparent.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data/model training → Explanation algorithm selection → Parameter tuning → Non-deterministic runs → Post-processing selection → User delivery
- Critical path: User request → explanation generation (algorithm + parameters) → selection/filtering → delivery. Manipulation occurs mainly in the selection/filtering step.
- Design tradeoffs: Flexibility in explanation methods vs. consistency and auditability; non-determinism for exploration vs. reproducibility for accountability.
- Failure signatures: Inconsistent explanations across runs, explanations that shift blame away from the model, absence of parameter transparency, unexplained selection criteria.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the same explanation method (e.g., LIME) multiple times on a fixed input and record variation in feature importance rankings.
  2. Compare explanations generated by two different methods (e.g., SHAP vs. LIME) on the same model decision and quantify disagreement.
  3. Simulate a manipulation scenario by generating multiple counterfactual explanations and selectively choosing one that minimizes apparent model bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we effectively detect and prevent fairwashing in XAI systems?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper discusses fairwashing as a potential misuse of XAI and mentions that it has been studied in literature, but it does not provide a comprehensive solution.
- Why unresolved: Fairwashing involves presenting biased or discriminatory AI decisions as fair, which can be challenging to detect, especially when explanation providers manipulate the explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of robust methods to detect and prevent fairwashing, potentially through external audits or regulatory frameworks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we ensure that explanation providers do not manipulate explanations to avoid blame or responsibility for controversial or erroneous AI decisions?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper discusses the potential for explanation providers to shift blame or evade responsibility by manipulating explanations, but it does not provide a solution.
- Why unresolved: This involves ethical considerations and the potential for manipulation at various stages of the explanation generation process, making it difficult to detect and prevent.
- What evidence would resolve it: Establishment of clear guidelines and regulations for explanation generation and sharing, potentially with external audits or oversight.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we balance the need for transparency and explainability in AI systems with the potential for manipulation and misuse of explanations?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper discusses the potential for manipulation and misuse of explanations, but it does not provide a clear solution for balancing transparency and explainability with the need to prevent manipulation.
- Why unresolved: This involves complex ethical and technical considerations, as well as potential trade-offs between transparency and the risk of manipulation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of best practices and guidelines for the use of explanations in AI systems, potentially with input from multiple stakeholders and ongoing evaluation and adaptation.

## Limitations

- The paper presents a largely theoretical framework without empirical validation or quantitative metrics for measuring manipulation risk.
- Claims about the prevalence and impact of explanation manipulation in real-world systems are speculative without supporting evidence.
- The effectiveness of proposed regulatory solutions is untested in practical deployment scenarios.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The existence of the disagreement problem and multiple valid explanations is well-established in XAI literature. The identification of non-deterministic explanation methods is factually accurate.
- **Medium Confidence**: The proposed manipulation strategies are logically sound but lack empirical validation. The effectiveness of regulatory solutions is plausible but untested.
- **Low Confidence**: Claims about the prevalence and impact of explanation manipulation in real-world systems are speculative without supporting evidence.
