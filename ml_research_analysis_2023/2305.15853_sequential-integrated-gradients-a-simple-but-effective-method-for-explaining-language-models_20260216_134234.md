# Sequential Integrated Gradients: a simple but effective method for explaining language models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.15853
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15853
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 6
- Key outcome: Sequential Integrated Gradients (SIG) addresses a limitation of Integrated Gradients (IG) and similar path-based explanation methods when applied to language models: creating interpolated sentences by interpolating all words simultaneously can result in sentences with different meanings from the original.

## Executive Summary
Sequential Integrated Gradients (SIG) is a novel explanation method for language models that addresses a key limitation of existing path-based attribution methods like Integrated Gradients. When applied to language models, traditional methods interpolate all words simultaneously, which can create sentences with different meanings from the original. SIG computes word importance by keeping all other words fixed and only interpolating the target word with a baseline, preserving the original sentence meaning during the explanation process. The paper proposes using the "mask" token as the baseline instead of "pad", aligning with language model training procedures.

## Method Summary
Sequential Integrated Gradients (SIG) computes word importance by interpolating each word individually with a baseline while keeping all other words fixed at their original values. This sequential approach prevents meaning drift that occurs when all words are interpolated simultaneously. The method uses the "mask" token as baseline instead of the commonly used "pad" token, as mask embeddings are trained to represent meaningful word replacements in BERT-style models. For each word, SIG calculates the integrated gradient along the path from the mask-replaced sentence to the original sentence, summing the gradients at each interpolation step and normalizing by the number of steps.

## Key Results
- SIG outperforms IG, DeepLift, GradientShap, Grad*Inp, and DIG on comprehensiveness (Comp) and sufficiency (Suff) metrics across BERT, DistilBERT, and RoBERTa models on SST2, IMDB, and Rotten Tomatoes datasets
- Using mask as baseline significantly improves IG performance compared to using pad
- SIG with 10 interpolation steps achieves better performance than IG with 250 steps, though at higher computational cost
- SIG achieves comparable log-odds (LO) scores to baseline methods while providing better interpretability

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Sequential Integrated Gradients (SIG) improves interpretability by preventing meaning drift during interpolation.
- Mechanism: When computing importance for a word, all other words remain fixed at their original values, creating interpolations only between the baseline and the target word. This prevents the sentence from shifting to an entirely different meaning during the interpolation process.
- Core assumption: Meaning drift occurs when all words are interpolated simultaneously, leading to sentences that diverge significantly from the original meaning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "However, when applied to language models, these methods produce a path for each word of a sentence simultaneously, which could lead to creating sentences from interpolated words either having no clear meaning, or having a significantly different meaning compared to the original sentence."
  - [section 2]: "However, a path-based method applied on such models creates straight lines between each word and a baseline simultaneously. When interpolated points are grouped together to form a sentence, this sentence could have a very different meaning compared with the original one."
- Break condition: If the model's behavior is sufficiently local that small perturbations to individual words don't significantly change sentence meaning, or if the baseline is already very close to the original input.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using the "mask" token as baseline improves attribution quality compared to "pad" because mask embeddings are trained to represent meaningful word replacements.
- Mechanism: The mask token is specifically trained in BERT-style models to replace random words during pretraining, making it an informative baseline that represents "word absence" rather than arbitrary padding. This creates more meaningful interpolations between mask and the actual word.
- Core assumption: Mask embeddings are semantically closer to actual word embeddings than pad embeddings, which are untrained and arbitrary.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2]: "We propose in this study to replace, as the baseline, the commonly used 'pad' token with the 'mask' token, on language models having such token... Because <PAD> (denoting the embedding of 'pad') is untrained, it could be arbitrarily close to some words, and far from others. On the other hand, <MASK> has been trained to replace random words, making it ideally as close to one word as to any other."
- Break condition: For models without mask tokens (like GPT-2), or if the mask embedding is not representative of word absence for the specific task.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: SIG satisfies completeness for individual words, ensuring attributions sum to the difference between model output and baseline for that word.
- Mechanism: For each word i, the sum of its attribution across all features j equals F(x) - F(xi), where xi is the sentence with only that word replaced by mask. This provides a local completeness guarantee.
- Core assumption: The mathematical formulation of SIG preserves the completeness property on a per-word basis.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2]: "SIG follows completeness in a specific way: for each word xi, we have the following result: Xj SIGij(x) = F(x) − F(xi)"
- Break condition: If the model's output is not differentiable with respect to the input, or if the interpolation path doesn't capture the true relationship between word presence and model output.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Tokenization -> Embedding lookup -> Baseline construction (mask replacement) -> Sequential interpolation -> Gradient computation -> Attribution aggregation -> Evaluation
- Critical path: Tokenization → Baseline construction → Interpolation → Gradient computation → Attribution aggregation → Evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: 
  - Computational cost vs. attribution quality (SIG is N times more expensive than IG where N is sequence length)
  - Fixed baseline vs. adaptive baseline (mask vs pad)
  - Sequential vs. parallel computation (tradeoff between memory and speed)
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on short sentences (where meaning drift is less likely)
  - Degraded results when mask embedding is not well-aligned with word embeddings
  - High computational cost for long documents
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify that SIG produces meaningful attributions on a simple sentiment analysis task with known important words
  2. Compare SIG performance using mask vs pad baseline on the same model and dataset
  3. Measure computational overhead of SIG vs IG on varying sequence lengths to identify scaling issues

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does Sequential Integrated Gradients (SIG) preserve symmetry for the overall sentence when using different baselines for each word?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates that SIG preserves symmetry for each word along the embedding dimension, but explicitly states that symmetry does not hold in general due to the choice of baseline.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides a counterexample showing that SIG(x)1 ≠ SIG(x)2 when x1 ≠ x2 even if the model F is symmetric, indicating that overall sentence symmetry is not preserved.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical testing of SIG on symmetric models with various sentence structures to determine if attribution symmetry breaks down in practice for different word combinations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of SIG compare on non-English languages or non-sentiment classification tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge that their experiments were limited to English sentiment analysis tasks using BERT, DistilBERT, and RoBERTa, and explicitly state that results should be validated on more diverse languages and tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested SIG on three similar English-language sentiment datasets (SST2, IMDB, Rotten Tomatoes) using transformer architectures with similar underlying designs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing SIG on diverse language families, different NLP tasks (question answering, named entity recognition, machine translation), and various model architectures to determine generalizability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal number of interpolation steps for SIG when balancing computational efficiency and explanation quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses time complexity issues and shows that reducing interpolation steps decreases performance but SIG with 10 steps still outperforms IG with 250 steps, yet doesn't identify an optimal step count.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates that fewer steps can be used, it doesn't systematically explore the trade-off curve or provide guidance on how to choose the number of steps based on input length or task requirements.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies varying step counts across different sentence lengths, model sizes, and computational resources to identify efficiency-quality trade-offs and provide practical recommendations.

## Limitations

- Computational scalability: SIG is N times more expensive than IG where N is sequence length, creating uncertainty about practical applicability to long-form text
- Evaluation metric dependencies: The comprehensiveness and sufficiency metrics depend on the arbitrary choice of 20% feature masking threshold without exploring sensitivity to this parameter
- Model coverage: The method is only evaluated on BERT-style models with mask tokens, not addressing how SIG would work for autoregressive models like GPT-2

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The core mechanism of sequential interpolation (keeping other words fixed) is mathematically sound and the experimental results show consistent improvements over baselines. The improvement from using mask vs pad baseline is clearly demonstrated.

**Medium confidence**: The superiority of SIG over other explanation methods (DeepLift, GradientShap, etc.) is demonstrated on the specific datasets and models tested, but generalizability to other tasks, domains, or model architectures remains uncertain.

**Low confidence**: The paper's claims about interpretability improvements are based on proxy metrics (Comp, Suff, LO) rather than human evaluation studies. The actual improvement in human understanding of model decisions is not directly measured.
