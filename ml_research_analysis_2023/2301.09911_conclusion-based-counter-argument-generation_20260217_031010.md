# Conclusion-based Counter-Argument Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09911
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09911
- Reference count: 15
- Primary result: Multitask learning approach with stance-based ranking outperforms baselines in counter-argument generation, especially for implicit conclusions

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses counter-argument generation by hypothesizing that explicit modeling of an argument's conclusion is key to producing relevant and stance-opposing counters. The proposed multitask approach jointly learns to generate conclusions and counters using BART-based models, employing a stance-based ranking component to select the most contrastive counter from diverse candidates. Evaluated on the ChangeMyView dataset, the approach outperforms strong baselines in both automatic metrics (BERT F1-score up to 0.889, stance contrastiveness up to 0.661) and human evaluation. The results demonstrate that modeling conclusions, particularly when implicit, significantly improves counter-argument quality.

## Method Summary
The approach uses BART-based models to jointly generate conclusions and counter-arguments, with two variants: Joint One-seq (shared encoder-decoder) and Joint Two-seq (shared encoder, separate decoders). At inference, diverse counters are generated using nucleus sampling and ranked using a stance classifier trained on Kialo data to select the most contrastive counter. Wikipedia concepts extracted from premises are used to prompt generation for increased diversity. The approach is evaluated on ChangeMyView data with automatic metrics (BLEU, BERT F1, stance contrastiveness) and human evaluation of relevance, stance correctness, and conclusion validity.

## Key Results
- Multitask learning outperforms pipeline baselines, with Joint One-seq achieving BERT F1 of 0.889 vs 0.856 for pipeline approach
- Stance-based ranking improves counter quality, achieving stance contrastiveness of 0.661 vs 0.594 for pipeline
- Manual evaluation shows Joint One-seq with stance ranking achieves best average rank (2.26) for relevance and stance correctness
- Multitask learning produces more valid conclusions (average validity 2.03/3.0) than pipeline baselines (1.50/3.0)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Jointly modeling conclusion and counter-argument generation improves stance correctness compared to pipeline approaches.
- Mechanism: The multitask approach shares encoder layers between conclusion and counter generation tasks, allowing the model to learn representations that capture the relationship between premises, conclusion, and counter-argument simultaneously.
- Core assumption: The conclusion contains crucial information for generating a relevant counter-argument, and learning to generate both jointly helps the model capture this relationship better than treating them as separate tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we hypothesize that the key to effective counter-argument generation is to explicitly model the argument's conclusion and to ensure that the stance of the generated counter is opposite to that conclusion"
  - [section 3.1] "we propose an approach that jointly learns to generate both the conclusion and the counter of an argument"
- Break condition: If the conclusion generation task becomes too difficult or noisy, it could hurt counter-argument quality through negative transfer in the shared encoder.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Stance-based ranking component ensures generated counters have correct stance towards the conclusion.
- Mechanism: After generating multiple candidate counters, a stance classifier evaluates each counter's stance towards the generated conclusion. Counters are ranked by their contrastiveness score, selecting the one with stance most opposite to the conclusion.
- Core assumption: Even if the generation model sometimes produces counters with incorrect stance, a separate stance classifier can reliably identify and rank the most contrastive counters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The approach employs a stance-based ranking component that selects the counter from a diverse set of generated candidates whose stance best opposes the generated conclusion"
  - [section 3.2] "we rank them based on their stance contrastiveness towards the corresponding generated conclusion and select the top-ranked as our final output"
- Break condition: If the stance classifier is unreliable or biased, the ranking component could select poor counters or introduce systematic errors.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Prompting generation with Wikipedia concepts increases diversity of generated conclusions and counters.
- Mechanism: For the Joint One-seq model, relevant Wikipedia concepts are extracted from premises and used as prompts during decoding. This encourages the model to generate conclusions addressing different aspects of the premises, leading to more diverse candidate counters after ranking.
- Core assumption: The model can effectively use external concept prompts to guide generation toward different argumentative directions while maintaining coherence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "we used Project Debater's API that we used to prompt the output sequence (conclusion and counter-argument) to encourage diversity"
  - [section 3.2] "we use these concepts to prompt our trained model by masking all logits except the ones matching the prompt tokens"
- Break condition: If concept extraction is poor or prompts confuse the model, generation quality could degrade significantly.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture
  - Why needed here: The approach relies on BART, a transformer-based sequence-to-sequence model, for both generation and classification tasks. Understanding how transformers work is essential for modifying the architecture and interpreting results.
  - Quick check question: How does the attention mechanism in transformers help capture long-range dependencies in argumentative text?

- Concept: Multitask learning and parameter sharing
  - Why needed here: The approach experiments with sharing encoder layers between conclusion and counter generation. Understanding how parameter sharing affects learning and potential interference is crucial for interpreting the results and designing variations.
  - Quick check question: What are the trade-offs between sharing encoder vs decoder layers in multitask generation models?

- Concept: Stance classification and contrastiveness scoring
  - Why needed here: The ranking component relies on a stance classifier to score how contrastive each counter is to the conclusion. Understanding how stance classifiers work and how to define/measure contrastiveness is essential for implementing and evaluating this component.
  - Quick check question: How would you define and compute a contrastiveness score between two argumentative statements?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Premises -> BART-based model -> Generated conclusion and counters -> Stance classifier -> Ranked counters -> Final output
- Critical path: 1. Extract Wikipedia concepts from premises 2. Generate diverse conclusions and counters 3. Split counters into sentences 4. Classify stance of each sentence towards conclusion 5. Compute contrastiveness scores and rank candidates 6. Select top-ranked counter as final output
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Shared vs separate decoders: Joint One-seq shares both encoder and decoder, Joint Two-seq only shares encoder. Sharing more parameters may help learn task relationships but could also cause interference.
  - Prompting vs no prompting: Using Wikipedia concepts increases diversity but adds complexity and dependency on external API.
  - Stance classifier quality: The effectiveness of ranking depends heavily on the stance classifier's accuracy and reliability.
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor conclusion quality → irrelevant or off-topic counters
  - Stance classifier errors → counters with wrong stance selected
  - Concept extraction failures → poor prompting and limited diversity
  - Overfitting to training data → poor generalization to new arguments
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train Joint One-seq model on training data, evaluate BLEU/BERT F1 on validation set, check if conclusion generation is reasonable
  2. Test stance classifier on held-out Kialo pairs, verify it achieves target F1-score (~0.8)
  3. Run inference on sample validation arguments, manually inspect generated conclusions and counters for quality and diversity

## Limitations

- The approach heavily depends on stance classifier quality for ranking, which could fail if the classifier has systematic biases or poor accuracy on CMV data
- The Wikipedia concept prompting mechanism adds complexity and external dependencies, but its specific contribution to performance is not empirically validated
- Manual evaluation was conducted on a relatively small sample (50 arguments) that may not capture the full range of argumentative scenarios, particularly for implicit conclusions

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core finding that multitask learning improves both conclusion validity (2.03 vs 1.50 average score) and counter quality (2.26 vs 2.80 average rank) compared to pipeline approaches is well-supported by both automatic metrics and human evaluation.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that stance-based ranking significantly improves counter quality depends heavily on the stance classifier's performance, which is validated on Kialo but not directly on CMV data.

**Low Confidence**: The effectiveness of the Wikipedia concept prompting mechanism for increasing diversity is the weakest claim, as there is no direct comparison between models with and without prompting.

## Next Checks

1. **Stance classifier domain adaptation**: Evaluate the stance classifier's performance specifically on CMV data using a held-out validation set. If performance drops significantly from the Kialo benchmark, fine-tune the classifier on a small sample of CMV data to assess whether domain adaptation improves ranking quality.

2. **Ablation study for prompting mechanism**: Train and evaluate a Joint One-seq model without the Wikipedia concept prompting to quantify the contribution of this component to overall performance. Compare both automatic metrics (diversity measures, BERT F1) and manual evaluation results to isolate the prompting effect.

3. **Error analysis on implicit vs explicit conclusions**: Perform detailed error analysis on the 50 manually evaluated arguments, specifically examining cases where conclusions are implicit versus explicit. This would validate the claim that the approach is particularly effective for implicit conclusions and identify specific failure patterns for each case.
