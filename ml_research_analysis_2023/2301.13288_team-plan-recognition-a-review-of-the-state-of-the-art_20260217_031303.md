# Team Plan Recognition: A Review of the State of the Art

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.13288
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13288
- Reference count: 0
- This review surveys recent logic-based approaches to team plan recognition (TPR), a critical AI capability for understanding coordinated group behavior.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a comprehensive review of logic-based approaches to team plan recognition (TPR), where the goal is to infer latent structures and dependencies within team actions toward shared objectives. The review focuses on two primary logic-based methodologies: plan-library based methods that use pre-constructed data structures to represent team plans, and domain-theory based methods that leverage automated planners to reconstruct observed plans. The authors identify key challenges including computational complexity with multiple agents, handling incomplete or noisy observations, and the need for more online TPR methods.

## Method Summary
The review synthesizes existing literature on logic-based TPR approaches, categorizing them into plan-library based methods that frame TPR as an exact cover problem and domain-theory based methods that treat TPR as planning. Plan-library approaches typically employ modified search algorithms like Knuth's algorithm X to find exact solutions, while domain-theory approaches leverage various planner types including epistemic planning to handle belief modeling. The review draws from academic publications spanning several decades to provide a historical perspective and identify trends in TPR research.

## Key Results
- Logic-based methods dominate TPR research due to superior expressiveness for complex relational structures
- Plan-library approaches solve TPR by reducing to exact cover instances, typically using modified search algorithms
- Domain-theory approaches leverage automated planners, with epistemic planning enabling modeling of agents' beliefs and uncertainty
- Computational complexity remains a significant challenge, particularly with multiple agents and noisy observations
- Most TPR methods are offline, with limited online/real-time capabilities

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Logic-based methods dominate TPR research due to historical precedent and superior expressiveness for complex relational structures.
- Mechanism: Logic-based approaches use symbolic reasoning and structured knowledge representations (e.g., first-order logic, PDDL) to model team plans, goals, and dependencies, which aligns with how TPR problems are formally defined.
- Core assumption: TPR requires capturing hierarchical, temporal, and belief-based relationships that are difficult to represent with statistical learning alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "logic-based methods are the most prevalent" and "plan recognition has been continually studied using mainly logic-based methodologies"
  - [section] "machine learning and deep learning methods are generally incapable of representing highly complex logical structures"
  - [corpus] Weak: neighbor papers focus on unrelated topics (fuzzy scheduling, ChatGPT survey, gesture recognition), providing no direct support for TPR logic-based dominance.
- Break condition: If new datasets or problems emerge where deep learning models significantly outperform logic-based methods in accuracy and scalability, the dominance claim would weaken.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Plan-library based methods solve TPR by reducing the problem to an exact cover instance.
- Mechanism: Observations are matched against a pre-constructed library of plan templates; the problem becomes finding a subset of templates that exactly covers the observed action sequence, often solved via search algorithms like Knuth's algorithm X.
- Core assumption: Team plans can be effectively represented as combinations of pre-defined sub-plan templates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Most plan-library based approaches specify their problem as a particular case of the exact cover (EC) problem"
  - [section] "Banerjee, Kraemer, and Lyle (2010) find an exact solution by modifying... algorithm X"
  - [corpus] Weak: no corpus papers discuss exact cover or plan-library approaches.
- Break condition: If real-world team plans frequently deviate from library templates or if missing/noisy observations become severe, exact cover formulations become brittle.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Domain-theory based methods leverage automated planners to reconstruct observed plans, allowing richer modeling of beliefs and uncertainty.
- Mechanism: Planners generate candidate plans that explain observations; epistemic planning extends this to model agents' beliefs, enabling recognition even with incomplete or uncertain knowledge.
- Core assumption: Reconstructing plans via planning algorithms captures the causal structure of team behavior better than matching templates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Domain-theory based approaches are generative methods that attempt to reconstruct the observed plan using an automated planner"
  - [section] "Shvo et al. (2020) use epistemic planning... to model the agents' belief systems and knowledge acquisition"
  - [corpus] Weak: corpus neighbors do not cover epistemic planning or plan reconstruction.
- Break condition: If planning algorithms become too slow for real-time use or if planner expressivity does not match the complexity of team interactions, this approach loses effectiveness.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Plan recognition as parsing
  - Why needed here: Many plan-library based TPR methods frame the problem as parsing an observed action sequence against a grammar of team plans, enabling efficient search and inference.
  - Quick check question: How does parsing a sequence of actions against a plan grammar differ from matching against a flat list of templates?
- Concept: Epistemic planning
  - Why needed here: Domain-theory based TPR methods use epistemic planning to model agents' beliefs and knowledge states, crucial for understanding team behavior under uncertainty.
  - Quick check question: In what way does epistemic planning extend classical planning to handle belief and knowledge?
- Concept: Exact cover problem
  - Why needed here: Plan-library based TPR methods often reduce the recognition task to an exact cover problem, where observations must be exactly partitioned by selected plan templates.
  - Quick check question: What makes the TPR problem an instance of exact cover, and why is this formulation useful?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Observation parser -> Knowledge base -> Inference engine -> Output formatter
- Critical path:
  1. Parse observations → 2. Match/query knowledge base → 3. Run inference algorithm → 4. Generate explanations → 5. Present results
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Plan-library vs. domain-theory: Libraries are fast but inflexible; planners are versatile but computationally heavier
  - Offline vs. online: Offline allows full trace analysis; online supports real-time assistance but must handle partial data
  - Noise handling: Some methods support missing/noisy observations; others assume clean data, affecting robustness
- Failure signatures:
  - High false positives when library templates are too generic
  - Timeout or memory errors in planner-based methods on large or complex scenarios
  - Inability to recognize novel plan structures not in the library
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test exact cover solver on a small synthetic team plan dataset with known ground truth
  2. Run epistemic planner-based recognition on a scenario with belief changes and missing observations
  3. Compare parsing-based vs. planner-based TPR on the same noisy observation trace to evaluate accuracy and runtime

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the computational complexities of plan-library based methods compare to domain-theory based methods for TPR, and what specific factors most significantly impact runtime performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that domain-theory based methods are often more computationally complex than plan-library based methods, with Shvo et al. (2020) and Shvo, Sohrabi, and McIlraith (2018) admitting their implementations became increasingly prone to "timing out" as scenario complexity increased
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies this as a general trend, it doesn't provide direct empirical comparisons of runtime performance between the two approaches across multiple problem scenarios, nor does it analyze which specific factors (number of agents, plan complexity, etc.) have the most significant impact
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing runtime performance of representative methods from both classes across varying numbers of agents, plan lengths, and observation noise levels, with detailed profiling of computational bottlenecks

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of integrating team communication analysis into TPR systems, and how does it affect accuracy compared to methods using only action observations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies this as a future research direction, noting that "Combining concepts from these works with the techniques seen in this review could produce powerful new approaches to handle more realistic and complex team scenarios"
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions related work on communication-based plan recognition but doesn't empirically compare the performance of communication-aware TPR systems against traditional action-only methods
- What evidence would resolve it: Head-to-head comparison of TPR accuracy and robustness when using only action observations versus systems that also incorporate team communication data, tested across scenarios with varying levels of communication availability and noise

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the efficiency of epistemic planning-based TPR approaches be improved to handle real-time scenarios with many agents and complex belief structures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that epistemic planning approaches are among the most versatile but also mentions they "became increasingly more prone to 'timing out' as the complexity of their test scenarios increased"
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the versatility of epistemic planning, it doesn't explore specific techniques for improving computational efficiency or handling real-time constraints
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and evaluation of optimization techniques (such as belief structure compression, heuristic guidance, or approximation methods) that enable epistemic planning TPR to scale to real-time scenarios with multiple agents and complex belief structures

## Limitations
- The review's claim that logic-based methods dominate TPR is primarily supported by citation patterns rather than empirical performance comparisons
- Neighboring papers in the corpus do not engage with TPR or logic-based approaches, suggesting limited community interest or narrow review scope
- The review does not provide quantitative evidence comparing logic-based TPR methods against emerging machine learning alternatives

## Confidence
- High confidence: Logic-based methods are prevalent in TPR literature (supported by systematic review of TPR publications)
- Medium confidence: Plan-library approaches reduce TPR to exact cover problems (methodologically clear but limited empirical validation)
- Low confidence: Logic-based methods are superior for TPR due to expressiveness (lacks comparative performance data)
