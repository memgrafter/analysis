# Revisiting Discriminative vs. Generative Classifiers: Theory and Implications

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02334
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02334
- Reference count: 40
- This paper shows that multiclass naive Bayes requires O(log n) samples to approach asymptotic error while multiclass logistic regression requires O(n) samples.

## Executive Summary
This paper revisits the classical comparison between discriminative and generative classifiers, extending theoretical analysis to deep representation learning contexts. The authors improve upon classical theory by considering surrogate losses and generalizing from binary to multiclass settings. Through rigorous mathematical analysis, they establish that under mild assumptions, naive Bayes classifiers can achieve comparable performance with exponentially fewer samples than logistic regression classifiers. The theoretical findings are supported by experiments using pre-trained deep vision models, demonstrating faster convergence of naive Bayes as data increases.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a theoretical framework analyzing sample complexity differences between generative and discriminative classifiers. The authors develop a multiclass H-consistency bound framework and derive explicit bounds for logistic loss. They prove that naive Bayes requires O(log n) samples to approach asymptotic error while logistic regression requires O(n) samples, where n is feature dimension. The theoretical analysis assumes feature independence for naive Bayes and mild regularity conditions for both approaches. Experiments validate these theoretical predictions using pre-trained deep vision models on various datasets.

## Key Results
- Under mild assumptions, multiclass naive Bayes requires O(log n) samples to approach asymptotic error
- Corresponding multiclass logistic regression requires O(n) samples for similar performance
- Experiments show naive Bayes consistently converges faster than logistic regression as data increases
- Results suggest fundamental differences between representations learned by supervised and self-supervised methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The sample complexity advantage of naive Bayes stems from its generative modeling approach that captures joint data distributions. By modeling P(X,Y) rather than just P(Y|X), naive Bayes can leverage the structure of the data distribution more efficiently. The exponential sample complexity gap arises because naive Bayes can learn the underlying data structure with fewer samples when the naive independence assumption approximately holds. This advantage becomes particularly pronounced in high-dimensional spaces where the feature dimension n grows large.

## Foundational Learning
- Multiclass logistic regression: A discriminative classifier modeling P(Y|X) directly, needed for understanding the baseline approach being compared
- Naive Bayes classifier: A generative classifier modeling P(X,Y), fundamental to the theoretical analysis
- H-consistency bounds: Framework for analyzing learning algorithm consistency, critical for establishing theoretical results
- Surrogate loss functions: Loss functions used in place of zero-one loss for tractability, important for extending classical theory
- Sample complexity analysis: Study of how many samples are needed for learning, central to the paper's theoretical contributions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but implicit questions include how these theoretical results extend to other classifier families, the impact of approximate feature independence in practical scenarios, and whether similar sample complexity gaps exist for other generative-discriminative pairs.

## Limitations
- Strong assumption of feature independence in naive Bayes may not hold in practice
- Experiments limited to pre-trained deep vision models, potentially biasing results
- Focus on image classification tasks limits generalizability to other domains
- Theoretical bounds may not fully capture practical performance differences
