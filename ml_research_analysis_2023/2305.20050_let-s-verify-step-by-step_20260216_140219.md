# Let's Verify Step by Step

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.20050
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050
- Reference count: 33
- A process-supervised reward model solves 78% of problems from a representative MATH test set subset

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the effectiveness of process supervision versus outcome supervision for training reward models on mathematical reasoning tasks. Process supervision provides feedback on each intermediate step of a solution, while outcome supervision only evaluates the final answer. The authors demonstrate that process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision, with a model trained using this approach achieving a 78% success rate on a subset of the MATH test set. Additionally, they introduce active learning to improve data collection efficiency, achieving a 2.6x improvement in data utilization. The study also releases PRM800K, a dataset of 800,000 step-level human feedback labels.

## Method Summary
The authors generate step-by-step solutions to MATH problems using a base model (GPT-4), then train reward models to predict correctness at either the final answer (outcome supervision) or individual steps (process supervision). Human labelers provide step-level feedback on solution correctness, creating the PRM800K dataset. An active learning approach is used to select the most informative solutions for labeling, focusing on cases where the reward model rates a solution highly but the final answer is incorrect. Small-scale models are trained using synthetic supervision from a large reward model to enable controlled comparisons.

## Key Results
- Process supervision achieves 78.2% success rate on representative MATH test set subset
- Process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision across all evaluation metrics
- Active learning improves data efficiency by approximately 2.6x for process supervision

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Process supervision provides more precise feedback than outcome supervision by specifying the exact location of errors in multi-step reasoning.
- Mechanism: By labeling each intermediate step as correct or incorrect, the reward model receives detailed information about where reasoning goes wrong, rather than just knowing the final answer is wrong.
- Core assumption: Intermediate step correctness can be reliably determined by human labelers.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision"
  - [section 6.1]: "A reward model trained with outcome supervision faces a difficult credit-assignment task"
  - [corpus]: "Average neighbor FMR=0.44" - moderate similarity suggests this is a recognized but not dominant approach in literature
- Break condition: If step-level correctness becomes ambiguous or subjective, the precision advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Active learning improves data efficiency by 2.6x by focusing human feedback on the most informative examples.
- Mechanism: The system selects solutions that are highly rated by the current reward model but have incorrect final answers, targeting cases where the model is most likely to be wrong.
- Core assumption: The current reward model can identify solutions that are "convincing wrong-answers" worth human review.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we show that active learning significantly improves the efficacy of process supervision"
  - [section 4.2]: "we estimate that this form of active learning is approximately 2.6x more data efficient"
  - [corpus]: Missing strong direct evidence - this appears to be a novel contribution
- Break condition: If the reward model becomes too confident in incorrect patterns, it may miss important failure modes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using a large reward model to supervise smaller models enables efficient synthetic supervision and ablation studies.
- Mechanism: The large PRM acts as an oracle to label steps for smaller models, avoiding the need for expensive human labeling while maintaining high quality supervision.
- Core assumption: The large PRM's step-level judgments are reliable enough to serve as ground truth for training smaller models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4]: "we use a large reward model to supervise small-scale model training"
  - [section 4.1]: "we determine the labels for individual steps based on the step-level probabilities outputted by PRM large"
  - [corpus]: "Uncertainty-Aware Step-wise Verification with Generative Reward Models" suggests related approaches exist
- Break condition: If the large PRM has systematic biases, these will propagate to all supervised models.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Generator -> Solution generation -> PRM scoring -> Best solution selection -> Final answer verification
- Critical path: Generator → Solution generation → PRM scoring → Best solution selection → Final answer verification
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Precision vs. cost: Process supervision is more precise but requires expensive human labeling
  - Scale vs. supervision type: Large-scale experiments use GPT-4, while small-scale allows controlled comparisons
  - Active learning vs. uniform sampling: Active learning is more efficient but may introduce bias
- Failure signatures:
  - ORM outperforming PRM on certain problem types suggests domain where step-level errors don't propagate

  - Instability in iterative PRM selector training suggests active learning implementation issues

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of process supervision compare to outcome supervision in domains other than math?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper states that process supervision is under-explored and that future work should investigate its impact in other domains. It also mentions that process supervision is safer and more interpretable, but this needs to be validated in different contexts.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses specifically on the MATH dataset, which is a challenging but narrow domain. The benefits of process supervision in other areas of reasoning or problem-solving are unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies applying process supervision to other datasets (e.g., coding, scientific reasoning, or logical puzzles) with direct comparisons to outcome supervision would clarify its generalizability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal trade-off between the number of solutions generated per problem and the use of active learning for data collection?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses active learning improving data efficiency by 2.6x, but also notes that generating too many samples can reduce diversity and limit active learning benefits. It mentions instability in iterative retraining of the selector model.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides preliminary results on active learning but does not fully explore the balance between sample quantity, diversity, and active learning effectiveness. The impact of iterative retraining remains unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the number of generated samples, the fraction selected for labeling, and the frequency of retraining the selector model would clarify optimal strategies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How robust is the PRM to subtle forms of test set contamination, and what are the implications for its real-world applicability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges the possibility of test set contamination due to online problem discussions but argues that the relative comparisons between methods should remain unaffected. It also notes that the PRM finds correct solutions to problems with low generator pass-rates, suggesting minimal contamination.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide quantitative evidence of contamination levels or its impact on performance. The generalizability of the PRM to unseen problems is also not fully established.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of the overlap between training and test data, along with experiments on entirely new problem sets, would clarify the extent of contamination and its effects.

## Limitations
- Process supervision requires expensive human labeling of individual steps, limiting scalability
- The 78% success rate leaves 22% of problems unsolved, indicating fundamental limitations
- Active learning effectiveness depends heavily on the quality of the initial reward model

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Process supervision outperforms outcome supervision on the MATH dataset (supported by direct experimental comparison)
- **Medium confidence**: Active learning provides 2.6x data efficiency improvement (estimates provided but not extensively validated across different problem distributions)
- **Medium confidence**: PRM800K dataset quality and representativeness (dataset described but limited independent validation)
