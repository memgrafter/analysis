# Language acquisition: do children and language models follow similar learning stages?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03586
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03586
- Reference count: 27
- Children and language models show some similarities in the order of acquiring linguistic skills

## Executive Summary
This study compares the learning trajectories of language models to those of children during language acquisition. The authors train 48 variants of GPT-2 from scratch and evaluate their linguistic abilities at each training step using 96 syntactic and semantic probes. They then compare a subset of these probes to the behavior of 54 children aged 18-71 months. The key findings are: (1) Language models learn linguistic skills in a systematic order, similar to children. (2) This learning scheme is parallel: all linguistic tasks improve from the beginning of training, but at different rates. (3) Some, but not all, learning stages are shared between children and the language models.

## Method Summary
The authors train 48 GPT-2 models (12-layer, 774M parameters) with different random seeds on WikiText103. They evaluate the models on 96 linguistic probes every 100 training steps, measuring accuracy via zero-shot acceptability judgments. Acquisition time is defined as the steps needed to reach 90% of final accuracy. The order of acquisition is compared across models using rank correlation. A subset of probe results is compared to child language data from 54 Hebrew-speaking children aged 18-71 months. Learning rates are analyzed to determine if acquisition is parallel or sequential.

## Key Results
- Language models acquire linguistic skills in a systematic, non-random order correlated across different training runs (R = 0.743, p < 0.001)
- Linguistic skill acquisition is parallel: all skills begin improving from early training steps but at different rates
- Some acquisition stages are shared between children and models (e.g., subject-verb agreement in simple sentences before questions)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Language models acquire linguistic abilities in a systematic, non-random order during training.
- Mechanism: The models progressively improve on linguistic probes in a consistent sequence, reflecting a structured learning trajectory where certain skills are mastered before others.
- Core assumption: The training data and architecture induce an implicit hierarchy of linguistic complexity, causing models to learn simpler structures before more complex ones.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The key findings are: (1) Language models learn linguistic skills in a systematic order, similar to children."
  - [section 3.1] "We perform the permutation analysis outlined in 2.3, to evaluate whether the order of acquisition is shared between models, and find that their order of acquisition is correlated with R = 0.743 and p < 0.001."
  - [corpus] Weak: Corpus contains related works but no direct evidence of systematic learning order in models.
- Break condition: If training data is heavily biased toward certain structures or if random initialization dominates, the systematic order may not emerge or be consistent.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Linguistic skill acquisition in language models is parallel rather than strictly sequential.
- Mechanism: All linguistic abilities begin improving from early training steps, but at different rates; no skill waits for another to be fully mastered before starting to improve.
- Core assumption: The model's architecture and optimization allow simultaneous learning of multiple linguistic features, even if some are acquired faster than others.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2] "Overall, we observe parallel learning between the three groups: their performances all increase from the beginning of training: 95% of tests in all three groups have a positive derivative within the first three hundred steps."
  - [figure 3 caption] "This figure demonstrates that linguistic skills are learned in parallel not in sequence."
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct corpus evidence, but related works on child language acquisition suggest parallel skill emergence.
- Break condition: If the training objective or data distribution strongly favors one skill type, learning may become more sequential.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Some, but not all, stages of linguistic skill acquisition are shared between children and language models.
- Mechanism: For a subset of syntactic skills (e.g., subject-verb agreement in simple sentences before questions or relative clauses), both children and models follow the same order, while other skills diverge.
- Core assumption: There are universal or domain-general constraints on the order of linguistic acquisition that both biological and artificial learners encounter.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Some, but not all, learning stages are shared between children and the language models."
  - [section 3.3] "For this subset of language abilities, models and children thus seem to acquire syntactic skills in a similar order."
  - [corpus] Weak: Corpus contains related works but no direct evidence of shared acquisition stages.
- Break condition: If models rely heavily on heuristics rather than true syntactic understanding, or if training data differs greatly from child-directed input, the shared stages may not hold.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Zero-shot linguistic probes
  - Why needed here: To evaluate linguistic abilities of models at each training step without requiring model retraining or fine-tuning.
  - Quick check question: How does a zero-shot probe measure whether a model understands a linguistic phenomenon?
- Concept: Acquisition time and rank correlation
  - Why needed here: To quantify and compare the order in which models learn different linguistic skills, and to test if this order is consistent across models.
  - Quick check question: What does a high rank correlation between models' acquisition times indicate about their learning trajectories?
- Concept: Parallel vs sequential learning schemes
  - Why needed here: To determine whether all linguistic skills improve simultaneously (but at different rates) or if complex skills only start improving after simpler ones are mastered.
  - Quick check question: How can you tell from a learning curve whether acquisition is parallel or sequential?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - GPT-2 (12-layer) architecture -> WikiText103 dataset for training -> Zero-shot probes from BLiMP, Zorro, BIG-Bench -> Evaluation pipeline (perplexity, accuracy on probes) -> Statistical analysis (rank correlation, ANOVA)
- Critical path:
  1. Initialize 48 GPT-2 models with different seeds
  2. Train each model on WikiText103
  3. At regular intervals, evaluate all models on 96 linguistic probes
  4. Compute acquisition time for each probe (steps to 90% final accuracy)
  5. Rank probes by acquisition time per model
  6. Correlate ranks across models to test systematicity
  7. Group probes and analyze learning rates for parallel vs sequential learning
  8. Compare model results with child language acquisition data
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a single architecture and dataset limits generalizability but allows controlled comparison
  - Evaluating at fixed intervals may miss rapid changes between checkpoints
  - Grouping probes for analysis reduces noise but may obscure individual probe dynamics
- Failure signatures:
  - Low or no correlation in acquisition ranks across models (lack of systematicity)
  - All probes improving at the same rate (no differentiation, parallel learning not detected)
  - Models failing to reach above-chance performance on most probes (inadequate learning)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a single GPT-2 model and plot probe accuracy over time to visualize learning curves.
  2. Compute acquisition time and rank probes for one model, then repeat for another model and correlate ranks.
  3. Group probes by final accuracy and analyze their learning rates to test for parallel vs sequential acquisition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the specific computational principles that lead to the systematic learning trajectory observed in both children and language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that the computational principles leading to the learning trajectory in children remain largely unknown, and aims to investigate this through comparison with language models.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies similarities in learning trajectories, it does not delve into the underlying computational mechanisms driving this systematic order of skill acquisition.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of the model architectures and training data, coupled with controlled experiments varying these factors, could reveal the computational principles governing the learning trajectory.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent do frequency and complexity of linguistic phenomena in natural language influence their acquisition order in both children and language models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the acquisition time of linguistic phenomena might relate to their frequency or intrinsic complexity in natural language, but does not explicitly investigate this.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not systematically control for the frequency of linguistic structures in the training data or their complexity, making it difficult to isolate the factors influencing acquisition order.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments manipulating the frequency and complexity of linguistic structures in the training data, while controlling for other variables, could elucidate their impact on acquisition order.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different neural network architectures and training data impact the learning trajectory of language models, and how does this compare to the trajectory observed in children?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that its findings are based on a single architecture (GPT-2) and a unique textual corpus (WikiText103), and calls for further research to test the generalizability of the results.
- Why unresolved: The paper only examines one architecture and training corpus, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other language models and training data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic comparison of learning trajectories across various architectures (e.g., transformers, LSTMs) and training data (e.g., different corpora, sizes) could reveal the impact of these factors and their alignment with human learning trajectories.

## Limitations
- The study uses a single architecture (GPT-2) and dataset (WikiText103), limiting generalizability to other language models and training conditions
- Comparison with children's data is restricted to a subset of syntactic abilities and a specific age range, potentially missing broader developmental patterns
- The zero-shot probe evaluation method may not capture all nuances of linguistic understanding

## Confidence

- **High Confidence:** Language models learn linguistic skills in a systematic, non-random order during training. This is strongly supported by the high rank correlation (R = 0.743, p < 0.001) across 48 model variants and the consistent patterns observed in acquisition curves.

- **Medium Confidence:** Linguistic skill acquisition in language models is parallel rather than strictly sequential. While the data shows all skills improve from early training steps, the exact degree of parallelism versus sequentiality is difficult to quantify precisely.

- **Medium Confidence:** Some stages of linguistic acquisition are shared between children and language models. The study demonstrates shared stages for specific syntactic skills, but the extent and generality of these parallels remain uncertain.

## Next Checks

1. **Generalization to Other Architectures:** Replicate the study using different language model architectures (e.g., transformers with varying depths, recurrent neural networks) and training datasets to assess whether the observed systematic learning order is architecture-dependent or more universal.

2. **Fine-grained Analysis of Parallel vs Sequential Learning:** Conduct a more detailed analysis of individual probe trajectories to determine if there are subtle sequential dependencies masked by the overall parallel trend, perhaps by examining second-order derivatives of learning curves.

3. **Broader Comparison with Child Language Data:** Extend the comparison to include a wider range of linguistic abilities and developmental stages in children, using diverse child language corpora, to better understand the extent and limitations of parallels between human and machine language acquisition.
