# Automatic Assessment of Oral Reading Accuracy for Reading Diagnostics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03444
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03444
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Kaldi system with LM containing reading prompts and manual transcriptions achieved highest agreement with human evaluations (MCC = .63)

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates six automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems for assessing Dutch oral reading accuracy in children. The research compares four Kaldi systems with different language models and two Whisper variants using the JASMIN corpus of 71 children aged 7-11 reading 1455 sentences. Results show that including reading errors in the language model significantly improves assessment performance, with the best system achieving a Matthews Correlation Coefficient of .63 when compared to human evaluations. The study also demonstrates that forced decoding confidence scores correlate moderately well with word correctness, suggesting potential for automated reading diagnostics while identifying areas for future improvement.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates six ASR systems on the JASMIN corpus containing Dutch child speech data with manual orthographic transcriptions. Four Kaldi systems were trained using the same adult speech acoustic model but different language models: one with general-purpose CGN data, one with only reading prompts, one with only manual orthographic transcriptions, and one combining both. Two Whisper systems (with and without prompt hints) were also evaluated. Performance was measured using ADAPT alignment for error detection agreement with human assessments (MCC, precision, recall, F1, specificity) and forced decoding confidence scores correlated with word correctness.

## Key Results
- Kaldi system with LM containing both reading prompts and manual transcriptions achieved highest MCC (.63) for agreement with human evaluations
- Same system reached highest correlation between forced decoding confidence scores and word correctness (r = .45)
- Including reading errors in the language model improves assessment performance
- Whisper systems showed comparable performance to Kaldi variants in some metrics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Including reading errors in the language model improves ASR performance for reading diagnostics.
- Mechanism: The language model trained on both reading prompts and manual orthographic transcriptions captures the distribution of reading errors, allowing the ASR to better match children's actual speech patterns rather than just the target text.
- Core assumption: Reading errors follow a predictable pattern that can be learned from a corpus of error-containing transcriptions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The same system reached the highest correlation between forced decoding confidence scores and word correctness (r = .45). This system's language model (LM) consisted of manual orthographic transcriptions and reading prompts of the test data, which shows that including reading errors in the LM improves assessment performance."
  - [section 3.2] "The highest MCC (.65) was found for REA by ASR Kaldi-MO, closely followed by Kaldi-PM (.62)."
- Break condition: If the distribution of reading errors is too idiosyncratic or changes significantly across different reading levels or languages.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Forced decoding confidence scores correlate with word correctness when using a language model that includes both prompts and errors.
- Mechanism: The ASR's confidence in its decoding output reflects the match between the acoustic signal and the expected word sequence, and when the LM includes actual reading errors, the confidence scores become more discriminative between correct and incorrect words.
- Core assumption: The ASR's confidence estimation is meaningful and correlates with actual word correctness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The same system reached the highest correlation between forced decoding confidence scores and word correctness (r = .45)."
  - [section 3.3] "The highest correlation was found for Kaldi -PM at r = .45."
- Break condition: If the acoustic model is poor or if confidence scores are not properly calibrated.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The acoustic model trained on adult speech can recognize child speech sufficiently well for reading error detection when paired with an appropriate language model.
- Mechanism: While child speech has different acoustic properties than adult speech, the reading task constrains the acoustic variation enough that an adult-trained acoustic model can still perform adequately, especially when the language model compensates for error patterns.
- Core assumption: The acoustic differences between child and adult speech are not so extreme as to prevent recognition in a constrained task like reading.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The study highlights the potential of ASR for reading diagnostics while identifying areas for future improvement, particularly in developing child-specific acoustic models."
  - [section 2.2] "Each system had the same acoustic model (AM) but a different language model (LM)."
- Break condition: If the child speech acoustic characteristics differ too greatly from the adult training data.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Language models for ASR
  - Why needed here: The language model determines what word sequences the ASR considers likely, which is crucial for recognizing both correct readings and errors.
  - Quick check question: What happens to ASR performance if the language model only contains the reading prompts but not the actual errors children make?

- Concept: Forced decoding and confidence scoring
  - Why needed here: Forced decoding allows the system to evaluate how well the acoustic signal matches a specific target word, providing a confidence score that can be used to assess word correctness.
  - Quick check question: Why is forced decoding only performed on ASR systems that contain prompt words in their lexicon?

- Concept: Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)
  - Why needed here: MCC is used to evaluate agreement between human and automatic assessment, particularly appropriate for imbalanced data where most words are read correctly.
  - Quick check question: Why is MCC preferred over Cohen's kappa in this study?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Audio input → ASR decoding with specific LM → alignment with prompts → comparison with manual transcriptions → confidence scoring → performance evaluation
- Critical path: Audio input → ASR decoding with specific LM → alignment with prompts → comparison with manual transcriptions → confidence scoring → performance evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Using adult-trained acoustic models saves resources but may reduce accuracy; including manual transcriptions in the LM improves performance but requires human annotation
- Failure signatures: Low MCC values indicate poor agreement with human assessment; low correlation between confidence scores and correctness indicates poor discriminative ability
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train and evaluate Kaldi with each of the four LMs (CGN, PR, MO, PM) on the same test data to compare performance
  2. Implement forced decoding on Kaldi-PR and Kaldi-PM to obtain confidence scores and correlate with word correctness
  3. Compare Kaldi results with Whisper systems using the same evaluation metrics to benchmark performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal balance between using general-purpose and child-specific acoustic models for ASR-based reading assessment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "much can be gained by using ASR systems trained on child speech" and identifies this as a key area for improvement
- Why unresolved: The study only used adult speech data for acoustic modeling and compared different language models, but did not explore the impact of child-specific acoustic models
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing ASR systems with child-specific acoustic models against the adult-trained models used in this study, measuring MCC, WER, and other metrics

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the inclusion of reading errors in language models affect ASR performance across different age groups and reading proficiency levels?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that "including reading errors in the LM improves assessment performance" but this was tested only on children aged 7-11
- Why unresolved: The study's age range was limited and did not examine whether the benefits of error-inclusive LMs vary by reading proficiency or age
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the same ASR systems with different LM configurations across multiple age groups and reading levels, comparing agreement metrics and error rates

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the trade-off between false positives and false negatives in offline versus online reading assessment contexts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that "false positives and false negatives are not as detrimental as they would be in an online feedback setting" for offline assessment
- Why unresolved: The paper does not quantify the acceptable error rates for offline versus online contexts or explore how these trade-offs affect diagnostic utility
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental comparison of the same ASR systems in offline diagnostic and online feedback contexts, measuring how different error tolerances affect practical utility

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can automatic reading assessment systems be fine-tuned for specific instructional contexts (e.g., practicing two-syllable nouns in grade 1)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest that "the context in which automated oral reading assessment is employed...can be utilised to fine-tune the ASR's LM"
- Why unresolved: The paper does not demonstrate or quantify how context-specific fine-tuning affects performance, only mentions it as a possibility
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments showing the performance improvement of context-specific LMs versus general LMs across different instructional scenarios

## Limitations

- Reliance on adult-trained acoustic models for recognizing child speech, which may limit accuracy
- Moderate agreement with human assessments (MCC = .63) indicating room for improvement
- Limited generalizability to languages other than Dutch
- Correlation between confidence scores and correctness is only moderate (r = .45)

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Including reading errors in the language model improves assessment performance
- **Medium Confidence**: Forced decoding confidence scores correlate with word correctness when using appropriate LMs
- **Low Confidence**: Adult-trained acoustic models are sufficient for child reading assessment in constrained tasks

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-validation on larger datasets**: Evaluate the best-performing Kaldi system on an independent Dutch reading corpus to assess generalization beyond the JASMIN dataset and verify that the .63 MCC and .45 correlation are reproducible.

2. **Child-specific acoustic model comparison**: Train and evaluate a Kaldi system with an acoustic model specifically trained on child speech data, then compare its performance against the adult-trained model to quantify the benefit of child-specific acoustic modeling.

3. **Confidence score calibration and threshold optimization**: Systematically vary confidence score thresholds and evaluate how precision-recall trade-offs change, determining optimal thresholds for clinical deployment and testing whether calibration improves the .45 correlation with word correctness.
