# Streaming Active Learning with Deep Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.02535
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02535
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: VeSSAL achieves comparable performance to state-of-the-art pool-based methods in streaming active learning settings

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces VeSSAL, an algorithm for batch active learning with deep neural networks in streaming settings where data arrives sequentially and labeling decisions must be made immediately. The method performs approximate volume sampling over gradient representations of data points, trading off between uncertainty and diversity while automatically tuning its sampling rate to match a desired query frequency without hand-tuned hyperparameters. Experiments on three benchmark datasets and one real-world dataset with two neural network architectures and various batch sizes show that VeSSAL achieves performance comparable to state-of-the-art pool-based methods despite the added streaming constraints.

## Method Summary
VeSSAL addresses the streaming active learning problem by performing approximate volume sampling over gradient representations of data points in the last layer of a neural network. The algorithm samples each incoming point with probability proportional to the determinant of its contribution to the covariance matrix over already selected samples, balancing uncertainty (captured by the gradient embedding) and diversity (captured by the inverse covariance). A streaming-friendly approximation of the scaling parameter automatically tunes the sampling rate to match the desired query frequency, and efficient rank-1 Woodbury updates maintain the inverse covariance matrix without recomputing full matrix inverses. The method assumes a known number of label classes and processes each sample only once, making immediate labeling decisions.

## Key Results
- VeSSAL achieves accuracy comparable to state-of-the-art pool-based methods (BADGE, coreset) despite streaming constraints
- The method demonstrates robustness to non-IID data streams with feature drift and provides label amplification similar to non-streaming baselines
- Auto-tuning of sampling rate successfully matches target query frequencies across different datasets, architectures, and batch sizes without hand-tuned hyperparameters

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: gradient embedding computation (g(xt)) -> probability calculation (pt) -> stochastic decision -> Woodbury update (ˆΣ−1t)
- **Critical path**: For each incoming sample xt, the algorithm must: (1) compute g(xt), (2) calculate pt using the current ˆΣ−1t, (3) make a stochastic decision to query or not, and (4) if queried, update ˆΣ−1t using the Woodbury update. This sequence must complete before the next sample arrives.
- **Design tradeoffs**: The algorithm trades off between computational efficiency (streaming updates vs. batch processing) and sampling quality (approximate volume sampling vs. exact DPP sampling). The automatic tuning of zt trades hyperparameter tuning for potential suboptimal query rates in non-stationary streams.
- **Failure signatures**: Poor performance may manifest as: (1) query rate significantly different from target q, (2) model accuracy stagnating despite sufficient queries, (3) numerical instability in ˆΣ−1t updates, or (4) samples appearing clustered rather than diverse.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run on a small synthetic dataset with known ground truth to verify that VeSSAL achieves the target query rate q and that samples are reasonably diverse.
  2. Compare VeSSAL against random sampling on a benchmark dataset to confirm that active sampling provides label amplification (better accuracy per labeled sample).
  3. Test VeSSAL with adversarial data ordering (sorted by principal component) to verify robustness to non-IID streams.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would VeSSAL perform on truly committal active learning scenarios where samples not selected in one round are never seen again?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that their current setup technically makes VeSSAL committal only for a fixed round of active learning, as samples not selected are made available again in subsequent rounds. They acknowledge this setup is used to compare with non-committal baselines but state VeSSAL is designed for truly committal environments.
- Why unresolved: The experiments use a setup where samples can be reconsidered in future rounds, which is less restrictive than truly committal settings where each sample is seen only once. This means the current evaluation might overestimate VeSSAL's performance in real-world streaming scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments in a truly committal setting where samples not selected in one round are permanently discarded and never seen again. This would test VeSSAL's performance under the full streaming constraints it's designed for.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does VeSSAL handle scenarios where the number of label classes is unknown and may grow over time?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion section explicitly states that VeSSAL assumes the number of label classes is known a priori, and notes this as a limitation for real-world applications where the interaction environment continues to evolve.
- Why unresolved: The current algorithm design and experiments all assume a fixed, known number of classes. The paper acknowledges this is unrealistic for many real-world scenarios but doesn't provide solutions or preliminary results for handling dynamic class sets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments or theoretical analysis showing how VeSSAL would adapt to growing class sets, possibly through online class discovery mechanisms or incremental learning approaches that can handle new classes appearing during the streaming process.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How would VeSSAL perform when different samples have different labeling costs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion section mentions that some queries can be costlier than others, using the example of asking a user for an object label in their peripheral vision potentially distracting them from their current task.
- Why unresolved: The current formulation treats all samples as having equal labeling cost, which doesn't reflect many real-world scenarios where some samples are more expensive or disruptive to label than others. The paper identifies this as an exciting topic for further work but provides no preliminary investigation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments incorporating variable labeling costs into the VeSSAL framework, possibly by modifying the probability calculation to account for different costs, and evaluating performance in terms of cost-benefit trade-offs rather than just accuracy per label.

## Limitations
- Assumes known number of label classes, limiting applicability to evolving real-world environments
- Current experimental setup allows samples to be reconsidered in future rounds, which may overestimate performance in truly committal settings
- Treats all samples as having equal labeling cost, ignoring real-world scenarios where some queries are more expensive or disruptive than others

## Confidence

**Confidence Assessment:**
- **High confidence**: The volume sampling mechanism and Woodbury update implementation are well-founded and directly supported by the paper text.
- **Medium confidence**: The auto-tuning mechanism's effectiveness across diverse datasets and architectures, while demonstrated, relies on the assumption that gradient covariance can be accurately estimated in a streaming fashion.
- **Medium confidence**: The label amplification results, though promising, are based on comparisons with a limited set of baselines and specific experimental conditions.
