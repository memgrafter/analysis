# Towards Understanding the Generalization of Medical Text-to-SQL Models and Datasets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.12898
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12898
- Reference count: 39
- High accuracy results on MIMICSQL led to claims the dataset was "solved"

## Executive Summary
The MIMICSQL dataset, widely used for medical text-to-SQL tasks, has been considered "solved" with high accuracy results. This paper challenges that assumption by introducing MIMICSQL 2.0, a new set of splits designed to better measure model generalizability. The new splits achieve significantly lower accuracy (28% vs 92%), revealing that previous models may have been overfitting to template data rather than truly understanding medical schemas. To address this, the authors introduce data augmentation via back-translation and joint training with the Spider dataset, demonstrating improved performance particularly when models are trained on template data and evaluated on paraphrased data.

## Method Summary
The authors create MIMICSQL 2.0 by removing specific tables (PROCEDURES, LAB, PRESCRIPTIONS) from training splits to force models to reason about unseen schemas. They implement a T5-based text-to-SQL model with schema encoding and execution-guided decoding. Back-translation is applied by translating questions to French and German then back to English to generate synthetic paraphrases. Models are trained both on MIMICSQL alone and in combination with the Spider dataset, with performance evaluated using ACCLF (Logic Form Accuracy) and ACCEX (Execution Accuracy) metrics.

## Key Results
- Original MIMICSQL splits achieve 92% accuracy while MIMICSQL 2.0 drops to 28%, demonstrating overfitting to templates
- Back-translation augmentation improves performance on MIMICSQL 2.0 splits
- Joint training with Spider dataset further enhances model generalizability
- Training on template data and evaluating on paraphrased data shows significant performance gaps, highlighting the need for robust training methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Data augmentation via back-translation improves model generalizability by introducing diverse paraphrases
- Mechanism: Back-translation translates questions to another language (e.g., French, German) and back to English, generating synthetic paraphrases that enrich the training data and reduce overfitting to specific phrasings
- Core assumption: The back-translated paraphrases retain semantic meaning and provide sufficient variation to improve robustness
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We translate every question in the MIMICSQL corpus to French and German, then back to English. The back-translated English text is used as synthetic paraphrases for training..."
- Break condition: If back-translation introduces semantic drift or noise, performance may degrade instead of improve

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Joint training on non-medical text-to-SQL data (e.g., Spider) improves generalization to new medical schemas
- Mechanism: Spider provides diverse SQL query patterns and schemas from non-medical domains, enabling the model to learn general relational reasoning skills transferable to medical contexts
- Core assumption: Structural patterns in SQL are domain-agnostic and learning them from diverse datasets improves adaptability
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We use the entire Spider dataset for training... we explore jointly training on the Spider and MIMICSQL datasets to understand if out-of-domain data can improve medical text-to-SQL model performance."
- Break condition: If Spider's SQL patterns differ too much from medical use cases, transfer learning benefits may be minimal

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: New train/dev/test splits that isolate table usage better measure true model generalization
- Mechanism: By ensuring certain tables (e.g., PROCEDURES, LAB) appear only in test/dev sets and not in training, the split forces models to reason about unseen schemas rather than memorizing query patterns
- Core assumption: Template-based datasets contain exploitable correlations between phrasing and table usage that mask lack of genuine schema understanding
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We remove each table from the training split... Our goal is to create a more challenging split that is not as easily solvable—the idea for our new split forces the model to reason about tables."
- Break condition: If models still succeed by exploiting residual correlations between tables and question topics, the split fails to isolate schema reasoning

  - Why needed here: The paper evaluates whether models trained on one dataset generalize to unseen schemas, a key challenge in real-world deployment
  - Quick check question: Why might a model trained on MIMICSQL fail when applied to a different hospital's database?

- Concept: Data augmentation and synthetic data generation
  - Why needed here: The paper uses back-translation to generate synthetic paraphrases and improve robustness
  - Quick check question: What are the risks of using synthetic data for training NLP models?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: T5 language model (encoder-decoder) -> schema encoder (tables, columns, attributes) -> back-translation pipeline -> Spider dataset integration -> MIMICSQL 2.0 evaluation framework
- Critical path: Input (schema + question) -> T5 model -> SQL generation -> execution-guided decoding -> evaluation (ACCLF/ACCEX)
- Design tradeoffs: Larger T5 models improve accuracy but increase computational cost; back-translation adds diversity but risks semantic drift; Spider integration helps generalization but may introduce domain mismatch
- Failure signatures: High ACCLF but low ACCEX indicates syntactically correct but semantically wrong queries; low performance on T→P split suggests overfitting to training phrasing
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train T5 on original MIMICSQL splits and evaluate on MIMICSQL 2.0 to establish baseline performance drop
  2. Apply back-translation augmentation and retrain on MIMICSQL 2.0 to measure improvement
  3. Combine Spider and MIMICSQL training with back-translation and evaluate on MIMICSQL 2.0 to assess cumulative benefit

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do models trained on MIMICSQL perform on real-world hospital databases with significantly different schemas?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "While MIMICSQL provides an excellent test bed because of its public availability, it does not match databases seen in practice [8]. Hence, measuring and understanding cross-hospital performance is essential for broad applicability in the medical domain."
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this gap but does not provide empirical evidence or experimental results measuring cross-hospital performance
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of MIMICSQL-trained models on diverse real-world hospital databases with varying schemas, reporting accuracy metrics and analyzing failure modes

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of integrating complex SQL queries (nested queries, GROUP BY, HAVING clauses) into MIMICSQL on model performance and generalizability?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] "First, more work is needed to create a dataset with more complex queries. While the MIMICSQL dataset contains JOINS, it does not contain nested queries or highly complex queries as defined in the Spider dataset [5]."
- Why unresolved: The current MIMICSQL dataset lacks complex query types that are common in real-world medical database queries
- What evidence would resolve it: Creation and evaluation of an enhanced MIMICSQL dataset with complex queries, measuring model performance and generalization capabilities on this enriched dataset

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal balance between template-based synthetic data generation and manually paraphrased data for training robust medical text-to-SQL models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] "Moreover, we report of training and evaluating on the template data (T→ T), training and evaluating on the paraphrased data (P→ P), and training on the template data and evaluating on the paraphrased data (T → P)."
- Why unresolved: The paper explores this question but does not provide definitive guidance on the optimal data mix for different scenarios
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies varying the ratio of template vs. paraphrased data, measuring accuracy and generalization across multiple test scenarios and database schemas

## Limitations

- The evaluation framework assumes MIMICSQL 2.0 splits accurately measure true generalization, but may not capture the full complexity of real-world medical databases
- Back-translation may introduce semantic drift that affects the quality of augmented data, particularly for domain-specific medical terminology
- The effectiveness of Spider dataset integration for improving medical text-to-SQL performance is assumed but not empirically validated for medical contexts

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The observation that original MIMICSQL achieves near-perfect accuracy (92%) while MIMICSQL 2.0 shows significantly lower performance (28%) demonstrates that the original benchmark is indeed too easy and doesn't adequately test model generalization

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that back-translation improves model generalizability is supported by experimental results but depends on the quality of back-translated paraphrases and their semantic preservation

**Low Confidence**: The assertion that joint training on Spider and MIMICSQL significantly improves medical text-to-SQL performance is based on promising results but lacks direct evidence of transfer learning benefits specific to the medical domain

## Next Checks

1. **Semantic Drift Analysis**: Conduct a detailed analysis of the back-translated questions to measure semantic drift and identify cases where meaning is altered, using human evaluation or automated semantic similarity metrics

2. **Cross-Database Generalization**: Test the trained models on a completely different medical database schema (not MIMIC) to evaluate whether improvements from MIMICSQL 2.0 and data augmentation translate to true domain generalization

3. **Ablation Study on Spider Integration**: Perform an ablation study that systematically varies the amount and type of Spider data used in training to isolate which aspects contribute most to performance improvements in the medical domain
