# Analyzing and Reducing the Performance Gap in Cross-Lingual Transfer with Fine-tuning Slow and Fast

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11449
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11449
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: A method that reduces performance gaps in cross-lingual transfer by selectively protecting cross-lingual knowledge and encouraging forgetting of pre-training task knowledge

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the performance gap in cross-lingual transfer during fine-tuning of multilingual pre-trained models. Through detailed analysis, it identifies that the gap arises due to forgetting of cross-lingual knowledge in early fine-tuning phases and the presence of pre-training task knowledge that is irrelevant or harmful to the downstream task. The proposed method, Fine-tuning slow and fast, uses four learning rate policies to selectively protect cross-lingual knowledge and encourage forgetting of pre-training task knowledge. Experiments on XNLI, MLQA, and NER datasets show that the method reduces the performance gap and improves overall performance compared to baselines in both zero-shot and few-shot settings.

## Method Summary
The method consists of four learning rate policies applied to different weight sets based on layer position and training phase. The first four layers contain cross-lingual knowledge and use policies that protect this knowledge (stability), while the last two layers contain pre-training task knowledge and use policies that encourage forgetting (plasticity). Learning rates are dynamically adjusted based on the training phase and loss curve, with multipliers applied to different weight sets to balance stability and plasticity during fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- The proposed method reduces performance gaps between source and non-source languages while maintaining or improving overall performance
- Experiments on XNLI, MLQA, and NER datasets show consistent improvements in both zero-shot and few-shot settings
- The method effectively protects cross-lingual knowledge in early layers while encouraging forgetting of pre-training task knowledge in later layers

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Early fine-tuning causes forgetting of cross-lingual knowledge, creating a performance gap that grows during initial training.
- Mechanism: Large gradient updates in the first training phase (P1) overwrite cross-lingual knowledge concentrated in the first four layers.
- Core assumption: Cross-lingual knowledge is primarily stored in the first four layers.
- Evidence anchors: Performance gap increases quickly in P1; cross-lingual knowledge is forgetting quickly in early layers.
- Break condition: If cross-lingual knowledge is not concentrated in first four layers, or gradient updates are small during early training.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Pre-training task knowledge in the last two layers can negatively impact downstream task performance.
- Mechanism: Pre-training knowledge (e.g., from Masked Language Modeling) in the last two layers interferes with learning the downstream task and should be selectively forgotten.
- Core assumption: Pre-training task knowledge is primarily stored in the last two layers and can interfere with downstream learning.
- Evidence anchors: Re-initializing weights in last two layers sometimes improves performance; pre-training task differs from downstream task.
- Break condition: If pre-training knowledge is not concentrated in last two layers, or if it benefits the downstream task.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Dynamic learning rate strategy balances stability (preserving cross-lingual knowledge) and plasticity (learning new task knowledge).
- Mechanism: Four learning rate policies selectively protect first four layers while encouraging forgetting in last two layers, with dynamic adjustment based on training phase and loss curve.
- Core assumption: Different layers require different learning rate policies based on their knowledge content and training phase.
- Evidence anchors: Method consists of four learning rate policies to protect cross-lingual knowledge and encourage forgetting of pre-training task knowledge.
- Break condition: If four-layer division doesn't accurately capture knowledge distribution, or dynamic rates don't improve performance.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Catastrophic forgetting in continual learning
  - Why needed here: Fine-tuning exhibits similar forgetting behavior to continual learning, where cross-lingual knowledge is overwritten by new task knowledge.
  - Quick check question: What is the main challenge in continual learning that is also relevant to cross-lingual fine-tuning?

- Concept: Plasticity vs. stability tradeoff
  - Why needed here: Fine-tuning requires balancing learning new task knowledge (plasticity) with preserving cross-lingual knowledge (stability).
  - Quick check question: What are the two opposing forces that must be balanced during fine-tuning, and why is this balance important?

- Concept: Layer-wise knowledge distribution
  - Why needed here: Understanding which layers contain cross-lingual versus pre-training task knowledge is crucial for designing learning rate policies.
  - Quick check question: How does the distribution of knowledge types across different layers inform the design of learning rate policies?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: XLM-R model with 12 transformer layers -> Four learning rate policies (I, II, III, IV) -> Dynamic learning rate multiplier based on loss curve -> Weight sets for different policies (SI_θ, SII_θ, VI_θ, VII_θ)

- Critical path: 1) Identify weight sets for each policy based on re-initialization/freezing experiments; 2) Apply policies I and II to protect cross-lingual knowledge in first four layers; 3) Apply policies III and IV to encourage forgetting in last two layers; 4) Dynamically adjust learning rates based on training phase and loss curve

- Design tradeoffs: Stability vs. plasticity (protecting knowledge may limit learning); Layer granularity (finer divisions may provide better control but increase complexity); Dynamic vs. static learning rates (dynamic may adapt better but require more tuning)

- Failure signatures: Performance gap increases despite applying method; Overall performance decreases significantly; Method works well on one dataset but not others

- First 3 experiments: 1) Re-initialization experiment: Re-initialize weights in each layer and measure performance impact to identify important layers for cross-lingual transfer; 2) Freezing experiment: Freeze weights in each layer and measure performance impact to identify key weights for cross-lingual transfer; 3) Learning rate multiplier ablation: Test different values for c1, c2, and r to find optimal hyperparameters for dynamic learning rate strategy

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance gap between source and non-source languages evolve when fine-tuning multilingual models on datasets with varying degrees of linguistic similarity to the source language?
- Basis in paper: The paper analyzes performance gap on XNLI, MLQA, and NER datasets with English as source language but doesn't systematically vary linguistic distance.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on English as source language without exploring how linguistic similarity affects the performance gap.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the source language and measuring performance gaps across linguistically diverse target languages, particularly comparing closely related vs. distant language pairs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the precise relationship between forgetting of pre-training task knowledge (Hpre_task) and learning of new task knowledge (Hnew_task) during fine-tuning?
- Basis in paper: The paper identifies Hpre_task as potentially harmful and proposes policies to encourage its forgetting but doesn't quantify the trade-off across different task types.
- Why unresolved: While proposing methods to encourage forgetting Hpre_task, the paper doesn't measure the exact impact on Hnew_task acquisition or how this varies by task complexity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of representation similarity (e.g., CKA) showing how Hpre_task forgetting correlates with Hnew_task learning across multiple task types, with controlled experiments isolating these effects.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different fine-tuning strategies compare to the proposed Fine-tuning slow and fast approach in reducing performance gap and maintaining cross-lingual knowledge?
- Basis in paper: The paper proposes a novel method but only compares it to direct fine-tuning and a few related baselines, without exploring other established fine-tuning strategies.
- Why unresolved: The experimental comparison is limited to a few baselines, and the paper doesn't explore how other well-established fine-tuning techniques might perform on the same tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive comparison of the proposed method with other fine-tuning strategies (e.g., gradual unfreezing, learning rate scheduling, adapter-based methods) across multiple datasets and language pairs, measuring both performance gap reduction and cross-lingual knowledge retention.

## Limitations

- The core mechanisms rely on specific layer-wise knowledge distributions that may not generalize across different model architectures or tasks.
- The dynamic learning rate strategy's performance depends heavily on correctly identifying training phase boundaries, but the paper lacks sufficient detail on these implementations.
- The assumption that cross-lingual knowledge is concentrated in the first four layers and pre-training task knowledge in the last two layers could be task-dependent rather than universal.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The observation that cross-lingual transfer performance gaps exist and can be measured across different tasks and languages.
- Medium Confidence: The identification of forgetting of cross-lingual knowledge in early fine-tuning phases as a primary cause of performance gaps.
- Medium Confidence: The claim that pre-training task knowledge in the last two layers can negatively impact downstream performance.
- Low Confidence: The universal applicability of the four-layer division for learning rate policies across different model sizes and architectures.

## Next Checks

1. **Layer-wise knowledge distribution validation**: Conduct ablation studies that systematically re-initialize and freeze individual layers (not just groups of four or two) across multiple tasks to verify the specific knowledge concentration patterns claimed in the paper.

2. **Dynamic learning rate boundary verification**: Implement the φ(Lt) and R(φ(Lt)) functions with different threshold values and compare the resulting performance gap reduction to validate whether the specific phase identification method is critical to the approach's success.

3. **Architecture generalization test**: Apply the method to a different multilingual model architecture (e.g., mBERT or mT5) and measure whether the same four-layer division and learning rate policies produce similar performance improvements, testing the universality of the proposed mechanism.
