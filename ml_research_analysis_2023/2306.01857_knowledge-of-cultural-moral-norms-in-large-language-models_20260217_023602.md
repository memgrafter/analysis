# Knowledge of cultural moral norms in large language models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01857
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01857
- Reference count: 26
- Monolingual English pre-trained language models encode stronger moral norms for Western cultures, with fine-tuning improving cross-cultural inference at the cost of accuracy on English norms.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether monolingual English pre-trained language models (EPLMs) capture knowledge about moral norms across different cultures. The authors develop a fine-grained probing method to assess EPLMs' understanding of cultural moral variation, analyzing two levels: (1) whether EPLMs reflect fine-grained moral norms across countries on various topics, and (2) whether they capture cultural diversity and shared tendencies in moral judgments. Using data from the World Values Survey (55 countries) and PEW Global Attitudes survey (40 countries), the authors find that pre-trained EPLMs predict empirical moral norms across countries worse than English moral norms, with higher accuracy for Western cultures than non-Western ones. Fine-tuning EPLMs on the survey data improves cross-cultural inference but reduces accuracy on English moral norms, revealing a trade-off between cultural and homogeneous moral representations.

## Method Summary
The study probes monolingual English pre-trained language models (BERT, GPT-2, GPT-3) using prompts combining topics and countries to elicit moral judgments. Moral scores are generated via semantic similarity (BERT) or next-token probability (GPT models). Fine-tuning is performed on survey data using supervised learning with prompts like "A person in [Country] believes [Topic] is [Moral rating]". Models are evaluated by Pearson correlation between inferred moral scores and empirical survey ratings across 55 countries (WVS) and 40 countries (PEW), with separate analysis for homogeneous English norms.

## Key Results
- Pre-trained EPLMs predict empirical moral norms across countries worse than English moral norms.
- EPLMs show higher accuracy for Western cultures than non-Western ones.
- Fine-tuning improves cross-cultural inference but reduces accuracy on English moral norms, revealing a trade-off between cultural and homogeneous moral representations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Monolingual English language models (EPLMs) encode moral norms primarily aligned with Western cultures, leading to systematic bias when inferring norms in non-Western contexts.
- Mechanism: EPLMs are trained on English-language web corpora, which overrepresent Western perspectives. During training, moral associations (e.g., "divorce is morally negative") are reinforced by textual patterns more common in Western discourse. When the model is probed on non-Western contexts, these same associations are projected, resulting in misaligned moral judgments.
- Core assumption: The training corpus composition directly shapes the model's implicit moral knowledge.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "pre-trained EPLMs predict empirical moral norms across countries worse than the English moral norms reported previously" and "higher accuracy for Western cultures than non-Western ones."
  - [section 5.3]: "EPLMs contain more knowledge about moral norms of the Rich West countries as opposed to non-western and non-rich countries."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence—no direct textual analysis of training data provided, but inferred from performance disparities.
- Break condition: If the training corpus were balanced across global moral perspectives or if the model were explicitly fine-tuned on diverse cultural datasets, the bias would diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning EPLMs on global survey data improves cross-cultural moral inference at the expense of accuracy on English moral norms.
- Mechanism: During fine-tuning, the model updates its parameters to minimize loss on culturally diverse survey data. This shifts the model's representations toward better matching non-Western moral judgments, but the same updates degrade performance on previously well-learned Western norms due to the fixed parameter budget and competing objectives.
- Core assumption: Model capacity is finite, so improvements in one domain must trade off against another.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "fine-tuning language models on the survey data improves inference across countries at the expense of a less accurate estimate of the English moral norms."
  - [section 6]: Table 1 shows correlation increases for cross-cultural inference and decreases for homogeneous norms after fine-tuning.
  - [corpus]: Not directly evidenced—assumes representational trade-off without explicit ablation studies.
- Break condition: If the model were expanded in size or if separate heads were used for cultural vs. homogeneous inference, the trade-off could be mitigated.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The phrasing of prompts critically affects the moral judgments inferred by EPLMs.
- Mechanism: Autoregressive models generate text by predicting the next token based on context. Different prompt templates alter the semantic space the model navigates, changing which tokens are most probable. A template emphasizing "justifiability" might elicit different moral judgments than one emphasizing "acceptability," even for the same underlying topic-country pair.
- Core assumption: The model's moral judgments are sensitive to surface form rather than underlying semantics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section C]: "The results are substantially worse than our initial template, suggesting that extracting the moral knowledge in language models is sensitive to the wording used in the input."
  - [section 3]: Multiple prompt designs tested, with performance differences noted.
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence—no controlled linguistic analysis of prompt sensitivity provided.
- Break condition: If the model had stronger grounding in context-invariant moral concepts or if prompt engineering were optimized, sensitivity to phrasing could be reduced.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Cultural variation in moral norms
  - Why needed here: The study hinges on comparing moral judgments across cultures; without understanding how norms differ, the analysis of model bias lacks foundation.
  - Quick check question: Why might "abortion" be judged differently in Western vs. non-Western countries according to the surveys cited?

- Concept: Cross-lingual representation bias in NLP models
  - Why needed here: EPLMs are trained on English corpora; understanding how this biases learned representations is key to interpreting their cross-cultural performance.
  - Quick check question: How might training on English web data lead to overrepresentation of Western moral perspectives?

- Concept: Prompt engineering and autoregressive generation
  - Why needed here: The experiments rely on carefully constructed prompts to elicit moral judgments; small changes in wording significantly affect outputs.
  - Quick check question: What role does the choice of moral judgment pair (e.g., "always justifiable" vs. "ethically right") play in shaping the model's output?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Survey Data -> Pre-trained EPLM (BERT/GPT variants) -> Prompt Template -> Moral Score Generation -> Correlation with Empirical Ratings -> (Optional) Fine-tuning -> Repeat Evaluation
- Critical path: 1) Load EPLM; 2) Construct prompts (topic + country + moral judgment pair); 3) Generate moral score (either semantic similarity or token probability difference); 4) Compare to empirical survey ratings; 5) Aggregate results (correlation, clustering); 6) (Optional) Fine-tune and repeat.
- Design tradeoffs: Autoregressive models (GPT) allow richer prompt engineering but are sensitive to phrasing; BERT-based models are more stable but less flexible. Fine-tuning improves cultural coverage but harms homogeneous norm inference due to representational trade-offs.
- Failure signatures: Low correlation between model scores and empirical ratings indicates bias or insufficient cultural knowledge; inconsistent results across prompt templates suggest high sensitivity to phrasing; fine-tuned models showing degraded performance on homogeneous norms confirm the utility-bias trade-off.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Probe a GPT-2 model on "getting a divorce in Japan" using the "always justifiable/never justifiable" template and compare to empirical survey ratings.
  2. Cluster countries by moral inference accuracy and compare to Rich West vs. non-Western groupings.
  3. Fine-tune GPT-2 on WVS data using random partition and evaluate cross-cultural correlation before and after fine-tuning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- To what extent do fine-tuned language models maintain their ability to accurately represent English moral norms after being trained on cross-cultural survey data?
- How do different religious traditions within non-Western cultures influence the moral norms captured by language models?
- Can language models be fine-tuned to better represent the moral norms of underrepresented cultures without introducing additional biases?

## Limitations

- The study assumes equivalence between human moral reasoning and language model token predictions without rigorous validation.
- Prompt sensitivity findings are based on limited template variations; the full space of possible phrasings remains unexplored.
- The trade-off between cross-cultural and homogeneous moral accuracy during fine-tuning is observed but not mechanistically explained.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: EPLMs show systematically lower accuracy on non-Western moral norms compared to Western ones, and fine-tuning improves cross-cultural inference while degrading homogeneous norm accuracy.
- **Medium confidence**: The interpretation that training corpus composition drives cultural bias is plausible but indirect.
- **Low confidence**: Claims about prompt sensitivity being a fundamental limitation of moral knowledge extraction are weakly supported.

## Next Checks

1. Conduct a controlled experiment varying prompt templates systematically (e.g., different moral judgment pairs, syntactic structures) to quantify the full extent of prompt sensitivity on moral inference accuracy.
2. Perform an ablation study on fine-tuned models to determine whether the trade-off between cross-cultural and homogeneous moral accuracy is due to representational capacity limits or optimization dynamics.
3. Analyze the training corpus composition (e.g., via topic modeling or moral sentiment analysis) to directly link observed cultural biases to specific patterns in the data.
