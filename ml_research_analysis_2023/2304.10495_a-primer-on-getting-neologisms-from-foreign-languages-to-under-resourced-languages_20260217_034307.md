# A primer on getting neologisms from foreign languages to under-resourced languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.10495
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10495
- Reference count: 1
- One-line primary result: Computational approach identifies Quechua-compatible neologisms from any language source, achieving 11.81% and 22.87% translation rates into English.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a computational method for generating neologisms for Quechua by identifying words from multilingual pronunciation databases whose phonetic transcriptions match Quechua phonology. The approach challenges traditional borrowing practices that restrict neologisms to colonial languages by proposing that any language can be a source, provided words conform to Quechua's sound system. Using Open Dictionary and Wikipron databases, the authors identified 14,970 and 26,752 candidate neologisms respectively, though translation verification using WordNet yielded modest rates of 11.81% and 22.87%. The study demonstrates the potential of computational methods to expand vocabulary resources for endangered languages while highlighting the limitations of existing translation verification tools.

## Method Summary
The method involves two main computational steps: first, identifying candidate neologisms by filtering words from multilingual pronunciation databases (Open Dictionary and Wikipron) whose IPA transcriptions conform to Quechua phonological constraints using the GettingNeologisms.ipynb script; second, verifying translations into English using WordNet through the SincronizationWordNet.ipynb script, which finds synsets for candidate words and delivers English translations when available. The approach uses broad phonetic transcriptions and does not code diacritics, which may limit the number of eligible words found.

## Key Results
- Identified 14,970 candidate neologisms from Open Dictionary and 26,752 from Wikipron that fit Quechua phonology
- Translation rates into English were 11.81% (Open Dictionary) and 22.87% (Wikipron) using WordNet verification
- Only 0.39% and 0.76% of words from each database met Quechua phonological criteria
- Method successfully challenges the practice of restricting neologisms to colonial language sources

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal level of phonetic transcription detail (broad vs. narrow) needed to maximize the identification of Quechua-compatible neologisms while minimizing computational complexity?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note that their sources contain both broad and narrow transcriptions, but they didn't code diacritics, resulting in only 0.39-0.76% of words being eligible. They suggest that coding diacritics might increase eligible words.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't experimentally compare the impact of using broad vs. narrow transcriptions or explore different thresholds for phonetic matching.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled experiment testing different transcription detail levels and their effects on the number of eligible neologisms and computational efficiency.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do speakers of endangered languages actually perceive and accept neologisms generated from foreign languages, and what factors influence their acceptance or rejection?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss that neologism introduction is more complex in endangered languages due to social and political factors, and that their approach is a proposal that needs consensus among stakeholders.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents a computational method but doesn't include any sociolinguistic research on how speakers would actually receive these proposed neologisms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies surveying endangered language speakers about their attitudes toward foreign-derived neologisms and identifying the social/political factors affecting acceptance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between the spelling regularity of a source language and the efficiency of identifying Quechua-compatible neologisms from that language?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest that languages with regular spelling systems (like pinyin) might be more efficient sources for Quechua neologisms since they don't require IPA notation.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't test this hypothesis by comparing neologism generation from languages with different levels of orthographic depth.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative study of neologism identification rates across languages with varying degrees of orthographic regularity, measuring the trade-off between ease of processing and number of suitable candidates.

## Limitations
- The computational approach achieves only 0.39% and 0.76% eligibility rates, suggesting either overly strict filtering or insufficient transcription precision
- Translation verification is severely constrained by WordNet's limited language coverage and word counts, resulting in modest translation rates of 11.81% and 22.87%
- The study does not address potential sociolinguistic resistance to computationally-generated vocabulary or test actual language community adoption of the proposed neologisms

## Confidence
- Computational methodology: High - The phonological filtering approach is well-grounded in linguistic principles and the database processing is straightforward
- Practical impact: Medium - The low translation rates and absence of user acceptance testing limit confidence in real-world utility
- Sociolinguistic claims: Low - Assertions about challenging linguistic dominance are not empirically validated beyond the computational results
