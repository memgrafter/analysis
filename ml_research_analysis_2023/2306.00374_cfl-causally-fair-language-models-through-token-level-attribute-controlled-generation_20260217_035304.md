# CFL: Causally Fair Language Models Through Token-level Attribute Controlled Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00374
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00374
- Reference count: 19
- The paper proposes a causally fair language model (CFL) framework to mitigate toxicity in text generation while reducing unintended bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a causally fair language model (CFL) framework that addresses toxicity in text generation through token-level causal attribution. The approach uses counterfactual augmentation and Average Treatment Effect (ATE) scores to identify each token's contribution to toxic attributes like toxicity, offense, abuse, and hate. By building a structural causal model (SCM) from these token-level effects, the framework can fine-tune pre-trained language models to reduce toxic outputs while mitigating unintended bias against protected groups. The CFL approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on toxic degeneration metrics, particularly for non-toxic prompts, while maintaining low perplexity and reducing bias.

## Method Summary
The CFL framework computes token-level ATE scores by generating counterfactual sentences through masked language model replacements, measuring how each token's removal affects toxicity probability. These ATE scores are combined recursively using Lp norms (L1, L∞) in an SCM to compute sentence-level attribute scores. The pre-trained language model is then fine-tuned using these attribute scores as losses, alternating between toxic prompts (to reduce toxicity) and non-toxic data (to maintain language quality). The approach claims to distinguish true causal effects of tokens on toxicity from spurious correlations, enabling more nuanced detoxification that avoids over-penalizing mentions of protected groups.

## Key Results
- CFL achieves state-of-the-art performance on toxic degeneration metrics, especially for non-toxic prompts
- The framework maintains low perplexity while reducing toxicity
- CFL mitigates unintended bias against protected groups as measured on the BOLD dataset

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Token-level ATE scores allow precise attribution of each token's contribution to toxic attributes, reducing spurious correlations.
- Mechanism: By masking each token and generating counterfactual sentences, the model measures the average change in toxicity probability when that token is replaced. This isolates the causal effect of the token rather than relying on observed co-occurrence patterns.
- Core assumption: The replacement token generation (via MLM) produces realistic counterfactuals that preserve the sentence structure while changing the token's influence on the attribute.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We employ counterfactual generation to obtain token level Average Treatment Effect (ATE) scores"; [section 3.3] "The change in probability of attribute, on replacement of token t in a sentence may be thought of as the treatment effect (TE)."
- Break condition: If the MLM model fails to generate realistic replacements, the ATE scores may not reflect true causal effects, and spurious correlations may persist.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The SCM built from token-level ATE scores computes attribute scores for entire sentences efficiently, enabling fine-tuning without expensive classifier inference at each step.
- Mechanism: The SCM recursively combines ATE scores using Lp norms (L1, L∞) to produce a scalar attribute loss for a sentence. This loss is then used to update the LM parameters directly, bypassing the need for repeated classifier evaluations.
- Core assumption: The recursive combination of ATE scores accurately reflects the overall attribute intensity of the sentence.
- Evidence anchors: [section 3.4] "We describe a recursive method to compute the attribute score of a sentence"; [abstract] "Our architecture is based on a Structural Causal Model (SCM) that is mathematically transparent and computationally efficient."
- Break condition: If the recursive combination does not capture the true attribute intensity, the fine-tuning signal may be incorrect, leading to suboptimal detoxification.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fine-tuning on toxic prompts while using the SCM loss from non-toxic data mitigates unintended bias against protected groups.
- Mechanism: By training the LM to reduce attribute scores only when they arise from spurious correlations (identified via ATE), the model learns to avoid toxic outputs without over-penalizing mentions of protected groups that are not causally linked to toxicity.
- Core assumption: The ATE computation is not biased by the distribution of protected group mentions in the training data, so the causal attribution is fair.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We also show that CFL mitigates the unintended bias problem through experiments on the BOLD dataset"; [section 5.5] "We measure average LM losses per sentence with respect to the baseline model as measured over prompts from the BOLD dataset."
- Break condition: If the ATE computation itself inherits bias from the toxicity classifier, the mitigation of unintended bias will fail.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Counterfactual reasoning in causal inference
  - Why needed here: To distinguish true causal effects of tokens on toxicity from mere correlations, which is essential for avoiding over-filtering of protected groups.
  - Quick check question: If a token like "Muslim" frequently appears in toxic sentences in the data, how does counterfactual reasoning prevent the model from learning that "Muslim" is inherently toxic?

- Concept: Structural Causal Models (SCMs) and recursive attribute computation
  - Why needed here: To efficiently compute sentence-level attribute scores from token-level ATE scores, enabling scalable fine-tuning without repeated classifier inference.
  - Quick check question: How does the recursive combination of ATE scores using Lp norms (L1 vs L∞) affect the final attribute score, and why might one choose one norm over another?

- Concept: Average Treatment Effect (ATE) in NLP
  - Why needed here: To quantify the average causal impact of each token on the toxicity attribute across many contexts, forming the basis for the SCM.
  - Quick check question: Why is it important to average the treatment effect over all sentences containing a token, rather than just using the effect from a single sentence?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-trained LM -> ATE computation pipeline -> SCM -> Fine-tuning loop
- Critical path: 1. Compute ATE scores for all tokens using counterfactual generation; 2. Build SCM lookup tables from ATE scores; 3. Fine-tune LM on toxic prompts using SCM losses; 4. Alternate with CLM losses on non-toxic data
- Design tradeoffs: ATE computation is a one-time cost but requires significant GPU time; benefits are realized in efficient fine-tuning. Using Lp norms for attribute aggregation allows flexibility but requires choosing the right norm for the task. Alternating between toxic and non-toxic data balances detoxification and language quality but complicates the training schedule.
- Failure signatures: If ATE scores are inaccurate, the SCM will provide incorrect attribute signals, leading to poor detoxification or unintended bias. If the recursive SCM aggregation does not reflect true attribute intensity, the LM may not learn the correct detoxification behavior. If the fine-tuning schedule is not well-tuned, the model may degrade in language quality or fail to detoxify effectively.
- First 3 experiments: 1. Compute ATE scores for a small subset of tokens using counterfactual generation and verify that they differ from simple co-occurrence-based toxicity scores. 2. Build a simple SCM using ATE scores and test it on a held-out set of sentences to ensure it produces reasonable attribute scores. 3. Fine-tune a small LM on a small set of toxic prompts using the SCM loss and evaluate if toxicity decreases without harming language quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly call out specific open questions, but several limitations and areas for future work are discussed:

- The potential for distribution shifts between datasets used for ATE computation, fine-tuning, and testing
- The need for more generalizable and multilingual solutions
- The possibility that detoxification approaches optimized for automatic toxicity metrics might not perform equally well on human evaluations
- The potential misuse of controlled generation methods by extremist groups

## Limitations

- The quality of counterfactual generation is not empirically validated, raising concerns about whether ATE scores reflect true causal effects
- The recursive SCM aggregation mechanism lacks theoretical justification for choosing specific Lp norms
- Unintended bias mitigation is primarily demonstrated through perplexity reduction rather than direct bias-specific metrics
- The approach is evaluated only on English language datasets, limiting generalizability

## Confidence

- High confidence in the overall detoxification performance metrics (expected toxicity, perplexity)
- Medium confidence in the causal attribution mechanism's ability to distinguish spurious from causal toxicity
- Low confidence in the direct evidence for unintended bias mitigation beyond perplexity reduction

## Next Checks

1. **Counterfactual Quality Validation**: Conduct a human evaluation study where annotators rate whether the counterfactual sentences generated by the MLM preserve the original sentence's meaning while appropriately changing the token's influence on toxicity. This would validate whether the ATE scores reflect true causal effects.

2. **Ablation Study on SCM Aggregation**: Systematically test different aggregation methods (L1, L2, L∞, sum, max) and correlate their performance with specific detoxification outcomes and bias metrics. This would clarify whether the recursive combination mechanism is critical to achieving causal fairness.

3. **Bias-Specific Evaluation**: Apply standard bias detection metrics (such as those from BOLD) directly to the generated text, not just to perplexity scores. This would provide concrete evidence that the causal framework successfully mitigates unintended bias against protected groups.
