# On the Copying Problem of Unsupervised NMT: A Training Schedule with a Language Discriminator Loss

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17182
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17182
- Reference count: 22
- Key outcome: Language discriminator loss significantly reduces copying in UNMT, especially on low-resource and distant pairs, improving BLEU by 0.44-0.69 (Zh-En) and up to 1.55 (Kk-En).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the copying problem in unsupervised neural machine translation (UNMT), where models directly copy input sentences instead of translating, especially for low-resource and distant language pairs. The authors introduce a language discriminator loss that constrains the first token of the intermediate translation to be in the target language, reducing copying behavior. Experiments on WMT and Multi30K datasets show that this method significantly reduces copying ratios and improves BLEU scores, with the greatest gains on low-resource and distant language pairs.

## Method Summary
The method adds a language discriminator loss during back-translation to force the intermediate translation to be in the correct target language. A language discriminator (LD) classifies the first-time-step outputs of the decoder, and gradients are backpropagated to the main model. The model is trained with denoising autoencoding (DAE) and back-translation (BT) objectives, using cross-lingual pretrained models (XLM) with tied embeddings. The LD is trained during DAE and frozen during BT, with a tunable weight λLD for the loss contribution.

## Key Results
- The language discriminator loss reduces copying ratios significantly, especially on low-resource (Guj-En, Kk-En) and distant language pairs.
- BLEU scores improve by 0.44-0.69 on Chinese-English and up to 1.55 on Kazakh-English.
- On similar, high-resource language pairs (En-Fr, En-De), the method has smaller gains or may slightly harm performance if λLD is not optimally tuned.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The language discriminator loss reduces copying by constraining the first token of the intermediate translation to be in the correct target language.
- Mechanism: The discriminator classifies the first-time-step decoder output, and gradients are backpropagated to the main model to encourage target-language embeddings for the first generated token.
- Core assumption: The first generated token strongly influences subsequent tokens, so enforcing its language improves overall translation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The loss "imposes constraints on the intermediate translation so that the translation is in the desired language."
  - [section 2.2] "We could simply force the first token to be in the correct language, because the first generated token will influence the generation of all the subsequent tokens."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: neighboring work focuses on low-resource or GAN methods, not language-discriminator based copying prevention.
- Break condition: If the first token's embedding space is too entangled across languages, or if the discriminator is poorly trained, the constraint may not effectively reduce copying.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The copying problem in UNMT is amplified by the tied weight architecture that shares embeddings across languages.
- Mechanism: Tied embeddings allow the decoder to directly output source-language tokens, especially when no constraint is imposed, leading to copying shortcuts during back-translation.
- Core assumption: Without explicit supervision on the target language, the model will exploit the shared embedding space to copy rather than translate.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "The representations of tokens from two languages are shared in the same space...it unfortunately also allows the models to generate a token in an unexpected language at any time step."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: neighbors discuss subword embeddings and morphological modeling, but not explicitly tied embedding effects on copying.
- Break condition: If separate decoders or language-specific embeddings are used, this copying shortcut may be blocked, but cross-lingual transfer may suffer.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Adding a language discriminator loss reduces copying more effectively on distant and low-resource language pairs than on similar, high-resource ones.
- Mechanism: On distant or low-resource pairs, the baseline model has weaker cross-lingual alignment, so copying is more frequent; the discriminator loss provides needed guidance. On similar, high-resource pairs, the baseline already learns good alignment, so additional loss can distract training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4] "The experiments on the small dataset Multi30K indicate that issue (1) is not the major obstacle when two similar languages are considered... In such a case, it is the issue (2) that prevents the model from performing the actual translation."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: neighbors focus on consistency regularization and GAN for low-resource NMT, not explicitly on copying reduction by language discrimination.
- Break condition: If the discriminator is not well calibrated to the distance between languages, it may over-constrain and hurt performance on high-resource pairs.

  - Why needed here: Understanding how intermediate translation is generated without parallel data is key to seeing why copying happens and how the discriminator loss helps.
  - Quick check question: In back-translation, which direction (source→target or target→source) is considered the "intermediate" step that the discriminator constrains?

  - Why needed here: The method relies on a shared encoder/decoder initialized from cross-lingual PLMs, so understanding their cross-lingual alignment properties explains the copying risk.
  - Quick check question: What is the main advantage and disadvantage of tying the decoder's final layer to the shared embedding layer in UNMT?

- Concept: Language discrimination and adversarial training
  - Why needed here: The discriminator loss is inspired by adversarial methods that separate language-specific representations, so knowing how these work clarifies the design.
  - Quick check question: In this work, does the discriminator encourage language-agnostic or language-specific decoder outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Encoder (fixed, shared, cross-lingual) -> Decoder (trainable, tied final layer) -> Language Discriminator (FFNN, 2-layer, target language classifier)
- Critical path: Pretrained model → DAE step (update encoder/decoder, train LD) → BT step (freeze LD, update encoder/decoder with LD loss)
- Design tradeoffs: Tied embeddings allow cross-lingual sharing but enable copying; separate decoders prevent copying but hurt cross-lingual transfer.
- Failure signatures: High copying ratios, BLEU scores not improving despite reduced copying, or inconsistent LD loss weights causing training instability.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train vanilla UNMT on Multi30K, measure copying ratio and BLEU.
  2. Add LD loss with λLD=0.01, compare copying ratio and BLEU.
  3. Sweep λLD over {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100}, plot copying ratio vs BLEU to find optimal weight.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal weight for the language discriminator loss (λLD) that balances alleviating the copying problem and maintaining translation quality across different language pairs and dataset sizes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explores different weights for λLD and finds that moderate weights (e.g., λLD = 1) are optimal, but notes that the optimal weight might vary depending on the dataset size and language pairs involved.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not conduct an exhaustive search for the optimal λLD across various language pairs and dataset sizes. It only experiments with a limited set of weights and suggests that future research could explore adapting the weight to different scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study varying λLD across a wide range of language pairs (high-resource, low-resource, similar, and distant) and dataset sizes, measuring both the copying ratio and translation quality (e.g., BLEU scores), would provide insights into the optimal weight for each scenario.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the language discriminator loss affect the cross-lingual alignment information learned by the model, and can it be combined with other techniques to improve cross-lingual representation learning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that the language discriminator loss does not provide additional cross-lingual information but acts as implicit supervision to prevent copying. It also mentions that the copying problem might be related to the lack of cross-lingual alignment information, especially for distant language pairs.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the impact of the language discriminator loss on the quality of cross-lingual alignment learned by the model. It also does not explore combining this loss with other techniques aimed at improving cross-lingual representation learning, such as static cross-lingual embeddings or adversarial training.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the cross-lingual alignment quality (e.g., using metrics like CSLS or MUSE) of models trained with and without the language discriminator loss, and studies combining the loss with other cross-lingual representation learning techniques, would shed light on its impact and potential synergies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the language discriminator loss be extended to multilingual settings with more than two languages, and how would it handle the increased complexity of distinguishing between multiple language domains?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on bilingual unsupervised machine translation and mentions that the copying problem is also relevant in multilingual NMT. However, it does not explore the applicability of the language discriminator loss to multilingual settings.
- Why unresolved: The paper only considers two languages in its experiments, and the language discriminator is designed to distinguish between two language domains. Extending this to multilingual settings would require modifications to the discriminator and potentially new strategies for handling the increased complexity of distinguishing between multiple languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying the language discriminator loss to multilingual UNMT models with more than two languages, and comparing the copying ratio and translation quality with monolingual and bilingual baselines, would demonstrate its effectiveness and limitations in multilingual settings.

## Limitations
- The method's effectiveness depends on the optimal choice of the loss weight λLD, which may vary across language pairs and dataset sizes.
- The approach may not be as effective for similar, high-resource language pairs, where the baseline model already learns good cross-lingual alignment.
- The reliance on the first token to guide the entire translation may not always be sufficient, especially for longer sentences or more complex translation scenarios.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The method's ability to reduce copying ratios is well-supported by the reported results across multiple language pairs, especially for low-resource and distant languages.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that the method improves BLEU scores is supported by the results, but the magnitude of improvement varies significantly across language pairs and may depend on the choice of λLD.
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that the copying problem is primarily due to tied embeddings and that the first token's language is the main determinant of subsequent tokens is plausible but not rigorously proven; other factors (e.g., data quality, model capacity) may also play a role.

## Next Checks

2. **Ablation Studies**: Remove or modify the language discriminator loss and observe the impact on copying ratio and BLEU. For example, apply the discriminator loss to multiple time steps instead of just the first, or use a different classifier architecture, to test the necessity and sufficiency of the current design.

3. **Cross-Lingual Transfer Evaluation**: Test the method on a broader set of language pairs, including those with varying degrees of similarity and resource availability, to confirm the generalizability of the findings and to identify any failure cases where the method may not be effective.
