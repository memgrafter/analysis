# Iterated Decomposition: Improving Science Q&A by Supervising Reasoning Processes

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2301.01751
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.01751
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Iterated decomposition improves Q&A accuracy from 38% to 69% on Qasper dataset

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces iterated decomposition, a human-in-the-loop workflow for developing compositional language model programs that significantly improves performance on complex scientific tasks. The approach starts with minimal task decomposition, evaluates results, uses visualization tools to identify failures, and iteratively refines components through further decomposition or adjustments. Applied to three case studies (placebo extraction, participant adherence classification, and NLP question answering), the method demonstrates substantial accuracy improvements over baseline approaches while maintaining interpretability of the reasoning process.

## Method Summary
The iterated decomposition workflow involves four main steps: (1) initial task decomposition into subtasks, (2) execution and evaluation of the decomposition on test cases, (3) failure identification using the Interactive Composition Explorer (ICE) visualizer to trace execution and pinpoint problematic components, and (4) refinement through further decomposition, additional context, or other adjustments. The process iterates until performance goals are met. The approach uses a combination of selection models (monoT5) for finding relevant information and generation models (text-davinci-002) for producing answers, with human experts guiding the decomposition and refinement process based on visualized execution traces.

## Key Results
- Placebo extraction accuracy improved from 25% to 65% on held-out test sets
- Participant adherence classification improved from 53% to 70%
- Qasper NLP Q&A accuracy improved from 38% to 69%
- Demonstrated the effectiveness of process supervision over outcome supervision

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Decomposing tasks into interpretable subtasks improves performance by making it easier for LMs to use long context effectively
- **Mechanism**: Breaking complex tasks into smaller, independent subtasks allows LMs to better focus on relevant information within limited context windows and reduce confusion from contradictory evidence
- **Core assumption**: The task can be meaningfully decomposed into subtasks that preserve or improve performance
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "We improve the performance of compositions by zooming in on failing components and refining them through decomposition, additional context, chain of thought, etc."
  - [section]: "The baseline fails primarily by failing to make good use of the long context of the paper"
- **Break condition**: When subtasks become too granular and the overhead of managing multiple calls outweighs benefits

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Process supervision with human-in-the-loop iteration improves accuracy by allowing targeted refinement of failing components
- **Mechanism**: Humans identify specific failure points through visualization tools, then improve those components through further decomposition or adjustments, creating a feedback loop that progressively enhances performance
- **Core assumption**: Human expertise can effectively diagnose and correct LM errors in the decomposition
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Iterated decomposition, a human-in-the-loop workflow for developing and refining compositional LM programs"
  - [section]: "We improve the performance of compositions by zooming in on failing components and refining them through decomposition"
- **Break condition**: When human supervision becomes too costly relative to the performance gains achieved

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Visualization tools enable effective process supervision by making LM execution traces interpretable to humans
- **Mechanism**: ICE records and visualizes function calls, inputs, outputs, and intermediate values, allowing developers to trace failures to specific components and understand the reasoning process
- **Core assumption**: Making intermediate states observable improves debugging and refinement of LM programs
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: "To support the error identification step in iterated decomposition, we have developed the Interactive Composition Explorer (ICE), an open-source debugger and execution trace visualizer"
  - [section]: "ICE provides three views into this data: 1. An expandable tree of function calls shown in order of execution"
- **Break condition**: When visualization complexity overwhelms the ability to extract actionable insights

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Task definition -> Initial decomposition -> Execution engine -> ICE visualizer -> Evaluation -> Human refinement -> Improved decomposition

- **Critical path**:
  1. Define initial task decomposition
  2. Execute decomposition on test cases
  3. Evaluate results using gold standards
  4. Use ICE to identify failing components
  5. Refine failing components through further decomposition or adjustments
  6. Repeat until performance goals are met

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Granularity vs. overhead: More subtasks provide better focus but increase complexity
  - Automation vs. human oversight: More automation reduces costs but may miss subtle errors
  - Generalizability vs. specialization: More specialized approaches work better for specific tasks but don't transfer

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Selection failures: Wrong context chosen for generation
  - Generation failures: Incorrect answers despite correct context
  - Coordination failures: Subtasks not properly integrated
  - Interpretation failures: Human unable to understand trace outputs

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Implement baseline select-then-generate approach for a simple question-answering task
  2. Apply basic decomposition (e.g., find relevant section, then answer) and measure improvement
  3. Use ICE to analyze failures and implement targeted refinements to failing components

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can we automate the iterated decomposition workflow so that language models can create, debug, and refine task decompositions with minimal human intervention?

2. What developer tools and infrastructure are needed to significantly speed up the iteration cycle from overall task results to identifying failure sources and proposing solutions?

3. How can we reduce the boundary between task design and execution so that decompositions, debugging, and iteration can happen at runtime during complex task execution?

4. What techniques can we develop to reduce the computational cost of complex decompositions so they become more cost-competitive with end-to-end execution approaches?

5. How can we make developer tools for process supervision machine-accessible so that the diagnosis and improvement steps can be learned by language models?

## Limitations
- The human-in-the-loop requirement may become prohibitively expensive for tasks requiring extensive decomposition
- Performance gains appear heavily dependent on prompt engineering and few-shot examples not fully specified in the paper
- The method was tested on three specific case studies in scientific domains, limiting generalizability

## Confidence

**High Confidence (8/10)**:
- The iterated decomposition workflow is technically sound and logically coherent
- The ICE visualization tool effectively supports debugging and refinement
- The three case studies demonstrate measurable performance improvements

**Medium Confidence (6/10)**:
- The magnitude of performance improvements (25%→65%, 53%→70%, 38%→69%) is well-documented
- The core claim that decomposition improves performance by enabling better use of long context
- The assertion that process supervision is more effective than outcome supervision

**Low Confidence (4/10)**:
- Claims about the approach's scalability to arbitrary complex tasks
- Generalizability across diverse domains without adaptation
- Long-term cost-effectiveness compared to alternative approaches

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-Domain Transferability Test**: Apply the iterated decomposition workflow to a completely different domain (e.g., legal document analysis or financial reports) without adapting the core methodology, measuring performance relative to domain-specific baselines.

2. **Human Cost Analysis**: Conduct a detailed time-motion study quantifying the actual human effort required at each iteration step, comparing this to the performance gains achieved to determine the break-even point where additional iteration becomes cost-prohibitive.

3. **Prompt Robustness Experiment**: Systematically vary the prompts and few-shot examples used in each decomposition step while keeping the workflow structure constant, measuring how sensitive performance is to these variations and establishing confidence intervals for expected improvements.
