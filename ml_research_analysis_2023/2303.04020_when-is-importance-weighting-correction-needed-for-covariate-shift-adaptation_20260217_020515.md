# When is Importance Weighting Correction Needed for Covariate Shift Adaptation?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04020
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04020
- Reference count: 12
- One-line primary result: IW correction is necessary for nonparametric and misspecified models under covariate shift, extending classic results from parametric to nonparametric settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates when importance weighting (IW) correction is needed for covariate shift adaptation in supervised learning. The authors analyze IW-corrected kernel ridge regression (KRR) and show that the IW correction is necessary when the model is nonparametric and misspecified. Specifically, they demonstrate that IW-KRR converges to the projection of the true regression function for the test distribution, while standard KRR converges to the projection for the training distribution. This result extends the classic work of Shimodaira (2000) on parametric models and recovers recent results on nonparametric models as special cases. The paper also analyzes IW-KRR using generic weighting functions and clipped IW functions, showing potential improvements in convergence rates.

## Method Summary
The paper analyzes importance weighting-corrected kernel ridge regression (IW-KRR) for covariate shift adaptation. The method involves defining an IW function as the ratio of test and training input densities, then incorporating this into a regularized least-squares objective. The analysis covers various settings including parametric/nonparametric models, well-specified/misspecified cases, and arbitrary weighting functions. The authors derive convergence rates under assumptions on the target function smoothness, weighting function moments, and effective dimension of the problem.

## Key Results
- IW correction is necessary for nonparametric and misspecified models because it ensures convergence to the projection under the test distribution rather than the training distribution
- The convergence rate depends on the smoothness of the target function (quantified by r) and the degree of covariate shift (quantified by q)
- Clipped IW functions can improve convergence rates by balancing bias and variance in the IW-KRR predictor

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: IW correction is necessary for nonparametric and misspecified models because the IW-KRR predictor must converge to the projection of the true regression function under the test distribution.
- Mechanism: The IW-KRR predictor using the correct IW function converges to a data-free limit that approximates the projection of the regression function under the test input distribution, while standard KRR without IW converges to the projection under the training distribution. This difference matters when the model is misspecified and the two projections differ.
- Core assumption: The projection of the regression function onto the RKHS exists and is unique, and it depends on the input distribution used for defining the projection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We examine the missing case in the literature where the model is nonparametric and misspecified, and show that the IW correction is needed for obtaining the best approximation of the true unknown function for the test distribution."
  - [section]: "If the weights are uniform as in the standard KRR, the predictor converges to the projection for the training input distribution. Therefore, under a covariate shift, the standard KRR is inconsistent as an estimator of the projection for the test distribution."
- Break condition: If the RKHS contains the regression function (well-specified case) or if the RKHS is universal, the projection becomes identical to the regression function and is independent of the input distribution, making IW correction unnecessary.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The convergence rate of IW-KRR depends on the degree of misspecification and the degree of covariate shift, quantified by constants r and q.
- Mechanism: The constant r in the source condition quantifies the smoothness of the target function, with larger r implying faster convergence. The constant q in the moment condition on the IW function quantifies the degree of covariate shift, with larger q implying slower convergence. These factors combine to determine the overall convergence rate.
- Core assumption: Assumptions 2 and 3 hold with constants r and q that characterize the smoothness of the target function and the degree of covariate shift, respectively.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We do this by analyzing IW-corrected kernel ridge regression, covering a variety of settings, including parametric and nonparametric models, well-specified and misspecified settings, and arbitrary weighting functions."
  - [section]: "The constant r in Assumption 2 quantifies the smoothness of the target function fH. Therefore, as r increases, the problem becomes easier, and the rate (21) becomes faster. The constant q in Assumption 3 quantifies the degree of covariate shift on the hardness of the learning problem."
- Break condition: If the IW function is bounded (q=0), the convergence rate matches the optimal rate of standard KRR without covariate shift, and the effects of misspecification and covariate shift are minimized.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Clipped IW functions can improve convergence rates by balancing bias and variance in the IW-KRR predictor.
- Mechanism: Clipping the IW function at a threshold D introduces a bias but can reduce the variance of the IW-KRR predictor. The choice of the threshold D and the regularization constant λ optimally balances this bias-variance tradeoff, leading to faster convergence rates even when the IW function is unbounded.
- Core assumption: The clipping threshold D increases at an appropriate rate as the sample size n increases, as specified in Theorem 10.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We also analyze the IW-KRR using a generic weighting function and a clipped IW function, showing that the latter can improve convergence rates."
  - [section]: "Theorem 10 shows that the IW-KRR predictor using the clipped IW function wD converges to the target function fH as the sample size n increases, if the clipping threshold D increases at an appropriate rate as n increases."
- Break condition: If the clipping threshold D is not chosen appropriately or if the IW function is already bounded, the benefits of clipping may not materialize, and the convergence rate may not improve.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Kernel K -> RKHS H -> IW function w(x) -> Regularization parameter λ -> Training data -> Test data -> IW-KRR predictor

- Critical path: The critical path for implementing IW-KRR is as follows: (1) Choose a kernel function K and define the corresponding RKHS H. (2) Compute the IW function w from the training and test input distributions. (3) Given the training data and the IW function, construct the IW-KRR predictor by solving the regularized least-squares problem. (4) Evaluate the performance of the IW-KRR predictor on the test data using appropriate metrics, such as the L2 distance or the expected prediction error.

- Design tradeoffs: The choice of the kernel function K and the regularization parameter λ involves a tradeoff between model complexity and generalization. A more complex kernel (e.g., a high-degree polynomial kernel) may lead to a richer hypothesis space but may also increase the risk of overfitting. Similarly, a smaller regularization parameter λ may lead to a more complex model but may also increase the risk of overfitting. The choice of the IW function w also involves a tradeoff, as a more accurate IW function may lead to better performance but may also be more difficult to estimate from the data.

- Failure signatures: The IW-KRR predictor may fail to generalize well if the kernel function K is not appropriate for the data, if the regularization parameter λ is not chosen properly, or if the IW function w is not accurate. In particular, if the IW function is not bounded (i.e., q > 0 in Assumption 3), the convergence rate of IW-KRR may be slow, and the predictor may not perform well on the test data. Additionally, if the model is misspecified (i.e., the RKHS does not contain the regression function), the IW-KRR predictor may converge to a suboptimal solution.

## Limitations

- The analysis assumes that the kernel K is bounded, which may not hold for all kernels. The authors mention that the results can be extended to unbounded kernels, but the specific conditions and convergence rates may differ. (Confidence: Medium)
- The paper focuses on the L2 distance with respect to the test input measure ρte_X as the performance metric. While this is a standard choice, other metrics, such as the expected prediction error, may provide additional insights into the performance of IW-KRR. (Confidence: High)
- The authors assume that the training and test distributions have the same marginal output distribution, which may not hold in practice. Extending the analysis to the case of different output distributions could be an interesting direction for future work. (Confidence: Medium)
