# Artificial Artificial Artificial Intelligence: Crowd Workers Widely Use Large Language Models for Text Production Tasks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07899
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07899
- Reference count: 17
- Researchers found that 33-46% of crowd workers used LLMs (primarily ChatGPT) when summarizing medical abstracts on MTurk.

## Executive Summary
This study reveals that a significant portion of crowd workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk are using Large Language Models like ChatGPT to complete text production tasks, specifically abstract summarization. The researchers developed a fine-tuned classifier that can detect ChatGPT-generated summaries with 97-99% accuracy by identifying consistent stylistic patterns in the AI-generated text. Using keystroke logging and overlap analysis, they validated their classifier's low false positive and false negative rates. Their findings suggest that LLM usage is widespread among crowd workers for text production tasks, raising concerns about the validity of crowdsourced data and the future of human annotation work.

## Method Summary
The researchers fine-tuned an e5-base model to classify text as synthetic or real, training on both human-written and ChatGPT-generated summaries. They used summary-level and abstract-level data splits, with post-hoc validation using keystroke data and overlap analysis. The classifier was applied to a dataset of 889 MTurk summaries to estimate LLM usage prevalence. Keystroke logging was used to validate classifier predictions, with summaries written without pasting assumed to be human-generated.

## Key Results
- 33-46% of crowd workers used LLMs (primarily ChatGPT) when summarizing medical abstracts on MTurk
- Classifier achieved 97-99% accuracy in distinguishing ChatGPT-generated summaries from human-written ones
- Keystroke validation showed low false positives (0% in sample of 5 summaries) and low false negatives (76% of summaries with <10% overlap flagged as synthetic)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: ChatGPT summaries have identifiable artifacts that can be used to distinguish them from human-written summaries
- Mechanism: ChatGPT exhibits consistency in its summarization style, creating patterns (fingerprints) that a fine-tuned classifier can detect. The classifier uses contrastive embeddings to capture these subtle differences in phrasing, structure, and content overlap
- Core assumption: ChatGPT's summarization output has consistent stylistic and structural characteristics across different abstracts
- Evidence anchors:
  - "Early work indicates that LLMs are remarkable data annotators, outperforming both crowd workers and experts"
  - "In the summary-level setting, our fine-tuned model achieved an accuracy of 99% and a macro-F1 of 99%. The high performance indicates a lack of diversity in ChatGPT's summarizations, a known issue in RLHF-trained LLMs"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Crowd workers use ChatGPT to increase productivity by copying task instructions into the model and pasting the output
- Mechanism: Workers copy the task instructions (which are already formatted as prompts) into ChatGPT, generate summaries, and paste them back into the MTurk interface, creating a workflow that maximizes their hourly earnings
- Core assumption: Workers have financial incentive to maximize productivity and will use available tools to do so
- Evidence anchors:
  - "crowd workers have financial incentives to use LLMs to increase their productivity and income"
  - "Under the assumption that pasted summaries that have very little to do with the original abstract are synthetically generated, we can get a sense of the false negative rate of our model"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The classifier can generalize across abstracts it hasn't seen during training
- Mechanism: By training on summary-level splits, the model learns universal features of ChatGPT-generated text that apply across different topics, allowing it to detect synthetic summaries even for abstracts not present in the training data
- Core assumption: ChatGPT's summarization artifacts are universal across different input topics
- Evidence anchors:
  - "Even not seeing a subset of abstracts during training, our model was still capable of successfully identifying synthetic summaries 97% of the time"
  - "human experimental subjects, annotators, and survey-takers remain critical of the validity of results derived from LLMs"

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the prevalence of LLM usage among crowd workers for tasks beyond text summarization, such as image annotation or data categorization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges uncertainty about generalization to other tasks, stating "Although our study specifically considers a text summarization task, we caution that any text production task whose instructions can be readily passed on to an LLM as a prompt are likely to be similarly affected."
- Why unresolved: The study focuses solely on text summarization, and while it suggests potential applicability to other tasks, empirical evidence is lacking.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting similar studies across diverse task types (e.g., image annotation, data categorization, survey completion) to measure LLM usage prevalence in each.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the prevalence of LLM usage among crowd workers change over time as LLMs become more advanced and accessible?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the "explosive popularity of LLMs" and notes that "LLMs are becoming more popular by the day," implying that usage rates may increase as these tools improve.
- Why unresolved: The study captures a snapshot in time (June 2023), but does not track changes in LLM usage prevalence over time.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking LLM usage among crowd workers at regular intervals as LLM capabilities and accessibility evolve.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the most effective methods for platforms, researchers, and crowd workers to ensure human-generated data remains human in the age of LLMs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper calls for "platforms, researchers, and crowd workers to find new ways to ensure that human data remain human," and proposes their keystroke logging and synthetic-text classification method as a potential starting point.
- Why unresolved: While the paper introduces a detection method, it acknowledges this is a first step and does not provide a comprehensive solution for preventing LLM usage in crowdsourced data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies evaluating the effectiveness of various detection and prevention methods (e.g., keystroke logging, watermarking, human verification) in different crowdsourcing scenarios.

## Limitations

- The study only examined one task type (medical abstract summarization) on one platform (MTurk), limiting generalizability to other crowdsourcing contexts or task types.
- The classifier's high accuracy was achieved on a relatively small dataset (366 real, 365 synthetic summaries), and performance on the full MTurk dataset shows more variability.
- The keystroke logging validation relies on conservative assumptions that may not account for all ways workers could interact with AI-generated content.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The classifier's ability to distinguish ChatGPT-generated summaries from human-written ones in the controlled experimental setting (99% accuracy on summary-level split). The keystroke logging validation showing very low false positive rates (0% in the sample of 5 summaries).

**Medium Confidence**: The overall estimate of 33-46% LLM usage among MTurk workers for this specific task. While the classifier shows good performance, the prevalence estimate depends on several assumptions about the distribution of synthetic vs. real summaries and the choice of logit threshold.

**Low Confidence**: Generalizability of findings to other task types, platforms, or time periods. The study's results are specific to medical abstract summarization on MTurk in early 2023, and ChatGPT's behavior or worker practices may have evolved since then.

## Next Checks

1. Apply the classifier to similar summarization tasks on other crowdsourcing platforms (Prolific, Figure Eight) to test generalizability across different worker populations and task formats.

2. Re-run the classifier on the same MTurk dataset after a significant time gap (e.g., 6-12 months) to assess whether ChatGPT's summarization patterns have changed enough to affect detection accuracy.

3. Have human annotators independently review a stratified sample of the MTurk summaries and compare their judgments against the classifier's predictions to better understand sources of disagreement and potential systematic biases.
