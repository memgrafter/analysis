# Modeling What-to-ask and How-to-ask for Answer-unaware Conversational Question Generation

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SG-CQG, a two-stage conversational question generation framework designed for answer-unaware settings. The method constructs a semantic graph to select coherent rationales and employs explicit control signals to determine question types. It demonstrates state-of-the-art performance on the CoQA benchmark while introducing a novel Conv-Distinct metric for measuring conversation diversity.

## Method Summary
SG-CQG operates in two stages: first, it builds a semantic graph to identify and select coherent rationales from the context; second, it uses explicit control signals to determine appropriate question types for generation. The framework addresses the challenge of generating conversational questions without knowledge of potential answers, focusing on maintaining context coverage and conversation diversity.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art performance on CoQA with Context Coverage of 68.52%
- Demonstrates Conv-Distinct score of 38.99% for conversation diversity
- Obtains BERTScore-entailment of 79.29% on the benchmark

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework's effectiveness stems from its two-stage approach that first identifies semantically relevant context through graph construction, then generates questions with appropriate types based on explicit control signals. This separation of concerns allows for more coherent question generation that maintains conversation flow while covering relevant context.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Context -> Semantic Graph Construction -> Rationale Selection -> Control Signal Generation -> Question Generation

Critical path: The semantic graph construction and rationale selection stages are critical, as they determine which context segments inform question generation.

Design tradeoffs: The framework trades computational complexity for improved conversation coherence and context coverage, using graph-based methods rather than simpler sequential approaches.

Failure signatures: Poor semantic graph construction may lead to incoherent question sequences; ineffective control signals may result in repetitive or irrelevant question types.
