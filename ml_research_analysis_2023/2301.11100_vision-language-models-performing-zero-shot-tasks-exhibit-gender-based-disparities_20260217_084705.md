# Vision-Language Models Performing Zero-Shot Tasks Exhibit Gender-based Disparities

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.11100
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11100
- Reference count: 6
- Zero-shot vision-language models (CLIP, Detic, LSeg) exhibit gender-based performance disparities

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that zero-shot vision-language models exhibit measurable gender-based performance disparities when classifying objects co-occurring with people of different perceived genders. The authors systematically evaluate CLIP, Detic, and LSeg across two large-scale datasets (Visual Genome and MS-COCO) and find consistent disparities that persist across individual concepts rather than being masked by aggregation. The study also reveals differences in model calibration (Expected Calibration Error) between gender groups and shows alignment between these disparities and existing gender biases in word embeddings. These findings highlight significant fairness concerns when deploying open-vocabulary vision models in real-world applications where gender diversity is present.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct zero-shot evaluations of CLIP, Detic, and LSeg on filtered subsets of Visual Genome (108k images, 408 object classes) and MS-COCO (123k images, 76 object classes) datasets. They preprocess images by mapping MS-COCO object categories to Visual Genome synsets and filter based on caption gender terms. For each model, they compute average precision (AP) differences between perceived male and female gender groups per concept, measure Expected Calibration Error (ECE) differences, and analyze alignment between concept embeddings and gender terms using word2vec cosine similarity. The study focuses on detecting whether objects co-occurring with people of different perceived genders are classified with different accuracy levels.

## Key Results
- All three models (CLIP, Detic, LSeg) exhibit consistent gender-based performance disparities across multiple datasets and concepts
- Disparities persist when analyzing individual concepts rather than aggregating across all concepts
- Model calibration differs by perceived gender, with measurable differences in Expected Calibration Error between gender groups
- Performance disparities align with existing gender biases in word embeddings from language models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Vision-language models exhibit gender-based performance disparities due to co-occurrence patterns between objects and people in training data. When certain objects co-occur more frequently with one gender in training images, the model learns stronger associations between those objects and that gender. During inference, these learned associations lead to better performance for images where the gender-object co-occurrence matches the training pattern. The mechanism assumes that training data distributions reflect societal gender associations that propagate through the model's learned representations.

### Component Map

### Critical Path

### Design Tradeoffs
Fixed vocabulary vs open vocabulary: Zero-shot models offer flexibility but inherit language biases
Pre-trained vs fine-tuned: Zero-shot evaluation reveals inherent model biases but may miss task-specific optimizations
Binary gender classification vs multi-category: Simplifies analysis but excludes non-binary identities

### Failure Signatures
Disparities masked by aggregation → Need per-concept analysis
Mismatched concept mappings → Verify synset correspondences carefully
Calibration differences without performance differences → Investigate confidence calibration separately from accuracy

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Do zero-shot vision-language models exhibit gender bias when performing tasks beyond image classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation? The paper focuses on these three specific tasks but acknowledges that zero-shot models can be applied to other vision tasks. Systematic evaluation of additional zero-shot vision tasks (e.g., image captioning, visual question answering) and model architectures for gender-based performance disparities would resolve this question.

### Open Question 2
Can the observed gender-based performance disparities be mitigated through fine-tuning or architectural modifications to the zero-shot vision-language models? The study is observational and does not explore potential mitigation strategies. Experiments comparing the performance of fine-tuned versions of the models or models with modified architectures against the original zero-shot models for gender-based disparities would resolve this question.

### Open Question 3
How do the gender-based performance disparities in zero-shot vision-language models compare to those in traditional supervised vision models with fixed vocabularies? The paper contrasts zero-shot models with traditional models that required task-specific labels and fine-tuning, but does not directly compare their performance disparities. Direct comparison of gender-based performance disparities between zero-shot and supervised vision models on the same tasks and datasets would resolve this question.

## Limitations

- Binarization of gender excludes non-binary gender labels and fails to capture the full spectrum of gender identities
- Reliance on external annotators' perceptions of gender introduces potential bias and uncertainty in ground truth labels
- Study does not investigate specific sources of disparities within training data or model architecture
- Observational study cannot establish causation between language model biases and vision task disparities

## Confidence

High: Gender-based performance disparities exist in zero-shot vision-language models
High: Disparities are consistent across concepts and not masked by aggregation
Medium: Model calibration differs by perceived gender
Low-Medium: Alignment between model disparities and word embedding biases does not establish causation

## Next Checks

1. Replicate with expanded gender categories using non-binary gender labels or continuous gender representations to assess whether observed disparities extend beyond binary classification
2. Conduct source attribution analysis through controlled experiments comparing models trained on gender-balanced versus imbalanced datasets to isolate data versus architecture effects
3. Design cross-modal bias propagation experiments to establish causal links between language model biases and vision task disparities through controlled training with debiased versus biased language embeddings
