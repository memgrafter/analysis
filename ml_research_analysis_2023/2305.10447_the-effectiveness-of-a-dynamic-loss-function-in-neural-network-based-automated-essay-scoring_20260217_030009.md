# The Effectiveness of a Dynamic Loss Function in Neural Network Based Automated Essay Scoring

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10447
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10447
- Reference count: 11
- Key outcome: Dynamic loss function achieves QWK score of 0.752 on ASAP dataset, outperforming previous LSTM-based systems

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental limitation in neural network-based automated essay scoring: regression models tend to predict only the mean of training data, especially with unbalanced datasets. The authors propose a dynamic loss function that combines standard mean squared error with a distribution-matching penalty, weighted by a time-varying parameter that shifts emphasis from error minimization to distribution prediction during training. The approach is evaluated on the ASAP AES dataset using an LSTM encoder with attention mechanism, achieving state-of-the-art performance for this architecture. The method demonstrates both improved prediction accuracy and prevention of underfitting in the scoring task.

## Method Summary
The proposed method introduces a dynamic loss function that addresses the tendency of regression models to predict only the mean of training data. The loss function combines standard mean squared error (MSE) with a distribution-matching term that penalizes deviation from the correct standard deviation of each batch. A time-varying weight parameter controls the relative importance of these components, starting with emphasis on error minimization and gradually shifting toward distribution prediction. The model architecture uses an LSTM encoder with attention mechanism to process essay text, and is trained on the ASAP AES dataset. The dynamic weighting allows the model to first learn accurate predictions while gradually learning to capture the full distribution of scores rather than collapsing to the mean.

## Key Results
- Achieves Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) score of 0.752 on ASAP dataset
- Outperforms previous LSTM-based automated essay scoring systems
- Successfully prevents underfitting while maintaining strong prediction accuracy
- Demonstrates effective handling of unbalanced datasets through distribution-aware training

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The dynamic loss function works by addressing the fundamental bias in regression models that causes them to predict the mean of the training distribution. By combining MSE with a distribution-matching penalty and using a time-varying weight, the model first learns to make accurate predictions while the network weights are still flexible. As training progresses and the weight shifts toward the distribution-matching term, the model learns to predict scores that match both the mean and variance of the true score distribution. This prevents the model from collapsing to a single mean prediction and enables it to capture the full range of possible scores, particularly important for unbalanced datasets where certain score ranges may be underrepresented.

## Foundational Learning

**Mean Squared Error (MSE)**: Standard regression loss that penalizes squared differences between predictions and targets. Needed because it provides the foundation for accurate point predictions. Quick check: Verify that basic MSE training works before adding distribution components.

**Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)**: Evaluation metric specifically designed for ordinal classification tasks like essay scoring. Needed because it measures agreement between predicted and actual score distributions while accounting for the ordinal nature of scores. Quick check: Calculate QWK on validation set after each epoch.

**LSTM with Attention**: Sequence modeling architecture that processes text while focusing on relevant parts. Needed because essays are sequential text requiring context understanding. Quick check: Verify attention weights highlight relevant essay content.

**Batch Statistics**: Mean and standard deviation calculations over training batches. Needed because the dynamic loss function requires real-time distribution information. Quick check: Monitor batch mean/stdev to ensure they reflect the true score distribution.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Essay text -> Tokenizer -> LSTM Encoder -> Attention Layer -> Dense Layers -> Score Prediction

**Critical Path**: The attention mechanism over LSTM outputs is the critical path, as it determines which essay features most influence the final score prediction. The dynamic loss function directly influences how the LSTM and attention parameters are updated during training.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The choice of LSTM over transformers reduces computational cost but may limit the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies. The time-varying weight schedule requires careful tuning - too fast a shift may cause instability, while too slow may not fully address the mean prediction problem.

**Failure Signatures**: If the time-varying weight shifts too quickly, the model may become unstable and predictions may oscillate. If it shifts too slowly, the model may still collapse to mean prediction. Monitoring the balance between MSE and distribution loss terms helps identify these issues.

**First Experiments**:
1. Train with static loss function (no time-varying weight) to establish baseline behavior
2. Visualize predicted vs. actual score distributions to confirm mean collapse problem
3. Test different time-varying weight schedules to find optimal transition point

## Limitations
- Limited comparison to modern transformer-based models that have become standard since 2020
- Dataset (ASAP AES) is relatively small at 12,976 essays and may have domain-specific biases
- Heavy dependence on careful tuning of the time-varying weight parameter without extensive sensitivity analysis

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Experimental results on ASAP dataset demonstrating improved QWK scores compared to baseline LSTM models are reproducible and methodologically sound within the tested framework.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that the dynamic loss function addresses the "predicting only the mean" problem is supported by empirical evidence but could benefit from more extensive ablation studies and visualization of the learned distributions.

**Low Confidence**: The generalizability of the approach to other neural architectures (particularly transformers) and different essay scoring datasets remains unverified and should not be assumed without further testing.

## Next Checks

1. **Architecture Generalization Test**: Implement and evaluate the dynamic loss function with transformer-based architectures (BERT, RoBERTa) on the same ASAP dataset to determine if performance gains persist with more powerful models.

2. **Cross-Dataset Validation**: Test the approach on at least two additional automated essay scoring datasets from different domains or educational systems to assess generalizability beyond the ASAP corpus.

3. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis**: Conduct systematic experiments varying the time-varying weight schedule parameters and batch size to establish the robustness of the method and identify optimal configurations.
