# VideoOFA: Two-Stage Pre-Training for Video-to-Text Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.03204
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03204
- Authors: Multiple authors from universities and research labs
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: Achieves state-of-the-art performance on four video captioning benchmarks, improving average CIDEr score by 9.7 points

## Executive Summary
VideoOFA introduces a two-stage pre-training framework for video-to-text generation tasks. The approach first pre-trains a generative encoder-decoder model on massive image-text data to learn fundamental vision-language concepts, then adapts this knowledge through intermediate video-text pre-training to acquire video-specific skills like spatio-temporal reasoning. By leveraging the abundance of image-text data and combining it with targeted video pre-training, VideoOFA achieves new state-of-the-art results across multiple video captioning and open-ended video question answering benchmarks, outperforming existing methods by significant margins.

## Method Summary
VideoOFA employs a two-stage pre-training strategy where an OFA-large generative encoder-decoder model is first pre-trained on image-text data using tasks like image captioning, visual question answering, and conditional image generation. The model is then adapted to video data through intermediate pre-training on three video-text datasets (S-MiT, Kinetics 700-2020, and HowTo100M) using three tasks: video captioning, video-text matching, and contrastive frame order modeling. For video input, the model preserves the image tokenization from the pre-trained backbone while adding temporal embeddings to indicate frame position, avoiding the computational complexity of 3D patch tokenization. The final model is fine-tuned on downstream video captioning and video QA tasks.

## Key Results
- Achieves new state-of-the-art performance on four video captioning benchmarks (MSRVTT, MSVD, LSMDC, DiDeMo) with an average CIDEr score improvement of 9.7 points
- Outperforms existing models on two open-ended video question answering datasets (ActivityQA, NExT-QA)
- Ablation studies show that all three intermediate pre-training tasks (video captioning, video-text matching, contrastive FOM) are beneficial
- VideoOFA-FiD variant reduces GPU memory usage by up to 60% compared to the full attention version while maintaining competitive performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Two-stage pre-training enables better transfer from image-text to video-text tasks
- Mechanism: Pre-training on abundant image-text data teaches fundamental vision-language concepts. Intermediate video-text pre-training adapts these representations to video-specific skills like temporal reasoning before fine-tuning
- Core assumption: The image-text pre-trained model has learned transferable visual-language representations that can be effectively adapted to video tasks
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "A generative encoder-decoder model is first jointly pre-trained on massive image-text data to learn fundamental vision-language concepts, and then adapted to video data in an intermediate video-text pre-training stage to learn video-specific skills such as spatio-temporal reasoning."
  - [section]: "We instead propose a straightforward approach where we simply sparsely sample frames from an input video and concatenate the image tokens from each video frame to form a single sequence of tokens."
  - [corpus]: Weak. No direct citations supporting this specific two-stage claim, though related work exists on video-language pre-training
- Break condition: If the image-text pre-training does not produce representations that transfer well to video, or if the intermediate pre-training stage does not effectively teach video-specific skills

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Preserving the image tokenization while adding temporal embeddings maintains the integrity of the pre-trained backbone
- Mechanism: Instead of treating video as 3D patches (which changes the tokenization), the model treats video as a sequence of frames, each tokenized as in the image backbone. Temporal embeddings are added to indicate frame position
- Core assumption: The pre-trained image backbone's tokenization is optimal and should not be changed when adapting to video
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We instead propose to preserve the image tokenization in the backbone model, and simply formulate the video as a sequence of images (frames)."
  - [section]: "We then add an additional positional embedding to indicate which frame each token is from, and these temporal embeddings are zero-initialized learnable parameters added on top of the spatial position embeddings."
  - [corpus]: Weak. While the approach is described, there's no direct evidence comparing this to 3D patch approaches in the corpus
- Break condition: If the added temporal embeddings are insufficient to capture video-specific information, or if the frame-level tokenization misses important video dynamics

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The combination of video captioning, video-text matching, and contrastive frame order modeling provides the most effective intermediate pre-training
- Mechanism: Video captioning teaches generation, video-text matching teaches alignment, and contrastive FOM teaches temporal reasoning without requiring generation of undesirable artifacts
- Core assumption: Each pre-training task teaches a distinct and complementary skill needed for video-to-text generation
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We conduct an empirical study on various IPT strategies to select the optimal solution... and find that all three tasks are beneficial."
  - [section]: "In Table 4, row 7 uses both while row 8 only uses contrastive FOM. We observe that adding the generative FOM results in lower performance overall..."
  - [corpus]: Weak. The ablation study is described in the paper, but there's no external corpus evidence validating this specific combination
- Break condition: If one or more of these tasks does not actually contribute to downstream performance, or if a different combination would be more effective

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Video frames -> Backbone tokenization -> Temporal embeddings -> Transformer layers -> Text generation
- Critical path: Video frames → Backbone tokenization → Temporal embeddings → Transformer layers → Text generation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Full attention vs. local attention: Full attention allows maximum interaction but is computationally expensive. Local attention is more efficient but may miss long-range dependencies
  - Image tokenization vs. 3D patch tokenization: Preserving image tokenization maintains the integrity of the pre-trained backbone but may miss video-specific information
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on downstream tasks despite good pre-training performance: Could indicate that the pre-training tasks are not effectively teaching the skills needed for the downstream tasks
  - High memory usage or slow training: Could indicate that the model architecture or pre-training strategy is not efficient enough
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation study on intermediate pre-training data: Train VideoOFA with different subsets of the three IPT datasets (S-MiT, Kinetics, HowTo100M) and evaluate on downstream tasks to determine the optimal combination
  2. Ablation study on intermediate pre-training tasks: Train VideoOFA with different combinations of the three IPT tasks (video captioning, video-text matching, FOM) and evaluate on downstream tasks to determine the optimal combination
  3. Architecture comparison: Compare the full VideoOFA model with the VideoOFA-FiD variant on downstream tasks to determine the impact of the attention mechanism on performance and efficiency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of VideoOFA change when using different backbone models instead of OFA, such as CLIP or GIT?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that the two-stage pre-training framework is compatible with more effective generative image-text backbone models that may come out in the future. It also mentions that CLIP4Caption achieved state-of-the-art performance by adapting CLIP to video captioning
- Why unresolved: The paper only experiments with OFA as the backbone model. While it suggests the framework is backbone-agnostic, it does not provide empirical evidence of performance with other backbones
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing VideoOFA performance using different backbone models (e.g., CLIP, GIT) on the same video captioning and video QA datasets

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of frames to sample from videos for the best trade-off between performance and computational efficiency?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that 8 frames are linearly sampled per video during both training and inference. It also mentions that VideoOFA-FiD can save up to 60% GPU memory compared to VideoOFA
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the impact of varying the number of sampled frames on model performance or efficiency. The choice of 8 frames appears to be arbitrary
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies showing VideoOFA performance on video captioning and QA tasks with different numbers of sampled frames (e.g., 4, 8, 16, 32) and corresponding GPU memory usage

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the two-stage pre-training framework perform on video-to-text tasks beyond captioning and question answering, such as video summarization or highlight detection?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on video captioning and video QA tasks. It mentions that future work could extend the framework to other tasks like text-to-video, video-to-image, and video-to-video generation
- Why unresolved: The paper does not experiment with other video-to-text tasks beyond captioning and QA. The generalization of the two-stage pre-training to these tasks is speculative
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying VideoOFA to video summarization or highlight detection tasks, comparing its performance to state-of-the-art models on those specific tasks

## Limitations

- The computational overhead of the full attention mechanism, while acknowledged, is not quantified in terms of training time or inference latency
- The model's scalability to longer videos (beyond 8 frames) and more diverse video domains remains untested
- The claim of 9.7 average CIDEr improvement requires careful interpretation as the magnitude varies significantly by dataset

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The empirical results showing VideoOFA outperforming existing models on the tested benchmarks. The methodology for two-stage pre-training is clearly described and reproducible
- **Medium confidence**: The mechanism claims about why two-stage pre-training works better. While the ablation studies support this, the underlying reasons are inferred rather than proven
- **Low confidence**: The claim that the specific combination of three pre-training tasks is optimal. The paper shows these tasks are beneficial, but does not explore the full space of possible pre-training strategies

## Next Checks

1. **External validation of pre-training benefits**: Train a baseline model without the intermediate video-text pre-training stage and compare performance across all four video captioning benchmarks to independently verify the claimed 9.7 average CIDEr improvement

2. **Efficiency benchmarking**: Measure training time, memory usage, and inference latency for both VideoOFA and VideoOFA-FiD variants across different video lengths to quantify the practical trade-offs between the full attention and local attention approaches

3. **Cross-dataset generalization**: Test VideoOFA on video captioning datasets not used in training (such as YouCookII or ActivityNet Captions) to assess whether the two-stage pre-training approach generalizes beyond the specific benchmarks reported
