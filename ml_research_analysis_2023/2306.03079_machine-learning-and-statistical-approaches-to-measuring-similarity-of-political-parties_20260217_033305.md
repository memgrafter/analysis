# Machine Learning and Statistical Approaches to Measuring Similarity of Political Parties

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03079
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03079
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Text-based similarity measures correlate well with expert assessments but poorly with behavioral benchmarks like voting records

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates multiple computational methods for measuring political party similarity through textual analysis of party programs. The authors compare word frequency distributions, stylometry, static word embeddings, and transformer-based language models against expert surveys, voting records, coalition patterns, candidate networks, and electoral data. They find that most textual methods correlate strongly with expert assessments but show weak correlations with behavioral benchmarks. The research suggests that while computational approaches can effectively supplement traditional expert-based party positioning methods, they may capture linguistic patterns rather than genuine ideological positions.

## Method Summary
The study applies multiple similarity measures to Polish party programs from 2001-2019, including word frequency (TF/TF-IDF), stylometry (Burrows' delta variants), static embeddings (word2vec, FastText, GloVe with WMD), and transformer models (BERT-based). Various length normalization techniques are employed including sampling and summarization. Results are benchmarked against expert surveys (Chapel Hill Expert Survey, V-DEM, Manifesto Project) and behavioral data (voting agreement, coalition patterns, genealogical similarity, electoral correlation). The methodology involves extensive correlation analysis between different similarity measures and benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Word frequency and stylometric methods show strong correlations with each other and with expert surveys
- Transformer-based methods demonstrate different correlation patterns compared to traditional approaches
- Most textual similarity measures correlate poorly with behavioral benchmarks like voting records
- Length normalization through sampling or summarization significantly impacts similarity measure performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Textual similarity measures can capture party ideology through linguistic patterns
- Mechanism: Party programs encode ideological positions through consistent word choice patterns and stylistic features
- Core assumption: Political parties use systematically different vocabulary that correlates with ideological positions
- Evidence anchors: Word frequency and stylometric methods show strong correlations with each other; corpus analysis shows related work on manifestos and speeches

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Transformer-based models capture contextual semantic similarity better than static embeddings
- Mechanism: Contextual embeddings capture word meanings based on surrounding text, providing nuanced understanding of party positions
- Core assumption: Semantic content depends on context and modern language models can capture these relationships
- Evidence anchors: Transformer models are applied to solve similarity measurement; corpus shows papers using transformer models for political text analysis

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multiple similarity measures can be combined for robust benchmark validation
- Mechanism: Different measures capture different aspects of party similarity, providing comprehensive coverage
- Core assumption: Party similarity is multidimensional and no single measure can capture all relevant aspects
- Evidence anchors: Methods are evaluated against multiple benchmarks including expert surveys and behavioral data; extensive work exists on combining multiple data sources

## Limitations

- Results may reflect linguistic patterns rather than genuine ideological positions due to weak correlation with behavioral benchmarks
- Study focuses exclusively on Polish party programs, limiting generalizability to other political contexts
- Computational intensity of transformer models for long documents remains a practical limitation

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Correlation between word frequency/stylometric methods and expert surveys is well-established
- **Medium confidence**: Transformer-based methods show promise but require further validation
- **Medium confidence**: Overall framework is methodologically sound but results suggest significant limitations
