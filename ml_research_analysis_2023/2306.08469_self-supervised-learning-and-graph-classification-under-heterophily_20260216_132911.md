# Self-supervised Learning and Graph Classification under Heterophily

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08469
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08469
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: Novel metric (MGS) and pre-training strategy (PGM) improve graph classification under heterophily

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the gap in understanding how self-supervised pre-training performs on graphs with heterophily, where connected nodes have dissimilar labels or features. The authors propose a novel metric (MGS) that quantifies how well graph embeddings preserve structural information by correlating graph fingerprint similarity with embedding similarity. Based on this metric, they develop a simple pre-training strategy (PGM) that optimizes for structural correlation, showing improved downstream classification accuracy. Experiments on molecular and protein datasets demonstrate that PGM outperforms existing methods, especially when combined with node-level pre-training strategies, and that selecting appropriate filters can sometimes be more effective than advanced pre-training techniques.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a two-stage approach for graph classification under heterophily. First, it introduces the MGS metric that measures structural information preservation by calculating the Spearman correlation between cosine similarity of pre-trained embeddings and structural similarity of graph fingerprints (Topological fingerprints for molecules, top k eigenvalues for proteins). Second, it develops the PGM pre-training strategy that maximizes this correlation. The method is evaluated through extensive experiments on molecular property prediction tasks from MoleculeNet and protein function prediction tasks from PPI networks, comparing against baseline self-supervised strategies including Infomax, EdgePred, ContextPred, AttrMasking, GROVER, InfoGraph, GPT-GNN, GraphCL, and MICRO-Graph.

## Key Results
- PGM strategy improves downstream ROC-AUC by 0.5-3.0% compared to baseline strategies on molecular datasets
- High-pass filters (ChebNet) outperform low-pass filters (GCN) on chemistry datasets with homophily ratio < 0.5, while adaptive filters (FAGCN) work best on biology datasets with ratio > 0.5
- The MGS metric successfully correlates with downstream task performance and provides a theoretically grounded way to measure structural information capture
- Node-level and graph-level pre-training strategies cooperate to achieve better performance than either alone

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper's approach works by explicitly optimizing for structural information preservation during pre-training, rather than relying on heuristic objectives. By measuring and maximizing the correlation between graph fingerprint similarity and embedding similarity, the method ensures that the learned representations capture the underlying structural patterns of the graphs. This is particularly effective under heterophily conditions where traditional methods that assume homophily may fail to learn meaningful representations.

## Foundational Learning
- Graph fingerprints: Compact representations of graph structure used for similarity comparison; needed for measuring structural information preservation
- Homophily ratio: Measure of how often connected nodes share the same label; critical for understanding when traditional methods fail
- Self-supervised pre-training: Learning representations without labels to improve downstream task performance; foundational to the proposed approach
- GNN architectures (GIN, GCN, GraphSAGE): Different message-passing mechanisms with varying sensitivity to node feature similarity; understanding their behavior under heterophily is key
- Filter types (ChebNet, FAGCN): Low-pass vs high-pass spectral filtering affecting how node information propagates; selection impacts performance under different heterophily conditions
- Quick check: Verify that the proposed MGS metric correlates with downstream task performance across different GNN architectures and filter types

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: Graph datasets -> Pre-training (PGM with MGS optimization) -> Fine-tuning (linear classifier) -> Downstream evaluation
- Critical path: Pre-training phase is critical as it determines the quality of initial embeddings for downstream tasks
- Design tradeoffs: Simple correlation maximization vs complex pre-training objectives; spectral filter selection vs universal approaches
- Failure signatures: Poor MGS values during pre-training indicate structural information not being captured; low downstream accuracy suggests pre-training objectives not aligned with task requirements
- First experiments: 1) Test MGS correlation with downstream performance on small datasets, 2) Compare PGM with baseline strategies on simple molecular datasets, 3) Analyze filter performance on graphs with varying homophily ratios

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How do low-pass and high-pass filters perform on graph classification when the homophily ratio is close to 0.5 (neutral heterophily/homophily)? The paper shows that ChebNet (high-pass) outperforms GCN (low-pass) on chemistry datasets (mostly heterophily, ratio < 0.5), while FAGCN (adaptive) performs best on biology datasets (ratio > 0.5). It suggests choosing the suitable filter can be more effective than pre-training design. Experimental results comparing low-pass, high-pass, and adaptive filters on datasets with homophily ratios near 0.5 would clarify optimal filter choice for neutral cases.

### Open Question 2
Can the MGS metric be generalized to measure structural information capture in node-level self-supervised pre-training strategies? The MGS metric is developed and validated for graph-level pre-training strategies. The paper shows cooperation between node-level and graph-level strategies but does not extend MGS to node-level methods. Applying MGS or a modified version to node-level pre-training strategies (like AttrMasking or ContextPred) and validating its correlation with structural information capture would demonstrate generalizability.

### Open Question 3
How does the choice of fingerprint and similarity algorithm affect the performance of PGM pre-training? The paper tests different fingerprint/similarity combinations (Topological/Morgan fingerprints with Tanimoto/Dice similarity) and finds Topological fingerprint with Tanimoto similarity performs best, but only explores a limited set. Systematic comparison of multiple fingerprint types (e.g., MACCS keys, ECFP) and similarity measures across diverse graph datasets would reveal optimal combinations for different domains.

## Limitations
- Focus on binary classification tasks may not represent challenges of multi-class or regression problems
- Limited experimental validation to specific molecular and protein datasets, leaving performance on other graph types unexplored
- Claims about filter selection being more effective than pre-training strategies for heterophilic graphs are supported by limited ablation studies
- Minimal computational cost analysis, not addressing trade-offs between proposed method and simpler alternatives

## Confidence
- PGM strategy effectiveness: Medium confidence (strong molecular results, weaker protein results)
- MGS metric validity: High confidence (theoretically grounded and empirically validated)
- Filter selection claims: Low-Medium confidence (limited ablation studies)

## Next Checks
1. Test the MGS-guided pre-training approach on multi-class classification tasks and regression problems to assess generalizability beyond binary classification
2. Conduct extensive ablation studies varying the pre-training dataset size to determine the minimum effective dataset requirements for PGM strategy
3. Evaluate the proposed method on non-biomedical graph datasets (e.g., social networks, citation graphs) to assess performance across different heterophily levels and graph domains
