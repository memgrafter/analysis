# Feedback-Gated Rectified Linear Units

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.02610
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02610
- Reference count: 19
- One-line primary result: Feedback-gated ReLUs show faster convergence and better performance on MNIST autoencoders but mixed results on CIFAR-10, particularly with batch normalization

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a biologically inspired feedback mechanism that gates rectified linear units in artificial neural networks. Based on neuroscience literature showing that feedback connections modulate pyramidal neuron gain in the brain, the method introduces additional weights for feedback signals that adjust the gain of ReLU activations. Experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets demonstrate that networks with feedback-gated ReLUs can achieve faster convergence and better performance compared to standard networks, though results are less consistent on CIFAR-10 when batch normalization is used.

## Method Summary
The feedback-gated ReLU mechanism introduces additional feedback connections from higher to lower layers in neural networks. These feedback signals (µD) modulate the gain of standard feedforward inputs (µS) through a gating function that adjusts the effective activation threshold. The network is trained using backpropagation through time across multiple timesteps, allowing iterative refinement of activations based on both feedforward and feedback information. The method is tested on autoencoder and classification tasks using MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets, with experiments varying the number of feedback timesteps, noise levels, and network configurations.

## Key Results
- Autoencoders with feedback show faster convergence and better reconstruction quality on MNIST compared to standard autoencoders
- Feedback-gated ReLUs demonstrate improved robustness to noise in MNIST experiments
- CIFAR-10 results are less consistent, with some experiments showing negative effects when batch normalization is used
- The mechanism shows promise for simpler datasets but requires further investigation for complex image classification tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Feedback-gated ReLUs improve convergence speed and performance by modulating the gain of neurons based on feedback signals from higher layers.
- Mechanism: The feedback signal (µD) is used to adjust the gain of the ReLU activation according to the equation f = max(0,µS) / (1 - min(βmax/η * µD, βmax)). When feedback is strong, the gain increases, allowing small feedforward inputs to produce larger activations.
- Core assumption: The gain modulation through feedback is beneficial for learning and generalizes across different datasets and tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "autoencoders with feedback show faster convergence, better performance, and more robustness to noise compared to their counterparts without feedback."
  - [section 4.1.2] "the configuration explained above does not only converge faster than a standard autoencoder, but also settles to a smaller loss value"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers focus on activation functions and feedback mechanisms but do not directly validate gain modulation through feedback in this specific way.
- Break condition: If feedback signals become noisy or misaligned with feedforward inputs, the gain modulation could become detrimental, leading to unstable training or degraded performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The feedback-gated ReLU mechanism introduces a form of top-down attention that helps the network focus on relevant features.
- Mechanism: Feedback connections from higher layers provide contextual information that gates lower layer activations. This creates a form of iterative refinement where initial feedforward processing is adjusted based on higher-level predictions.
- Core assumption: The brain's use of feedback connections for attention and prediction error signals can be effectively mimicked in artificial networks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "feedback input appears to modulate the gain of the corresponding neuron" and "feedback to a neuron may predict whether this particular neuron should be firing"
  - [section 4.1.4] "the use of feedback significantly increases the network's robustness to noise"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - papers on RNNs and gated units suggest feedback can help with temporal dependencies, but direct evidence for top-down attention in static image tasks is limited.
- Break condition: If the feedback connections are not properly aligned with the hierarchical structure of the network, they may introduce confusion rather than helpful context.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The feedback-gated mechanism creates a dynamic thresholding effect that adapts to input statistics.
- Mechanism: Through the combination of feedforward input and feedback-modulated gain, the effective threshold for activation becomes context-dependent rather than fixed. This allows the network to adjust sensitivity based on both local and global information.
- Core assumption: Adaptive thresholding based on both feedforward and feedback signals provides better discrimination than fixed thresholds.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3] Derivation shows how feedback affects the effective activation function, creating a dynamic relationship between input and output
  - [section 4.1.3] "the overwhelming majority of values are the maximum gain the network can produce" suggesting the mechanism is operating in a high-gain regime
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - while activation function analysis exists, specific evidence for dynamic thresholding through feedback is not directly present in related works.
- Break condition: If the feedback signal becomes saturated or the gain parameters are poorly tuned, the dynamic thresholding effect may collapse to a simple scaling operation with no adaptive benefit.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Standard feedforward path with ReLU activations -> Additional feedback path with separate weights connecting higher to lower layers -> Time-unrolled network structure for incorporating feedback over multiple passes -> Modified ReLU activation function incorporating both feedforward and feedback inputs

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass through network to compute initial activations
  2. Compute feedback signals from higher layers
  3. Apply feedback-gated ReLU activation using both feedforward and feedback inputs
  4. Backpropagation through time across all timesteps
  5. Weight update incorporating both feedforward and feedback path gradients

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of timesteps: More timesteps allow more feedback refinement but increase computational cost and may not provide additional benefit
  - Feedback connection pattern: Partial feedback (single connection) vs comprehensive feedback (all possible connections) affects parameter count and potential performance
  - Gain parameters (βmax and η): These hyperparameters control the strength of feedback modulation and require careful tuning

- Failure signatures:
  - NaN or Inf values during training: Likely caused by gain values approaching or exceeding 1.0
  - No improvement over standard ReLU: May indicate poor hyperparameter tuning or inappropriate dataset for feedback mechanism
  - Instability during training: Could result from conflicting gradients between feedforward and feedback paths

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement basic autoencoder on MNIST with single feedback connection, compare convergence speed and final loss to standard autoencoder
  2. Test different numbers of timesteps (1, 2, 4, 6, 8) to determine optimal feedback refinement depth
  3. Add Gaussian noise to activations and measure robustness difference between feedback-gated and standard ReLU networks

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific role does feedback play in the brain, and can we infer this from artificial neural network experiments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper aims to investigate whether feedback can improve the performance of artificial neural networks and whether observing feedback in artificial models can provide insights into its function in the brain.
- Why unresolved: The experiments show mixed results, with feedback improving performance on MNIST but not consistently on CIFAR-10. The interaction between feedback and batch normalization complicates the interpretation of results.
- What evidence would resolve it: More extensive experiments on different datasets and network architectures, as well as further analysis of how feedback interacts with other neural network components, could help clarify its role.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why does feedback increase robustness to noise on MNIST but not on CIFAR-10?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that feedback increases robustness to noise in the MNIST autoencoder but makes the CIFAR-10 autoencoder more sensitive to noise.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests that the effectiveness of the feedback mechanism may be data-dependent, but this hypothesis is not tested further.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the feedback mechanism on a wider variety of datasets and analyzing the learned feedback patterns could help explain the difference in behavior.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Is there a fundamental problem with using feedback in current artificial neural networks, such as incompatibility with backpropagation or the need for more realistic models like spiking neural networks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that feedback may not be useful beyond MNIST and raises the possibility that backpropagation may not be an ideal learning algorithm for feedback.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide conclusive evidence for or against these hypotheses.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing feedback with different learning algorithms and network architectures, including more biologically realistic models, could help determine if there are fundamental limitations to using feedback in artificial neural networks.

## Limitations
- Effectiveness varies significantly across datasets, with consistent improvements on MNIST but mixed results on CIFAR-10
- Critical hyperparameters (βmax and η) are not specified, limiting reproducibility
- Negative interaction with batch normalization on complex datasets requires further investigation

## Confidence
- High confidence: The basic mechanism of feedback-gated ReLUs is clearly defined and the MNIST autoencoder results showing faster convergence are robust across multiple experiments.
- Medium confidence: The claim of improved noise robustness and the general mechanism of gain modulation through feedback are supported but require more extensive validation across different noise types and levels.
- Low confidence: The interaction between feedback-gated ReLUs and batch normalization, and the generalization of results to complex datasets like CIFAR-10, need more thorough investigation.

## Next Checks
1. Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis: Systematically test different values of βmax and η across a grid to determine optimal configurations and understand the sensitivity of results to these parameters.

2. Batch Normalization Interaction Study: Conduct controlled experiments varying batch normalization placement (before vs after feedback-gated ReLU) and strength to precisely characterize the negative interaction observed in CIFAR-10 results.

3. Ablation on Feedback Structure: Compare the performance of different feedback connection patterns (single vs multiple vs all-to-all) to determine the minimum effective feedback structure and isolate which aspects of the mechanism drive improvements.
