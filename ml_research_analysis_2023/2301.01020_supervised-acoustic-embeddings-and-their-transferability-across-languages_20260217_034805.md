# Supervised Acoustic Embeddings And Their Transferability Across Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2301.01020
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.01020
- **Authors:** 
- **Reference count:** 15
- **One-line primary result:** Supervised acoustic word embeddings trained on English and transferred to other languages consistently outperform self-supervised models trained on target languages.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates acoustic word embeddings (AWE) and their cross-lingual transferability. The authors compare self-supervised AWE models trained on target languages against zero-shot cross-lingual transfer of supervised AWE models trained on English. They experiment with different acoustic features including MFCC, wav2vec, MCPC, and XLSR-53 as input to AWE models. The key finding is that supervised training reduces speaker variability more effectively than self-supervised training, with supervised models consistently outperforming self-supervised alternatives across multiple languages and evaluation metrics.

## Method Summary
The paper employs a two-layer bidirectional LSTM encoder with a unidirectional LSTM decoder to generate acoustic word embeddings. Two training paradigms are compared: self-supervised auto-encoding using mean squared error loss, and supervised training using phoneme-level negative log-likelihood. The supervised models are trained on English data and then zero-shot transferred to other languages. Four different acoustic feature sets are extracted using s3prl: MFCC (13+delta+delta-delta), wav2vec2 (last hidden layer), MCPC (last hidden layer), and XLSR-53 (layer 12). Evaluation is performed using minimal-pair ABX error rates and K-means clustering accuracy across multiple languages including English, French, German, Spanish, and Arabic.

## Key Results
- Supervised AWE models trained on English and transferred to other languages consistently outperform self-supervised models trained on target languages
- Supervised training reduces speaker variability more effectively than self-supervised training
- Pre-trained features like wav2vec and MCPC, trained on English speech, show superior performance when used with supervised AWE models

## Foundational Learning
- **Acoustic Word Embeddings (AWE):** Fixed-dimensional representations of variable-length speech segments that capture phonetic content
  - Why needed: Enables efficient comparison and retrieval of spoken words
  - Quick check: Verify embedding dimensionality and temporal alignment with input segments
- **Self-supervised vs Supervised Learning:** Self-supervised uses reconstruction objectives while supervised uses labeled phoneme targets
  - Why needed: Determines whether models learn general phonetic patterns or language-specific representations
  - Quick check: Compare loss functions and training data requirements
- **Zero-shot Cross-lingual Transfer:** Applying models trained on one language to another without fine-tuning
  - Why needed: Enables application to low-resource languages with limited training data
  - Quick check: Verify no target language data was used during model training
- **Speaker Variability:** Differences in pronunciation due to speaker characteristics
  - Why needed: A key factor affecting model generalization across speakers
  - Quick check: Analyze within-speaker vs across-speaker ABX error rates
- **Minimal-pair ABX Error Rate:** Evaluation metric comparing acoustically similar word pairs
  - Why needed: Measures model's ability to distinguish phonetically similar words
  - Quick check: Verify cosine similarity computation and segment selection criteria
- **Force Alignment:** Technique to determine precise word boundaries in speech
  - Why needed: Essential for extracting correct word-level segments for training
  - Quick check: Validate alignment accuracy and segment filtering criteria

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:** Acoustic features -> BiLSTM encoder -> Embedding -> LSTM decoder (training only) -> Cosine similarity (evaluation)

**Critical Path:** Feature extraction → BiLSTM encoding → Embedding generation → Similarity computation → ABX error calculation

**Design Tradeoffs:** 
- Encoder depth (2 layers) balances representational capacity with computational efficiency
- Supervised training requires phoneme labels but improves speaker normalization
- Pre-trained features reduce training data requirements but may introduce language bias

**Failure Signatures:**
- Self-supervised AWEs underperforming DTW suggests insufficient encoder capacity or inappropriate decoder architecture
- Transfer performance drops indicate feature-layer mismatch or domain shift issues
- High within-speaker vs across-speaker error rate gaps suggest poor speaker normalization

## Limitations
- Self-supervised training configurations and hyperparameters are not fully specified, making fair comparison difficult
- Force alignment procedures for Arabic data lack sufficient detail regarding segment validity filtering
- The paper does not address potential language bias introduced by using English-trained features

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** Supervised training consistently outperforms self-supervised approaches when using pre-trained features
- **Medium confidence:** Speaker variability reduction claims due to incomplete training procedure specifications
- **Medium confidence:** Cross-lingual transferability results given unclear self-supervised model configurations
