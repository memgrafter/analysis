# Pacos: Modeling Users' Interpretable and Context-Dependent Choices in Preference Reversals

## Executive Summary
This paper presents Pacos, a unified framework for modeling users' context-dependent choices in preference reversals. The key idea is to decompose the total utility function into three parts: attribute utility, comparison utility, and position utility, each addressing one of the three factors contributing to context effects. Two design methods are proposed: an additive method with high interpretability and an ANN-based method with high accuracy. The effectiveness of Pacos is theoretically proven, and experiments on real user data demonstrate its superior performance in predicting users' choices compared to prior works, as well as its great interpretability in understanding the cause of preference reversals.

## Method Summary
Pacos models user choice by decomposing total utility into attribute utility (capturing adaptive feature weights), comparison utility (modeling inter-item effects), and position utility (accounting for display rank). Two implementation methods are provided: an additive method with interpretable parameters and an ANN-based method using deep sets for permutation-invariant learning. The framework is trained with a custom learning algorithm that dynamically adjusts an upper limit to address imbalance among utility modules.

## Key Results
- Pacos achieves superior performance in predicting user choices compared to prior works
- The additive method provides high interpretability in understanding preference reversals
- The ANN-based method offers high accuracy while maintaining permutation invariance
- Theoretical proofs show Pacos can model preference reversals under specified conditions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Decomposing total utility into attribute, comparison, and position utilities captures three distinct sources of context effects that individually explain user choice behavior.
- Mechanism: Attribute utility models how market composition changes the importance of item features; comparison utility models how the presence of similar or decoy items shifts relative preferences; position utility models how display rank influences choice. By summing these independent terms, the model can represent preference reversals that arise from any of these factors.
- Core assumption: The three factors are additive and can be estimated separately without interference.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] identifies three factors: "users' adaptive weights, the inter-item comparison, and display positions."
  - [section III-A] shows the total utility function as the sum of the three modules: U(si|S) = AU(si|Satt) + CU (si|Satt) + PU (si|Spos).
  - [corpus] lacks direct empirical validation that the additive assumption holds; this is an inference from the model formulation.
- Break condition: If interaction effects between factors are significant, the additive assumption will fail and the model will underfit preference reversals.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The additive method can always find parameters that fit preference reversal conditions by exploiting the flexibility of adaptive weights and comparison utilities.
- Mechanism: The proof in Section IV shows that when a new item joins a market, the update term f(si,sC) can be tuned so that the difference in utilities reverses the original preference ordering. By designing g(si) and h(si,sj) as sum-over-items functions, the model ensures permutation invariance and scalability.
- Core assumption: There exist parameter values such that the sign changes required by Theorem 2 can be achieved through the attribute and comparison utility modules.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section IV-B] presents Theorem 2, which gives explicit conditions for preference reversals to be modeled.
  - [section III-B] details the additive method's formulation of g and h functions.
  - [corpus] does not contain direct experimental evidence of these conditions being met in practice; this is a theoretical claim.
- Break condition: If the required sign changes are not achievable within the function space of g and h, the additive method will fail to predict the reversal.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The ANN-based method uses deep sets to learn permutation-invariant representations of context effects, enabling high accuracy without manual feature engineering.
- Mechanism: Deep sets apply the form ρ(∑x∈X φ(x)), ensuring the learned weights β(Satt) and comparison utilities are invariant to item order. This allows the network to capture complex interactions between attributes and market composition.
- Core assumption: The permutation-invariant property is sufficient to model all relevant context effects, and the network capacity is adequate to approximate the true utility function.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section III-B] explains the use of deep sets and their permutation-invariant property.
  - [section V-A] reports that Pacos-NN achieves the highest accuracy among all compared methods.
  - [corpus] lacks evidence that the deep sets architecture is necessary; this is inferred from the design choice.
- Break condition: If context effects depend on item order or pairwise interactions not captured by sum-invariant functions, the ANN-based method will mispredict preferences.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Product attributes and positions -> Attribute utility module -> Comparison utility module -> Position utility module -> Total utility -> Softmax probabilities
- Critical path:
  1. Encode attributes and positions
  2. Compute attribute utility via adaptive weights
  3. Compute comparison utility via inter-item interactions
  4. Add position utility
  5. Apply softmax to get probabilities
  6. Optimize parameters via gradient descent
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Additive vs. ANN-based: interpretability vs. accuracy
  - Fixed position weights vs. learned position preferences
  - Single vs. multiple utility modules (complexity vs. flexibility)
- Failure signatures:
  - Persistent imbalance between modules despite update rule
  - Inability to predict preference reversals in controlled experiments
  - Overfitting when training data is small

## Limitations
- Theoretical proofs rely on additive utility assumptions that may not capture all interaction effects
- ANN-based method lacks theoretical analysis due to complexity and low interpretability
- Dynamic adjustment mechanism for upper limit B lacks specific implementation details

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The decomposition of total utility into three interpretable modules (attribute, comparison, position) is well-defined and theoretically sound.
- **Medium confidence**: The additive method can find parameters to fit preference reversal conditions, but this is based on theoretical conditions without direct empirical validation.
- **Low confidence**: The ANN-based method's superior accuracy is claimed based on reported metrics, but the exact architecture and hyperparameters are not specified, making reproduction difficult.
