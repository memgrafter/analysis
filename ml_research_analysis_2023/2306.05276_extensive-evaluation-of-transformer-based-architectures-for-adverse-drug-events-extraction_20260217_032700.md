# Extensive Evaluation of Transformer-based Architectures for Adverse Drug Events Extraction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.05276
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05276
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Comprehensive evaluation of 19 Transformer variants for ADE extraction from informal social media texts, showing AutoEncoding models outperform others with larger models and social media pre-training being most beneficial.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of 19 Transformer-based architectures for adverse drug event (ADE) extraction from informal social media texts. The authors compare model performance on two datasets with different levels of informality - forum posts and tweets. They analyze the effect of combining Transformers with additional processing layers (CRF and LSTM), and use Shapley values to correlate model characteristics (architecture, pre-training domain, training from scratch, model size) with performance. The key findings are that AutoEncoding models outperform text-generation models on this task, larger models generally achieve better results, social media pre-training is beneficial, and medical pre-training only helps on more formal social media texts.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates 19 Transformer variants across three categories (AutoEncoding, AutoRegressive, Text-to-Text) on two ADE extraction datasets (CADEC forum posts and SMM4H tweets). Models are tested with and without additional CRF and LSTM modules. The authors use entity-level relaxed F1 score as the primary metric, conducting 30 runs with different random seeds for each configuration. Hyperparameter tuning is performed via grid-search, and Shapley values are computed to analyze the contribution of different model characteristics to performance. The evaluation covers model architecture, pre-training domain, training approach, and model size as key variables.

## Key Results
- AutoEncoding models significantly outperform Text-to-Text and AutoRegressive models for ADE extraction on informal texts
- Larger models consistently achieve higher performance, with size being the second most impactful feature according to Shapley values
- Social media pre-training provides substantial benefits, while medical pre-training only helps on more formal social media texts
- Additional processing modules (CRF and LSTM) show mixed results, with CRF helping Text-to-Text models but hurting others

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: AutoEncoding models outperform Text-to-Text and AutoRegressive models for ADE extraction on informal texts.
- Mechanism: AutoEncoding models process input tokens and produce contextualized embeddings that preserve the original text structure, enabling direct token-level classification with BIO labels. Text-to-Text models must first generate ADE mentions as text, then map them back to the original text, introducing noise and ambiguity.
- Core assumption: The structure of informal social media texts benefits more from token-level classification than from sequence-to-sequence generation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] states that "AutoEncoding models outperform text-generation models on this task"
  - [section] reports that "AutoEncoding models ⃝ in the top right (together with XLNet), which reach the highest performance; the Text-to-Text models □, which have a considerably lower Precision"
- Break condition: If the task requires generating new text (e.g., summarizing ADEs) rather than extracting spans, Text-to-Text models might outperform.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Larger models generally achieve better performance on ADE extraction.
- Mechanism: Larger models have more parameters and capacity to capture complex patterns in informal text, including misspellings, slang, and domain-specific terminology.
- Core assumption: The informal nature of social media texts requires higher model capacity to handle linguistic variability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] shows that "Smaller models generally lead to a lower F1 score, which is also attested by the Shapley values (Size is the second most impactful feature on the performance)"
  - [section] notes that "the best-performing model overall is EnDR-BERT, which is also the largest AutoEncoding model (solid outline)"
- Break condition: If computational resources are severely limited, smaller models might be preferred despite lower performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Pre-training on social media texts is beneficial for ADE extraction from informal social media data.
- Mechanism: Models pre-trained on social media data learn representations that better handle informal language, slang, and domain-specific terminology commonly found in user-generated content.
- Core assumption: The informal writing style of social media posts is sufficiently similar across platforms to benefit from cross-platform pre-training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] states that "knowledge of social media language is highly beneficial"
  - [section] shows that "BERTweet and EnDR-BERT, which are trained on social media texts (tweets and forum posts), highlighting that, in this case, social media pre-training is more valuable than medical knowledge"
- Break condition: If the social media data contains highly specialized terminology not present in general social media corpora, domain-specific pre-training might be more beneficial.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input text -> Tokenizer -> Transformer model -> (CRF/LSTM) -> BIO labels -> ADE entities
- Critical path: Input text → Tokenizer → Transformer model → (CRF/LSTM) → BIO labels → ADE entities
- Design tradeoffs:
  - AutoEncoding vs Text-to-Text: AutoEncoding preserves original text structure but Text-to-Text might be better for generating new text
  - Model size: Larger models perform better but require more computational resources
  - Pre-training domain: Social media pre-training helps with informal language, medical pre-training helps with technical terminology
- Failure signatures:
  - Low Precision: Model is generating too many false positives
  - Low Recall: Model is missing many actual ADE mentions
  - Poor performance on informal texts: Model lacks exposure to social media language patterns
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test a base AutoEncoding model (e.g., BERT) on the SMM4H dataset to establish baseline performance
  2. Add a CRF layer to the base model and compare performance to understand its impact
  3. Test a model pre-trained on social media (e.g., BERTweet) vs one pre-trained on general domain to measure the benefit of social media pre-training

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the three main Transformer categories (AutoEncoding, AutoRegressive, Text-to-Text) perform differently on social media texts with varying levels of informality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper directly compares these three categories across datasets with different informality levels (forum posts vs. tweets).
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows performance differences, it doesn't fully explain the underlying reasons why certain categories struggle with informal texts or how architectural differences contribute to these gaps.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying text informality levels while holding other factors constant, and ablation studies isolating specific architectural components.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal combination of pre-training domains (general, medical, social) for ADE extraction across different social media text styles?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tests models with various pre-training domain combinations and finds that social media pre-training helps but medical pre-training only helps on more formal texts.
- Why unresolved: The analysis shows correlations but doesn't establish causation or determine the optimal weighting of different pre-training domains for specific text styles.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies varying the proportion of pre-training data from each domain, and experiments testing transfer learning from one domain to another.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do additional processing modules (CRF and LSTM) interact with different Transformer architectures and pre-training strategies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tests CRF and LSTM modules with various Transformer models but finds mixed and inconsistent results across different architectures and datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper observes performance differences but doesn't provide a theoretical framework for understanding when and why these modules help or hurt performance with different base models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of the learned representations with and without additional modules, and experiments testing different module configurations and integration points.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is limited to informal social media texts and may not generalize to formal medical documents or other domains
- The study tested 19 models representing a snapshot in time; newer architectures may yield different results
- Medical pre-training could not be tested on the SMM4H dataset due to lack of appropriate models
- Computational efficiency analysis is limited to comparing performance across model sizes

## Confidence

**High Confidence:**
- AutoEncoding models outperform Text-to-Text models for ADE extraction on informal texts
- Larger models generally achieve better performance on this task
- Social media pre-training is beneficial for ADE extraction from informal social media data

**Medium Confidence:**
- The relative ranking of specific Transformer architectures may shift with newer models or different hyperparameter settings
- The benefit of medical pre-training is context-dependent and may not generalize beyond formal social media texts

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-domain validation**: Test the top-performing architectures on formal medical documents (e.g., clinical notes, FDA reports) to assess whether the advantage of AutoEncoding models and social media pre-training extends beyond informal texts.

2. **Temporal validation**: Re-run the evaluation with newer Transformer variants released after this study to determine if the relative performance rankings remain consistent or if emerging architectures change the landscape.

3. **Efficiency benchmarking**: Conduct a comprehensive computational efficiency analysis across different hardware configurations (CPU, GPU, varying memory constraints) to provide actionable guidance for resource-constrained deployment scenarios.
