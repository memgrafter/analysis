# Structural Ambiguity and its Disambiguation in Language Model Based Parsers: the Case of Dutch Clause Relativization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.14917
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14917
- Reference count: 13
- Key outcome: Neural parsers show persistent structural biases toward subject-relative interpretations in Dutch relative clauses, only partially mitigated by context or fine-tuning

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how neural parsers handle structural ambiguity in Dutch relative clauses, specifically the subject-object ambiguity in constructions like "de dokter die de patiënt geneest." The authors create a novel test set with controlled ambiguity and evaluate two neural parsing architectures - one based on proof nets and another on universal dependencies - both built on top of BERT-style language models. Without context, both parsers show a strong bias towards subject-relative interpretations. When given disambiguating context, accuracy improves but the bias persists. Fine-tuning the parsers on the task partially mitigates the bias, with the proof net-based parser showing better adaptability.

## Method Summary
The study extracts (s, v, o) triples from the Lassy Large corpus, classifies them by semantic category and reversibility, then generates relative clauses with and without prior disambiguating context. Two parsers (neural proof nets and UD-based sequence labeling) are trained on Lassy-Small with random token prepending, then evaluated on the generated test set under three conditions: without context, with context, and after fine-tuning the parser components only. The evaluation measures parser accuracy on subject-relative versus object-relative interpretations.

## Key Results
- Without context, both parsers show strong subject-relative bias, with accuracy for object-relative interpretations around 20-40%
- Disambiguating context improves accuracy but doesn't eliminate the bias, with object-relative accuracy increasing to 50-60%
- Fine-tuning partially mitigates the bias, with proof net parser achieving more balanced accuracy across both interpretations
- The UD-based parser remains more susceptible to the structural bias even after fine-tuning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Language models encode structural biases that persist in downstream parsing tasks
- Mechanism: Pretraining data distribution creates strong statistical preferences (e.g., subject-relative bias) that transfer to parsing models through fine-tuning
- Core assumption: Training data statistics directly influence model behavior even after fine-tuning
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Results show that a neurosymbolic parser, based on proof nets, is more open to data bias correction than an approach based on universal dependencies, although both setups suffer from a comparable initial data bias"
  - [section]: "These results are somewhat to be expected: the subject-relative this reading prevails in the training data that the parsers were trained on"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct citations but multiple studies on BERT pretraining data influence
- Break condition: If finetuning on balanced data successfully eliminates bias, this mechanism would be weakened

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Contextualization through language models partially mitigates but doesn't eliminate structural bias
- Mechanism: BERT-style contextualization provides lexical information that helps parser make better decisions, but cannot fully overcome learned structural preferences
- Core assumption: Language models can access and utilize contextual information but structural biases are too deeply embedded
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "When given disambiguating context, accuracy improves but the bias persists"
  - [section]: "We observe a stable accuracy for the cases of subject-relative readings, with significant accuracy gains for the object-relative reading"
  - [corpus]: Supported by prior work on BERT contextualization capabilities
- Break condition: If complete bias elimination were achieved through contextualization alone

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Parser architecture determines susceptibility to bias correction through finetuning
- Mechanism: Different parsing paradigms (proof nets vs universal dependencies) have varying capacities to adapt to bias correction tasks
- Core assumption: Architectural differences affect how easily models can learn to override learned biases
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Results show that a neurosymbolic parser, based on proof nets, is more open to data bias correction than an approach based on universal dependencies"
  - [section]: "we observe that it is indeed possible to leverage the training task to even out the parsers' bias through grounding, leaving the neural proof net parser accuracy evenly distributed"
  - [corpus]: Supported by prior work on different parsing architectures' generalization properties
- Break condition: If both architectures showed equal adaptability to bias correction

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Language model (BERT) -> Parser component (proof nets or UD) -> Output interpretation
  Data generation pipeline: Corpus extraction -> Triple classification -> Relative clause generation
  Evaluation framework: Context manipulation -> Parser testing -> Bias measurement

- Critical path:
  1. Generate test cases with controlled ambiguity
  2. Feed to parser with/without disambiguating context
  3. Measure interpretation accuracy
  4. Compare bias across architectures and conditions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using pretrained LMs provides strong baselines but inherits biases
  - Proof nets offer more structured representation but may be harder to train
  - UD sequence labeling is simpler but may be more rigid in adaptation
  - Data augmentation helps but risks overfitting to specific patterns

- Failure signatures:
  - Persistent subject-relative bias despite context indicates deep structural preference
  - Large accuracy drops during finetuning suggest overfitting or task incompatibility
  - Inconsistent performance across word orders indicates sensitivity to presentation format

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test baseline parser accuracy on ambiguous relative clauses without context
  2. Add disambiguating prior sentences and measure improvement
  3. Finetune parsers on the task and compare bias correction capabilities between architectures

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different Dutch relative pronoun types (die vs. dat) affect parser performance in ambiguous relative clauses?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions Dutch relative pronouns die and dat but does not test their differential impact on parsing accuracy
- Why unresolved: The authors focus on syntactic ambiguity in general but do not distinguish between the neuter (dat) and non-neuter (die) pronouns
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing parser performance separately on relative clauses with die vs. dat pronouns while controlling for other variables

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the magnitude of lexical selectional preference affect parser disambiguation accuracy differently than the mere presence/absence of preference?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors categorize verbs as having "strong" vs "weak" selectional preferences but do not analyze whether the degree of preference matters
- Why unresolved: The experiments only distinguish between reversible vs irreversible triples, not the strength of preferences within reversible cases
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing parser accuracy across a continuum of selectional preference strengths rather than binary categories

### Open Question 3
- Question: How would incorporating explicit syntactic ambiguity handling into the parser architecture affect disambiguation performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "the inability to accommodate syntactic ambiguity" in both training corpora and parser architectures poses a problem
- Why unresolved: The study uses existing parsers without modifications for explicit ambiguity handling
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing parsers with built-in mechanisms for representing multiple syntactic analyses simultaneously

## Limitations
- The study only examines Dutch, limiting cross-linguistic generalizability of the observed biases
- The evaluation focuses on a specific type of structural ambiguity (relative clause attachment) that may not generalize to other ambiguity types
- The comparison is limited to two specific parsing architectures, making it unclear whether the observed differences are architecture-specific or reflect broader parsing paradigm differences

## Confidence

**Major uncertainties and limitations:**

The study demonstrates robust evidence that language models encode structural biases that persist through parsing tasks, with **High confidence** in the core finding that both neural parsing architectures show strong subject-relative biases that are only partially mitigated by context or fine-tuning. The evidence is particularly strong for Mechanism 1 (structural bias transfer) and Mechanism 2 (contextualization limitations), with clear empirical demonstrations across multiple test conditions. However, **Medium confidence** exists in the claim that proof net-based parsing is superior for bias correction, as the study only compares two specific architectures and the evaluation is limited to a single language. **Low confidence** exists in the generalizability of the specific bias patterns, as Dutch relative clause structure may not directly transfer to other languages with different word order properties or relative clause typologies.

## Next Checks
1. Test the same parsing architectures on relative clause ambiguity in a different language family (e.g., SOV languages) to assess cross-linguistic generalizability of the observed biases.

2. Conduct ablation studies on the fine-tuning process by varying the amount of bias-correction training data and measuring the point of diminishing returns for bias mitigation.

3. Implement a third parser architecture (e.g., transition-based parsing) to determine whether the observed differences between proof nets and UD are architecture-specific or reflect broader parsing paradigm differences.
