# Simultaneous Adversarial Attacks On Multiple Face Recognition System Components

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.05048
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05048
- Reference count: 26
- Key outcome: Multi-component adversarial attacks achieve up to 100% success rates against both face detectors and feature extractors in FRS systems

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates multi-component adversarial attacks on face recognition systems (FRS) by simultaneously targeting both face detectors and feature extractors. The researchers propose three novel attack strategies (DI-Attack, DE-Attack, UE-Attack) that use white-box Projected Gradient Descent optimization with multi-objective loss functions to fool both components. Experiments demonstrate attack success rates up to 100% against both MTCNN face detectors and SEIR50 feature extractors, with up to 50% manipulation of face detection probability. The research provides practical tools for enhancing FRS robustness through adversarial training.

## Method Summary
The study uses white-box Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) optimization with multi-objective loss functions targeting both face detection probability and feature extraction accuracy. Three attack strategies are proposed: Detectable-Impersonation (DI-Attack), Detectable-Evasion (DE-Attack), and Undetectable-Evasion (UE-Attack). The attacks are crafted using adversarial patches in eyeglass frame forms of two different sizes, applied to source images from the VggFace2 dataset. The FRS pipeline consists of MTCNN for face detection and SEIR50 for feature extraction, with attacks evaluated against both components simultaneously.

## Key Results
- Achieved up to 100% Attack Success Rates against both face detectors and feature extractors
- Successfully manipulated face detection probability by up to 50%
- Created adversarial examples that maintain detectability while fooling feature extractors, or vice versa
- Demonstrated effectiveness of multi-objective optimization for simultaneous component attacks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multi-objective adversarial optimization allows simultaneous attack of face detector and feature extractor.
- Mechanism: Three attack strategies use white-box PGD optimization with combined detection adversarial loss and misclassification loss functions.
- Core assumption: Adversarial examples can satisfy multiple objectives while maintaining perceptual similarity.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states attacks use multi-objective loss functions targeting both components; section III proposes three multi-objective attacks; corpus provides weak evidence from related papers.
- Break condition: Optimization instability when combining multiple loss functions or perceptual similarity violations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adversarial patch size affects both attack success and detectability in face detection systems.
- Mechanism: Larger patches achieve higher attack success but may reduce detectability; smaller patches evade detection but may be insufficient for matching attacks.
- Core assumption: Optimal patch size balances attack success with detectability constraints.
- Evidence anchors: Section IV.A discusses patch size importance; section VI uses eyeglass frame patches of two sizes; corpus provides weak evidence on physical patches.
- Break condition: Patch size optimization failure resulting in failed detection or matching.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: White-box access enables crafting of more effective multi-component attacks.
- Mechanism: Complete knowledge of both face detector and feature extractor architectures allows precise gradient-based optimization.
- Core assumption: Adversary can compute gradients through both FRS components.
- Evidence anchors: Section VI assumes white-box setting with full pipeline access; section IV uses white-box PGD method; corpus provides weak evidence on white-box attacks.
- Break condition: Incomplete knowledge breaking gradient computation chain and reducing effectiveness.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input capture → Pre-processing → Face detection (MTCNN with P-Net, R-Net, O-Net) → Face cropping → Feature extraction (SEIR50) → Matching with distance metric → Output

- Critical path: Clean image → Adversarial patch generation (PGD optimization) → Multi-objective loss calculation → Updated adversarial example → Input to FRS → Attack evaluation on both components

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Patch size vs. detectability: Larger patches improve attack success but may reduce face detection
  - White-box vs. black-box: White-box access enables more effective attacks but may not reflect real-world scenarios
  - Perceptual similarity vs. attack effectiveness: Maintaining human imperceptibility while achieving high attack success

- Failure signatures:
  - Attack success rate below 50% against either component
  - Loss of perceptual similarity (adversarial examples become visually obvious)
  - Instability in PGD optimization (oscillating or diverging loss values)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement single-component white-box PGD attacks on MTCNN face detector alone to establish baseline effectiveness
  2. Implement single-component white-box PGD attacks on SEIR50 feature extractor alone to establish baseline effectiveness
  3. Combine the two single-component attacks into a multi-objective optimization framework and evaluate on both components simultaneously

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the proposed attacks perform against more complex FRSs with additional components like liveness detection or anti-spoofing mechanisms?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that practical FRSs may include additional security mechanisms but does not evaluate performance against more complex multi-component systems.
- Why unresolved: Experiments only tested against basic FRS pipeline without sophisticated security measures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical testing against FRSs with liveness detection, anti-spoofing, or multi-modal verification systems.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the transferability of multi-objective attacks to black-box FRSs with different architectures or training datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Paper demonstrates white-box attacks but doesn't investigate black-box scenarios or cross-architecture transferability.
- Why unresolved: Experiments conducted in white-box settings without addressing limited knowledge scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical evaluation of attack success rates with limited or no knowledge of target FRS architecture.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do proposed attacks perform in real-world conditions with varying lighting, pose, and occlusion?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Paper acknowledges real-world challenges but experiments used controlled dataset with consistent conditions.
- Why unresolved: Experiments used standardized dataset without testing under realistic environmental variations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing attacks on real-world data captured under varying lighting, angles, occlusions, and image quality scenarios.

## Limitations

- White-box access assumptions may not reflect real-world scenarios where attackers have limited knowledge of FRS pipeline
- Perceptual similarity constraints may be violated with larger adversarial patches, making attacks detectable to humans
- Evaluation dataset of five identities from VggFace2 may not represent diversity and complexity of real-world FRS deployments

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Core methodology of using white-box PGD optimization for multi-component adversarial attacks is well-established and experimental results are reproducible
- **Medium Confidence**: Specific implementation details of the three novel attack strategies and their relative effectiveness, as some implementation specifics are not fully specified
- **Low Confidence**: Practical applicability in real-world scenarios without complete white-box access and long-term robustness of defenses trained using these adversarial examples
