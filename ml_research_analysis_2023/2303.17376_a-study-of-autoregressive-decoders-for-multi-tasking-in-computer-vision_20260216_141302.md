# A Study of Autoregressive Decoders for Multi-Tasking in Computer Vision

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.17376
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17376
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Multi-task learning with autoregressive decoders on frozen encoders works well, with task conditioning, data mixing, and decoder depth as key design factors.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates multi-task learning using autoregressive decoders on top of pretrained image encoders across diverse vision tasks. The study systematically explores design choices including task conditioning, data mixing, decoder depth, and encoder freezing. A key finding is that a small decoder on a frozen encoder (LiT decoder) performs surprisingly well, matching single-task baselines when conditioned with task prompts. The work demonstrates that multi-tasking reduces overfitting and hyperparameter sensitivity, with performance being less sensitive to task count than decoder size. Transfer between tasks is minimal, but task addition has a regularizing effect.

## Method Summary
The study uses a frozen ViT-B/16 encoder pretrained on WebLI with sigmoid-based contrastive loss, paired with a Transformer-based autoregressive decoder. Ten tasks are studied including classification, captioning, OCR, and QA. Key design choices investigated include task conditioning via prompts (e.g., "coco: <caption>"), data mixing strategies, decoder depth scaling, and encoder freezing versus fine-tuning. Training uses ScalingViT-AdaFactor optimizer with label smoothing, and sequence length is fixed at 64 tokens. Experiments compare single-task versus multi-task performance, ablate decoder depth, test mixing strategies, and evaluate frozen vs. fine-tuned encoders.

## Key Results
- Task-conditioned multi-task decoders match single-task performance across most tasks when using frozen encoders.
- Shallow decoders (8-12 layers) perform surprisingly well in multi-task settings, reducing computational cost.
- Multi-tasking provides regularization benefits, reducing overfitting and hyperparameter sensitivity compared to single-task training.
- Frozen encoders work well except for dense tasks like panoptic segmentation, which benefit from fine-tuning.

- Mechanism: Adding a task-specific prefix allows the decoder to self-attend to task identity and adjust its output distribution accordingly, preventing confusion between tasks.
- Core assumption: The decoder can effectively use the prompt to disambiguate task identity without explicit architectural changes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Frozen pretrained image encoder (ViT-B/16) -> Autoregressive decoder (Transformer) -> Task conditioning via prompts -> Dataset mixing strategy

- Critical path:
  1. Pretrain image encoder on diverse image-text data
  2. Freeze encoder weights
  3. Train autoregressive decoder on multi-task dataset with task conditioning
  4. Evaluate performance on individual tasks

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Frozen vs. fine-tuned encoder: Frozen encoders save compute but may underperform on dense tasks.
  - Decoder depth: Shallow decoders are surprisingly effective but may limit performance on complex tasks.
  - Task mixing strategy: Balancing dataset sizes and task diversity is crucial for optimal performance.

- Failure signatures:
  - Unconditioned decoder: Poor performance on non-ImageNet tasks due to inability to disambiguate task identity.
  - Shallow decoder: Slight performance drop on complex tasks like OCR-VQA and QA.
  - Frozen encoder: Poor performance on dense tasks like panoptic segmentation.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare single-task vs. multi-task performance with task conditioning.
  2. Ablate decoder depth to study the effect of capacity on multi-task performance.
  3. Experiment with different task mixing strategies to optimize performance across tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of image encoder architecture (e.g., ViT vs ConvNet) impact the effectiveness of autoregressive decoders in multi-task learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper primarily uses ViT encoders and compares to DeiT-III and ViT-AugReg, but does not explore other architectures.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to ViT-based encoders, leaving open whether similar findings hold for convolutional architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing autoregressive decoders with various encoder types (e.g., ConvNeXt, Swin) across the same multi-task setup.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal sequence length for autoregressive decoders when scaling to more tasks or denser output formats?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper fixes sequence length at 64 tokens, noting it covers most tasks but may be suboptimal for some.
- Why unresolved: The chosen length is a practical compromise; longer sequences may improve performance on dense tasks but increase computational cost.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies varying sequence length (e.g., 32, 64, 128) on tasks with varying output complexity, measuring trade-offs in accuracy and compute.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can task-conditioned autoregressive decoders effectively transfer knowledge between semantically related tasks without explicit prompting?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Experiments show minimal transfer across tasks, even when related (e.g., languages), suggesting conditioning isolates tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore soft conditioning or shared representations that might enable cross-task transfer.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with shared embeddings or task-agnostic decoders trained on related tasks, measuring if transfer emerges without hard prompting.

## Limitations

- The study relies on a specific WebLI-pretrained ViT-B/16 encoder that is not publicly available, limiting reproducibility.
- Findings are primarily based on autoregressive decoder architectures, leaving open whether similar benefits apply to non-autoregressive or diffusion-based approaches.
- The evaluation focuses mainly on English-language tasks, with limited exploration of multilingual robustness beyond the tokenizer choice.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Task conditioning via prompting significantly improves multi-task performance; multi-task learning reduces overfitting and hyperparameter sensitivity.
- **Medium Confidence**: Frozen encoders work well across most tasks except dense prediction tasks; minimal transfer between tasks while task addition provides regularization.
- **Low Confidence**: Performance is less affected by task count than decoder size; shallow decoders (8-12 layers) are effective for complex vision tasks.

## Next Checks

1. **Encoder Ablation with Different Pretraining**: Validate the frozen encoder effectiveness claim by reproducing the multi-task experiments using alternative publicly available pretrained encoders (e.g., CLIP, DINO, or MAE) to test whether the findings generalize beyond the specific WebLI checkpoint.

2. **Task Diversity Expansion**: Test the multi-task regularization hypothesis by adding a broader range of task types, particularly including more diverse dense prediction tasks and multilingual variants, to determine if the observed benefits hold across more varied task combinations.

3. **Decoder Architecture Generalization**: Evaluate whether the key findings about task conditioning and frozen encoders transfer to non-autoregressive decoder architectures, such as non-causal Transformers or diffusion models, to assess the broader applicability of the insights beyond autoregressive decoding.
