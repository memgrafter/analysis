# Differentially Private Synthetic Control

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.14084
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14084
- Reference count: 40
- This paper introduces the first differentially private algorithms for synthetic control, a causal inference technique for estimating treatment effects.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first differentially private algorithms for synthetic control, a causal inference method for estimating treatment effects. The authors develop two algorithms, DP SCout and DP SCobj, that adapt output and objective perturbation techniques from differentially private empirical risk minimization to the synthetic control setting. The key innovation is handling the "transposed" regression structure where privacy is required along rows while regression is performed along columns. The algorithms provide explicit error bounds showing the cost of privacy is O(1/ε), meaning the RMSE increases by only a factor of O(1/ε) compared to non-private methods.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces two differentially private algorithms for synthetic control. DP SCout uses output perturbation by adding Laplace noise to regression coefficients and post-intervention donor data, while DP SCobj uses objective perturbation by adding noise to the objective function and exactly minimizing it. Both algorithms handle the unique "vertical regression" structure of synthetic control where privacy must be maintained along rows while regression is performed along columns. The methods provide explicit error bounds and experimental validation on synthetic datasets demonstrating accuracy close to theoretical predictions.

## Key Results
- First differentially private algorithms for synthetic control causal inference
- Privacy cost is O(1/ε), meaning RMSE increases by only a factor proportional to 1/ε
- DP SCout and DP SCobj achieve accuracy close to their theoretical bounds
- Experimental results provide practical guidance for parameter tuning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Privacy is achieved through noise calibrated to sensitivity, but the "vertical regression" structure of synthetic control changes the sensitivity calculation compared to standard ERM.
- Mechanism: The DP algorithms use output and objective perturbation techniques from DP-ERM, but must first compute the sensitivity of the synthetic control regression under the transposed data layout.
- Core assumption: Each row (donor) in the data matrix must be protected for privacy, even though regression is performed column-wise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DP methods add noise that scales with the sensitivity of the function being computed... synthetic control performs a regression in a vertical way, treating each time point, rather than each donor's data, as one sample – thus, the transposed setting changes the definition of neighboring databases, completely altering the impact of a single donor's data."
  - [section 4.1] "The crux of the difference comes from the vertical regression (i.e., along the columns) of synthetic control as illustrated in Figure 1, while privacy must still be maintained along the rows."
- Break condition: If the data structure changes from the donor/time matrix layout assumed, the sensitivity analysis no longer holds.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The cost of privacy in synthetic control is O(1/ε), meaning RMSE increases by only a factor proportional to 1/ε compared to non-private methods.
- Mechanism: The algorithms add noise proportional to 1/ε in both the regression coefficient estimation and the prediction steps, and the theoretical analysis shows the privacy-induced error term scales as O(n/ε).
- Core assumption: The number of donors n does not grow with the problem size in synthetic control settings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that our algorithms produce accurate predictions for the target unit, and that the cost of privacy is small."
  - [section 5.1.1] "Thus we conclude that the cost of privacy in the DP SCout algorithm is at most a factor of O(1/ε)."
- Break condition: If n grows proportionally with the number of observations T, the O(n/ε) term could dominate the error.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The DP algorithms achieve privacy by adding Laplace or Gaussian noise to either the output coefficients or the objective function, with the noise magnitude calibrated to the sensitivity of the regression in the transposed setting.
- Mechanism: DP SCout adds Laplace noise to the regression coefficients and the post-intervention donor data, while DP SCobj adds noise directly to the objective function and exactly minimizes the perturbed objective.
- Core assumption: The high-dimensional Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms can be applied to the vector-valued regression outputs and objective functions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "The algorithm then samples a noise vector v from a high-dimensional Laplace distribution with parameter ∆freg/ε1, as described in Section 2.3."
  - [section 3.2] "The algorithm augments the objective function with two terms. The first is an additional regularization term... The second is the noise term b⊤f to ensure privacy."
- Break condition: If the noise distributions are not properly calibrated to the computed sensitivity values, the privacy guarantees fail.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: DP SCout/DP SCobj -> Ridge regression with noise -> Private coefficients -> Prediction with privatized donor data
- Critical path: The critical path for privacy is the composition of the learning and prediction steps. The critical path for accuracy is the sensitivity calculation and noise calibration in the learning step.
- Design tradeoffs: The choice between DP SCout and DP SCobj involves a tradeoff between the simplicity of output perturbation and the potential accuracy benefits of objective perturbation. The choice of ε1 vs ε2 allocation involves a tradeoff between protecting the regression coefficients vs the post-intervention data.
- Failure signatures: If the sensitivity is underestimated, the privacy guarantee fails. If the noise is over-calibrated, accuracy suffers unnecessarily. If the composition is not done correctly, the final output may not be private.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run DP SCout and DP SCobj with the same parameters on a synthetic dataset and compare the RMSE and runtime to verify the theoretical tradeoffs.
  2. Vary the privacy budget allocation (ε1 vs ε2) to find the optimal split for a given dataset and accuracy target.
  3. Test the algorithms on datasets with different numbers of donors (n) to empirically verify the O(n/ε) cost of privacy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal value of the regularization parameter λ for DP SCout and DP SCobj across different problem parameters (n, T0, ϵ)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the impact of λ on RMSE and suggests λ = T0 yields near-optimal performance, but also notes this depends on the specific parameter regime.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows λ = T0 works well empirically but doesn't prove this is theoretically optimal or characterize how the optimal λ varies with n, T0, and ϵ.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis proving λ = T0 is optimal (or providing a formula for optimal λ) under the model assumptions, or extensive empirical testing across a wider range of parameter values.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of noise distribution (Laplace vs Gaussian) affect the privacy-accuracy tradeoff in synthetic control, and when should each be preferred?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper implements both Laplace and Gaussian noise for DP SCobj and observes better RMSE with Gaussian noise for certain parameter regimes, especially when T0 is large.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores this empirically for limited parameter values and doesn't provide theoretical guidance on when Gaussian noise provides better privacy-accuracy tradeoffs than Laplace noise.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical comparison of the privacy-accuracy tradeoff for Laplace vs Gaussian noise under the synthetic control model, or more extensive empirical testing across a wider range of parameter values.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the asymptotic dependence on n in the sensitivity of the regression coefficients be reduced or eliminated through domain-specific knowledge or algorithmic modifications?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows the sensitivity grows as O(n) and proves this is asymptotically tight, but also notes that in practice n doesn't typically grow with problem size and suggests domain knowledge might help.
- Why unresolved: While the paper proves the O(n) dependence is necessary in the worst case, it doesn't explore whether this can be improved in practice through careful donor selection or algorithmic modifications.
- What evidence would resolve it: An analysis showing that with certain donor selection criteria or algorithmic modifications, the sensitivity can be bounded independently of n in practical scenarios, or empirical evidence demonstrating reduced sensitivity with domain-specific constraints.

## Limitations
- The privacy cost scales as O(n/ε), and performance degrades significantly when the number of donors n is large.
- The algorithms require careful parameter tuning (λ, ε allocation) for optimal performance, which may be challenging in practice.
- The sensitivity analysis relies heavily on the specific "vertical regression" structure of synthetic control, limiting generalizability to other methods.

## Confidence
- **High confidence** in the privacy mechanisms and sensitivity analysis for the DP SCout algorithm, supported by rigorous proofs and clear theoretical bounds.
- **Medium confidence** in the DP SCobj algorithm's theoretical guarantees, as the proof is provided but the empirical performance comparison is less comprehensive.
- **Medium confidence** in the experimental results, as they demonstrate the algorithms work on synthetic data but lack real-world validation on established datasets.
