# Personalisation within bounds: A risk taxonomy and policy framework for the alignment of large language models with personalised feedback

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.05453
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05453
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Proposes a three-tiered policy framework to balance the benefits of personalised LLMs with risks of bias reinforcement, echo chambers, and manipulation.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a taxonomy and policy framework for the personalisation of large language models (LLMs). Personalisation aims to adapt LLMs to individual user preferences, values, and contextual knowledge, but also raises risks of bias reinforcement, echo chambers, and manipulation. To mitigate these risks, the authors propose a three-tiered policy framework that defines immutable restrictions at the national or supra-national level, optional restrictions or requirements from the technology provider, and tailored requirements from the end-user. The framework aims to balance the benefits of personalised LLMs, such as improved utility and autonomy, with the risks of polarisation and malicious use.

## Method Summary
The paper conducts a literature review on LLM alignment, RLHF, personalised NLP systems, and related technologies. Based on this review and informed speculation about future LLM development, the authors propose a taxonomy of benefits and risks from personalised LLMs, and a three-tiered policy framework for governing them. The framework specifies restrictions and requirements at each tier, but does not provide specific principles for determining which fall under each tier.

## Key Results
- Personalisation in LLMs can improve individual utility by adapting outputs to user intent, preferences, and contextual knowledge.
- A three-tiered policy framework can enable safe personalisation by separating immutable restrictions, optional provider requirements, and user-tailored settings.
- Personalisation increases autonomy by giving users control over how the model behaves with their personal data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Personalisation in LLMs improves individual utility by adapting outputs to user intent, preferences, and contextual knowledge.
- Mechanism: The LLM learns from explicit user feedback, allowing it to predict intent more accurately and adapt outputs to match user communication styles, length preferences, and background context.
- Core assumption: User feedback is representative and stable enough to train the model without overfitting or reinforcing biases.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Personalising LLMs through micro-level preference learning processes may result in models that are better aligned with each user."
  - [section] "Personalised LLMs may increase perceived or realised usefulness of model outputs which better match the needs of their end-users."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; corpus papers focus on alignment datasets and multilingual studies, not the utility mechanism itself.
- Break condition: If user feedback is sparse, noisy, or unrepresentative, the model fails to generalise and may overfit to individual quirks.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: A three-tiered policy framework enables safe personalisation by separating immutable restrictions, optional provider requirements, and user-tailored settings.
- Mechanism: Higher-tier policies cascade down, ensuring that model-level legal restrictions cannot be overridden by application providers or users, while still allowing flexibility within safe bounds.
- Core assumption: Model and application providers act in good faith and enforce the framework consistently.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose a three-tiered policy framework that allows users to experience the benefits of personalised alignment, while restraining unsafe and undesirable LLM-behaviours within (supra-)national and organisational bounds."
  - [section] "Tier One: Immutable restrictions... Tier Two: Optional restrictions and requirements... Tier Three: Tailored requirements."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; corpus focuses on alignment datasets and risk studies, not policy enforcement mechanisms.
- Break condition: If providers act maliciously or enforcement mechanisms are weak, the framework fails to prevent harmful personalisation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Personalisation increases autonomy by giving users control over how the model behaves with their personal data.
- Mechanism: Users define their preferences, values, and context, transforming the technology into "my technology" and promoting self-determination.
- Core assumption: Users have sufficient understanding and ability to provide meaningful feedback without excessive effort.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Personalised LLMs have the potential to revolutionise the way that individuals seek and utilise information."
  - [section] "Personalisation increases user control to adapt LLMs to their own goals, preferences and values, avoiding top-down constraints on freedom from technological providers."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; corpus papers discuss alignment and multilingual studies, not autonomy mechanisms.
- Break condition: If the feedback process is too burdensome or users lack digital literacy, autonomy benefits are lost.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: User interface (Tier Three) -> Feedback collection -> Preference embedding -> Personalised model branch -> Application provider layer (Tier Two) -> Model provider layer (Tier One) -> Core LLM
- Critical path: User feedback -> Preference embedding -> Personalised inference -> Output generation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Granularity of personalisation vs. computational cost (many branches vs. shared representations)
  - User effort in feedback vs. quality of personalisation
  - Flexibility in personalisation vs. risk of harmful outputs
- Failure signatures:
  - Cold start problem: New users cannot personalise effectively due to lack of feedback
  - Overfitting: Model becomes too tailored to one user, losing general capability
  - Policy bypass: Users or providers find ways to override restrictions
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test personalisation effectiveness with synthetic user feedback on a small model branch.
  2. Evaluate the impact of different feedback formats (binary, ranked, demonstrations) on personalisation quality.
  3. Measure the policy enforcement strength by attempting to override Tier One restrictions at lower tiers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal data efficiency and quantity for personalized LLM training?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the challenge of determining the amount of data needed for effective personalization, citing examples of studies that test performance over a range of data requirements.
- Why unresolved: The exact amount of data needed for effective personalization in LLMs is unclear, and the paper highlights the cold start problem and the need for solutions to adapt to new users without any feedback data points.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the performance of personalized LLMs trained on different amounts of data, as well as investigations into data augmentation techniques and active learning methods to reduce the amount of data needed.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the alignment tax be minimized in personalized LLMs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the concern of model overfitting in RLHF techniques and the need to balance the effects on performance from degraded language representation, known as the "alignment tax."
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions that many works have demonstrated little to no alignment tax, but the exact methods and their effectiveness in minimizing the alignment tax in personalized LLMs are not fully explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of different techniques to minimize the alignment tax, such as KL-divergence penalties, rules-based approaches, and constitutional AI, in the context of personalized LLMs.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the most effective methods for enforcing the proposed three-tiered policy framework for personalized LLMs?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper proposes a three-tiered policy framework for governing personalized LLMs, but it also acknowledges the challenges in enforcement, such as compliance with existing regulations, defining new regulations, and distributed responsibility.
- Why unresolved: The paper outlines the framework but does not provide specific principles for determining which restrictions or requirements fall under each tier, nor does it address the challenges of enforcement in detail.
- What evidence would resolve it: Case studies of implementing the policy framework in real-world scenarios, as well as empirical research on the effectiveness of different enforcement mechanisms and the impact of the framework on the development and deployment of personalized LLMs.

## Limitations
- The paper relies heavily on informed speculation about future LLM development and deployment, as personalised LLMs do not yet exist at scale.
- The proposed policy framework assumes ideal enforcement conditions and good faith behavior from model and application providers, which may not hold in practice.
- The taxonomy of benefits and risks is derived from existing literature on related technologies rather than empirical studies of personalised LLMs specifically.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The identification of polarisation and malicious use as risks of personalised LLMs is well-supported by existing literature on recommender systems and filter bubbles.
- Medium confidence: The proposed three-tiered policy framework is logically coherent and builds on established principles of AI governance, though practical enforcement challenges are not fully addressed.
- Low confidence: Specific claims about the magnitude of benefits (improved utility, autonomy) from personalised LLMs are speculative without empirical evidence from actual deployments.
