# Gender, names and other mysteries: Towards the ambiguous for gender-inclusive translation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04573
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04573
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Names are unreliable gender proxies in MT; training with target-side gender tags can enable more gender-inclusive translation

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses gender translation in machine translation, focusing on cases where the source language lacks explicit gender markers but the target requires them due to richer grammatical gender. The authors argue that person names are often unreliable indicators of gender and that ambiguous-gender inputs are common but understudied in MT research. They develop methods to identify ambiguous gender cases in parallel corpora and propose new evaluation schemes and training approaches to improve gender inclusivity in translation systems.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a high-recall method for identifying person names in parallel sentences using a "title-copy" (TC) approach that matches Unicode sequences starting with titlecase characters present in both source and target. They then use dependency parsing to identify gendered target language terms associated with these names. The method is applied to English-French, English-German, and English-Spanish parallel corpora (OpenSubtitles and Europarl), followed by human evaluation to assess precision and coreference. The paper also proposes two evaluation schemes for ambiguous gender translation and suggests incorporating target-side gender tags into training to improve gender inclusivity.

## Key Results
- Person names are unreliable proxies for gender in source languages, with many name-gender co-occurrences being non-resolvable
- Ambiguous-gender examples can comprise a large proportion of training data across different language pairs and domains
- Target language gender is often not coreferent with the source entity, indicating current MT systems may introduce gender bias
- Training with target-side gender tags could enable models to learn gender associations without relying on name proxies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: High-recall name detection enables identification of ambiguous gender cases in parallel data
- Mechanism: The title-copy method identifies person names by matching Unicode sequences starting with titlecase characters that appear identically in both source and target, exploiting the assumption that names are transliterated or copied verbatim across languages
- Core assumption: Person names in parallel corpora are preserved across language pairs, and their presence signals potential gender ambiguity in target language morphology
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We develop a high-recall method to identify parallel sentences containing person names and likely gendered target language"; [section] "For our 'title-copy' (TC) approach, we recognize only names containing Unicode characters matching regex [A-Za-z`A-ˇz'-.], beginning with a titlecase character and present in both source and target text"
- Break condition: Name transliteration rules differ significantly between languages, or parallel data contains corrupted or anonymized names

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Dependency parsing of target sentences identifies gendered terms likely associated with source names
- Mechanism: By parsing target sentences to find grammatical dependencies between detected names and gendered words, the method isolates likely human-referent gendered language to analyze whether target gender is coreferent with source names
- Core assumption: Gendered target words that depend on detected names are more likely to be coreferent with those names than independent gendered words
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We perform a dependency parse of each target language sentence to identify the head of each person name, and then subsequently any dependents of that head with masculine or feminine grammatical gender"; [abstract] "We find that many name-gender co-occurrences in MT data are not resolvable with 'unambiguous gender' in the source language"
- Break condition: Target language gendered terms are not marked by dependency relations, or coreference is expressed through other linguistic means

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training with target-side gender tags mitigates name-based gender bias
- Mechanism: Incorporating gender tags from target sentences into training data allows models to learn associations between names and target gender without relying on name-to-gender proxies, enabling more inclusive outputs
- Core assumption: Target-side gender information is a more reliable signal for training than source-side name gender proxies, which are often inaccurate
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Incorporating a tag would let the model learn to associate gender tags with gendered output in the ambiguous case, without requiring either external information or names as a proxy for gender"; [abstract] "The authors propose training approaches incorporating target-side gender tags to enable more gender-inclusive translation"
- Break condition: Target-side gender tags are unavailable, noisy, or systematically biased

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Dependency parsing and grammatical gender marking
  - Why needed here: To identify target language words that are grammatically gendered and likely associated with source names, enabling analysis of gender coreference
  - Quick check question: How would you modify the dependency parsing approach if the target language uses gender agreement but doesn't mark gender morphologically?

- Concept: Parallel corpus preprocessing and filtering
  - Why needed here: To ensure clean data for name detection and gender analysis, removing duplicates and length ratio outliers that could skew results
  - Quick check question: What preprocessing steps would you add if you discovered significant domain-specific name patterns?

- Concept: Evaluation metrics for gender bias mitigation
  - Why needed here: To measure whether interventions like gender tagging or name replacement actually reduce bias in translation outputs
  - Quick check question: How would you design an evaluation metric that accounts for both over-generation and under-generation of gender information?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Name detection (TC method) -> Dependency parsing -> Gender tagging -> Evaluation
- Critical path: The pipeline starts with high-recall name detection (TC method), followed by dependency parsing to find gendered terms, then evaluation of coreference and bias. Any failure in early stages propagates downstream.
- Design tradeoffs: TC method prioritizes recall over precision to capture all potential gender-ambiguous cases, accepting more false positives. Dependency parsing assumes grammatical gender marking is consistent, which may not hold for all target languages.
- Failure signatures: High false negative rates in name detection mean missing gender-ambiguous cases. Poor dependency parsing results in incorrect gender associations. Evaluation metrics showing high gender bias despite interventions indicate problems with the training data or model architecture.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Measure TC method precision and recall on a manually annotated subset of parallel data to establish baseline performance
  2. Test dependency parsing accuracy for gendered term detection in target sentences with known gender
  3. Evaluate model performance with and without target-side gender tags on a benchmark of ambiguous gender translation cases

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the prevalence of ambiguous-gender sentence pairs in MT training data across different language pairs and domains?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors find that "gender-ambiguous examples can make up a large proportion of training examples" and show varying percentages across languages and domains in Tables 1 and 3
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides specific examples for English-to-French/German/Spanish translation, the analysis is limited to two domains (OpenSubtitles and Europarl). The prevalence may differ significantly for other language pairs or domains not studied
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic analysis of ambiguous-gender sentence pairs across a wider range of language pairs (both high-resource and low-resource), domains, and data sources would provide a comprehensive understanding of their prevalence in MT training data

### Open Question 2
- Question: How effective are current MT systems at handling ambiguous gender inputs compared to unambiguous ones?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "the goal [of ambiguous gender translation] is less well-defined" and suggest that "offering multiple gendered outputs to the user or having an human-in-the-loop, controllable machine translation" may be required. They also identify potential evaluation schemes but don't evaluate current systems
- Why unresolved: The paper proposes evaluation methods for ambiguous gender translation but doesn't actually apply them to assess current MT system performance on this task
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical evaluation of state-of-the-art MT systems using the proposed evaluation schemes (name change robustness and unnecessary gendered words) would reveal how well current systems handle ambiguous gender compared to their performance on unambiguous inputs

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the most effective training strategies for improving MT performance on ambiguous gender inputs?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors suggest training approaches incorporating target-side gender tags as a potential direction for "gender-inclusive translation which accepts the ambiguity in both gender and translation"
- Why unresolved: While the paper proposes this approach, it doesn't experimentally compare it against other possible strategies or evaluate its effectiveness
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing various training strategies (including target-side gender tags, multiple output generation, user preference incorporation) on ambiguous gender translation tasks would identify the most effective approaches for this challenging problem

## Limitations

- Reliance on automated name detection and gender association methods with unknown precision rates that could affect quantitative claims about ambiguous gender prevalence
- Limited empirical validation of proposed training approaches incorporating target-side gender tags, with no experiments demonstrating their effectiveness
- Small sample sizes in human evaluation (20 for precision, 50 for coreference) that may not be representative of broader patterns

## Confidence

- **High**: The conceptual framework for addressing ambiguous gender in MT is sound and addresses an important research gap
- **Medium**: Core findings about name unreliability and ambiguous gender prevalence are supported by methodology, but precision metrics for key automated components are lacking
- **Low**: Effectiveness of proposed training approaches has not been empirically validated through experiments
