# ProsAudit, a prosodic benchmark for self-supervised speech models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12057
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12057

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ProsAudit, a novel benchmark for evaluating the prosodic knowledge of self-supervised speech models. The benchmark consists of two tasks: protosyntax and lexical, which test models' ability to identify prosodic boundaries in different contexts. The authors evaluate several self-supervised models, including GSLM, pGSLM, and STELA, and find that all perform above chance on both tasks. They also observe that non-native models perform significantly worse on the lexical task, highlighting the importance of lexical knowledge in this task. Additionally, the authors find a clear effect of model size, with larger models trained on more data performing better on both tasks.

## Method Summary
ProsAudit is a benchmark designed to assess the structural prosodic knowledge of self-supervised speech models. It uses the Boston University Radio News Corpus (BU) dataset, which includes word and phone-level transcriptions along with prosodic hierarchy annotations based on the American English ToBI system. The benchmark consists of two tasks: protosyntax and lexical. The protosyntax task tests the model's ability to identify strong versus weak prosodic boundaries, while the lexical task tests the model's ability to distinguish between pauses inserted between and within words. The models are evaluated using a pseudo-probability metric, where the probability of the "natural" stimuli is compared to the "unnatural" stimuli. If the probability of the "natural" stimuli is higher, a score of 1 is given; otherwise, a score of 0 is given. The final score corresponds to the average score for all dev or test pairs in the given subtask.

## Key Results
- All evaluated models performed above chance on both the protosyntax and lexical tasks.
- Non-native models performed significantly worse than native ones on the lexical task, highlighting the importance of lexical knowledge.
- There was a clear effect of model size, with models trained on more data performing better on both tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Self-supervised models can learn prosodic boundary detection without explicit labels by learning to predict masked frames.
- Mechanism: Models like HuBERT or CPC learn continuous representations that capture temporal patterns; these patterns encode prosodic structure (intonation, rhythm, stress) as co-variates of the acoustic signal.
- Core assumption: Prosodic cues are acoustically manifest and redundant enough to be captured by statistical dependencies in raw audio.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "self-supervised learning (SSL) speech models such as Wav2Vec [1], CPC [2, 3], HuBert [4] have made groundbreaking advancements, while removing the need for labeled data as they use information extracted from the input raw audio itself."
  - [section] "The protosyntax task tests the model's ability to identify strong versus weak prosodic boundaries (e.g., see [13] for an evaluation of human performance)."
- Break condition: If prosodic structure is underspecified acoustically (e.g., ambiguous boundary positions), the model's predictions degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Lexical knowledge improves prosodic boundary detection when the boundary is within a word rather than between words.
- Mechanism: Lexical embeddings learned during SSL training provide additional cues (phonotactics, word-internal stress patterns) that disambiguate within-word vs. between-word pauses.
- Core assumption: Lexical representations and prosodic representations are jointly encoded in the same SSL latent space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "non-native models performed significantly worse than native ones on the lexical task, highlighting the importance of lexical knowledge in this task."
  - [section] "The lexical task requires both hierarchical prosodic knowledge at the sentence level and some lexical knowledge, as prosody is not always enough to differentiate between word boundary and within word breaks."
- Break condition: If the model is trained on insufficient data, lexical cues may be too sparse to reliably disambiguate.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Increasing training data improves both prosodic and lexical task performance, but lexical gains plateau faster for non-native models.
- Mechanism: More data provides more exposure to lexical items and their prosodic contexts, enabling finer-grained prosodic pattern learning; however, lexical learning saturates earlier for non-native models due to lack of language-specific distributional knowledge.
- Core assumption: Prosodic and lexical knowledge are acquired in tandem but with different data-efficiency curves.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also found a clear effect of size with models trained on more data performing better in the two subtasks."
  - [section] "There is a much larger native effect in this task, with non-native models performing only slightly above chance, regardless of the size of the training set."
- Break condition: If training data is domain-mismatched (e.g., audiobooks vs. spontaneous speech), gains may be limited.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Self-supervised speech representation learning (e.g., CPC, HuBERT)
  - Why needed here: Provides a framework to extract acoustic features without manual labeling, which is essential for benchmarking prosodic knowledge.
  - Quick check question: How does the CPC objective differ from masked prediction in HuBERT, and what implications does that have for prosodic feature learning?

- Concept: Prosodic hierarchy and ToBI annotation
  - Why needed here: Understanding the relationship between prosodic break levels (IP, intermediate) and pause placement is necessary to construct valid test stimuli.
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes a level-3 prosodic boundary from a level-1 boundary in ToBI, and why does that matter for the protosyntax task?

- Concept: Contrastive predictive coding (CPC) and its role in SSL
  - Why needed here: CPC is one of the core SSL methods evaluated; knowing how it works clarifies why models can detect prosodic boundaries.
  - Quick check question: In CPC, what is the predictive context window, and how might that influence the model's sensitivity to prosodic phrasing?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Acoustic encoder (CNN/LSTM/transformer) -> Continuous latent space
  - Quantizer (k-means or vector quantization) -> Discrete units
  - Language model (LSTM or transformer) -> Sequence modeling over units
  - Downstream evaluator -> Computes pseudo-probability for stimulus pairs

- Critical path:
  1. Raw audio -> Encoder -> Latent representations
  2. Latents -> Quantizer -> Discrete tokens
  3. Tokens -> Language model -> Probability distribution over next tokens
  4. Evaluator -> Compare P(natural) vs P(unnatural) for each stimulus pair

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Continuous vs. discrete latent spaces: Continuous allows smoother gradients but may lose discrete prosodic cues; discrete may better capture categorical prosodic events.
  - Deduplication: Reduces redundancy but may remove duration cues; affects lexical vs prosodic balance.
  - Model size: Larger models capture more nuanced prosodic patterns but require more data.

- Failure signatures:
  - Chance-level performance on lexical task: Indicates lack of lexical knowledge or domain mismatch.
  - Better protosyntax than lexical performance: Suggests model relies more on coarse prosodic structure than fine-grained lexical cues.
  - Degradation on non-native data: Signals over-reliance on language-specific cues.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the protosyntax and lexical tasks on a small HuBERT model trained on 50h of data; verify above-chance performance.
  2. Compare performance when deduplicating vs. not deduplicating units; check impact on duration-sensitive cues.
  3. Evaluate a French-trained model on the English benchmark; measure native vs. non-native gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the inclusion of pitch and duration prediction tasks in pGSLM models affect their performance on prosodic tasks when considering the full model loss rather than just the token-level pseudo-probability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that pGSLM models perform only slightly better than GSLM models, and suggests that this might be because the pseudo-probability is only computed at the token level, ignoring the pitch and duration losses.
- Why unresolved: The authors hypothesize that including pitch and duration losses in the pseudo-probability calculation could improve performance, but this has not been tested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Rerunning the evaluation of pGSLM models with a modified pseudo-probability calculation that incorporates pitch and duration losses, and comparing the results to the current benchmark scores.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does lexical knowledge in a model correlate with its performance on the ProsAudit lexical task?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper observes that GSLM models perform better on the lexical task than STELA models, which could be due to GSLM's stronger lexical knowledge. It suggests further research could examine this correlation.
- Why unresolved: While the paper notes a potential link between lexical knowledge and lexical task performance, it does not provide quantitative analysis of this relationship.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a systematic study that measures the lexical knowledge of various models (e.g., using existing lexical metrics) and correlates these measurements with their performance on the ProsAudit lexical task.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the deduplication of units impact the performance of different model architectures (e.g., LSTM vs. Transformer) on prosodic tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that deduplication improves GSLM model performance but worsens STELA model performance, and suggests this could be due to differences in data quantity and language model architecture.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how deduplication affects different model components or architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting controlled experiments that vary the deduplication process, data quantity, and language model architecture (LSTM vs. Transformer) independently to isolate their individual effects on prosodic task performance.

## Limitations

- The benchmark's reliance on pause insertion to test prosodic knowledge may not fully capture the richness of natural prosodic phenomena.
- The metric's binary scoring (1 or 0) for each stimulus pair may not provide sufficient granularity to distinguish subtle differences in model performance.
- The evaluation corpus being limited to radio news data may constrain the generalizability of results to other speech domains.

## Confidence

- High confidence: Claims about above-chance performance across all evaluated models on both subtasks are well-supported by the experimental results presented.
- Medium confidence: The claim about non-native models performing significantly worse on the lexical task is supported, but the underlying reasons require further investigation.
- Medium confidence: The assertion that prosodic and lexical knowledge are acquired in tandem but with different data-efficiency curves is plausible given the results, but the exact nature of this relationship remains to be fully characterized.

## Next Checks

1. **Domain generalization test**: Evaluate the same models on a spontaneous speech corpus (like Switchboard) to determine if the observed performance patterns hold across different speech styles and domains.

2. **Feature ablation study**: Systematically remove or modify different acoustic features (duration, pitch, intensity) in the test stimuli to identify which cues the models are actually using to make their decisions.

3. **Native vs. non-native model comparison**: Train multiple non-native models with varying amounts of target-language data to precisely map out the learning curve and identify where lexical knowledge acquisition plateaus.
