# Word-Level Explanations for Analyzing Bias in Text-to-Image Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.05500
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05500
- Reference count: 13
- This paper introduces a method to attribute bias in text-to-image models to specific words in the input prompt.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel approach for analyzing bias in text-to-image models by computing word-influence scores that attribute demographic underrepresentation to specific words in the input prompt. The method uses masked language models to systematically replace words in prompts, then measures how these replacements affect the demographic distribution of generated images. Applied to Stable Diffusion v.1.4, the technique successfully identifies stereotypical associations between certain words and demographic groups, providing actionable insights for bias mitigation. The word-influence scores quantify how much each word contributes to observed bias, enabling targeted interventions in prompt engineering.

## Method Summary
The method computes word-influence scores by systematically replacing each word in a prompt with alternatives generated by a masked language model (BERT-base), then measuring changes in the demographic distribution of images generated by Stable Diffusion v.1.4. For each modified prompt, the approach generates images and classifies them into demographic groups using CLIP. The word-influence score for each word quantifies its contribution to bias relative to other words, using a Shapley value-inspired framework that averages marginal contributions across all possible word subsets. The method was evaluated on 150 prompts of the form "a [ADJECTIVE] [PERSON] at the [PLACE]" and successfully identified stereotypical associations between words like "doctor" and male images, and "nurse" and female images.

## Key Results
- Successfully identifies stereotypical associations between words and demographic groups (e.g., "doctor" → male, "nurse" → female)
- Demonstrates that systematic word replacement reveals causal influence on demographic bias
- Shows word-influence scores can guide targeted interventions for bias mitigation in text-to-image models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Systematic word replacement reveals causal influence on demographic bias
- Mechanism: By replacing each word in the prompt with alternatives generated by a masked language model, the method creates counterfactual prompts that maintain grammatical coherence while varying semantic content. The change in demographic distribution between original and modified prompts indicates which words drive bias.
- Core assumption: Masked language models can generate semantically coherent replacements that preserve grammatical structure while sufficiently varying meaning to reveal causal relationships.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "leveraging masked language models to calculate the influence scores"
  - [section] "We use a pretrained masked language model (MLM) to replace words in the original prompt"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows related work on perturbation-based methods but doesn't directly support this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the masked language model consistently generates replacements that don't meaningfully alter the semantic content or fail to maintain grammatical coherence, the causal inference becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Word-influence scores quantify relative responsibility for bias using Shapley value framework
- Mechanism: The method calculates influence scores by comparing probability distributions of demographic attributes across systematically varied prompts. The score represents how much each word contributes to observed bias relative to other words, using a Shapley-like averaging over all possible word subsets.
- Core assumption: The Shapley value framework appropriately captures the marginal contribution of each word to demographic bias, accounting for interactions between words.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "These scores represent its influence on biases in the model's output"
  - [section] "We define a word-influence score...quantifies how much each word pi is responsible for producing the property G(x) = g"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus mentions related work on perturbation-based QE but doesn't validate the Shapley framework application here
- Break condition: If word interactions are highly non-additive or the number of possible word subsets becomes computationally intractable, the Shapley approximation may not accurately capture true influence.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: CLIP-guided image generation and classification creates a consistent bias measurement pipeline
- Mechanism: The same CLIP model is used both to guide image generation and to classify resulting images into demographic groups, creating a closed loop that measures bias consistently within the same feature space.
- Core assumption: CLIP's representation space is sufficiently aligned between generation guidance and classification tasks that bias measured in one is meaningful in the other.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We use CLIP as a classifier by assigning x to the group that maximizes the CLIP score"
  - [section] "CLIP is also used by the image generator"
  - [corpus] Missing - no corpus evidence directly addresses CLIP's dual use for generation and classification
- Break condition: If CLIP's internal representations shift significantly between generation and classification modes, or if the model's training data contains demographic biases that affect both processes, the measured bias may be artificially inflated or deflated.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Text Transmuter (BERT-based MLM) → Image Generator (CLIP-guided Stable Diffusion) → Group Classifier (CLIP) → Influence Calculator
  - Data flow: Original prompt → word-by-word replacement → image generation → demographic classification → influence score computation

- Critical path:
  - Word replacement generation (MLM inference) → Image generation (diffusion sampling) → Demographic classification (CLIP embedding comparison) → Influence score aggregation
  - Latency bottleneck: Image generation typically dominates runtime, especially for multiple word replacements

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using CLIP for both generation guidance and classification ensures consistency but may amplify any CLIP-specific biases
  - Increasing r-level word influence captures more complex interactions but grows computationally expensive (2^k subsets)
  - Sampling single images per prompt reduces computational cost but increases variance in demographic estimates

- Failure signatures:
  - If all word influence scores are near zero despite visible bias in generated images, the MLM may not be generating sufficiently varied replacements
  - If influence scores don't sum to expected bias levels, the Shapley approximation may be breaking down for complex word interactions
  - If classification accuracy drops significantly on generated images, the CLIP classifier may not generalize well to diffusion-generated content

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the pipeline on a prompt known to produce biased output (e.g., "a respected doctor") and verify that the word "doctor" receives the highest influence score
  2. Test the r=1 and r=2 influence scores on a prompt with complex word interactions to observe the impact of increasing r on score informativeness
  3. Replace the BERT MLM with a different masked language model (e.g., RoBERTa) and compare the resulting influence scores to test model sensitivity

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the word-influence method perform when applied to text-to-image models other than Stable Diffusion v.1.4?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that the pipeline is model-agnostic and not restricted to the particular model choices made (CLIP-guided Stable Diffusion).
- Why unresolved: The experiments were only conducted on Stable Diffusion v.1.4, so the generalizability to other T2I models remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying the method to other T2I models like DALL-E or Midjourney and comparing the word-influence scores and identified biases across models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of levels (r) for the word-influence score in terms of balancing informativeness and computational cost?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates that r=1 can be uninformative for some prompts, while r=2 provides more useful scores, but notes that increasing r leads to higher computational costs.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests r=1 and r=2, leaving the optimal r value for different types of prompts and bias detection scenarios unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying r across a range of values and prompt types, measuring both the informativeness of the scores and computational resources required.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is the word-influence metric to the choice of masked language model used for text transmutation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses BERT-base for text transmutation but notes that alternative approaches could include other MLMs or different word replacement strategies.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests one MLM, so the robustness of the word-influence scores to different text transmutation methods is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Repeating the experiments with different masked language models (e.g., RoBERTa, GPT-2) or alternative word replacement strategies and comparing the resulting word-influence scores and identified biases.

## Limitations

- The reliance on single image generation per modified prompt introduces significant variance in demographic classification
- CLIP's classification capability for demographic attributes is limited as it wasn't specifically trained for this task
- The method only addresses binary sex classification, excluding intersectional factors like race and age

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims:**
- The mathematical framework for computing word-influence scores using Shapley value-inspired aggregation is correctly formulated and internally consistent.
- The experimental methodology of systematically replacing words and measuring demographic shifts is technically sound.

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- The qualitative findings that words like "doctor" and "scientist" show male bias while "nurse" and "salon" show female bias are supported by the data, though results may vary with different prompts or models.
- The approach provides actionable insights for bias mitigation by identifying problematic words.

**Low Confidence Claims:**
- The claim that these word-influence scores provide a complete picture of bias attribution is questionable given the limited scope and potential variance in measurements.
- The generalizability of results to other text-to-image models beyond Stable Diffusion v.1.4 remains untested.

## Next Checks

1. **Variance Analysis**: Run multiple image generations (m=10 or higher) per modified prompt to quantify the variance in demographic classification and assess whether the current single-image approach introduces significant noise in the influence scores.

2. **Model Transferability Test**: Apply the same methodology to a different text-to-image model (e.g., DALL-E 2 or Imagen) to determine whether the word-influence patterns remain consistent or vary significantly across architectures.

3. **Intersectional Bias Evaluation**: Extend the analysis beyond binary sex classification to include multiple demographic attributes simultaneously (e.g., sex and race) to test whether the influence score framework scales to more complex bias scenarios.
