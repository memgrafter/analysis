# Atomic and Subgraph-aware Bilateral Aggregation for Molecular Representation Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12618
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12618
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ASBA achieves average ROC-AUC of 74.1% on molecular classification tasks and lower RMSE on regression tasks, outperforming state-of-the-art methods

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ASBA (Atomic and Subgraph-aware Bilateral Aggregation), a molecular representation learning model that combines atom-wise and subgraph-wise information through two parallel GNN branches. The key innovation is a decomposition-polymerization GNN architecture for subgraph representation learning, which preserves subgraph identity across molecules, and a novel masked subgraph-token modeling self-supervised task. ASBA demonstrates significant improvements over state-of-the-art methods on both classification and regression molecular property prediction tasks, with the bilateral aggregation approach showing consistent performance gains across diverse datasets.

## Method Summary
ASBA uses two parallel branches: an atom-wise branch with standard GNN aggregation and a subgraph-wise branch with decomposition-polymerization GNN architecture. The subgraph branch first constructs a vocabulary of functional groups using principle subgraph extraction, then decomposes molecules into non-overlapping subgraphs, extracts subgraph embeddings independently, and finally updates them based on inter-subgraph connectivity. Both branches undergo self-supervised pre-training (masked atom prediction and masked subgraph-token modeling) followed by graph-level contrastive learning to align representations. The final model output is the average of both branch predictions, fine-tuned on downstream molecular property prediction tasks.

## Key Results
- ASBA achieves average ROC-AUC of 74.1% across 8 classification tasks on MoleculeNet, outperforming previous state-of-the-art methods
- For regression tasks, ASBA achieves lower RMSE compared to baseline models on 3 MoleculeNet datasets
- Ablation studies show bilateral aggregation provides consistent improvements over single-branch models
- Pre-training with masked subgraph-token modeling significantly improves subgraph branch performance compared to random initialization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Combining atom-wise and subgraph-wise representations yields better molecular property prediction because different properties depend on different levels of molecular structure.
- Mechanism: ASBA uses two parallel branches—one aggregates atom-level embeddings, the other extracts subgraph-level embeddings—then averages their outputs to capture both perspectives.
- Core assumption: Properties are not determined by atoms or subgraphs alone; both structural levels contain complementary predictive information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "However, not all molecular properties are determined by individual atoms, and some chemical properties are closely related to functional groups (subgraphs)..."
  - [section] "Differently, different properties are sensitive to atoms or functional groups but often determined by both of them at the same time."
- Break condition: If a property is uniformly dependent on either atoms or subgraphs, the bilateral aggregation would not improve and might add noise.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The decomposition-polymerization GNN architecture preserves subgraph identity across molecules, enabling consistent substructure-aware representation learning.
- Mechanism: First, the decomposition phase maps each subgraph independently (edges within subgraphs only). Then, the polymerization phase connects these subgraph embeddings based on inter-subgraph edges, allowing structural relationships between subgraphs to be modeled.
- Core assumption: Functional groups (subgraphs) have stable semantics across different molecular contexts, and preserving their embeddings improves generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Compared with previous strategies... our strategy first extracts subgraph-level embeddings. When a subgraph appears in different molecules, both its atom-wise embeddings and the subgraph embedding remain the same."
- Break condition: If subgraph semantics are highly context-dependent, preserving subgraph identity could harm performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Masked subgraph-token modeling (MSTM) is more effective than atom-level masking because subgraph tokens correspond to stable functional groups with consistent semantics.
- Mechanism: Subgraphs are tokenized into dictionary indices, masked randomly, and the model predicts the masked indices from corrupted subgraph-level representations, learning robust substructure-aware features.
- Core assumption: Subgraph tokens have more consistent and stable semantics than individual atom tokens, making the self-supervised task more meaningful.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Different from previous strategies, which randomly mask atoms or edges to predict the attributes, our method randomly masks some subgraphs and predicts their indices in the vocabulary V..."
- Break condition: If subgraph tokens do not capture stable functional groups or the vocabulary is incomplete, the self-supervised task loses effectiveness.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Atom-wise branch: GNN → mean pooling → linear classifier
  - Subgraph-wise branch: vocabulary construction → decomposition GNN (intra-subgraph) → polymerization GNN (inter-subgraph) → linear classifier
  - ASBA output: average of both branch outputs
  - Pre-training: MSTM for subgraph branch, masked atom prediction for atom branch, contrastive consistency between branches

- Critical path:
  1. Construct subgraph vocabulary (e.g., BRICS, principle subgraph extraction)
  2. Decompose each molecule into non-overlapping subgraphs
  3. Extract subgraph embeddings independently (intra-subgraph GNN)
  4. Update subgraph embeddings based on inter-subgraph connectivity (polymerization GNN)
  5. Aggregate subgraph embeddings to graph-level representation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger subgraph vocabularies capture more diverse functional groups but increase computational cost and risk overfragmentation
  - Deeper decomposition or polymerization layers increase expressiveness but may overfit or slow training
  - MSTM prediction is harder (larger output space) but encourages richer substructure learning

- Failure signatures:
  - Overfragmentation: Subgraphs too small, losing functional group semantics → poor subgraph branch performance
  - Underfragmentation: Subgraphs too large, missing fine-grained substructure → redundancy with atom branch
  - Vocabulary mismatch: Subgraphs not representative of meaningful functional groups → MSTM task becomes meaningless

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train atom-wise and subgraph-wise branches independently on a small dataset, compare individual performance to confirm complementarity.
  2. Vary subgraph vocabulary size (e.g., 50, 100, 300) and observe impact on downstream property prediction accuracy.
  3. Apply MSTM pre-training and measure improvement in subgraph branch performance versus atom-level masking baseline.

## Limitations

- Subgraph vocabulary construction quality is critical but not thoroughly explored across different methods or sizes
- Computational complexity increases significantly with larger subgraph vocabularies and deeper decomposition-polymerization layers
- Limited analysis of how context-dependent subgraph semantics might affect the decomposition-polymerization approach's effectiveness

## Confidence

- Mechanism 1 (Bilateral aggregation benefits): High - Supported by extensive experimental comparisons and ablation studies showing consistent improvements across multiple datasets
- Mechanism 2 (Decomposition-polymerization GNN): Medium - Novel architecture with strong theoretical motivation, but limited ablation studies on alternative subgraph aggregation methods
- Mechanism 3 (MSTM effectiveness): Medium - Logical reasoning provided, but direct comparison with atom-level masking ablation would strengthen the claim
