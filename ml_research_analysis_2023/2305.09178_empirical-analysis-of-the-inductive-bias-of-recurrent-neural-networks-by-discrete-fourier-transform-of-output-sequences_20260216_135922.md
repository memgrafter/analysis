# Empirical Analysis of the Inductive Bias of Recurrent Neural Networks by Discrete Fourier Transform of Output Sequences

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09178
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09178
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: RNNs exhibit distinct inductive biases toward specific output sequence frequencies, with LSTM/GRU favoring lower frequencies and Elman RNN favoring higher frequencies

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to analyzing RNN inductive bias by computing the frequency of output sequences using discrete Fourier transform (DFT) on model outputs. Rather than enumerating candidate patterns as in prior work, the authors treat RNN outputs as discrete-time signals and directly measure their dominant frequencies. Experiments with synthetic binary sequence classification datasets reveal that LSTM and GRU architectures have an inductive bias toward lower-frequency output patterns, while Elman RNN favors higher frequencies. The bias also varies with architectural choices like number of layers and hidden layer size.

## Method Summary
The authors generate synthetic binary sequence classification datasets with controlled label change patterns (1-5 changes per sequence of length 100). They train LSTM, GRU, and Elman RNN models on extremely small training sets (1-10 examples) with 4 hyperparameter combinations ((1,200), (2,200), (3,200), (2,2000) layers/hidden size). For each trained model, they compute outputs for all prefix sequences and apply DFT to extract dominant frequencies. The analysis compares test cross-entropy loss and frequency distributions across architectures to reveal inductive biases.

## Key Results
- LSTM and GRU exhibit inductive bias toward lower-frequency output patterns
- Elman RNN shows preference for higher-frequency output patterns
- Increasing layers in LSTM/GRU shifts bias toward even lower frequencies
- Hidden layer size also influences the frequency bias distribution

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Applying DFT to RNN outputs allows direct measurement of output sequence frequency without enumerating candidate patterns
- Mechanism: RNN outputs are treated as discrete-time signals; DFT decomposes these signals into frequency components, revealing dominant frequencies that indicate how often the model switches outputs
- Core assumption: The RNN's output sequence can be meaningfully interpreted as a discrete-time signal for frequency analysis
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose to directly calculate the output sequence frequency for each model by regarding the outputs of the model as discrete-time signals and applying frequency domain analysis"
  - [section 2.2] "When f[n] is a real-value signal, it is sufficient to consider only k∈{1,..., N/2}. Here, k = 1 corresponds to the lowest frequency component and k = N/2 to the highest."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus papers discuss frequency analysis but not specifically for RNN output sequences
- Break condition: If RNN outputs are not stationary or contain noise that obscures frequency patterns, DFT analysis may not yield meaningful dominant frequencies

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Different RNN architectures exhibit distinct inductive biases toward specific output sequence frequencies
- Mechanism: LSTM and GRU architectures inherently favor lower-frequency output patterns, while Elman RNN favors higher-frequency patterns, as revealed by their dominant frequency distributions
- Core assumption: The architectural differences in gating mechanisms and memory cells create systematic biases in output frequency selection
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "LSTM and GRU have an inductive bias towards lower-frequency patterns, while Elman RNN tends to learn patterns in which the output changes at high frequencies"
  - [section 4.2] "it is clear that, in all settings, LSTM and GRU tend to learn lower-frequency patterns, while the dominant frequencies of Elman RNN tend to be higher"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - corpus papers discuss spectral inductive bias but focus on different architectures and applications
- Break condition: If the synthetic datasets or training conditions artificially constrain the frequency space, the observed architectural differences may not generalize to real-world tasks

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Increasing the number of layers in LSTM and GRU shifts their inductive bias toward even lower frequencies
- Mechanism: Deeper architectures with more layers create stronger temporal smoothing, reducing output switching frequency and increasing dominance of lower frequency components
- Core assumption: Additional layers in recurrent networks enhance their ability to capture long-term dependencies and suppress high-frequency variations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also found that the inductive bias of LSTM and GRU varies with the number of layers and the size of hidden layers"
  - [section 4.3.1] "As for LSTM, it can be seen that the proportion of patterns in the lower-frequency domain tends to increase as the number of layers increases"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus papers discuss layer effects but not specifically on output frequency bias
- Break condition: If additional layers primarily serve to increase model capacity without changing temporal processing characteristics, the frequency bias shift may not occur

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input: Prefixes of binary sequences s[0:i] for i=0 to |s|-1 -> RNN models: LSTM, GRU, Elman RNN with varying layers and hidden sizes -> Processing: Sequential prediction of output probabilities for each prefix -> Output: Sequence of probabilities M(s[0:i]) treated as discrete-time signal -> Analysis: DFT computation and dominant frequency extraction -> Evaluation: Test cross-entropy loss and frequency distribution comparison

- Critical path:
  1. Generate synthetic datasets with controlled label change patterns
  2. Train RNN models on extremely small training sets (1-10 examples)
  3. Compute model outputs for all prefixes
  4. Apply DFT to output sequences
  5. Extract dominant frequencies
  6. Analyze frequency distributions across architectures

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Small training sets maximize visibility of inductive bias but may not reflect practical performance
  - Binary sequences simplify analysis but limit generalizability to real-world tasks
  - Frequency domain analysis provides global pattern information but may miss local temporal dynamics

- Failure signatures:
  - Uniform distribution of dominant frequencies suggests random output behavior
  - High test cross-entropy loss indicates failure to learn any systematic pattern
  - Unexpected frequency distributions that don't align with architectural expectations

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train LSTM with (num_layers=1, hidden_size=200) on a synthetic dataset with 5 label changes, compute DFT of outputs, and verify lower dominant frequency compared to Elman RNN
  2. Vary hidden_size parameter for LSTM from 200 to 2000 while keeping num_layers=2, and observe changes in frequency distribution
  3. Create a dataset with only 1 label change and verify that both LSTM and Elman RNN can learn to extend this pattern with near-zero frequency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the inductive bias toward lower-frequency patterns in LSTM and GRU translate to better performance on practical sequence classification tasks with real-world data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that "the fact that LSTM and GRU, which are known to be 'more practical' than Elman RNN, tend to learn lower frequency patterns may support the idea that output sequence frequency aligns with 'practical usefulness.'" However, they do not empirically test this on real-world tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper only analyzes synthetic datasets with binary sequences and extremely small training data, which limits generalizability to practical scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing LSTM, GRU, and Elman RNN on real-world sequence classification tasks (e.g., sentiment analysis, time series prediction) while measuring output sequence frequency and task performance would clarify if lower-frequency bias correlates with better practical performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the output sequence frequency bias vary across different types of tasks (e.g., sequence-to-sequence, language modeling) and data distributions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge limitations: "Due to the computational complexity, we only tried 4 combinations of hyperparameters. However, it is still necessary to exhaustively try combinations of hyperparameters for a more detailed analysis." They also note their experimental setup differs from practical tasks in several ways.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses only on binary sequence classification with extremely sparse training data, leaving open how these findings generalize to other architectures and tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across diverse NLP tasks (translation, language modeling, text classification) with varying data distributions and sequence lengths, measuring output sequence frequency for different RNN architectures.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What specific architectural features in LSTM and GRU cause the inductive bias toward lower-frequency patterns compared to Elman RNN?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors observe that LSTM and GRU show lower-frequency bias while Elman RNN shows higher-frequency bias, but they don't investigate the underlying mechanisms. They note this differs from the expressive capacity analysis showing LSTM > GRU = Elman RNN.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the pattern but doesn't examine what architectural components (gating mechanisms, memory cells, etc.) drive this frequency bias.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies comparing different RNN variants with isolated architectural components, or theoretical analysis connecting gating mechanisms to frequency domain behavior, would identify which features cause the observed bias.

## Limitations
- Reliance on synthetic datasets with controlled label change patterns limits generalizability to real-world tasks
- Extreme data efficiency (training on 1-10 examples) creates artificial scenarios that may not reflect practical performance
- DFT analysis assumes output sequences are stationary signals, which may not hold for RNNs with stochastic behavior

## Confidence

**High confidence:** The DFT methodology for measuring output sequence frequency is sound and the computational results are reproducible given the specified experimental setup

**Medium confidence:** The claim that architectural differences in gating mechanisms create systematic frequency biases, as this relies on specific synthetic dataset characteristics

**Medium confidence:** The observation that deeper LSTM/GRU architectures shift toward lower frequencies, pending verification that this effect isn't simply due to increased model capacity
