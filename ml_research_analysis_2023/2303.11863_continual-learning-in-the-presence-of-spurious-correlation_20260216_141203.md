# Continual Learning in the Presence of Spurious Correlation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.11863
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11863
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Dataset bias transfers forward and backward between tasks in continual learning, accumulating over longer sequences; proposed BGS method reduces bias with minimal accuracy loss.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how dataset bias affects knowledge transfer in continual learning, demonstrating that biases learned in one task can transfer to other tasks both forward and backward, accumulating over longer sequences. The authors show that CL methods prioritizing stability or plasticity exacerbate bias transfer in corresponding directions. To address this, they propose Group-class Balanced Greedy Sampling (BGS), a simple plug-in method that uses group-class balanced exemplar memory to retrain the last layer of a CL model, reducing bias with minimal accuracy loss. Results show BGS consistently reduces bias metrics across CL methods and datasets, though feature-level bias may persist.

## Method Summary
The authors systematically study bias transfer in continual learning using synthetic and real-world datasets with controlled bias levels. They compare naive methods (fine-tuning, freezing) and six CL methods (LWF, EWC, ER, iCaRL, PackNet, Group DRO) on Split CIFAR-100S, CelebA 2/8, and Split ImageNet-100 with various bias types. To mitigate bias transfer, they propose BGS, which maintains a group-class balanced exemplar memory and retrains the last layer of the CL model using this balanced data. The method is evaluated against baselines in terms of accuracy, forgetting-plasticity trade-off, and bias metrics (BMR/DCA) across two-task and longer sequence scenarios.

## Key Results
- CL methods unaware of dataset bias transfer biases forward and backward between tasks
- Bias accumulates in longer sequences when tasks share the same bias type
- CL methods prioritizing stability or plasticity exacerbate bias transfer in corresponding directions
- BGS consistently reduces bias metrics across CL methods and datasets with minimal accuracy loss
- Feature-level bias persists even after BGS application, as only the classifier layer is retrained

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Dataset bias in a task gets transferred to future tasks (forward transfer) and past tasks (backward transfer) in continual learning. A model trained on biased data learns spurious correlations between features and labels. When updated incrementally, the model retains these correlations and applies them to new data, even when the bias is no longer present. For stability-focused CL methods, the model preserves bias from previous tasks; for plasticity-focused methods, the model overfits to the current biased task and misapplies that bias backward.

### Mechanism 2
Bias accumulates over longer sequences of tasks if the same type of bias appears in multiple tasks. Each time a model encounters a task with a certain type of bias, it reinforces that bias in its feature representations. Without correction, this bias compounds as more biased tasks are learned sequentially.

### Mechanism 3
CL methods that prioritize stability or plasticity exacerbate bias transfer in corresponding directions. Stability-focused methods (e.g., high regularization strength, low plasticity) retain knowledge from previous tasks, including any biases. Plasticity-focused methods (e.g., low regularization, high plasticity) rapidly adapt to the current task, imprinting its bias onto the model, which then affects earlier tasks.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Stability-plasticity dilemma in continual learning
  - Why needed here: The paper's core claim is that CL methods focused too much on either stability or plasticity exacerbate bias transfer. Understanding this tradeoff is essential to grasp why naive CL methods fail in biased settings.
  - Quick check question: What happens to a CL model's performance on previous tasks if it focuses too much on plasticity? (Answer: It forgets previous tasks; too much stability and it cannot learn new tasks effectively.)

- Concept: Spurious correlation and dataset bias
  - Why needed here: The paper investigates how unintended correlations between features and labels (bias) affect CL. Recognizing what constitutes a spurious correlation is key to understanding the experiments and the proposed solution.
  - Quick check question: In the CelebA example, what spurious correlation is introduced? (Answer: Gender correlates spuriously with age, e.g., "male" correlates with "old" and "female" with "young" due to dataset imbalance.)

- Concept: Bias transfer metrics (BMR, DCA)
  - Why needed here: These metrics quantify how much bias a model has learned. BMR measures misclassification when bias features are flipped; DCA approximates this when bias-flipped samples cannot be generated. Understanding these helps interpret the experimental results.
  - Quick check question: How is BMR calculated? (Answer: It measures the fraction of samples that are correctly classified but become misclassified when their bias feature is flipped.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: CL backbone -> BGS module -> Bias metric evaluator -> Task sequence manager
- Critical path: Train CL model on task T1 with possible bias → Store balanced exemplars in BGS memory → Train CL model on task T2 → Retrain last layer using BGS memory to reduce bias → Evaluate bias metrics and accuracy on all tasks
- Design tradeoffs:
  - BGS vs. Group DRO: BGS requires no additional hyperparameter tuning and is plug-and-play; Group DRO may need careful tuning of the learning rate for q and can hurt accuracy if not tuned properly
  - Memory size: Larger memory improves debiasing but increases storage cost and computation time for retraining
  - Last-layer only retraining: Fast and stable but cannot fix feature-level bias; full model retraining would be more effective but risk catastrophic forgetting
- Failure signatures:
  - Bias metrics (BMR/DCA) remain high after BGS: indicates feature-level bias persists; exemplar memory may be too small or imbalanced
  - Accuracy drops significantly after BGS: indicates overfitting to the memory; reduce memory size or add regularization during retraining
  - CL performance degrades over long sequences: indicates BGS memory not updated or too small to cover all class-group combinations
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement BGS wrapper around ER and test on Split CIFAR-100S with varying bias levels; measure BMR and accuracy
  2. Test BGS with different memory sizes (e.g., 10 vs 20 images per class) to find the sweet spot between debiasing and accuracy
  3. Compare BGS + ER against ER + Group DRO on CelebA8 to quantify hyperparameter tuning burden and performance trade-offs

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but several emerge from the research:
- The effectiveness of BGS on feature-level bias versus just classifier bias
- The generalizability of BGS to different types of spurious correlations beyond the studied color/grayscale and demographic attributes
- The impact of BGS on different CL settings (Task-IL, Domain-IL, Class-IL) and whether one setting inherently handles bias better

## Limitations
- BGS only addresses bias in the classifier layer, not feature representations where bias may still persist
- Experiments use reduced subsets of datasets (e.g., 10k images from CelebA) which may not fully represent dataset diversity
- The study only examines two types of spurious correlations, limiting generalizability to other bias types

## Confidence
- High confidence: Claims about forward and backward bias transfer in CL methods
- Medium confidence: Effectiveness of BGS as a plug-in solution
- Medium confidence: Group DRO comparison findings
