# Few-Shot Open-Set Learning for On-Device Customization of KeyWord Spotting Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02161
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02161
- Reference count: 0
- This work proposes few-shot open-set learning methods for on-device customization of keyword spotting systems, achieving 76% accuracy in a 10-shot scenario with low false acceptance rates.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of customizing keyword spotting (KWS) systems for user-defined keywords with limited enrollment data, while operating within the computational and memory constraints of on-device deployment. The proposed method combines a deep feature encoder with a prototype-based classifier to enable open-set classification, distinguishing between known user-defined keywords and unknown speech. Experimental results demonstrate that using triplet loss with normalized output features of a depthwise-separable convolutional neural network (DSCNN) leads to the highest accuracy (76%) in a 10-shot scenario while maintaining a low false acceptance rate (5%) for unknown data.

## Method Summary
The method involves training a feature encoder (DSCNN) on a large, diverse source dataset (MSWC) using either prototypical networks, angular prototypical networks, or triplet loss. The trained encoder is then used to extract embeddings from few-shot enrollment samples from the target dataset (GSC). These embeddings are used to compute prototypes for known keywords and an unknown class, which are then used by an open-set classifier (openNCM, OpenMAX, or Dproto) to classify new test samples based on distances to the prototypes. The approach aims to enable on-device customization of KWS systems with limited computational resources and memory.

## Key Results
- Using triplet loss with normalized features achieves the highest accuracy (76%) in a 10-shot scenario
- The proposed method maintains a low false acceptance rate (5%) for unknown data
- The approach outperforms other iso-accuracy methods and features fewer parameters than other state-of-the-art solutions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Training the feature encoder with triplet loss on normalized features improves open-set classification accuracy compared to prototypical networks.
- Mechanism: The triplet loss explicitly enforces that embeddings of same-class samples are closer together than embeddings of different-class samples by a margin. Normalizing the output features makes the model rely on the angular relationships (phase) rather than absolute distances, which improves generalization to unseen classes in open-set scenarios.
- Core assumption: The phase information of normalized embeddings contains more discriminative power for distinguishing user-defined keywords from unknown speech than raw Euclidean distances.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "the usage of the triplet loss to train an encoder with normalized output features performs better than the prototypical networks"
  - [section]: "the triplet loss achieves the highest accuracies of 71% and 76% with openNCM and DSCNN-L-NORM for 5-shot and 10-shot, respectively"
  - [corpus]: Weak; corpus neighbors do not mention triplet loss or normalization, but focus on few-shot KWS improvements.
- Break condition: If the angular information does not capture sufficient discriminative features (e.g., if embeddings collapse or become too similar across classes), accuracy would degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using a prototype-based open-set classifier with a dummy prototype generator (DProto) or open nearest class mean (openNCM) enables distinguishing user-defined keywords from unknown speech.
- Mechanism: The classifier maintains prototypes for known keywords and a separate prototype for the unknown class. During inference, the test sample is classified as unknown if its distance to the nearest known prototype exceeds a threshold, or if it is closer to the unknown prototype.
- Core assumption: The feature space learned by the encoder separates known keyword classes from unknown speech sufficiently well for a simple distance-based decision boundary to be effective.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "combining a deep feature encoder with a prototype-based classifier to enable open-set classification, distinguishing user-defined keywords from unknown speech utterances"
  - [section]: "The classification output is determined by the shortest distance" and "probability score vector P = {pi}N i=0 based on the current prototype set"

- Break condition: If the feature encoder does not produce well-separated embeddings for known and unknown classes, the classifier cannot reliably distinguish them.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training the feature encoder on a large, diverse source dataset (MSWC) improves few-shot generalization to the target GSC dataset.
- Mechanism: The feature encoder learns a general speech representation from many classes in MSWC. This pre-trained representation is then fine-tuned or used directly for few-shot classification on the target GSC dataset, leveraging the learned generalization.
- Core assumption: The speech patterns and features learned from the diverse MSWC dataset transfer effectively to the target GSC dataset, even though the specific keywords differ.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we leverage the recent Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus (MSWC) dataset [12] to train a feature extractor"

- Break condition: If the source and target datasets are too dissimilar (e.g., different languages, recording conditions), the learned features may not transfer well, reducing accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Speech input -> DSCNN feature encoder -> Feature embeddings -> Open-set classifier (openNCM/Dproto) -> Classification output
- Critical path:
  1. Train feature encoder on source dataset (MSWC) using triplet loss or prototypical networks
  2. Extract embeddings for few-shot enrollment samples from target dataset (GSC)
  3. Compute prototypes for known keywords and unknown class
  4. Classify new test samples based on distances to prototypes
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Encoder architecture: DSCNN-L has more parameters and may perform better but requires more resources; DSCNN-S is more efficient but may have lower accuracy
  - Feature normalization: Normalized features may improve generalization but could lose some absolute distance information
  - Classifier type: DProto requires training a generator but may be more robust; openNCM is simpler but may be less effective with very few samples
- Failure signatures:
  - Low accuracy on known keywords: Feature encoder may not have learned discriminative features
  - High false acceptance rate: Classifier threshold may be too permissive or feature space may not separate known and unknown well
  - Poor generalization to new keywords: Source dataset may not be diverse enough or feature encoder may overfit
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train DSCNN-L with triplet loss on MSWC and evaluate on GSC with openNCM classifier (10-shot)
  2. Train DSCNN-L with prototypical networks on MSWC and evaluate on GSC with openNCM classifier (10-shot)
  3. Train DSCNN-S with triplet loss on MSWC and evaluate on GSC with openNCM classifier (10-shot)
