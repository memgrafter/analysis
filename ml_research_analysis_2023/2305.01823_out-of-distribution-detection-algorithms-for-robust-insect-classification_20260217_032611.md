# Out-of-distribution detection algorithms for robust insect classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.01823
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01823
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Energy-based models (EBM) achieve AUROC of 0.72 for OOD detection in insect classification, outperforming MSP and MAH methods

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical challenge of robust insect classification in agricultural settings where models may encounter out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs such as non-insect objects or untrained insect species. The authors evaluate three OOD detection algorithms—Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP), Mahalanobis distance-based (MAH), and Energy-Based Models (EBM)—on an insect classifier trained on 2 million images across 142 agriculturally relevant species. EBM consistently outperforms the other methods across different classifier architectures and OOD datasets, demonstrating robustness to data imbalance and contextually near-OOD scenarios. The study establishes that integrating OOD detection enhances model trustworthiness by enabling human intervention when encountering uncertain predictions.

## Method Summary
The method involves training an insect classifier on 2 million iNaturalist images across 142 classes using three architectures (ResNet50, RegNetY32, VGG11), then applying three OOD detection algorithms (MSP, Mahalanobis distance, and Energy-Based Models) to evaluate performance. The OOD detection algorithms are applied to the trained classifier's outputs, with performance measured using AUROC and FPR95 metrics across multiple OOD datasets including ImageNet, human faces, non-insect objects, and OOD insect species.

## Key Results
- EBM achieves AUROC of 0.72 with FPR=0.31 and TPR=0.69 at threshold 31.0
- EBM outperforms MSP and MAH across all classifier architectures tested
- EBM demonstrates robustness to data imbalance and performs well on contextually near-OOD data
- Classifier accuracy improvements don't necessarily improve OOD detection for large-parameter architectures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Energy-based models (EBM) outperform other OOD detection algorithms for insect classification.
- Mechanism: EBM maps inputs to an energy score where in-distribution samples have low energy and out-of-distribution samples exhibit higher energy, providing a robust threshold for classification.
- Core assumption: The energy function effectively captures the distributional differences between in-distribution and out-of-distribution data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "EBM consistently outperforms MSP and MAH across different classifier architectures and OOD datasets, with an AUROC of 0.72"
  - [section]: "Fig. 5(b) captures the boxplot illustration of OOD and ID data distributions. They exhibit the following inter-quantiles: In-distribution inter-quantile of {min: -11.23, IQ1: 29.00, median: 36.68, IQ3: 44.71, max: 676.69 }, and Out-of-distribution inter-quantile of {min: -43.73, IQ1: 16.33, median: 23.69, IQ3: 34.30, max: 2445.05 }"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: OOD detection performance improves with classifier accuracy for models with fewer parameters.
- Mechanism: As classifier accuracy increases, the model's feature representations become more discriminative, enhancing OOD detection performance.
- Core assumption: Improved classifier accuracy leads to better feature representations that can distinguish between in-distribution and out-of-distribution data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We observe several trends. First, the performance of the EBM algorithm is (almost always) better than the other two OOD algorithms across all three architectures. However, notice that improving the accuracy of the classifier does not improve the EBM-based OOD detection performance, especially for architectures with a very large number of trainable parameters"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: EBM OOD detection is robust to data imbalance in the training data.
- Mechanism: The discriminative nature of EBM allows it to effectively distinguish between in-distribution and out-of-distribution samples regardless of class balance.
- Core assumption: EBM's energy function is not sensitive to class frequency imbalances in the training data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Fig. 8 lays out the results of this analysis. EBM – a discriminative OOD algorithm – is robust to data imbalance, and performs very well with high AUROC"
  - [section]: "Over all, we observe that using EBM is particularly promising for agricultural applications, where there is often the possibility of data imbalance"

  - Model complexity vs. OOD detection performance: More complex models may not improve OOD detection
  - Computational cost: EBM may require more computation than MSP but provides better performance
  - Data requirements: Generative methods like MAH may need more balanced training data

- Failure signatures:
  - High false positive rates for OOD detection
  - Poor performance on contextually near OOD data
  - Sensitivity to data imbalance in the training set

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare OOD detection performance of MSP, MAH, and EBM on a pre-trained insect classifier using a simple dataset split

## Limitations

- The reported performance relies heavily on specific energy threshold (31.0) selection, which may not generalize across different deployment scenarios
- Evaluation primarily uses publicly available datasets that may not fully represent diverse OOD scenarios encountered in real agricultural fields
- Computational complexity of EBM compared to simpler methods like MSP is not thoroughly analyzed

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The comparative performance of EBM versus MSP and MAH across different classifier architectures is well-supported by experimental results
- Medium Confidence: The claim that EBM is robust to data imbalance is supported but based on limited imbalance scenarios (1.0 to 0.001 ratio)
- Low Confidence: The assertion that classifier accuracy improvements don't benefit OOD detection for large-parameter architectures requires further validation across a broader range of model sizes and architectures

## Next Checks

1. **Threshold Robustness Test**: Evaluate EBM performance across a range of energy thresholds (not just the optimal 31.0) on multiple OOD datasets to assess sensitivity to threshold selection and provide confidence intervals for FPR/TPR trade-offs.

2. **Agricultural Context Validation**: Test the trained EBM detector on real agricultural field images containing non-insect objects, weather conditions, and sensor artifacts to verify performance on contextually relevant OOD data beyond the current synthetic splits.

3. **Resource Efficiency Analysis**: Measure the inference-time computational overhead of EBM compared to MSP on target deployment hardware (e.g., embedded systems) to determine practical feasibility for real-time agricultural monitoring applications.
