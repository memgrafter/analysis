# Mathematics, word problems, common sense, and artificial intelligence

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09723
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09723
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 19
- Key outcome: Even state-of-the-art AI systems achieve at best 51.9% accuracy on single-operation commonsense word problems and 33.9% on two-operation problems.

## Executive Summary
This paper examines why current AI systems struggle with elementary commonsense word problems (CSWs) that combine basic mathematics with commonsense reasoning. The author analyzes three main approaches—direct answer generation, code synthesis, and formal representation—using large language models (LLMs) on benchmarks like SVAMP and L¯ila. Results show that while LLMs can handle simple problems, their performance degrades significantly for multi-step reasoning tasks, with accuracy dropping from 51.9% to 33.9% when problems require two operations. The paper argues these limitations stem from the fundamental challenge of commonsense reasoning rather than simple mathematical computation.

## Method Summary
The paper evaluates three LLM-based approaches to solving elementary CSWs: direct answer generation where models output answers immediately, code synthesis where models generate Python code to compute solutions, and formal representation where problems are translated into logical specifications for theorem provers. These methods are tested on standardized benchmarks including SVAMP (1000 problems) and the L¯ila collection, measuring accuracy across different mathematical categories and problem complexities. The evaluation focuses on both single-operation problems and those requiring multiple arithmetic steps, with particular attention to how performance varies across mathematical domains.

## Key Results
- Best-performing systems achieve 51.9% accuracy on single-operation problems but only 33.9% on two-operation problems
- Performance varies widely across mathematical categories, with some showing near-chance performance
- Code generation approaches (using Codex) outperform direct answer generation but still struggle with multi-step reasoning
- Failures often appear to stem from artifact learning rather than genuine comprehension of problem structure

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can current AI systems reliably solve elementary commonsense word problems that require both basic mathematics and commonsense reasoning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "No existing AI systems can solve these reliably" and reports accuracy rates of at most 51.9% on single-operation problems and 33.9% on two-operation problems
- Why unresolved: Current LLM-based systems show inconsistent performance across different mathematical categories and struggle with problems requiring multiple arithmetic operations or deeper commonsense reasoning
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic benchmark testing showing consistent high accuracy (>90%) across diverse elementary CSW problems, or demonstrating that current systems fail on specific categories of problems

### Open Question 2
- Question: How important are the current limitations in solving commonsense word problems for developing AI systems capable of reading and understanding human-written mathematical content?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The author argues these limitations "may well be important in developing programs capable of reading and understanding mathematical content written by humans"
- Why unresolved: There is no systematic analysis of the cognitive processes required to read extended mathematical proofs, and it's unclear whether current AI's inability to handle simple CSWs would impede understanding of complex mathematical articles
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of AI performance on mathematical reading tasks with varying levels of commonsense reasoning requirements, or demonstrations of AI systems successfully reading complex mathematical papers

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the fundamental relationship between an AI's ability to solve commonsense word problems and its potential to conduct advanced mathematical research?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The author notes that while current limitations may not be relevant for pure mathematical proof construction, they will likely be important for applications of mathematics and possibly for reading mathematical content
- Why unresolved: It remains unclear whether human mathematicians' use of commonsense knowledge in research is essential and whether AI systems would need similar capabilities to achieve comparable results
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing human and AI mathematicians' problem-solving approaches, or successful AI systems that can conduct advanced mathematical research without commonsense reasoning capabilities

## Limitations

- The analysis relies on existing benchmarks without creating new evaluation datasets specifically designed to probe LLM boundaries
- The study focuses on elementary-level problems, making it unclear how findings generalize to advanced mathematical reasoning
- The paper doesn't present direct evidence about LLM performance on formal proofs, limiting conclusions about pure mathematical research applications

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The claim that LLMs struggle with multi-operation problems (33.9% accuracy) is supported by direct benchmark evidence across multiple approaches and datasets
- **Medium confidence**: The assertion that failures stem from artifact learning rather than genuine comprehension is plausible but not definitively proven
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that these limitations are irrelevant for pure mathematical proof construction is reasonable but untested

## Next Checks

1. **Controlled experiments on artifact learning**: Design problems where surface patterns conflict with logical reasoning to determine whether failures are truly due to pattern-matching rather than understanding limitations
2. **Cross-domain generalization tests**: Evaluate the same LLM approaches on word problems from domains not well-represented in standard training corpora to test whether performance depends on memorization
3. **Transfer learning analysis**: Test whether fine-tuning on elementary word problems improves performance on similar but unseen problems, which would indicate whether limitations stem from data coverage versus fundamental architectural constraints
