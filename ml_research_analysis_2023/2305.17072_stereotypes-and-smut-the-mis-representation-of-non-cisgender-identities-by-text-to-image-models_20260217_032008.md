# Stereotypes and Smut: The (Mis)representation of Non-cisgender Identities by Text-to-Image Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17072
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17072
- Reference count: 36
- Non-cisgender identity terms in prompts trigger stereotypical, sexualized, or dehumanized outputs from text-to-image models

## Executive Summary
Text-to-image models often misrepresent non-cisgender identities by generating outputs that are less photorealistic, more stereotypical, and more sexualized compared to cisgender representations. A systematic analysis of 231 prompts across three major models (DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion, and DALL·E mini) revealed consistent patterns of misrepresentation, including cartoonish rendering, excessive use of pride flag colors, and implied nudity. Community surveys and interviews with non-cisgender individuals showed strong concerns about these harms and their potential to reinforce societal biases, while rejecting simple technical fixes like warnings or identity erasure.

## Method Summary
The study analyzed image outputs from three text-to-image models (DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion, DALL·E mini) using 231 prompts containing diverse gender identity terms. Prompts were generated through three phases: 16 seed terms, term expansion via gender diversity resources, and identity term pairing. Outputs were annotated for photorealism, presence of individuals, implied nudity, and flag/symbol presence. A survey of 217 non-cisgender participants and follow-up interviews assessed community perspectives on representation harms and potential solutions.

## Key Results
- Non-cisgender identity prompts generated significantly less photorealistic images compared to cisgender prompts
- Images for certain non-cisgender identities were more likely to contain implied nudity and stereotyped visual elements
- Community members strongly rejected simple heuristic fixes like warnings or ignoring identity terms, instead calling for community-led data curation and customizable outputs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Non-cisgender identity terms trigger stereotyped, sexualized, and dehumanized outputs because training data contains far fewer diverse gender identity examples.
- Mechanism: Sparse or biased training data means the model lacks sufficient reference examples for non-cisgender identities, causing it to fall back on stereotypical or sexualized patterns seen in the dominant data.
- Core assumption: Training data predominantly represents cisgender, white, and often sexualized depictions of gender diversity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "certain non-cisgender identities are consistently (mis)represented as less human, more stereotyped and more sexualised"
  - [section 3.4] "often cartoonish and figures were rendered using colours from associated flags, adding to the lack of realism"
  - [corpus] Weak anchor: neighbor papers discuss stereotypes in T2I models but do not directly cite training data composition.
- Break Condition: If training data is enriched with authentic, diverse, and non-sexualized images of non-cisgender people, the model would produce more realistic and respectful outputs.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Simple heuristic fixes like warnings or identity erasure are rejected because they perpetuate harm by reinforcing stigma or erasing identity.
- Mechanism: Non-cisgender participants view these heuristics as harmful because they either signal that non-cisgender identities are inherently offensive or they erase identity altogether, worsening representation issues.
- Core assumption: Community members prioritize authentic representation over temporary safety filters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Simple heuristics to limit offensive content are widely rejected"
  - [section 4.2.3] "The proposed heuristic solutions such as ignoring non-cisgender identities terms ... were all deeply unpopular"
  - [section 5] Interviewees express concerns that warning labels could associate transness with offensiveness.
- Break Condition: If heuristics are replaced with community-led, transparent, and customizable representation methods, user acceptance and model fairness would improve.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Community-led data curation and customizable outputs can reduce harm by ensuring authentic and diverse representation.
- Mechanism: Involving the affected community in training data selection and model output design leads to outputs that better reflect real identities and reduce stereotypical patterns.
- Core assumption: Non-cisgender individuals have unique insights into authentic representation that developers lack.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "respondents call for community involvement, curated training data and the ability to customise"
  - [section 6] "Careful, community led data curation may address some of these concerns"
  - [section 5] Interviewees emphasize the need for community input "at every step".
- Break Condition: If community input is tokenistic or lacks diversity within the community itself, representation issues may persist.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Prompt templates → Text-to-image model (DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion, DALL·E mini) → Image output → Annotation pipeline → Community feedback loop
  Training data curation module (community-led)
  Customizability interface (user-controlled output tuning)

- Critical path:
  1. Prompt generation with identity terms
  2. Image generation via chosen model
  3. Annotation of photorealism, presence of individuals, implied nudity, flags/symbols
  4. Community survey and interviews to assess harm and desired solutions
  5. Iterate on model training and interface based on feedback

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Data diversity vs. safety: Collecting more diverse training images may increase privacy risks.
  - Customizability vs. misuse: Allowing post-hoc image edits could enable harmful content creation.
  - Heuristic warnings vs. erasure: Warnings may stigmatize; ignoring terms may erase identities.

- Failure signatures:
  - High frequency of cartoon-like or abstract outputs for non-cisgender terms.
  - Consistent absence of non-white individuals in generated images.
  - Predominance of sexualized or nude imagery for certain identity prompts.
  - Rejection of prompts containing non-cisgender terms by safety filters.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replace generic identity prompts with community-sourced, diverse, and non-sexualized training images; measure change in photorealism and stereotype frequency.
  2. Implement a customizable output interface allowing users to adjust gender presentation and symbol inclusion; evaluate user satisfaction and representation accuracy.
  3. Compare outputs when using community-curated training data versus baseline; analyze changes in representational harm and community acceptance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do text-to-image models represent non-cisgender individuals in terms of photorealism and implied nudity, and how does this vary by identity term?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper's annotation study found that images generated for non-cisgender identities were less photorealistic and more likely to contain implied nudity compared to cisgender identities.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides a snapshot of current model behavior but does not explore the underlying reasons for these disparities or how they might change with future model updates.
- What evidence would resolve it: A longitudinal study comparing model outputs across multiple versions and datasets, including analysis of training data composition and model architecture changes.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the most effective methods for improving the representation of non-cisgender identities in text-to-image models without erasing or stereotyping these communities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found that respondents rejected simple heuristics like warnings or identity erasure, instead calling for community-led training data curation and customizable outputs.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies community preferences but does not empirically test which interventions actually improve representation quality and reduce harm.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative study of different model training and fine-tuning approaches (e.g., community-curated datasets, customizable outputs) with quantitative and qualitative assessments of representation quality and community feedback.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do text-to-image models perform across intersecting demographics (e.g., disability, age, ethnicity) when representing non-cisgender identities, and what unique challenges arise?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper acknowledges limitations in capturing perspectives from multiply marginalized individuals and intersecting demographics, noting that most respondents were white and Western.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses primarily on gender identity representation without thoroughly exploring how this intersects with other aspects of identity that may compound misrepresentation or harm.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study that explicitly examines model outputs for non-cisgender individuals across multiple intersecting identity categories, using diverse datasets and community feedback from multiply marginalized groups.

## Limitations
- Subjective annotations for image characteristics could introduce inter-rater variability
- Limited prompt set may miss important edge cases in gender identity representation
- Survey sample may not capture full diversity of non-cisgender community experiences
- Focus on three specific models limits generalizability to other generation systems

## Confidence
**High Confidence**: Claims about systematic differences in image outputs between cisgender and non-cisgender prompts are well-supported by quantitative analysis of 231 prompts across three models.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that training data bias is the primary mechanism driving stereotypical outputs is plausible but not directly measured.

**Low Confidence**: The claim that community-led curation will definitively solve representation problems is aspirational rather than empirically validated.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a systematic audit of the training data composition for each model, quantifying the representation of diverse gender identities versus cisgender identities, and correlating these distributions with output patterns.

2. Design a factorial experiment varying prompt characteristics (e.g., context, adjectives, cultural references) while holding identity terms constant to isolate which prompt features trigger stereotypical outputs.

3. Implement a small-scale pilot where community members select and annotate training images for underrepresented identities, then retrain or fine-tune a model and measure changes in output quality and representational harm.
