# Learning Zero-Shot Cooperation with Humans, Assuming Humans Are Biased

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.01605
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01605
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: HSP agents achieve higher rewards than baselines when cooperating with learned human models, scripted policies, and real humans in Overcooked.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Hidden-Utility Self-Play (HSP), a framework for training AI agents that can cooperate effectively with humans in zero-shot settings, assuming humans have hidden reward biases. The key innovation is modeling human biases as hidden reward functions and using self-play to generate a diverse policy pool covering various human preferences. Experiments in the Overcooked benchmark demonstrate that HSP agents achieve higher rewards and are rated as more assistive by human participants compared to baseline methods.

## Method Summary
HSP trains AI agents through a two-stage process: first, it generates a diverse policy pool by sampling hidden reward functions as linear combinations of event-based features, then trains an adaptive policy to maximize task reward against this pool. The method explicitly models human biases as hidden reward functions in a self-play objective, using policy filtering based on event-based diversity to maintain an efficient and effective policy pool. HSP combines policies trained under both biased rewards and the game reward (MEP) to capture diverse cooperative strategies.

## Key Results
- HSP agents achieve higher rewards than baselines when cooperating with learned human models across 5 Overcooked layouts
- Human participants rated HSP as the most assistive policy compared to baselines
- Policy filtering based on event-based diversity significantly improves HSP performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: HSP improves cooperation by explicitly modeling human biases as hidden reward functions, enabling the agent to adapt to diverse human preferences beyond optimal strategies.
- Mechanism: HSP introduces a hidden-utility Markov game where the human policy maximizes a hidden reward Rw while the AI policy maximizes the task reward Rt. By sampling Rw from a linear function space over event-based features, HSP generates a diverse policy pool covering various human biases.
- Core assumption: Human behaviors can be represented as policies optimal under some hidden reward function, and this hidden reward space can be approximated effectively by linear functions over event features.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "HSP explicitly models human biases as hidden reward functions in the self-play objective."
  - [section 5.1]: "Lemma 5.1 connects any human behavior to a hidden reward function."
  - [corpus]: Weak - the related papers focus on cooperation and human-AI teaming but don't directly validate the hidden-utility framework.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Policy filtering based on event-based diversity improves the efficiency and effectiveness of the policy pool.
- Mechanism: HSP uses a greedy algorithm to select K policies from N initial biased policies, maximizing event-based diversity measured by normalized pairwise differences in expected event counts. This removes duplicated policies that would slow training without improving generalization.
- Core assumption: Duplicated policies in the initial pool are common due to multiple reward functions yielding the same optimal policy, and diversity in event counts correlates with diversity in behavior.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.2]: "We notice that the derived pool often contains a lot of similar policies... For more efficient training, we adopt a behavior metric, i.e., event-based diversity, to only keep distinct ones."
  - [section 6.2]: "With policy filtering turned off, even though the policy pool size grows, the performance significantly decays."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Including MEP policies in the policy pool provides additional diversity beyond biased strategies, improving overall performance.
- Mechanism: HSP combines 18 MEP policies (trained under the game reward) with 18 filtered biased policies to create a 36-policy pool. MEP policies capture optimal strategies while biased policies capture human preferences.
- Core assumption: MEP policies provide complementary diversity to biased policies, covering strategies that may not emerge from biased reward functions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6.2]: "By excluding MEP policies, the HSP variant (HSP w.o. MEP) performs worse in the more complicated layout Counter Circ."
  - [section 5.3]: "In practice, we use half of the policy pool to train biased policies and the other half to train MEP policies under the game reward."

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Event feature extractor -> Hidden reward sampler -> Biased policy trainer -> Policy pool builder -> Adaptive policy trainer
- Critical path: Event features → Hidden reward sampling → Biased policy training → Policy pool construction → Adaptive policy training
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Linear vs. non-linear reward approximation: Linear functions are tractable but may miss complex preferences
  - Pool size vs. training efficiency: Larger pools provide more diversity but increase training time
  - Event selection vs. generality: More events capture more preferences but require more domain knowledge
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance despite diverse pool: May indicate ineffective event features or insufficient training
  - Instability during adaptive training: May indicate need for larger batch size or better policy initialization
  - No improvement over baselines: May indicate the hidden-utility framework isn't capturing relevant human biases
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train HSP with only biased policies (no MEP) and compare to full HSP
  2. Vary the number of event features used in random search and measure impact on performance
  3. Test different policy filtering criteria (e.g., behavioral cloning similarity instead of event diversity)

## Limitations
- The human models used for evaluation are learned from data rather than actual human behavior, which may not fully capture human decision-making patterns.
- The event-based reward features are designed with domain knowledge specific to Overcooked, limiting direct applicability to other domains without careful feature engineering.
- The policy filtering mechanism relies on greedy selection of diverse policies based on event counts, which may miss important behavioral patterns not captured by the chosen events.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The core framework of modeling human biases as hidden reward functions and using self-play to train adaptive policies is well-supported by experimental results. The improvement over baselines in both simulated and human evaluations is consistent across multiple Overcooked layouts.
- **Medium confidence**: The effectiveness of the policy filtering mechanism and the specific contribution of MEP policies to overall performance. While ablation studies show these components help, the analysis could be more detailed about why and when they matter most.
- **Low confidence**: The generalizability of the approach to domains beyond Overcooked and the robustness of the method to different types of human biases not captured by the event-based feature space.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain validation**: Test HSP on a different cooperative task (e.g., two-player gridworld or dialogue task) to evaluate generalizability beyond Overcooked. This would require defining appropriate event features for the new domain and assessing whether the hidden-utility framework still improves cooperation.
2. **Human bias coverage analysis**: Systematically vary the types of human biases in learned human models (e.g., preferences for specific actions, spatial biases, temporal preferences) and measure HSP's performance across this spectrum. This would validate whether HSP truly captures diverse human behaviors or is optimized for specific bias types.
3. **Real-time adaptation capability**: Evaluate whether HSP can adapt to new human partners during deployment, not just during training. This would involve testing the trained policy against humans with previously unseen biases and measuring how quickly and effectively it adapts to maximize cooperation reward.
