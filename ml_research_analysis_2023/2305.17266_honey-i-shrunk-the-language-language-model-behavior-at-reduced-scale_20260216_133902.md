# Honey, I Shrunk the Language: Language Model Behavior at Reduced Scale

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.17266
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17266
- **Reference count**: 5
- **Key outcome**: Pre-training benefits are observable in models as small as 1.25M parameters when using a reduced 21k-word vocabulary, with strong correlation between pre-training perplexity and downstream GLUE performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether the benefits of pre-training can be observed in small language models by reducing vocabulary size to mimic early language acquisition. The authors pre-train over 70 transformer models (1-100M parameters) using a 21k-word vocabulary from child-directed speech on filtered large text corpora. They find that pre-training benefits are observable even in models as small as 1.25M parameters, with a strong correlation between pre-training perplexity and downstream GLUE performance. The study reveals that smaller models (2-4 layers) achieve better GLUE scores than larger ones, and that compute-optimal training is not crucial for downstream performance. Additionally, they identify a break in the FLOPs-perplexity power law at 2.2 × 10^15 FLOPs, indicating limitations to scaling laws at this scale.

## Method Summary
The authors filtered large text corpora (C4, Wikipedia, Book Corpus, Simple Wikipedia, Children's Book Test) using a 21k-word vocabulary from child-directed speech to create a reduced-vocabulary language. They trained over 70 transformer models ranging from 1M to 100M parameters with varying architectures using masked language modeling (MLM) as the pre-training objective. The pre-trained models were then fine-tuned on filtered GLUE benchmark tasks. The study examined the relationship between pre-training perplexity and downstream performance, the impact of model architecture on performance, and the applicability of scaling laws to small models.

## Key Results
- Pre-training benefits are observable in models as small as 1.25M parameters when using reduced vocabulary
- Strong correlation (Spearman coefficient -0.67) between pre-training perplexity and downstream GLUE performance on filtered datasets
- Models with 2-4 layers outperform larger models (8 layers) in the reduced vocabulary setting
- Compute-optimal training is not crucial for downstream performance in this setting
- Break in FLOPs-perplexity power law at 2.2 × 10^15 FLOPs indicates limitations to scaling laws at this scale

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Reduced Vocabulary Lowers Problem Complexity
- **Claim**: Reducing vocabulary size allows smaller language models to benefit from pre-training by lowering the effective problem size
- **Mechanism**: By filtering large text corpora to include only 21k words from child-directed speech, the authors create a reduced-vocabulary language that reduces the complexity of the modeling task, allowing models as small as 1.25M parameters to effectively learn from pre-training
- **Core assumption**: The benefits of pre-training scale down with the problem size; reducing vocabulary size proportionally reduces the required model capacity to learn effectively
- **Evidence anchors**: The authors show benefits of pre-training with MLM objective in models as small as 1.25M parameters; related work on "Emergent Abilities in Reduced-Scale Generative Language Models" supports the plausibility of reduced-scale emergent behavior
- **Break condition**: Benefits may break down if vocabulary becomes too small to capture necessary linguistic features or if reduced vocabulary does not adequately represent downstream tasks

### Mechanism 2: Pre-training Perplexity Correlates with Downstream Performance
- **Claim**: Strong correlation between upstream pre-training perplexity and downstream GLUE performance in reduced-scale models
- **Mechanism**: Pre-training with MLM on reduced vocabulary improves model's understanding of language structure, which translates to better performance on downstream tasks like GLUE; strong correlation (Spearman coefficient -0.67) between perplexity and GLUE scores for filtered datasets
- **Core assumption**: Improvements in pre-training perplexity directly reflect improved language understanding that benefits downstream tasks
- **Evidence anchors**: "establish a strong correlation between pre-training perplexity and downstream performance (GLUE benchmark)"; "average GLUE score calculated for filtered GLUE datasets highly correlates with model perplexity with Spearman coefficient value of -0.67"
- **Break condition**: Correlation may weaken if downstream tasks require knowledge not captured by reduced vocabulary or if model capacity is insufficient to generalize beyond pre-training data

### Mechanism 3: Fewer Layers Optimize Performance in Reduced Vocabulary Setting
- **Claim**: Increasing number of layers does not always improve downstream performance in small models with reduced vocabulary
- **Mechanism**: Models with fewer layers (2-4) achieve better GLUE scores than larger ones (8 layers), suggesting that in reduced vocabulary setting, deeper models may overfit or fail to learn effectively due to limited complexity of language
- **Core assumption**: Optimal model architecture for reduced vocabulary task differs from standard language tasks; simpler architectures may be more effective
- **Evidence anchors**: "We also find that adding layers does not always benefit downstream performance"; "GLUE performance peaks for models with 2 and 4 hidden layers... average GLUE score decreases for model with 8 hidden layers to 56.29"
- **Break condition**: Benefits of fewer layers may reverse if vocabulary size or task complexity increases, requiring deeper architectures to capture nuanced patterns

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Masked Language Modeling (MLM)**
  - **Why needed here**: MLM is the pre-training objective used by the authors; understanding how MLM works is crucial to grasp why pre-training benefits small models in this context
  - **Quick check question**: How does MLM differ from standard causal language modeling, and why might it be more effective for natural language understanding tasks?

- **Concept: Scaling Laws**
  - **Why needed here**: The paper extends scaling laws to models as small as ~1M parameters and observes a break in the power law for compute-optimal models at 2.2 × 10^15 FLOPs
  - **Quick check question**: What is the general form of scaling laws in language models, and how do they typically relate model size, data size, and performance?

- **Concept: Compute-Optimal Training**
  - **Why needed here**: The authors find that compute-optimal training is not crucial for downstream results, which is an important insight for resource-constrained settings
  - **Quick check question**: What does compute-optimal training mean in the context of language models, and why might it be less important for downstream performance in reduced-scale settings?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: C4, Wikipedia, Book Corpus, Simple Wikipedia, Children's Book Test -> Vocabulary filtering (21k words) -> BPE tokenizer (19k tokens) -> Transformer encoder (configurable) -> MLM pre-training -> Filtered GLUE tasks

- **Critical path**:
  1. Filter large text corpora to create a reduced-vocabulary dataset
  2. Train a tokenizer suitable for the reduced vocabulary
  3. Pre-train transformer models with varying configurations on the filtered data
  4. Fine-tune pre-trained models on filtered GLUE tasks
  5. Evaluate and analyze the relationship between pre-training and downstream performance

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Vocabulary size vs. model complexity: Smaller vocabularies allow smaller models but may limit expressiveness
  - Model depth vs. performance: Fewer layers may be better for reduced vocabulary but insufficient for more complex tasks
  - Pre-training compute vs. downstream gains: Compute-optimal training may not be necessary, but under-training could hurt performance

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor downstream performance despite low pre-training perplexity: May indicate that reduced vocabulary does not capture necessary features for the task
  - Overfitting on pre-training data: Could occur if model is too large relative to reduced vocabulary
  - High perplexity on pre-training data: Suggests model is not learning effectively, possibly due to inappropriate architecture or insufficient training

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Pre-train a small model (e.g., 1.25M parameters) with a standard vocabulary and compare its performance to the reduced-vocabulary case to isolate the effect of vocabulary reduction
  2. Vary the number of layers in small models (e.g., 1, 2, 4, 8 layers) while keeping other parameters constant to confirm the finding that fewer layers can be better in reduced vocabulary settings
  3. Pre-train models with and without vocabulary filtering on the same downstream tasks to quantify the impact of vocabulary reduction on both pre-training and downstream performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Does the reduction in vocabulary size represent the best or most effective means of downscaling language models for improved downstream performance?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors acknowledge that their usage of vocabulary filtration as a means of downscaling input data size may not be the most effective means of limiting input data, and that alternative approaches such as curriculum learning and data pruning merit study
- **Why unresolved**: The authors only explored one method of downscaling (vocabulary filtration) and did not compare it to other methods
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments comparing the effectiveness of different downscaling methods (e.g., vocabulary filtration, curriculum learning, data pruning) on the downstream performance of language models

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How do the scaling laws observed in this study for reduced-vocabulary language models compare to those for standard vocabulary language models?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors observed a break in the FLOPs-Perplexity power law at the 2.2 × 10^15 FLOP region, which shows the limited applicability of scaling laws in the reduced-vocabulary setting
- **Why unresolved**: The authors only explored scaling laws in the reduced-vocabulary setting and did not compare them to standard vocabulary settings
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments comparing the scaling laws (e.g., FLOPs-Perplexity, parameter count-Perplexity) of reduced-vocabulary and standard vocabulary language models

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Do generative models demonstrate emergent abilities in a downscaled language setting, similar to the benefits of pre-training observed in this study?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors mention that the next logical steps as a follow-on to this work would be to check whether generative models would demonstrate any emergent abilities in a downscaled setting
- **Why unresolved**: The authors only explored the effects of pre-training on language understanding models and did not investigate generative models
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments investigating the effects of pre-training on the performance of generative models in a downscaled language setting

## Limitations

- The findings are based on a highly constrained vocabulary (21k words) that may not generalize to standard language tasks
- The strong correlation between pre-training perplexity and downstream performance was observed only on filtered GLUE datasets, not full benchmarks
- The break in FLOPs-perplexity power law at 2.2 × 10^15 FLOPs represents a fundamental limitation that may constrain applicability of scaling laws to very small models

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The observation that pre-training benefits are observable in models as small as 1.25M parameters when using reduced vocabulary is well-supported by experimental results and aligns with expected scaling of benefits with problem size reduction.

**Medium Confidence**: The finding that compute-optimal training is not crucial for downstream performance is supported by experiments but requires further validation across different model architectures and tasks beyond GLUE.

**Medium Confidence**: The break in FLOPs-perplexity power law at 2.2 × 10^15 FLOPs is based on observed data but the underlying reasons for this break are not fully explored or explained.

## Next Checks

1. **Vocabulary Generalization Test**: Evaluate the pre-trained models on standard GLUE benchmarks without vocabulary filtering to determine how much performance degrades compared to filtered tasks, establishing the practical limits of vocabulary reduction benefits.

2. **Scaling Law Break Analysis**: Conduct additional experiments with models spanning different compute regimes (below and above 2.2 × 10^15 FLOPs) to better characterize the nature and causes of the observed power law break, potentially testing whether this is an artifact of model size or reflects fundamental limitations.

3. **Architecture Sensitivity Validation**: Test whether the finding that fewer layers (2-4) outperform deeper architectures (8 layers) in reduced vocabulary settings holds across different model configurations, attention mechanisms, and training objectives to establish the robustness of this architectural insight.
