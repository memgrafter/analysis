# LMs stand their Ground: Investigating the Effect of Embodiment in Figurative Language Interpretation by Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.03445
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03445
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 13
- Key outcome: Larger LMs (>1B parameters) show significant positive correlation between verb embodiment ratings and metaphor interpretation accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether language models' ability to interpret figurative language depends on the degree to which the underlying action is embodied. Using a Winograd-style task with crowdsourced creative metaphors, the authors find that larger language models (with over 1 billion parameters) perform significantly better at interpreting metaphors when the action involved is more embodied. This effect is not observed in smaller LMs and cannot be explained by other linguistic features like concreteness, age of acquisition, word frequency, or word length. The findings suggest that embodied concepts in metaphors are more lexicalized, creating stronger statistical signals that larger LMs can leverage.

## Method Summary
The study uses the Fig-QA dataset containing crowdsourced creative metaphors paired with Winograd schema interpretations. The authors create a subcorpus (CEmb) containing phrases with embodied verbs using ratings from Sidhu et al. (2014). Various LMs (GPT-3, OPT, GPT-Neo, GPT-2) are evaluated in zero-shot mode on this subcorpus. Model performance is correlated with embodiment scores and other linguistic features including concreteness, age of acquisition, word frequency, and word length. The analysis uses point biserial correlation coefficients and variance inflation factors to assess multicollinearity.

## Key Results
- Larger LMs (>1B parameters) show significant positive correlation between embodiment ratings and metaphor interpretation accuracy
- This correlation is not present in smaller LMs
- Concreteness, age of acquisition, word frequency, and word length do not explain the embodiment effect

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Embodied concepts in metaphors are more lexicalized, enabling LMs to interpret them more accurately
- Mechanism: When a metaphor uses a verb with high embodiment (e.g., "dances"), the embodied action has been conventionally mapped to its source domain, creating a stronger statistical signal in the training data
- Core assumption: Embodiment ratings from Sidhu et al. (2014) accurately capture the degree to which a verb's meaning involves the human body
- Evidence anchors: "larger language models perform better at interpreting metaphoric sentences when the action of the metaphorical sentence is more embodied" and "This mapping draws from the human, bodily experience of dancing and therefore enables interpretation"
- Break condition: If embodiment ratings do not align with human intuitions about bodily involvement, or if metaphors with high embodiment are not more conventional, the correlation may not hold

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Larger LMs (>1B parameters) can better leverage the lexicalization of embodied concepts due to their increased representational capacity
- Mechanism: As model size increases, the model's ability to capture nuanced statistical patterns in language improves, allowing it to recognize the conventional mappings between embodied actions and their figurative meanings
- Core assumption: Model performance on figurative language interpretation improves monotonically with model size
- Evidence anchors: "All larger versions of the models show a significant correlation (p< 0.05) between the embodiment rating and task performance" and "In the case of GPT-3, both model variants show a significant correlation"
- Break condition: If the correlation between model size and performance is not robust across different model types or tasks, or if smaller models can match larger ones with fine-tuning, this mechanism may not be primary

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The effect of embodiment on LM performance is distinct from the effects of concreteness, AoA, word frequency, and word length
- Mechanism: By controlling for these other linguistic features, the study isolates the specific contribution of embodiment to figurative language interpretation, showing that embodiment has a unique predictive value
- Core assumption: Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis effectively rules out multicollinearity between embodiment and other linguistic features
- Evidence anchors: "All factors are close to 1.0, which indicates that there is no multicollinearity among predictor variables" and "concreteness, AoA, word length and word frequency do not inflate this effect"
- Break condition: If VIF analysis is insufficient to capture complex interactions between features, or if embodiment is merely a proxy for another unmeasured variable, the claim of distinctness may not hold

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Fig-QA dataset -> CEmb subcorpus creation -> LMs (GPT-3, OPT, GPT-Neo, GPT-2) -> Zero-shot evaluation -> Performance scoring -> Correlation analysis with embodiment ratings and linguistic features
- Critical path: The most critical path is the correlation analysis between LM performance and embodiment scores, which requires accurate embodiment ratings, reliable LM predictions, and robust statistical testing
- Design tradeoffs: The study trades off the use of a single embodiment rating dataset (Sidhu et al., 2014) for consistency, but this may limit the generalizability of the findings to other datasets or cultural contexts
- Failure signatures: If the correlation between embodiment and performance is not significant for larger models, or if multicollinearity is detected between embodiment and other linguistic features, the study's core hypothesis may be invalidated
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce the results on the original Fig-QA test set to establish a baseline for LM performance on figurative language tasks
  2. Evaluate the correlation between embodiment scores and LM performance on the CEmb subcorpus to test the main hypothesis
  3. Assess the correlation between concreteness scores and LM performance to rule out multicollinearity with embodiment

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the correlation between embodiment and LM performance on figurative language interpretation generalize to other figurative language tasks beyond Fig-QA, such as metaphor detection or sentiment analysis involving idioms?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge that their study is limited to one specific figurative language task (Fig-QA) and suggest testing other tasks like metaphor detection or sentiment analysis involving idioms in future work
- Why unresolved: The current study only examines the effect of embodiment on LM performance for one particular figurative language task. It is unclear whether the observed correlation would hold for other types of figurative language or different NLP tasks
- What evidence would resolve it: Future studies could test the same correlation between embodiment scores and LM performance on a variety of figurative language tasks, such as metaphor detection, simile interpretation, idiom understanding, or sentiment analysis involving figurative expressions

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the causal mechanism underlying the observed correlation between embodiment and LM performance on figurative language interpretation? Is it truly due to lexicalization of conventional metaphors, or are there other factors at play?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose that the correlation may be explained by the lexicalization of conventional metaphors, as more embodied concepts are more lexicalized. However, they acknowledge that a thorough explanation would require further studies
- Why unresolved: While the authors provide a plausible explanation, the current study does not directly test the causal mechanism. It is possible that other factors, such as the frequency of occurrence or the complexity of the embodied concepts, could contribute to the observed effect
- What evidence would resolve it: Future studies could manipulate the lexicalization of metaphors independently of their embodiment, or control for other factors like frequency and complexity

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the correlation between embodiment and LM performance vary across different LM architectures and training regimes? Are there specific architectural features or training strategies that enhance or diminish the effect?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that the correlation between embodiment and performance is present in larger LMs (>1 billion parameters) but not in smaller ones. However, they do not explore the impact of different architectures or training strategies
- Why unresolved: The current study focuses on a limited set of LM architectures and does not investigate how the correlation might vary across different model types or training approaches
- What evidence would resolve it: Future studies could systematically compare the correlation across a wide range of LM architectures (e.g., transformer-based, recurrent, or convolutional models) and training regimes (e.g., supervised, unsupervised, or self-supervised learning)

## Limitations

- The study only examines one specific figurative language task (metaphor interpretation) and cannot generalize findings to other figurative language phenomena
- The correlation-based approach cannot establish causation between embodiment and LM performance
- The reliance on a single embodiment rating dataset limits claims about cross-cultural or cross-linguistic validity

## Confidence

- Medium: The positive correlation between model size and embodiment performance
- Medium: The claim that concreteness, AoA, frequency, and length do not explain the effect
- Low: Causal claims about why embodiment helps LMs interpret metaphors
