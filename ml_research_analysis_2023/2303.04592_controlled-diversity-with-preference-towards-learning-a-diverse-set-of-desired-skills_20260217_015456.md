# Controlled Diversity with Preference : Towards Learning a Diverse Set of Desired Skills

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04592
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04592
- Reference count: 28
- Key outcome: The paper proposes a method to guide autonomous skill discovery in reinforcement learning toward human-preferred regions of the state space, enabling learning of diverse and desirable skills.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the problem of autonomous skill discovery in reinforcement learning, where unconstrained exploration often leads to learning unsafe or undesirable behaviors. The core idea is to guide skill discovery toward human-preferred regions of the state space using preference-based reinforcement learning. The method, Controlled Diversity with Preference (CDP), learns a reward model from human trajectory preferences and uses it to bias exploration toward preferred states, then discovers diverse skills within those regions. Experiments in 2D navigation and MuJoCo environments show that CDP successfully learns diverse, yet desirable skills compared to baselines. The method also scales to high-dimensional state spaces using a preferred latent representation learned from the preference reward model. Performance is measured by skill diversity and alignment with human preferences, with qualitative and quantitative results demonstrating improved exploration and skill quality.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes Controlled Diversity with Preference (CDP), a method that combines preference-based reinforcement learning with skill discovery to learn diverse and desirable skills. The method learns a reward model from human preferences on trajectory pairs, uses this to define a preferred region of the state space, and then discovers diverse skills within that region using a VQ-VAE discriminator. The exploration is guided by a modified State Marginal Matching (SMM) objective that incorporates the preference reward and discriminator-based diversity. For high-dimensional environments, the method uses the hidden layer of the preference reward model as a preferred latent representation. The method is evaluated in 2D navigation and MuJoCo environments, showing improved skill diversity and alignment with human preferences compared to baselines.

## Key Results
- CDP successfully learns diverse skills in preferred regions of 2D navigation and MuJoCo environments.
- The method scales to high-dimensional state spaces using preferred latent representations from the reward model.
- CDP outperforms baselines in terms of skill diversity and alignment with human preferences.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Preferred regions guide exploration toward states that yield desirable skills.
- Mechanism: Human preferences are used to train a reward model that estimates which states are desirable. The agent then explores within these high-reward regions, ensuring learned skills are both diverse and aligned with human preferences.
- Core assumption: Human preferences accurately reflect which states produce meaningful, safe skills.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The key principle is to restrict the discovery of skills to those regions that are deemed to be desirable as per a preference model trained using human preference labels on trajectory pairs."
  - [section 4.1.1] "We define controlled diversity as limiting diversity to a certain region of the state space... To achieve this, we follow the EDL framework... by modifying priors through a distribution over a target region p*(s) of the state space."
- Break condition: If the preference model fails to capture true human intent, exploration may still converge on undesirable states.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Skill diversity is maintained within the preferred region via a discriminator.
- Mechanism: A VQ-VAE discriminator assigns distinct regions within the preferred area to different latent skills, ensuring each skill explores a unique sub-region and thus remains diverse.
- Core assumption: The discriminator can effectively partition the preferred region into meaningfully distinct sub-regions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2.3] "The discriminator qφ, encourages each skill to explore distinct regions related to the potential preferred region... we define the diversity reward as: r_diversity_z = log qφ(ŝ|z), with ŝ ∈ Ŝ."
- Break condition: If the discriminator cannot differentiate between sub-regions, all skills may collapse to similar behaviors.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Preferred latent representations scale to high-dimensional environments.
- Mechanism: The hidden layer of the learned preference reward model is used as a prior for the discriminator, capturing human-relevant features and enabling skill diversity in complex state spaces like MuJoCo.
- Core assumption: The hidden layer of the preference model learns a representation aligned with human preferences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3] "we simply use the representation in the last hidden layer of the reward model ˆrψ learnt from human preferences as the prior... The intuition is that the last hidden layer... should learn a latent state representation that captures features that matter for human preferences."
  - [section 5.3] "we show that our method can be used to guide exploration towards possible preferred regions... Empirically, our agents demonstrated the ability to favor the exploration of the preferred regions and to learn diverse skills in these regions."
- Break condition: If the latent representation does not capture relevant features, skills may appear non-diverse to humans despite high discriminator scores.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Human preference collector -> Reward model trainer -> Preferred region estimator -> Skill discovery discriminator -> Latent-conditioned policy trainer -> Exploration agent
- Critical path:
  1. Collect human preference labels on trajectory pairs.
  2. Train reward model from preferences.
  3. Estimate preferred region using thresholded reward values.
  4. Train VQ-VAE discriminator on states from preferred region.
  5. Explore using SMM-based reward combining preference reward, state marginal, and discriminator diversity terms.
  6. Learn skills from discriminator output.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using full state space vs. preferred latent representation: Full state space is simpler but may not yield human-discernible diversity; latent representation is more complex but aligns better with human perception.
  - Threshold β for preferred region: Low β increases diversity but may include less desirable states; high β increases safety but may reduce diversity.
- Failure signatures:
  - All skills converge to similar behaviors -> Discriminator not effectively partitioning preferred region.
  - Agent explores outside preferred region -> Reward model not accurately capturing preferences or exploration not properly biased.
  - Skills appear non-diverse to humans -> Latent representation not capturing human-relevant features.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. 2D navigation with oracle reward: Validate that CDP learns skills in the top-right (preferred) corner vs. uniform distribution for baseline.
  2. MuJoCo half-cheetah with backward preference: Confirm that CDP learns diverse backward-moving skills using state space vs. preferred latent representation.
  3. Sensitivity to β: Sweep β values and measure skill diversity and alignment with preference reward.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across a wider variety of environments with varying complexity, along with a theoretical analysis of how 𝛽 interacts with environmental features and task requirements.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the preferred latent representation learned from human preferences be effectively combined with other state representation learning techniques to further improve skill diversity and desirability?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the preferred latent representation captures features relevant to human preferences but does not explore its integration with other representation learning methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses the preferred latent representation as a standalone approach without comparing or combining it with other state representation learning techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of CDP with and without the integration of other representation learning methods, and an analysis of how different representation techniques complement or conflict with the preferred latent representation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the quality and quantity of human preference labels affect the performance of CDP in discovering diverse and desirable skills?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the use of human preference labels to learn a reward model but does not provide a detailed analysis of how the quality and quantity of these labels impact the final skill discovery.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore scenarios with varying qualities or quantities of human feedback, nor does it provide guidelines for optimal human feedback collection.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the quality and quantity of human preference labels, along with an analysis of the trade-offs between human effort and skill discovery performance.

## Limitations
- The method's performance depends heavily on the quality of human preference labels; noisy or inconsistent preferences could lead to poor reward models and undesirable skill discovery.
- The choice of threshold β for preferred region estimation is critical but not extensively explored; different thresholds could significantly impact the balance between diversity and desirability of learned skills.
- The scalability of using preferred latent representations to high-dimensional state spaces is demonstrated but not deeply analyzed; it's unclear how well the hidden layer of the preference reward model captures human-relevant features in more complex environments.

## Confidence
- High confidence in the core mechanism of using preference-based RL to guide skill discovery toward desirable regions, supported by experimental results in 2D and MuJoCo environments.
- Medium confidence in the scalability claim for high-dimensional spaces using preferred latent representations, as the analysis is primarily qualitative and limited to specific environments.
- Medium confidence in the method's ability to maintain skill diversity within preferred regions, as the discriminator's effectiveness depends on the quality of the preferred region estimation and the granularity of the VQ-VAE.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation study on β threshold:** Sweep different values of β to measure its impact on skill diversity, alignment with preferences, and overall performance. This will clarify the sensitivity of the method to this critical hyperparameter.
2. **Real-world preference elicitation:** Conduct a user study where real humans provide preference labels on trajectory pairs, then compare the learned skills and reward models to those obtained with simulated oracle rewards. This will test the method's robustness to noisy or inconsistent human preferences.
3. **Diversity analysis in latent space:** For high-dimensional experiments, analyze the distribution of skills in the preferred latent representation to quantify how well the method maintains diversity. This could involve clustering analysis or measuring the coverage of the latent space by learned skills.
