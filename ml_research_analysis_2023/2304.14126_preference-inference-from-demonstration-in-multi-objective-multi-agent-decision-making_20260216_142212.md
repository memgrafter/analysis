# Preference Inference from Demonstration in Multi-objective Multi-agent Decision Making

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14126
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14126
- Authors: Multiple authors
- Reference count: 6
- Key outcome: Preference inference algorithm achieves 89.55% to 99.56% KL-divergence improvement over baselines in multi-objective multi-agent settings

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a preference inference algorithm for multi-objective multi-agent decision making that infers linear preference weights from demonstrations. The Dynamic Weight Preference Inference (DWPI) algorithm uses a trained dynamic weight reinforcement learning agent to generate behavior trajectories, which are then used to train an inference model under supervised learning. The algorithm is evaluated in three environments (Convex Deep Sea Treasure, Traffic, and Item Gathering) and demonstrates significant improvements in both time efficiency and inference accuracy compared to two baseline methods.

## Method Summary
The DWPI algorithm operates by first training a dynamic weight reinforcement learning (DWRL) agent to generate behavior trajectories conditioned on different preference vectors. These trajectories, along with their corresponding preferences, form a training dataset for a supervised learning model that learns to map trajectories to preferences. The inference model is made robust to sub-optimal demonstrations by adding random sampled sub-optimal noise to the training data. The algorithm is specifically designed to handle linear preference weights and demonstrates effectiveness in both single-agent and multi-agent environments.

## Key Results
- KL-divergence improvements ranging from 89.55% to 99.56% over baseline methods
- Mean squared error improvements ranging from 83.81% to 99.83% compared to baselines
- Significant improvements in time efficiency while maintaining high inference accuracy
- Robust performance on both optimal and near-optimal (sub-optimal) demonstrations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Preference inference works by training an inference model to predict linear preference weights from observed reward trajectories.
- Mechanism: The DWRL agent generates optimal reward trajectories conditioned on different preference vectors. These trajectories, along with corresponding preferences, form a training dataset for a supervised learning model that learns to map trajectories to preferences.
- Core assumption: Reward trajectories are uniquely determined by preference weights under optimal or near-optimal policies.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states DWPI uses DWRL agent to generate behavior trajectories for training inference model; section describes using DWRL to generate behavior trajectories and proposing DWPI for preference inference.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adding sub-optimal noise to training data makes the inference model robust to non-optimal demonstrations.
- Mechanism: Random sampled sub-optimal noise is added to reward trajectories during training, ensuring the inference model can handle imperfect demonstrations.
- Core assumption: Real-world demonstrations may be sub-optimal, so the model needs to handle noise.
- Evidence anchors: Section states adding sub-optimal noise ensures inference model is robust on sub-optimal reward trajectories; abstract mentions considering dominated points close to POS for testing PI algorithm.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Opponent preference modeling provides strategic advantage in multi-agent systems.
- Mechanism: An agent can use DWPI to infer opponents' preferences, allowing it to anticipate their behavior and optimize its own strategy accordingly.
- Core assumption: Knowing an opponent's preferences provides actionable information for strategic decision-making.
- Evidence anchors: Section mentions utilizing DWPI in multi-agent environment to enable agent to gain knowledge of other agents' preferences; abstract plans to evaluate algorithm's effectiveness in multi-agent system where agent infers opponent preferences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How robust is the DWPI algorithm to sub-optimal demonstrations with varying degrees of noise?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions adding sub-optimal noise to the data to ensure the inference model is robust on sub-optimal reward trajectories, but does not explore the limits of this robustness.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the algorithm's performance under different levels of sub-optimal noise.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments showing the algorithm's performance on demonstrations with varying degrees of sub-optimal noise, and a comparison of the results to the current performance metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the DWPI algorithm be extended to infer non-linear preference weights?
- Basis in paper: The authors mention this as a potential direction for future work in the conclusion section.
- Why unresolved: The current implementation of the DWPI algorithm is limited to inferring linear preference weights, and the paper does not explore the possibility of extending it to non-linear weights.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and evaluation of an extended version of the DWPI algorithm that can infer non-linear preference weights, and a comparison of its performance to the current linear version.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the DWPI algorithm perform in more complex multi-agent environments, such as those with more than two agents or with higher-dimensional state spaces?
- Basis in paper: The paper only evaluates the algorithm in two multi-agent environments with two agents each, and does not explore its performance in more complex scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The current evaluation of the algorithm is limited to simple multi-agent environments, and its performance in more complex scenarios is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating the algorithm's performance in more complex multi-agent environments, and a comparison of the results to the current performance metrics.

## Limitations
- Lack of implementation details for critical components (DWRL agent, inference model architecture)
- Limited evaluation to synthetic environments without real-world testing
- Insufficient analysis of algorithm's robustness across different noise levels
- Theoretical claims about opponent preference modeling lack experimental validation

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core algorithmic approach of using supervised learning to map reward trajectories to preference weights is well-established and theoretically sound. The use of KL-divergence and mean squared error as evaluation metrics is appropriate for this problem setting.

**Medium Confidence**: The reported improvements over baseline methods (89.55% to 99.56% KL-divergence improvement, 83.81% to 99.83% MSE improvement) are impressive but difficult to verify without access to implementation details and code.

**Low Confidence**: The robustness claims regarding sub-optimal noise handling are based on limited experimental evidence. The effectiveness of opponent preference modeling in multi-agent systems remains largely theoretical without concrete experimental validation.
