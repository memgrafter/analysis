# A Subspace Projection Approach to Autoencoder-based Anomaly Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.07643
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07643
- Reference count: 18
- Key outcome: HFR-AE improves AUROC for anomaly detection by up to 13.4% compared to standard AE methods on CIFAR-10

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the High-Fidelity Reconstruction Autoencoder (HFR-AE) framework for anomaly detection. The core innovation projects input samples onto a high-fidelity reconstruction (HFR) subspace before passing them through a trained autoencoder, creating a larger reconstruction error gap between normal and anomalous samples. The method treats the autoencoder as a communication channel and uses eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors to identify optimal subspaces. Experiments demonstrate consistent performance improvements across multiple autoencoder architectures (Vanilla AE, VAE, VQ-VAE) on CIFAR-10 without requiring re-training.

## Method Summary
The HFR-AE framework decomposes a trained autoencoder into high-fidelity and low-fidelity reconstruction subspaces based on eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors on training data. New inputs are projected onto the HFR subspace before reconstruction, increasing the reconstruction error gap between normal and anomalous samples. The method is architecture-agnostic and optimizes the HFR threshold without re-training by analyzing the trained model's behavior. The approach is evaluated on CIFAR-10 using VQ-VAE, VAE, and Vanilla AE architectures with ADAM optimizer (learning rate 2e-4) for 100 epochs.

## Key Results
- HFR-AE improves AUROC for anomaly detection by up to 13.4% compared to standard AE-based methods on CIFAR-10
- Performance improvements are consistent across different autoencoder architectures (Vanilla AE, VAE, VQ-VAE)
- The HFR subspace threshold can be optimized without re-training the autoencoder
- The framework achieves better separation between normal and anomalous samples in reconstruction error space

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Projecting input samples onto the high-fidelity reconstruction (HFR) subspace before reconstruction increases the reconstruction error gap between normal and anomalous samples.
- Mechanism: The trained autoencoder is decomposed into HFR and low-fidelity reconstruction (LFR) subspaces based on eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors on training data. By projecting inputs onto the HFR subspace, normal samples are reconstructed with minimal error while anomalous samples experience higher error due to their deviation from the training data distribution in this subspace.
- Core assumption: The eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors on training data can identify subspaces where the autoencoder achieves high-fidelity reconstruction.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The proposed HFR-AE framework treats the AE as a communication channel and decomposes it into HFR and low-fidelity reconstruction (LFR) subspaces based on the eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors on training data."

- Break condition: If the assumption that training data distribution is well-represented by the HFR subspace breaks down, or if anomalous samples happen to align well with the HFR subspace.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The HFR-AE framework is architecture-agnostic and does not require re-training to optimize its key hyperparameter.
- Mechanism: The HFR-AE framework treats the trained autoencoder as a communication channel and can be applied to any autoencoder architecture (Vanilla AE, VAE, VQ-VAE). The HFR subspace threshold can be optimized by simply feeding multiple samples without re-training the autoencoder.
- Core assumption: The trained autoencoder can be treated as a fixed communication channel whose properties can be analyzed without further training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The resultant HFR-AE framework is NN architecture-agnostic and free from re-training."
  - [section III-A] "The key design parameter of HFR-AE is the threshold separating HFR and LFR channels, which can be optimized by simply feeding multiple samples without re-training the AE."
- Break condition: If the trained autoencoder's performance significantly degrades over time or with different data distributions, requiring periodic re-training.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The HFR subspace projection increases the mutual information between input and reconstruction for normal samples while decreasing it for anomalous samples.
- Mechanism: By projecting inputs onto the HFR subspace, the mutual information between input and reconstruction is maximized for normal samples (which align with the training data distribution) and minimized for anomalous samples (which deviate from this distribution).
- Core assumption: The mutual information framework accurately represents the relationship between input and reconstruction in the HFR subspace.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Trained autoencoder -> Eigendecomposition module -> HFR subspace projection module -> Reconstruction module -> Anomaly detection module

- Critical path:
  1. Train autoencoder on normal data
  2. Compute eigendecomposition of reconstruction error covariance matrix
  3. Determine HFR subspace threshold (ϵ)
  4. Project new inputs onto HFR subspace
  5. Reconstruct projected inputs using trained autoencoder
  6. Compare reconstruction errors to detect anomalies

- Design tradeoffs:
  - HFR subspace threshold (ϵ): Balancing between maximizing the reconstruction error gap and maintaining sufficient HFR subspace dimension
  - Autoencoder architecture: Choosing between Vanilla AE, VAE, or VQ-VAE based on data characteristics and performance requirements
  - Computational complexity: Eigendecomposition and subspace projection add computational overhead compared to standard autoencoder-based anomaly detection

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor anomaly detection performance: May indicate incorrect HFR subspace threshold or insufficient autoencoder training
  - High computational cost: May indicate inefficient implementation of eigendecomposition or subspace projection
  - Unstable results: May indicate sensitivity to hyperparameter choices or data distribution changes

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a Vanilla AE on a subset of CIFAR-10, compute eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors, and visualize the HFR subspace eigenvalues to understand the data distribution.
  2. Implement HFR-AE with a fixed HFR subspace threshold and evaluate its anomaly detection performance on CIFAR-10 compared to standard AE-based methods.
  3. Optimize the HFR subspace threshold using a validation set and evaluate the impact on anomaly detection performance across different autoencoder architectures (Vanilla AE, VAE, VQ-VAE).

## Limitations

- The framework's performance may degrade if anomalous samples align well with the HFR subspace, reducing the reconstruction error gap
- The method requires computing eigendecomposition of reconstruction errors, adding computational overhead compared to standard autoencoder-based anomaly detection
- The optimal HFR subspace threshold may vary depending on the specific autoencoder architecture and data characteristics, requiring careful tuning
