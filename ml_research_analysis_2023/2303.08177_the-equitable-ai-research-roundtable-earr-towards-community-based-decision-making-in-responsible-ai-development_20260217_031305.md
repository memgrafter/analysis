# The Equitable AI Research Roundtable (EARR): Towards Community-Based Decision Making in Responsible AI Development

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08177
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08177
- Reference count: 32
- Key outcome: EARR expands expertise in AI development, fosters epistemic curiosity, and creates mutual learning space by involving community-based domain experts.

## Executive Summary
The Equitable AI Research Roundtable (EARR) is a coalition of external experts in law, education, community engagement, social justice, and technology that provides critical perspectives and feedback on AI technology's ethical and social harms. Through semi-structured workshops and discussions with a large tech firm, EARR has expanded the scope of expertise in AI development, fostered opportunities for epistemic curiosity and responsibility, and created a space for mutual learning. This approach addresses the gap between the epistemologies of privilege and power in large technology firms and the lived experiences of people most affected by machine learning models.

## Method Summary
EARR operates as an ongoing coalition of external experts who engage with internal product teams through monthly semi-structured workshops and discussions. The process involves product teams presenting technologies and concerns, followed by EARR feedback and discussion. Organizers document insights and share with both product teams and EARR members. The model emphasizes continuous engagement rather than one-off studies, allowing relationships to develop and trust to build over time.

## Key Results
- EARR expands the scope of expertise in AI development beyond technical metrics to include value-based, socially grounded considerations
- The coalition creates a space for mutual learning between tech firms and community experts, challenging traditional hierarchies of knowledge
- EARR enables deeper discussion of issues normally not considered relevant to technical AI development, revealing the sociotechnical nature of AI systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: EARR shifts the locus of epistemic authority from technical experts to community-based domain experts, improving fairness outcomes.
- **Mechanism**: By convening a coalition of external experts in law, education, social justice, and related fields to engage directly with ML product teams, EARR brings in lived experience and social structural knowledge that is otherwise absent from AI development. This expanded epistemic scope leads teams to reframe fairness problems from technical metrics to value-based, socially grounded considerations.
- **Core assumption**: The inclusion of diverse disciplinary perspectives changes how fairness and harm are conceptualized and prioritized in technical design decisions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "expands the scope of expertise in AI development, fosters opportunities for epistemic curiosity and responsibility, and creates a space for mutual learning."
  - [section 5.1] "EARR challenges the traditional notions of relevant knowledge to be an AI practitioner... By involving experts with lived experience and specialized academic knowledge... EARR enables a deeper discussion of issues that are normally not thought to be germane to technical discussions..."
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct citations or metrics on impact outcomes from the related literature; the anchor relies on the claims made in the paper itself.
- **Break condition**: If product teams do not incorporate or respect the feedback from EARR experts, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: EARR facilitates epistemic justice by valuing subjugated knowledge alongside technical knowledge in AI governance.
- **Mechanism**: The coalition format intentionally centers voices from structurally marginalized groups, creating a forum where their knowledge—often dismissed in traditional tech development—is treated as equally valid. This counters the "hierarchy of knowledge" described in the paper, where coding and quantitative skills are privileged over social science expertise.
- **Core assumption**: Epistemological diversity improves decision-making in complex sociotechnical systems by surfacing hidden assumptions and blind spots.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 2.1] "This narrow epistemic lens from which AI development is conducted contributes to a whole host of downstream social impacts... The hierarchy of knowledge in AI is a form of gatekeeping that prevents other kinds of knowledge... from entering into conversations..."
  - [section 5.2] "EARR offers an alternative way to discuss judgements of values based on expertise and experience... questions involving or affecting marginalized groups should begin with the lives and experiences of those marginalized groups..."
  - [corpus] Weak: No empirical data in related literature on epistemic justice outcomes from similar coalitions; the paper presents this as an intended design principle.
- **Break condition**: If the tech firm continues to prioritize technical solutions over social insights, the epistemic justice goal is undermined.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Long-term, iterative engagement between tech teams and EARR experts builds trust and enables deeper mutual learning.
- **Mechanism**: EARR is structured as an ongoing coalition rather than a one-off study. This continuity allows relationships to develop, trust to build, and learning to be iterative. Mutual learning is defined as "a process of information exchange between organizations within a coalition" that leads to joint actions and changed thinking.
- **Core assumption**: Trust and sustained relationships are prerequisites for meaningful epistemic exchange and the co-creation of knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.2] "EARR has been operating since 2019 and thus forms a continuous set of relationships among members... This allows deeper relationships to form among all those participating from the tech firm, NGOs, non-profits, and universities."
  - [section 5.3] "Mutual learning can be defined as a process of information exchange... This give-and-take learning between big tech and society leads to enhanced thinking and action around building fairer, equitable AI technology and importantly, trust building..."
  - [corpus] Weak: The related papers focus on community-based design but do not report on trust-building or long-term mutual learning outcomes in AI contexts.
- **Break condition**: If engagement becomes transactional or episodic, the mutual learning and trust-building benefits dissipate.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:
  - EARR Organizer (internal team) -> Coordinates sessions, manages relationships, synthesizes feedback
  - EARR Members (external experts) -> Provide domain expertise, critique product designs, suggest mitigations
  - Product Teams (internal) -> Present technologies, receive feedback, iterate on designs
  - Documentation & Artifacts -> Meeting notes, discussion summaries, adversarial datasets, follow-up reports
  - Governance Interface -> Referral pathway from internal AI governance to EARR

- **Critical path**:
  1. Product team identifies fairness or harm concern
  2. Team submits request to EARR organizers
  3. Organizers coordinate session with relevant EARR members
  4. Session: product presentation → EARR feedback → discussion
  5. Organizers document insights and share with product team and EARR members
  6. Product team iterates on design or research approach

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Speed vs. depth: EARR sessions take time, which may slow product timelines but yield deeper insights
  - Control vs. openness: EARR does not have veto power, preserving product autonomy but potentially limiting impact
  - Expertise diversity vs. coordination cost: Broader participation increases perspective but requires more coordination

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Product teams ignore EARR feedback or treat sessions as box-ticking
  - EARR members disengage due to perceived tokenism or lack of follow-through
  - Sessions become overly abstract with no actionable outcomes
  - Misalignment between EARR's equity lens and product team's technical framing

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run a mock EARR session with a low-stakes product prototype to test session format and feedback loops
  2. Pilot a follow-up mechanism where product teams report back on how EARR input influenced their design
  3. Conduct a lightweight impact assessment after three sessions to evaluate whether teams are changing their framing or approach

- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the benefits of EARR but does not provide concrete metrics or methods for evaluating its effectiveness.
- Why unresolved: The paper emphasizes the qualitative benefits and mutual learning aspects of EARR but lacks quantitative data or standardized evaluation criteria.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation of standardized metrics and longitudinal studies comparing products developed with and without EARR input.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the specific challenges and limitations faced by external experts when engaging with large tech companies in AI development processes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions managing expectations and internal stakeholder expectations but does not delve into specific challenges.
- Why unresolved: While the paper outlines the goals and benefits of EARR, it does not provide detailed accounts of the difficulties encountered by external experts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed case studies or interviews with EARR members discussing their experiences and challenges.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the EARR model be adapted to ensure equitable compensation and engagement for external experts across different cultural and economic contexts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions compensation as an ongoing process but does not explore how it can be adapted globally.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the need for fair compensation but does not address how to implement this in diverse global contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research on compensation models in different regions and feedback from EARR members in various countries.

## Limitations
- The paper's claims about EARR's impact are based primarily on theoretical framing rather than empirical outcomes or rigorous evaluation
- No direct evidence is provided that EARR feedback has changed actual product design decisions or improved fairness metrics
- The paper does not report on participant experiences, trust-building outcomes, or long-term relationship dynamics

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (epistemic authority shift): Medium — well-theorized but lacks empirical validation
- Mechanism 2 (epistemic justice): Medium — conceptually grounded but not demonstrated in practice
- Mechanism 3 (trust and mutual learning): Low — the claim is plausible but unsupported by participant data or longitudinal evidence
