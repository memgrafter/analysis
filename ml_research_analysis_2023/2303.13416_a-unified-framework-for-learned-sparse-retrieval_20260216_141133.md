# A Unified Framework for Learned Sparse Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.13416
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13416
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A framework unifying LSR methods shows document weighting is most crucial for effectiveness, while query expansion is unnecessary due to cancellation effects

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a unified framework for analyzing learned sparse retrieval (LSR) methods, identifying three key components that differentiate these approaches: encoder architecture, regularization, and supervision. By re-implementing and consistently training multiple LSR methods, the authors systematically evaluate how these components affect retrieval effectiveness and efficiency. The analysis reveals that document weighting is the most important factor for effectiveness, query weighting provides moderate benefits with latency improvements, and document and query expansion exhibit a cancellation effect. These insights lead to a modified state-of-the-art model that reduces latency by over 74% while maintaining performance on MSMarco and TripClick benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The unified framework consists of three components: sparse encoders (query and document), sparse regularizers (controlling sparsity), and supervision (negative examples and labels). The authors reproduce prominent LSR methods using a common codebase and train them in a consistent environment. They systematically vary encoder architectures (BINARY, MLP, expMLP, MLM, clsMLM), regularization strategies (FLOPs, Lp norms, Top-K), and supervision techniques (random, BM25, hard negatives, distillation) to analyze their impact. The methods are evaluated on MSMarco and TripClick datasets using standard retrieval metrics (MRR@10, NDCG@10, Recall@1000) alongside efficiency measurements (encoding and retrieval latency).

## Key Results
- Document weighting has the greatest impact on effectiveness among all architectural components
- Query expansion exhibits a cancellation effect with document expansion, making it unnecessary for good performance
- Modified state-of-the-art model reduces retrieval latency by 74%+ while maintaining effectiveness
- Query weighting moderately improves effectiveness while reducing latency by pruning non-useful terms

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Document weighting is the most crucial component for effectiveness in learned sparse retrieval.
- Mechanism: The document encoder produces term weights that capture the importance of each vocabulary term within a document. When these weights are learned effectively, they allow the retrieval system to match queries with relevant documents based on term significance rather than just presence.
- Core assumption: Learned term weights in documents provide more discriminative power than binary presence indicators.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "including document term weighting is most important for a method's effectiveness"
  - [section] "The common training environment we use to answer RQ2 allows us to quantify the effect of various architectural decisions, such as expansion, weighting, and regularization. We find that document weighting had the greatest impact on a system's effectiveness"
- Break condition: If the learned document weights fail to capture term importance or if the vocabulary size becomes too large relative to document length, diminishing returns may occur.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Query weighting has a moderate positive impact on effectiveness while improving latency by eliminating non-useful terms.
- Mechanism: The query encoder learns to assign weights to query terms, effectively pruning irrelevant terms by assigning them low or zero weights. This creates more focused queries that improve retrieval quality while reducing computational overhead during matching.
- Core assumption: Not all query terms contribute equally to retrieval effectiveness, and pruning irrelevant terms improves both quality and efficiency.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "including query weighting has a small positive impact, and (3) document expansion and query expansion have a cancellation effect"
  - [section] "Interestingly, it causes almost no harm to the index size or even reduces the latency. Note that the latency of the MLP query encoder here is measured on GPU; therefore, the encoding overhead is tiny. The improved overall latency is mostly due to the MLP reducing the weights of some non-useful query terms to zero, making queries shorter."
- Break condition: If query weighting becomes too aggressive and removes terms that actually contribute to retrieval effectiveness, or if the overhead of query encoding outweighs the benefits.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: There is a cancellation effect between document expansion and query expansion, making query expansion unnecessary for good performance.
- Mechanism: When both document and query expansion are used together, they partially negate each other's benefits. Document expansion adds relevant terms to documents, while query expansion adds relevant terms to queries. When both are present, the system doesn't gain as much benefit as when only one is used.
- Core assumption: The benefits of adding relevant terms through expansion are partially redundant when applied to both queries and documents simultaneously.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "document expansion and query expansion have a cancellation effect. As a result, we show how removing query expansion from a state-of-the-art model can reduce latency significantly while maintaining effectiveness"
  - [section] "We observe the cancellation effect between query expansion and document expansion. Indeed, with the absence of expansion on one side (3b: QM LP has no query expansion, 4b: DM LP has no document expansion), the expansion on the other side largely improves the ranking metrics with at least 7.4(2.6) points and at most 17.9(7.9) points overall on MSMarco (TripClick). The cost of latency, in this case, is rather low. The numbers in rows 3a and 4a indicate that query and document expansion have a cancellation effect. That is, query expansion reduces the benefit of performing document expansion and vice versa."
- Break condition: If the corpus has very different query and document vocabularies, or if expansion terms are not well-aligned with relevance.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Inverted indexing and sparse vector operations
  - Why needed here: Learned sparse retrieval methods generate sparse vectors that are stored in inverted indexes, which are then used for efficient retrieval. Understanding how these indexes work and how sparse vector operations function is crucial for implementing and optimizing LSR systems.
  - Quick check question: How does the dot product between sparse vectors translate to inverted index operations during retrieval?

- Concept: Transformer-based language models and MLM heads
  - Why needed here: Most LSR methods use transformer architectures with Masked Language Model heads to generate term weights. Understanding how transformers encode context and how MLM heads can be repurposed for term weighting is essential for implementing the encoder architectures described in the framework.
  - Quick check question: How does the MLM head in BERT produce logits that can be transformed into term weights for retrieval?

- Concept: Contrastive learning and negative sampling strategies
  - Why needed here: LSR methods are typically trained using contrastive learning objectives that require carefully selected negative examples. Understanding different negative sampling strategies (random, BM25, hard negatives) and their impact on training is crucial for reproducing and improving these methods.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between in-batch negatives and mined hard negatives, and how do they affect the training of retrieval models?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: The LSR framework consists of three main components: (1) sparse encoders (query and document), which transform text into sparse weight vectors; (2) sparse regularizers, which control the sparsity of these vectors; and (3) supervision, which includes negative examples and labels for training. The encoders can use different architectures (BINARY, MLP, expMLP, MLM, clsMLM) and can be applied separately or shared between queries and documents.
- Critical path: The critical path for implementing an LSR system involves: (1) defining the vocabulary and tokenization strategy; (2) implementing the chosen encoder architecture(s); (3) applying appropriate regularization during training; (4) setting up the training pipeline with suitable supervision (negatives and labels); (5) indexing the learned sparse representations; and (6) implementing efficient retrieval using the inverted index.
- Design tradeoffs: Key tradeoffs include choosing between shared vs. separate encoders for queries and documents (affecting latency and effectiveness), selecting between different regularization methods (FLOPs, Lp norms, Top-K) for balancing sparsity and performance, and deciding on expansion strategies (none, document-only, query-only, both) based on the cancellation effect observed. The choice of encoder architecture also involves tradeoffs between expressiveness (MLM > expMLP > MLP > BINARY) and computational efficiency.
- Failure signatures: Common failure modes include: (1) poor effectiveness due to inadequate document weighting or inappropriate regularization leading to either too sparse or too dense representations; (2) high latency from using query expansion when document expansion is already present (cancellation effect); (3) memory issues from large index sizes when using expansion techniques; (4) training instability from improper negative sampling or label assignment; (5) reduced effectiveness when using hard negatives without proper distillation techniques.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a basic LSR system with BINARY encoders for both queries and documents (no weighting or expansion) to establish a baseline and verify the indexing and retrieval pipeline works correctly.
  2. Replace the BINARY document encoder with an MLP document encoder while keeping BINARY for queries to measure the impact of document weighting on effectiveness.
  3. Add MLP query weighting to the system from experiment 2 to observe the moderate effectiveness gains and latency improvements from query term pruning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the cancellation effect between document expansion and query expansion hold consistently across different domains and datasets beyond MSMarco and TripClick?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes a cancellation effect between document expansion and query expansion on MSMarco and TripClick benchmarks.
- Why unresolved: The cancellation effect was only tested on two specific datasets. Different domains might have different characteristics that could affect this relationship.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the cancellation effect on a diverse set of datasets from different domains (e.g., medical, legal, news) and observing whether the effect persists or varies.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between document weighting and query weighting for different types of queries (e.g., short vs. long queries)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that document weighting is most important for effectiveness, while query weighting has a small positive impact.
- Why unresolved: The study treats all queries uniformly. Different query types might benefit differently from the weighting strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with query-specific weighting strategies and measuring performance across different query lengths and types.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the learned sparse representations compare to dense representations in zero-shot or few-shot learning scenarios across different domains?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses LSR methods and their properties but doesn't compare them to dense methods in transfer learning scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on in-domain performance and doesn't explore cross-domain generalization capabilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating both LSR and dense retrieval methods on out-of-domain datasets and measuring their zero-shot or few-shot learning performance.

## Limitations
- The cancellation effect between document and query expansion was only tested on MSMarco and TripClick datasets, limiting generalizability to other domains.
- The unified framework focuses on architectural components but doesn't fully address how vocabulary size and term distribution affect effectiveness.
- The study doesn't explore the impact of different tokenization strategies on the performance of LSR methods.

## Confidence

- High confidence: Document weighting being the most crucial component for effectiveness, based on consistent results across multiple methods and datasets.
- Medium confidence: The cancellation effect between document and query expansion, as it shows strong patterns but may be dataset-dependent.
- Medium confidence: Query weighting's moderate effectiveness gains, though the latency improvements appear more consistently observed.

## Next Checks

1. Test the cancellation effect on additional retrieval datasets with different characteristics (longer documents, different term distributions) to assess generalizability.
2. Experiment with varying vocabulary sizes and term frequency distributions to understand how these factors interact with the expansion cancellation effect.
3. Implement ablation studies on the hard negative sampling strategies to verify their impact on the observed effectiveness patterns.
