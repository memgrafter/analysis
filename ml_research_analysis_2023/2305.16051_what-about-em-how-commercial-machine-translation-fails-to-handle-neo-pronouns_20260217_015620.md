# What about em? How Commercial Machine Translation Fails to Handle (Neo-)Pronouns

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16051
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16051
- Reference count: 19
- Key outcome: Commercial MT systems show high rates of grammatical errors and misgendering when translating gender-neutral and neopronouns across six languages.

## Executive Summary
This study examines how commercial machine translation systems handle third-person pronouns, including gender-neutral and neopronouns. Using English source sentences with eight different pronoun categories, the authors analyze translations into five languages and back from Danish to English. The research reveals that gender-neutral pronouns are significantly more likely to result in translation errors and misgendering compared to traditional gendered pronouns. A survey of 49 participants reveals diverse preferences for pronoun handling in MT, suggesting that no one-size-fits-all solution exists. The authors recommend treating pronouns as an open word class, offering personalization options, and minimizing potential misgendering in MT systems.

## Method Summary
The researchers created English templates with eight pronoun categories (gendered, gender-neutral, traditional neopronouns, nounself, emojiself, numberself) in five grammatical cases. They translated these sentences using Google Translate, Bing, and DeepL to Danish, German, Farsi, French, and Italian, with Danish-to-English translations as a control. Native speakers annotated the outputs for grammatical correctness, semantic consistency, and pronoun translation strategies. Additionally, 49 survey participants with diverse gender identities provided feedback on their preferences for how MT systems should handle pronouns.

## Key Results
- Gender-neutral pronouns resulted in 50% grammatical correctness in Italian translations versus 97% for gendered pronouns
- 43% of pronoun copies occurred in Google Translate outputs when handling neopronouns
- Survey participants showed no consensus on preferred pronoun translation strategies, with the most popular option being translation to a target language pronoun when identity matches

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Commercial MT systems fail to translate gender-neutral pronouns accurately, leading to increased grammatical errors and semantic inconsistencies.
- Mechanism: The models are trained on biased datasets that predominantly feature binary gender pronouns, so they lack exposure to gender-neutral and neopronouns, causing them to either misgender or mistranslate these pronouns.
- Core assumption: The training data for commercial MT systems does not include sufficient examples of gender-neutral pronouns, resulting in poor handling of such cases.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Results show that gender-neutral pronouns often lead to translation errors and are more likely to be misgendered than gendered pronouns."
  - [section 3.2] "Even more severely, only half (!) of the translations to IT are grammatically correct when starting with the gender-neutral pronoun 'they'."
- Break condition: If the MT models are retrained on datasets that include a diverse range of pronouns, including gender-neutral and neopronouns, the translation quality should improve.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Different MT engines employ varied strategies for handling gender-neutral pronouns, with some opting to copy the pronoun from the source language rather than translate it.
- Mechanism: The MT engines lack a standardized approach for dealing with gender-neutral pronouns, leading to inconsistent strategies such as copying, translating to a gendered pronoun, or omitting the pronoun altogether.
- Core assumption: The lack of a standardized approach for handling gender-neutral pronouns results in inconsistent translation strategies across different MT engines.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2] "In contrast, GTranslate is the engine with the largest pronoun copies (43%)."
- Break condition: If a standardized approach for handling gender-neutral pronouns is implemented across MT engines, the inconsistency in translation strategies should decrease.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: User preferences for pronoun translation are diverse, and there is no one-size-fits-all solution for handling pronouns in MT.
- Mechanism: The survey results indicate that users have varying preferences for how their pronouns should be translated, with some preferring to avoid pronouns altogether, while others want them translated to a pronoun in the target language or listed as multiple options.
- Core assumption: The diversity in user preferences for pronoun translation is a result of the varied experiences and identities within the LGBTQIA+ community.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1] "While the most popular option is (c) Translate to a pronoun in the target language (if commonly associated identity matches), there is no clear consensus and also strong tendencies towards gender-agnostic solutions."
- Break condition: If MT systems offer customizable options for pronoun translation based on user preferences, the diverse needs of the LGBTQIA+ community should be better addressed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How do different types of neopronouns (e.g., nounself, emojiself, numberself) compare in terms of translation quality across various language pairs beyond the six studied here?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares translation quality for several neopronoun categories but only for six specific languages.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to a specific set of languages and neopronoun categories.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing a wider range of language pairs and neopronoun categories to see if observed patterns hold or if there are significant differences.

## Limitations
- Small sample size of 49 survey participants may not represent all user preferences across different linguistic and cultural contexts
- Manual annotation process introduces potential subjectivity, though inter-annotator agreement was not reported
- Focus on European and Persian languages potentially misses important patterns in non-European language families

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The finding that gender-neutral pronouns experience higher rates of grammatical errors and misgendering compared to gendered pronouns. Supported by concrete quantitative data showing 50% grammatical correctness for gender-neutral pronouns in Italian translations versus 97% for gendered pronouns.

**Medium Confidence**: The observation that different MT engines employ varied strategies for handling neopronouns, with some copying pronouns while others attempt translation. While patterns are clear, underlying reasons for engine-specific behaviors require further investigation.

**Low Confidence**: The survey results suggesting diverse user preferences for pronoun handling, due to the small sample size (49 participants) and potential selection bias in recruitment through LGBTQIA+ community channels.

## Next Checks

1. **Inter-annotator agreement verification**: Replicate the annotation process with additional native speakers to establish inter-annotator reliability scores for the grammaticality and semantic consistency criteria.

2. **Engine behavior analysis**: Conduct controlled experiments varying sentence complexity and pronoun position to determine whether the observed engine-specific strategies (copying vs. translating) are consistent across different linguistic contexts.

3. **Survey validation**: Expand the participant pool to 200+ respondents across multiple recruitment channels to test whether the observed diversity in pronoun preference patterns holds at scale.
