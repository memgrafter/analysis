# A Hierarchical Approach to Population Training for Human-AI Collaboration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16708
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16708
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: HiPT outperforms baselines in human-AI collaboration, with human partners preferring HiPT over other agents

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces HiPT, a hierarchical reinforcement learning approach for human-AI collaboration that trains agents to adapt to diverse partners with varying skill levels and play styles. The method learns multiple best-response policies as low-level policies, while a high-level manager policy dynamically switches between them based on the current partner. HiPT is evaluated in the Overcooked game environment and demonstrates superior performance compared to baseline methods when collaborating with both trained agents and real human partners. Human participants also show preference for HiPT as a collaboration partner, indicating its effectiveness in adapting to novel partners.

## Method Summary
HiPT uses a two-level hierarchical structure for population training. The low-level policy consists of multiple best-response (BR) sub-policies, each specialized for different partner types. The high-level policy acts as a manager that selects the appropriate BR sub-policy based on the current partner's characteristics. Training uses a diverse partner population including agents of varying skill levels and play styles. Influence rewards are added to encourage the high-level policy to select sub-policies that cause distinct behavioral shifts in the low-level policy, measured by KL-divergence between conditional and marginal policies.

## Key Results
- HiPT outperforms baseline methods (PPOBC and FCP) in collaboration with diverse trained agents and human proxy agents
- Human participants showed significant preference for HiPT as a partner over other agents in controlled studies
- The hierarchical structure enables effective adaptation to partners with varying skill levels and play styles
- Influence rewards successfully encourage distinct sub-policies, improving overall performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The hierarchical structure allows dynamic adaptation by learning multiple best-response sub-policies conditioned on partner characteristics. Low-level policies act as specialized best responses to different partner types, while the high-level policy selects the appropriate sub-policy based on the current partner's state. This works under the assumption that partner characteristics are inferable from state observations and the relationship between partner type and optimal response is stable enough to learn.

### Mechanism 2
Influence rewards encourage distinct sub-policies by maximizing the divergence between each sub-policy's behavior and the average behavior. The high-level policy receives KL-divergence reward when its chosen sub-policy causes significantly different low-level behavior compared to the marginal policy. This mechanism assumes that increasing behavioral diversity among sub-policies improves robustness to partner heterogeneity.

### Mechanism 3
Population training with diverse partners of varying skill levels and play styles creates robust agents that generalize to unseen partners. Training on a diverse partner population ensures the agent experiences a wide range of behaviors and skill levels, preventing overfitting to specific partner patterns. This assumes that partner diversity during training correlates with improved generalization to novel partners.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - High-level policy (manager) → Low-level policy (sub-policies) → Action
  - Partner population (diverse agents) → Training environment
  - Influence reward module → High-level policy training

- Critical path: State → High-level policy → Sub-policy prior z → Low-level policy → Action
  The high-level policy must quickly and accurately select appropriate sub-policies based on partner characteristics inferred from state.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of sub-policies vs. training complexity: More sub-policies allow finer specialization but increase training time and risk overfitting
  - Influence reward coefficient: Higher values encourage more distinct sub-policies but may reduce overall performance if taken to extremes
  - Partner population diversity vs. training stability: More diverse populations improve generalization but may make training less stable

- Failure signatures:
  - Low performance across all partners: Likely high-level policy failing to select appropriate sub-policies
  - Good performance on some partners but poor on others: Sub-policies may not be sufficiently distinct or high-level policy has selection bias
  - Performance similar to single policy baseline: Influence rewards may be too weak or sub-policies too similar

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test high-level policy selection accuracy: Fix low-level policies and evaluate if high-level can correctly identify optimal sub-policy for different partner types
  2. Measure sub-policy distinctiveness: Calculate pairwise KL-divergences between sub-policies to verify influence rewards are creating behavioral diversity
  3. Ablation on partner population diversity: Train with progressively less diverse populations and measure performance degradation on novel partners

## Limitations

- The hierarchical structure assumes partner characteristics can be reliably inferred from state observations, which may not hold for all partner types
- Human study sample size (21 participants) is relatively small, limiting generalizability of human preference results
- The exact diversity metrics for the partner population are not rigorously quantified

## Confidence

- High confidence: The hierarchical structure enabling dynamic sub-policy selection, and the population training approach for diversity
- Medium confidence: The effectiveness of influence rewards for creating distinct sub-policies, and the generalization claims to novel partners
- Medium confidence: Human preference results, given the limited sample size but strong qualitative agreement
