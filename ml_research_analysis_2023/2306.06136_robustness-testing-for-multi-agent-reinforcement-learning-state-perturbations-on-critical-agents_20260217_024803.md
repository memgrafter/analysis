# Robustness Testing for Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning: State Perturbations on Critical Agents

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.06136
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06136
- Reference count: 36
- Key outcome: RTCA outperforms state-of-the-art methods in reducing team rewards and winning rates by targeting critical agents for adversarial attacks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces RTCA, a robustness testing framework for multi-agent reinforcement learning that targets critical agents for adversarial attacks. The method uses differential evolution to identify critical agents and their worst-case joint actions, then generates adversarial observations using FGSM to disrupt team cooperation. Experiments on SMAC benchmarks demonstrate that RTCA achieves lower winning rates and team cumulative rewards compared to methods like FGSM, ATLA, and PAAD across multiple scenarios, particularly in heterogeneous agent environments.

## Method Summary
RTCA is a robustness testing framework for MARL that targets critical agents for adversarial attacks. The method uses differential evolution to identify critical agents and their worst joint actions, then generates adversarial observations using FGSM to disrupt team cooperation. A Sarsa-based joint action-value function is learned during execution to guide the differential evolution process. The framework is evaluated on SMAC benchmarks against pre-trained VDN and QMIX models, comparing performance across various scenarios with different numbers of victim agents.

## Key Results
- RTCA achieves lower winning rates compared to baselines (FGSM, ATLA, PAAD) across multiple SMAC scenarios
- RTCA reduces team cumulative rewards more effectively than competing methods
- The method shows particular effectiveness in heterogeneous agent environments where critical agent selection is more impactful

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Differential evolution identifies critical agents and worst joint actions without requiring retraining when victim sets change. DE encodes candidate solutions as tuples of victim indices and their joint actions, evolving a population using mutation and crossover while optimizing against the Sarsa-based joint action-value function. This works because DE doesn't require gradient information and can handle non-differentiable joint action-value networks like QMIX. The Sarsa-based Qjt approximates team performance well enough to guide DE toward worst-case actions.

### Mechanism 2
Adversarial observations are generated using FGSM to make victims take actions close to worst-case actions identified by DE. FGSM computes the gradient of a loss function (encouraging the victim's policy to output the target worst action) with respect to the victim's observation, then perturbs the observation to maximize this loss. This targeted attack forces victims to act suboptimally for the team. The mechanism relies on the victim's policy network being differentiable with respect to its input observation.

### Mechanism 3
The Sarsa-based joint action-value function learns to accurately estimate team performance even when some agents take suboptimal actions. A Sarsa-based critic is trained during execution using epsilon-greedy exploration, receiving state-action pairs and minimizing TD error to learn the joint value function. This critic isn't constrained by IGM conditions and can represent values of joint actions including suboptimal choices. Sufficient exploration via epsilon-greedy allows the critic to observe and learn from a wide range of joint actions.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (Dec-POMDPs)
  - Why needed here: The paper operates in the Dec-POMDP framework where agents have partial observability and must cooperate. Understanding this is essential to grasp the CTDE training paradigm and the formulation of robustness testing.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between a Dec-POMDP and a fully observable MDP in terms of agent decision-making?

- Concept: Value Decomposition Networks (VDN) and QMIX
  - Why needed here: These are the baseline MARL algorithms used in the experiments. Understanding how they factorize joint action-values and satisfy IGM is crucial for interpreting the attack results and the need for the Sarsa-based critic.
  - Quick check question: How do VDN and QMIX ensure that the optimal joint action can be computed by each agent independently?

- Concept: Adversarial Attacks in RL
  - Why needed here: The paper builds on concepts from adversarial attacks in single-agent RL (like FGSM) but extends them to the multi-agent setting. Knowing the difference between untargeted and targeted attacks, and how they are formulated as optimization problems, is key.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between an untargeted adversarial attack and a targeted one in the context of RL?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Environment -> DE algorithm -> Sarsa critic -> FGSM generator -> Victim agents

- Critical path:
  1. Environment provides current state and observations
  2. DE uses Sarsa critic to select M victims and worst joint actions
  3. Adversarial generator uses FGSM to create adversarial observations
  4. Victims take actions based on perturbed observations, others use clean observations
  5. Environment returns next state and rewards

- Design tradeoffs:
  - DE vs. gradient-based methods: DE is more flexible (no differentiability required) but potentially slower to converge
  - Sarsa critic vs. using trained QMIX/VDN: Sarsa critic can represent suboptimal joint actions but requires additional training time
  - FGSM vs. other attacks: FGSM is fast and targeted but relies on differentiability of victim policies

- Failure signatures:
  - DE converges to the same victim set every time: Sarsa critic may be biased or exploration insufficient
  - Adversarial observations have no effect on victim actions: FGSM perturbations too small or victim policies too robust
  - Team performance improves with adversarial observations: Random noise may be helping, or the Sarsa critic is inaccurate

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run RTCA on a simple SMAC map (e.g., 8 Marines) with M=1 victim and compare winning rate to baseline (no attack)
  2. Vary the perturbation budget (epsilon) and measure the effect on attack success rate and victim action deviation
  3. Replace the Sarsa critic with the trained QMIX critic and observe if DE can still select critical agents effectively

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does RTCA's performance scale when applied to environments with continuous action spaces, such as MADDPG or MAAC?
- Basis in paper: The authors state "Our next work tends to apply RTCA to continuous action spaces such as MADDPG [34] and MAAC [35] to test their robustness against observation perturbations of critical agents."
- Why unresolved: The current framework is validated only on discrete action spaces (SMAC benchmarks). Continuous action spaces present different optimization challenges and may require modifications to the differential evolution approach.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing RTCA's effectiveness on continuous control tasks like MuJoCo or other MARL benchmarks using MADDPG/MAAC, including comparisons with baseline methods and analysis of critical agent identification in continuous spaces.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the variability of victim agents at each time step affect the long-term stability and convergence of the trained MARL policies?
- Basis in paper: The paper states "In our RTCA, the victim set is changed at each time step" and claims this provides stealth, but does not analyze the impact on MARL policy stability.
- Why unresolved: While varying victim agents increases stealth, the paper doesn't investigate whether this dynamic targeting affects the underlying MARL training process or policy convergence when agents face adversarial observations intermittently.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments measuring MARL policy performance and convergence rates when trained with and without RTCA attacks, analyzing how varying victim selection affects policy stability and robustness over multiple training episodes.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the computational overhead of RTCA compared to training-based adversarial methods like ATLA or PAAD?
- Basis in paper: The paper emphasizes that RTCA "does not require any training, allowing for a variable set of victims M to be used" while methods like ATLA and PAAD require adversary training.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides extensive performance comparisons but lacks quantitative analysis of computational efficiency, including runtime comparisons between RTCA and training-based methods across different problem scales.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed benchmarking of computational resources (wall-clock time, memory usage) for RTCA versus training-based methods across various MARL scenarios, including analysis of how computational requirements scale with the number of agents and complexity of the environment.

## Limitations
- Limited evaluation on continuous action spaces - framework validated only on discrete SMAC benchmarks
- Incomplete ablation study - lacks comparison between Sarsa critic and trained VDN/QMIX critics
- No statistical significance testing - results presented without confidence intervals or p-values

## Next Checks

1. **Statistical Validation**: Re-run all experiments with at least 30 trials per condition and report confidence intervals and p-values to establish statistical significance of the performance differences between RTCA and baseline methods.

2. **Generalization Test**: Apply RTCA to additional MARL algorithms (e.g., QTRAN, COMA) and heterogeneous agent combinations to verify the claim that the method works across different MARL paradigms and that critical agent selection is more effective in heterogeneous environments.

3. **Online Learning Validation**: Implement and test the full online version where the Sarsa critic is learned during execution rather than using pre-trained models, to validate whether the approach remains effective when the critic must adapt to changing joint action distributions.
