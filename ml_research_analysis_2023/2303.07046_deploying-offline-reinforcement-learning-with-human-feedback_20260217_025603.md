# Deploying Offline Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.07046
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07046
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proposes methods for deploying offline RL models online using UCB-based model selection or human feedback fine-tuning, achieving high performance after 100-200 trials.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of deploying offline reinforcement learning models in online environments where perfect offline training is not guaranteed. The authors propose two complementary approaches to improve online performance: model selection using upper confidence bound (UCB) algorithm to choose from pre-trained models, and fine-tuning models online using human feedback. Experiments on robotics locomotion control and traffic light control tasks demonstrate that both methods can effectively identify or improve models to achieve high online scores that balance environment rewards and human preferences.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes two approaches for deploying offline RL models online. First, a UCB-based model selection algorithm that balances exploration and exploitation to identify the best pre-trained model with minimal online trials. Second, a fine-tuning approach where human experts can override model actions when they disagree, with these corrections used to update the model parameters. Both methods optimize an "online score" metric that combines environment return with penalties for human expert disagreement.

## Key Results
- UCB-based model selection achieves near-optimal performance after approximately 100 trials
- Fine-tuning approach significantly improves model performance with no more than 200 trials
- Both methods successfully balance environment rewards with human preferences in robotics and traffic light control tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm effectively balances exploration and exploitation in selecting the best offline model for online deployment.
- Mechanism: UCB maintains an optimistic estimate of each model's online score by adding an exploration bonus inversely proportional to the number of times the model has been deployed. This encourages trying less-tested models while still exploiting known good ones.
- Core assumption: The online score is stochastic and bounded, allowing for statistical confidence bounds to be meaningful.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The UCB-based model selection algorithm achieves near-optimal performance after approximately 100 trials"
  - [section] "We propose using the upper confidence bound (UCB) algorithm [26, 2] to determine which model can achieve the highest online score with minimal trials"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no directly related papers found in corpus
- Break condition: If the online score distribution has very high variance or is unbounded, the UCB exploration bonus may not properly balance exploration and exploitation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning offline models with human feedback improves online performance by aligning model behavior with human preferences.
- Mechanism: When human feedback indicates model actions deviate from expert preferences, those state-action pairs are logged and used to update the model parameters. For continuous control, this involves minimizing MSE between model and expert actions; for discrete control, using margin-based loss to increase expert action values.
- Core assumption: Human feedback is available in real-time and accurately reflects preferences that should guide the model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the fine-tuning approach significantly improves model performance with no more than 200 trials"
  - [section] "we consider a scenario where a human expert policy can override the model's action when the expert's decision deviates significantly from the model's action"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only one related paper found in corpus
- Break condition: If human feedback is sparse, noisy, or arrives with significant delay, the fine-tuning process may not effectively improve the model.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The online score metric effectively balances environment rewards with human preferences, making it suitable for evaluating deployment performance.
- Mechanism: Online score combines environment return with a penalty term for actions that disagree with human expert preferences. This creates a unified objective that captures both task performance and safety/ethical considerations.
- Core assumption: The relative weighting of environment return versus human preference penalty (α1, α2) can be appropriately set for the task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose to maximize the concept of online score, which integrates both the environment return and human feedback"
  - [section] "The online score is defined as follows: S = E[α1 · Σr(st,π(st)) − α2 · Σ1{π(st)≠πE(st)}]"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no directly related papers found in corpus
- Break condition: If the human preference penalty is set too high or too low relative to the environment reward, the metric may not effectively guide model selection or fine-tuning.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: CQL training pipeline -> Model storage -> Online deployment system (UCB selection OR fine-tuning module) -> Human feedback interface -> Evaluation framework

- Critical path:
  1. Train N offline models with varying hyperparameters
  2. Deploy selected model in online environment
  3. Collect environment rewards and human feedback
  4. Update model selection statistics (UCB) or fine-tuning dataset
  5. Repeat until performance converges

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model selection vs. fine-tuning: Model selection requires multiple pre-trained models but minimal online computation; fine-tuning requires only one model but more online computation
  - Exploration bonus scaling (β) in UCB: Higher values encourage more exploration but may slow convergence
  - Fine-tuning frequency: More frequent updates may improve performance but increase computational overhead

- Failure signatures:
  - Model selection fails: Online score plateaus below expected level, indicating UCB isn't effectively identifying best model
  - Fine-tuning fails: Human disagreement metric doesn't decrease despite fine-tuning, suggesting poor feedback integration
  - Both fail: Environment return is high but human disagreement is also high, indicating poor balance in online score metric

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run UCB model selection for 50 iterations and plot online score convergence curve
  2. Compare fine-tuning with and without human feedback on a single model
  3. Vary the α2 penalty weight in online score and observe impact on human disagreement metric

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the proposed methods for online deployment with human feedback scale when the number of offline models increases beyond 5?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using the UCB algorithm to select from a set of pre-trained models, but only tests with 5 models.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the UCB-based model selection approach with a small number of models (5) for robotics and traffic light control tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing the UCB algorithm with varying numbers of models (e.g., 10, 50, 100) to determine the scaling properties and performance degradation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of the penalty term scaling (λ) in CQL on the online performance of offline models during deployment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses CQL with different penalty scales (λ = 0, 1, 5, 10, 100) for training offline models.
- Why unresolved: While the paper reports the performance of models with different λ values, it doesn't analyze how this choice affects the online deployment phase or the trade-off between environment return and human feedback.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of how different λ values in CQL affect the online score during deployment, including ablation studies and sensitivity analysis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How robust are the fine-tuning approaches to different types of human feedback (e.g., explicit action suggestions vs. preference-based feedback)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that human feedback can take various forms, but only tests with explicit action suggestions in the fine-tuning approach.
- Why unresolved: The paper only implements and tests one type of human feedback (explicit action suggestions) for the fine-tuning approach, despite acknowledging that feedback can vary.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the fine-tuning performance under different feedback mechanisms, such as preference-based feedback, constraint-based feedback, or demonstrations, to determine the most effective feedback type for online deployment.

## Limitations
- Assumes a single human expert providing consistent feedback, which may not reflect real-world deployment scenarios
- Requires real-time human intervention capabilities, limiting applicability in high-speed or safety-critical environments
- Evaluation limited to relatively simple control tasks; scalability to complex environments with high-dimensional state spaces remains untested
