# Robust Deep Reinforcement Learning Scheduling via Weight Anchoring

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.10176
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10176
- Reference count: 12
- One-line primary result: Method reduces catastrophic forgetting in RL schedulers handling rare priority events, achieving comparable performance to augmented simulation with less forgetting.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces weight anchoring, a continual learning technique for deep reinforcement learning schedulers, to address catastrophic forgetting when learning to handle rare but important priority events. The approach uses Fisher information to fixate learned behavior for rare events while optimizing for overall system performance. By separating the learning process into two stages—first mastering priority handling in isolation, then anchoring this behavior while optimizing the main task—the method achieves robust performance without requiring large amounts of rare-event training data. Results show the approach matches or exceeds augmented simulation training while significantly reducing priority timeouts and improving steerability through a single anchoring parameter.

## Method Summary
The method employs a two-stage DDPG-based RL approach with weight anchoring. In stage one, the agent trains exclusively on high-frequency priority events (100% occurrence) to quickly learn robust handling. In stage two, it reuses these weights and optimizes for the full reward while anchored, avoiding relearning priority handling from scratch. The anchoring mechanism adds an elastic penalty term to the loss that grows with the squared distance from anchored parameters weighted by their Fisher information. The Fisher information, approximated from Adam's second moment estimate, quantifies parameter sensitivity and focuses optimization on solutions near the anchored parameters. A single anchoring weight hyperparameter controls the trade-off between reward maximization and priority timeout reduction.

## Key Results
- Achieves similar or better reward performance compared to baseline and augmented simulation methods
- Dramatically reduces priority timeouts compared to baseline (p_prio=0.01%)
- Shows comparable performance to augmented simulation (p_prio=20%) with significantly less forgetting
- Provides explicit control over the trade-off between reward and priority timeout through the anchoring weight parameter

- Claim: Weight anchoring fixes priority-handling behavior by constraining optimization to solutions near the anchored parameter values, preventing catastrophic forgetting.
- Mechanism: The method first trains a network to handle rare priority messages, records the Fisher information per parameter to measure sensitivity, and then adds an elastic penalty term to the loss that grows with the squared distance from anchored parameters weighted by their Fisher information.
- Core assumption: The Fisher information from Adam's second moment estimate is a reliable proxy for parameter sensitivity in this context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Weight anchoring may be used to find a solution to a learning problem that is nearby the solution of another learning problem."
  - [section] "Using the Fisher information to focus the optimization landscape on solutions in the vicinity of the anchored solution via an elastic penalty."

- Break condition: If the Fisher information estimate is noisy or the optimization landscape changes significantly, the anchored region may no longer contain good solutions for the main task.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Separating the learning of priority handling and general scheduling into two stages improves sample efficiency and reduces model gap.
- Mechanism: In stage one, the agent trains exclusively on high-frequency priority events (100% occurrence) to quickly learn robust handling. In stage two, it reuses these weights and optimizes for the full reward while anchored, avoiding relearning priority handling from scratch.
- Core assumption: Learning priority handling in isolation is easier and more stable than interleaving it with the main task from the start.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We design a two-stage learning process. First, our approach learns to handle priority messages exclusively."

- Break condition: If the priority-handling solution is suboptimal or if the main task requires very different representations, the anchored solution may hinder rather than help.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The anchoring weight hyperparameter allows explicit control over the trade-off between reward maximization and priority timeout reduction.
- Mechanism: By scaling the anchoring loss term, the scheduler can be steered to prioritize either higher overall reward or lower priority timeouts, enabling application-specific tuning.
- Core assumption: A single scalar can meaningfully control the balance between two objectives without introducing instability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A single scaling factor controls the anchored tasks pull on the main optimization objective."

- Break condition: If the objectives are too conflicting, adjusting the scalar may not resolve instability, and the optimization may oscillate or diverge.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Catastrophic forgetting
  - Why needed here: The method directly addresses the problem of overwriting learned priority handling when training on the main task.
  - Quick check question: What happens to the priority-handling performance when the agent continues training on the main task without anchoring?

- Concept: Fisher information
  - Why needed here: It quantifies how sensitive the network's output is to each parameter, guiding which parameters to anchor more strongly.
  - Quick check question: How does the Fisher information estimate relate to the second moment in Adam's update rule?

- Concept: Two-stage learning
  - Why needed here: Separating the learning of rare and common events allows more efficient and stable training.
  - Quick check question: Why might it be beneficial to train on a high-frequency version of rare events before applying the learned behavior to the real low-frequency setting?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: State pre-processor -> Actor network -> (Exploration) -> Allocation -> Environment -> Reward -> Memory -> (Training) -> Update actor/critic
- Critical path: State → pre-processor → actor → (exploration) → allocation → environment → reward → memory → (training) → update actor/critic → repeat
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Frequency augmentation vs. anchoring: Augmentation increases sample size but widens the reality gap; anchoring preserves rare-event behavior with less data but requires careful tuning of the anchoring weight.
  - Computation cost: Storing and using Fisher information adds overhead but is negligible compared to training.
  - Flexibility: Anchoring locks in behavior, which can be both a benefit (robustness) and a limitation (reduced adaptability).
- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in priority timeouts across runs: likely insufficient anchoring or unstable training.
  - Degraded reward compared to baseline: anchoring too strong, preventing effective learning of the main task.
  - No improvement over simple augmentation: anchoring weight mis-tuned or Fisher estimate inaccurate.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a scheduler on high-frequency priority events, evaluate its priority-handling performance in isolation.
  2. Apply weight anchoring with increasing anchoring weights and measure the trade-off curve between reward and priority timeouts.
  3. Compare forgetting behavior by continuing training on no-priority data with and without anchoring.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: What is the optimal weighting strategy for the anchoring parameter wθAnchor i to balance priority timeout performance and overall system reward in different network conditions?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that the degree of trade-off between reward performance and timeout performance is visibly influenced by the anchoring parameter wθAnchor i for the anchoring approaches.
  - Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the influence of wθAnchor i but does not explore optimal strategies for different network conditions or provide guidelines for tuning this parameter in practice.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying network conditions (SNR, user count, resource availability) while testing different anchoring weight strategies, potentially leading to a recommendation framework.

- Question: How does the two-stage learning approach perform in more complex, dynamic environments with varying priority message characteristics over time?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates effectiveness in a controlled environment with static priority message probabilities, but real-world systems often exhibit dynamic changes in traffic patterns and priority message frequency.
  - Why unresolved: The experiments focus on a single, static scenario with fixed priority message probability (pprio = 0.01%), leaving open questions about performance in dynamic or non-stationary environments.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Testing the method in environments with time-varying priority message probabilities, seasonal traffic patterns, or other dynamic conditions, measuring performance degradation and adaptation speed.

- Question: Can the weight anchoring approach be extended to multi-task learning scenarios beyond the two-task case demonstrated, and what are the scalability limitations?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly mentions "multi-task learning" in the context of weight anchoring and demonstrates a two-stage approach, but questions remain about scaling to more tasks.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only demonstrates a binary split between priority handling and general scheduling, without exploring scenarios with multiple rare events or more complex task hierarchies.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with three or more distinct learning tasks (e.g., handling multiple types of priority messages, different QoS classes), measuring performance degradation and computational overhead as task count increases.

## Limitations

- The method's effectiveness depends on the accuracy of Fisher information estimates, which may be unreliable for deeper networks or different optimizers
- The two-stage approach introduces additional hyperparameters that require careful tuning and may limit scalability to more complex multi-task scenarios
- The evaluation is limited to a single synthetic environment, leaving questions about performance in real-world scheduling systems

## Confidence

- **Medium**: Experimental results show clear improvements in priority timeout reduction while maintaining reward performance, but evaluation is limited to a single synthetic environment
- **Medium**: Comparison against only two baseline methods leaves open questions about performance relative to other continual learning or rare-event handling techniques
- **Low**: The method's scalability to more complex domains with multiple rare events or continuous action spaces remains uncertain
