# A Comparative Study of Methods for Estimating Conditional Shapley Values and When to Use Them

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09536
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09536
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Parametric methods produce most accurate Shapley values when data distribution is correctly specified; VAEAC and matching regression methods are good alternatives when distribution is unknown.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive comparison of methods for estimating conditional Shapley values, which explain predictions from complex machine learning models. The authors organize various existing and new methods into six distinct classes based on their approach to modeling conditional expectations. Through extensive simulations and real-world experiments, they evaluate the accuracy and computational efficiency of these methods under different data distributions, feature dependencies, and predictive model forms. The results provide practical guidance on selecting appropriate methods based on the characteristics of the data and predictive model.

## Method Summary
The study compares six classes of methods for estimating conditional Shapley values: independence, empirical, parametric (Gaussian, copula, Burr, GH), generative (ctree, VAEAC), separate regression (LM, GAM, PPR, RF, CatBoost), and surrogate regression (LM, GAM, PPR, RF, CatBoost, NN). Monte Carlo integration methods sample from conditional distributions, while regression methods directly model the conditional expectations. The authors conduct simulation studies using multivariate Gaussian and Burr distributions with varying correlation and scale parameters, along with four UCI real-world datasets. They evaluate methods using Mean Absolute Error for simulated data and Mean Squared Error of the contribution function for real-world data, while also measuring computational time.

## Key Results
- Parametric methods achieve the highest accuracy when the data distribution is correctly specified.
- VAEAC generative methods and regression methods with matching form to the predictive model provide stable performance when the distribution is unknown.
- Regression-based methods are slow to train but fast at prediction, while Monte Carlo methods show the opposite behavior.
- The number of training observations has minimal impact on method ranking, but high-dimensional feature spaces pose computational challenges.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Parametric methods produce the most accurate Shapley values when the data distribution is correctly specified.
- Mechanism: By assuming a specific parametric distribution (e.g., multivariate Gaussian), these methods can generate conditional Monte Carlo samples from analytically known conditional distributions, avoiding the need to estimate complex non-parametric conditional densities.
- Core assumption: The true data distribution is well-approximated by the assumed parametric form.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Roughly speaking, we recommend using parametric methods when we can specify the data distribution almost correctly, as they generally produce the most accurate Shapley value explanations."
  - [section 3.3.1]: "In the Gaussian approach, we sample the conditional samples x(k)S from p(xS|xS), for k = 1, 2, ..., K and S∈P∗(M), and use them in (3) to estimate the Shapley values in (1)."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus neighbors don't discuss parametric distribution assumptions specifically.
- Break Condition: If the true data distribution significantly deviates from the assumed parametric form, the parametric methods will produce biased estimates and perform poorly.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Separate regression methods with the same form as the predictive model provide more accurate Shapley values.
- Mechanism: These methods train individual regression models gS(xS) for each coalition S to directly estimate the conditional expectation E[f(xS,xS)|xS], matching the complexity and structure of the predictive model f.
- Core assumption: The regression model class can adequately approximate the true conditional expectation function for each coalition.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Regression-based methods are often slow to train but produce the Shapley value explanations quickly once trained."
  - [section 3.5.1]: "For each data set XS, we train a regression model gS(xS) with respect to the mean squared error loss function. The optimal model, with respect to the loss function, is g*S(xS) = E[z|xS] = E[f(xS,xS)|xS], which corresponds to the contribution function v(S)."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus neighbors don't discuss separate regression methods in detail.
- Break Condition: If the regression model class is too simple to capture the complexity of the predictive model, the estimates will be inaccurate.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: VAEAC generative methods provide accurate and stable Shapley values when the data distribution is unknown.
- Mechanism: These methods use a variational autoencoder to model all conditional distributions p(xS|xS) simultaneously without making parametric assumptions, generating samples from the learned conditional distributions.
- Core assumption: The VAEAC architecture can learn the underlying conditional distributions from the training data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "When the distribution is unknown, both generative methods and regression models with a similar form as the underlying predictive model are good and stable options."
  - [section 3.4.2]: "Olsen et al. (2022) use a type of variational autoencoder called VAEAC (Ivanov et al. 2019) to generate the conditional Monte Carlo samples."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus neighbors don't discuss VAEAC specifically.
- Break Condition: If the VAEAC model fails to learn the true conditional distributions (due to insufficient capacity, poor initialization, or inadequate training), the generated samples will be inaccurate.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input: Predictive model f, training data, test observation x*
  Shapley value estimation pipeline:
  - Data preprocessing (handling categorical features, scaling)
  - Conditional expectation estimation (Monte Carlo or regression-based)
  - Contribution function computation v(S) for all coalitions S
  - Shapley value calculation using weighted least squares
  Output: Shapley values φj for each feature j

- Critical path: The most computationally intensive step is typically the conditional expectation estimation, especially for Monte Carlo methods that require K*Ntest*(2^M-2) calls to the predictive model f.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs. computation time: Parametric methods are most accurate but require correct distributional assumptions; Monte Carlo methods are slower but more flexible; regression methods offer a middle ground.
  - Memory vs. speed: Surrogate regression methods require augmented training data which can be memory-intensive but enable fast prediction.
  - Model complexity vs. interpretability: More complex regression models may provide better accuracy but are harder to interpret.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on dependent features: Independence methods will fail when features are correlated.
  - Slow computation: Monte Carlo methods with expensive predictive models will be very slow.
  - Memory errors: Surrogate regression methods with large M and Ntrain may run out of memory.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test the independence method on a simple dataset with independent features to establish a baseline.
  2. Compare Gaussian parametric method vs. empirical method on a Gaussian-distributed dataset with moderate correlation.
  3. Evaluate separate regression vs. surrogate regression methods on a dataset with a known predictive model structure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the relative performance of the proposed methods when the feature dimension M is significantly larger than 8, as in many real-world applications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that high-dimensional settings pose computational challenges and suggests sampling subsets of coalitions or using tractable estimation strategies, but does not provide empirical results for high-dimensional data.
- Why unresolved: The simulation studies and real-world experiments in the paper are limited to M = 8 features. The authors acknowledge that the number of models to fit in the separate regression class becomes infeasible in high dimensions, but do not investigate the accuracy or computational efficiency of the proposed methods for larger M.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting simulation studies and real-world experiments with feature dimensions significantly larger than 8, using the proposed methods and comparing their performance to existing methods for high-dimensional data.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the proposed methods perform when the training data size N_train is very small or very large, and how does this affect the choice of method?

- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the performance of the proposed methods across a wide range of training data sizes, nor does it provide specific recommendations for choosing a method based on the size of the training data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting simulation studies and real-world experiments with a wide range of training data sizes, using the proposed methods and comparing their performance to existing methods for different training data sizes.

## Limitations
- High computational cost for regression-based methods on large datasets due to exponential growth of augmented training data.
- Potential overfitting of parametric methods when distributional assumptions are violated.
- Limited investigation of high-dimensional feature spaces and complex deep learning models.
