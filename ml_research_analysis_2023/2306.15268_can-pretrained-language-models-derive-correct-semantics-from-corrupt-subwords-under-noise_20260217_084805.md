# Can Pretrained Language Models Derive Correct Semantics from Corrupt Subwords under Noise?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.15268
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15268
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: Systematic evaluation of PLM robustness to noise-induced subword corruption reveals significant vulnerabilities

## Executive Summary
This study introduces CoLeS, a contrastive evaluation framework designed to assess how noise-induced corruption in subword segmentation affects pretrained language model (PLM) performance. The framework systematically categorizes four main types of corruption (complete, partial, additive, and missing) and creates contrastive datasets pairing canonical and noisy words. Through comprehensive experiments on BERT, RoBERTa, and ALBERT models, the research reveals that PLMs exhibit significant vulnerability to complete corruption, particularly when word meanings are stored in embeddings, while additive corruption proves especially damaging when subwords are inserted within others.

## Method Summary
The research develops a controlled evaluation framework that generates systematic corruption scenarios for subword segmentation. Four corruption types are defined: complete corruption (entire subword corrupted), partial corruption (part of subword corrupted), additive corruption (subwords added), and missing corruption (subwords removed). For each type, contrastive pairs of canonical and noisy words are created, enabling precise measurement of model performance degradation. The framework is applied across multiple PLM architectures including BERT, RoBERTa, and ALBERT, with evaluation on masked language modeling and word similarity tasks.

## Key Results
- PLMs show highest vulnerability to complete corruption, especially when semantic information is embedded in subword representations
- Additive corruption causes severe performance degradation when noise is inserted within subwords rather than appended
- Model performance decreases linearly with the number of additive corrupt tokens
- Retaining larger subword chunks significantly improves model comprehension under noise conditions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The study demonstrates that PLMs rely heavily on subword segmentation patterns for semantic interpretation, with corruption disrupting the learned relationships between subwords and their meanings. The mechanism reveals that models store semantic information differently across architectures - some rely more on embeddings while others depend on contextual relationships. Complete corruption breaks these learned patterns entirely, while additive corruption creates interference patterns that compound with the number of corrupt tokens. The findings suggest that subword segmentation serves as a critical bridge between token representation and semantic understanding.

Design tradeoffs: Precision vs. recall in corruption detection, computational cost vs. coverage, controlled vs. realistic noise patterns
Failure signatures: Complete corruption shows catastrophic performance drops, additive corruption shows gradual degradation, missing corruption shows partial information loss
First experiments: 1) Apply complete corruption to high-frequency words, 2) Test additive corruption with varying token counts, 3) Compare architectural responses to partial corruption

## Limitations
- Controlled corruption scenarios may not fully capture real-world noise complexity
- Limited to English language data and three model architectures
- Does not evaluate downstream task performance or adversarial defense mechanisms

## Confidence
High confidence in experimental methodology and controlled evaluation framework
Medium confidence in generalizability to real-world scenarios
Low confidence in practical implications for adversarial defense strategies
