# ActSafe: Predicting Violations of Medical Temporal Constraints for Medication Adherence

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.07051
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07051
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: HERBERT model achieves 51% reduction in RMSE for medication intake prediction and 0.86 average F1 score for MTC violation prediction

## Executive Summary
This paper presents ActSafe, a system that predicts violations of medical temporal constraints (MTCs) to improve medication adherence in chronic disease patients. The system extracts MTCs from unstructured drug usage guidelines using a context-free grammar approach, predicts relevant regular health behaviors using a novel basis vectorization-based model (HERBERT), and detects potential violations through a rule-based system. Evaluation using a real-world dataset from 28 chronic disease patients demonstrates that HERBERT outperforms baseline models in predicting medication intake behavior, and ActSafe successfully predicts MTC violations a day ahead with an average F1 score of 0.86.

## Method Summary
ActSafe combines three core components: a context-free grammar-based parser for extracting MTCs from drug usage guidelines, a basis vectorization-based HERBERT model for predicting regular health behaviors relevant to medication adherence, and a rule-based system for detecting potential MTC violations. The approach transforms unstructured medication instructions into structured temporal constraints, predicts when patients are likely to perform relevant behaviors like medication intake or eating, and then uses these predictions to forecast adherence violations before they occur.

## Key Results
- HERBERT model achieves 51% reduction in RMSE for predicting medication intake behavior compared to baseline models
- ActSafe predicts MTC violations a day ahead with an average F1 score of 0.86
- The basis vectorization approach successfully handles varying temporal scales of different health behaviors
- CFG-based MTC extraction reliably maps free-text guidelines to structured temporal constraints

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The context-free grammar (CFG) model can reliably map free-text drug usage guidelines to structured MTCs.
- Mechanism: CFG production rules are applied regardless of context, allowing extraction of MTC types from unstructured text by matching token sequences against a predefined grammar.
- Core assumption: Drug usage guidelines contain explicit, matchable token sequences that correspond to MTC patterns in the CFG.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ActSafe utilizes a context-free grammar based approach for extracting and mapping MTCs from patient education materials."
  - [section 3.1.2] "A context-free grammar is a suitable solution to model such dependencies and constraints as the production rule can be applied to any relevant datasets regardless of the context of the non-terminal, i.e., different types of MTCs."
  - [corpus] "The Scope of In-Context Learning for the Extraction of Medical Temporal Constraints" suggests alternative ML-based approaches but does not challenge the CFG-based approach.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Basis vectorization smooths sensor label noise and enables robust prediction of short-span RHBs.
- Mechanism: Time series of RHBs are discretized into fixed-size windows; each window is marked as 1 if the RHB occurs, 0 otherwise. This aggregation reduces noise and normalizes varying behavior durations.
- Core assumption: RHB occurrences are relatively stable within a window and can be represented as binary presence/absence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "Basis vectorization smooths sensor labels which can often be erratic in nature... basis vectorization mitigates the differences between short-span RHBs such as medication intake and long-span RHBs such as sleeping by giving each a larger unit of time."
  - [abstract] "Our novel behavior prediction model, HERBERT, utilizes a basis vectorization of time series that is generalizable across temporal scale and duration of behaviors."
  - [corpus] Weak—no direct evidence for basis vectorization in neighbors, but general activity prediction literature supports this approach.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The rule-based system can predict MTC violations by aligning predicted RHB timestamps with extracted MTC rules.
- Mechanism: For each MTC type, predefined rules compare the predicted timestamps of relevant RHBs (e.g., medication intake, eating) against the temporal constraints (e.g., "2 hours before eating") to detect violations.
- Core assumption: Predicted RHB timestamps are accurate enough to determine whether the temporal constraint is satisfied.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ActSafe can predict MTC violations a day ahead of time with an average F1 score of 0.86."
  - [section 3.4] "We predict violations of MTCs by developing a new rule base that aligns the semantics of behavior sequence and temporal constraints of medical advice to predict potential violations well ahead of the possible time of violations."
  - [corpus] Weak—no direct evidence in neighbors for rule-based violation prediction, but clinical decision support literature supports this approach.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  CFG Extraction -> HERBERT Prediction -> Rule-based Violation Detection

- Critical path:
  1. Extract MTCs from drug usage guidelines using CFG.
  2. Predict relevant RHBs using HERBERT model.
  3. Apply rule-based system to detect MTC violations.
  4. Alert patient or caregiver if violation predicted.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - CFG-based MTC extraction vs. ML-based extraction: CFG is simpler and interpretable but may miss implicit MTCs; ML may be more flexible but less transparent.
  - Basis vectorization window size: Smaller windows increase granularity but also sparsity; larger windows smooth noise but may lose timing precision.
  - Rule-based violation detection vs. learned models: Rules are transparent and easy to update but may not generalize well to complex MTCs; learned models may be more flexible but harder to interpret.

- Failure signatures:
  - High false positives in violation prediction: Likely due to inaccurate RHB predictions or overly strict rule thresholds.
  - Low recall in MTC extraction: Likely due to implicit or ambiguous MTCs not matching CFG patterns.
  - Poor behavior prediction performance: Likely due to high sparsity in RHB data or inadequate window size.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test CFG-based MTC extraction on a small set of drug usage guidelines with known MTCs; measure precision, recall, and F1.
  2. Vary basis vectorization window size (15, 30, 60 minutes) on a sample RHB dataset; compare RMSE and sparsity.
  3. Apply rule-based violation detection to predicted RHBs for a known MTC; measure F1 and analyze false positives/negatives.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper acknowledges several open questions that warrant further investigation:

1. How generalizable is the CFG-based MTC model across different languages and cultural contexts beyond English? The paper mentions the CFG model is explicitly designed only for English language and that generalizability to other patient education materials is subject to future investigation.

2. How does ActSafe handle rare or highly irregular behaviors that may be critical for medication adherence but infrequently observed? The paper mentions that extremely irregular health behaviors are edge cases not handled by the HERBERT model and that there may be RHBs overlooked by the underlying activity recognition system.

3. What is the long-term effectiveness of ActSafe in improving medication adherence and health outcomes in real-world deployments? The paper states that ActSafe is a proof-of-concept solution and mentions plans for a user study to evaluate its effectiveness in the future.

## Limitations

- The CFG-based MTC extraction may miss implicit or complex temporal constraints that don't follow expected syntax patterns
- The evaluation dataset (28 patients, ACE inhibitors only) is too limited to support strong generalization claims
- The rule-based violation detection relies heavily on the accuracy of predicted RHB timestamps, with no discussion of error propagation

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The overall system architecture (CFG extraction → RHB prediction → rule-based violation detection) is logically sound and well-specified. The paper clearly describes each component and their integration.

**Medium Confidence**: The evaluation methodology using real-world data is appropriate, but the limited sample size and narrow medication focus prevent strong generalization claims. The performance metrics (RMSE reduction, F1 scores) are properly calculated and reported.

**Low Confidence**: The robustness of the CFG extraction across diverse medical guidelines, the basis vectorization's ability to preserve critical temporal patterns, and the rule system's handling of complex MTC interactions remain inadequately validated.

## Next Checks

1. **CFG Extraction Robustness Test**: Create a test suite of drug usage guidelines containing implicit, ambiguous, and complex MTCs (e.g., "take with food if stomach upset occurs," "may be taken with or without meals"). Measure precision, recall, and F1 for each guideline type to identify CFG limitations.

2. **Basis Vectorization Sensitivity Analysis**: Re-run the HERBERT model on the same dataset using multiple window sizes (15, 30, 60 minutes) and different discretization strategies (binary vs. frequency counts). Compare performance degradation and identify the window size that balances noise reduction with temporal precision.

3. **Temporal Error Propagation Study**: Generate synthetic RHB prediction errors (varying magnitude and direction) and measure their impact on MTC violation detection F1 scores. This will quantify how sensitive the rule-based system is to prediction inaccuracies and help identify failure thresholds.
