# Exploring the Effect of Multi-step Ascent in Sharpness-Aware Minimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.10181
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10181
- Reference count: 9
- This paper analyzes the effect of ascent step count in SAM and proposes MSAM, which improves generalization performance by utilizing gradients from all intermediate perturbed weights.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why multi-step ascent Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) rarely improves generalization despite theoretically providing better approximations of maximum neighborhood loss. Through systematic analysis, the authors discover that multi-step ascent SAM achieves higher perturbed losses but suffers from gradient misalignment between current weights and perturbed weights, causing optimization inefficiency. To address this, they propose Multi-Step SAM (MSAM), which averages gradients from all intermediate perturbed weights during the update step. Experimental results show MSAM consistently outperforms standard SAM variants across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet datasets, achieving the highest performance among all tested variations.

## Method Summary
The paper analyzes Sharpness-Aware Minimization with varying ascent steps (SAM-1, SAM-2, SAM-3) and proposes a modification called MSAM. The key innovation is using all intermediate perturbed weights' gradients as update directions during multi-step ascent. For MSAM with N ascent steps, gradients are computed at each perturbed weight wp1, wp2, ..., wpN, and these gradients are averaged to update the original weights. This modification addresses the inefficiency observed in standard multi-step SAM where gradients become misaligned with the current weight direction as ascent steps increase.

## Key Results
- Multi-step ascent SAM achieves higher perturbed loss values but shows degraded generalization performance compared to single-step SAM
- MSAM modification improves accuracy of SAM-2 and achieves highest performance among all SAM variants tested
- Models trained with different numbers of ascent steps exhibit distinct loss landscapes, with SAM-1 showing lowest training loss but highest perturbed loss

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multi-step ascent SAM achieves higher perturbed loss but lower gradient alignment with current weight
- Mechanism: Multi-step ascent finds a more extreme perturbed weight in the loss landscape, but its gradient diverges from the gradient at the current weight, causing inefficiency in minimizing the original loss
- Core assumption: The loss landscape is sufficiently non-linear that multiple ascent steps can find better local maxima than single-step
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "multi-step ascent SAM rarely improves generalization performance, which is contrary to the expectation that it should provide a better approximation of the maximum neighborhood loss"
  - [section] "When we only consider v1, which is a commonly used direction for measuring perturbed loss and sharpness... it may seem that the loss landscape is locally linear. However, the loss value increases as N increases for all k"
  - [section] "The gradient of SGD has difficulty in minimizing the perturbed loss... In contrast, the gradients of perturbed weights lead to a significant decrease in perturbed losses. However, as the number of ascent steps increases, the loss decrease of w diminishes"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using multiple perturbed weights' gradients provides better optimization direction diversity
- Mechanism: By averaging gradients from multiple perturbed weights (wp1, wp2, ..., wpN), the optimization captures diverse directional information that single perturbed weight gradients miss
- Core assumption: The gradients from different perturbed weights provide complementary information about the loss landscape
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we suggest a simple modification that utilizes all gradients of intermediate perturbed weights as update directions"
  - [section] "Each gradient exhibits a distinct effect on each weight... ∇ℓ(w) shows the most significant loss decrease of w, yet it does not effectively minimize the perturbed losses of wp1 and wp2"
  - [section] "The proposed modification shows stable performance and improves the accuracy of SAM-2, leading to the highest performance among the variations of SAM"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Distinct loss landscapes emerge between single-step and multi-step SAM training
- Mechanism: Models trained with different numbers of ascent steps converge to fundamentally different minima in the parameter space, with single-step SAM favoring one type of flat region and multi-step SAM favoring another
- Core assumption: The optimization trajectory and final solution depend critically on the ascent step strategy
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "single-step ascent SAM and multi-step ascent SAM exhibit distinct loss landscapes"
  - [section] "SAM-1 has the lowest loss ℓ(w), but shows the highest perturbed loss ℓ(wp5). In contrast, SAM-2 and SAM-3 have a lower perturbed loss ℓ(wp5) than SAM-1, but exhibit a higher loss ℓ(w) than SAM-1"
  - [section] "models trained with different numbers of ascent steps exhibit different loss landscapes with respect to ascent directions"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  current weights -> multi-step ascent computation -> perturbed weights (wp1, wp2, ..., wpN) -> gradient computation at each perturbed weight -> gradient averaging -> weight update

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass at current weights to compute gradient
  2. Multi-step ascent to find perturbed weights wp1, wp2, ..., wpN
  3. Forward and backward passes at each perturbed weight
  4. Average gradients from all perturbed weights
  5. Update weights using averaged gradient

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computational cost vs. performance: MSAM requires N forward/backward passes per update but shows better performance
  - Memory usage: Storing intermediate perturbed weights and gradients
  - Hyperparameter sensitivity: Number of ascent steps N and step-size ratios affect stability

- Failure signatures:
  - Degraded performance with too few ascent steps (N < 3)
  - Instability with very large step sizes during ascent
  - No improvement over single-step SAM when intermediate gradients are too similar

## Limitations
- The theoretical explanation for why gradient misalignment causes optimization inefficiency is incomplete, relying primarily on empirical observations rather than rigorous mathematical proof
- Ablation studies are limited to only two ascent steps (N=2), leaving uncertainty about performance with more ascent steps
- Loss landscape analysis relies on visualizing only a few specific ascent directions, which may not capture the full complexity of the optimization space

## Confidence
- High confidence: The empirical observation that multi-step ascent SAM achieves higher perturbed loss but shows degraded generalization performance compared to single-step SAM
- Medium confidence: The claim that gradient misalignment between current weights and perturbed weights causes inefficiency in the optimization process
- Medium confidence: The proposed MSAM modification improves performance by averaging gradients from intermediate perturbed weights
