# A Method to Automate the Discharge Summary Hospital Course for Neurology Patients

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06416
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06416
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Automated generation of neurology discharge summary hospital courses achieved 62% quality rating by physicians, with ROUGE-2 score of 13.76

## Executive Summary
This study developed an automated method to generate the hospital course section of discharge summaries for neurology patients. The approach used a "day-to-day" strategy that segments the EHR data into history of present illness, daily narrative, and follow-up sections, and applies fine-tuned BERT and BART transformer models with constrained beam search to improve factuality. Physician evaluation of 50 randomly selected summaries showed that 62% of the automated summaries met quality standards, with high ratings for readability (7.0/10) and completeness (6.9/10), though factuality was lower (6.4/10) compared to physician-written summaries. ROUGE-2 scores for the final summaries were 13.76, within range of state-of-the-art long document summarization models. The method demonstrated clinical validity and potential to reduce physician documentation burden.

## Method Summary
The method segments EHR data into three parts: history of present illness (HPI) from admission notes, daily narrative from progress notes, and follow-up from discharge summaries. BERT models classify document relevance and extract follow-up information, while BART models generate abstractive summaries for each segment. Constrained beam search penalizes banned medical terms not present in source documents to improve factuality. The system was trained on 6,600 neurology hospital admissions from 2010-2020 at NewYork-Presbyterian/Weill Cornell Medical Center, with evaluation using ROUGE scores and physician Likert scale ratings.

## Key Results
- 62% of automated summaries met quality standards in physician evaluation
- ROUGE-2 score of 13.76, comparable to state-of-the-art long document summarization models
- Physician ratings: readability 7.0/10, completeness 6.9/10, factuality 6.4/10
- High precision and recall in document classification (0.87-0.95) and follow-up extraction (0.95-0.97)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Segmenting the EHR data into history of present illness (HPI), daily narrative, and follow-up sections improves summarization quality by reducing input length and focusing on relevant content.
- Mechanism: The day-to-day approach divides the medical chart into three separate segments, each ingesting only specific types of clinical notes. This limits the total amount of input words into the transformer models, overcoming the 512-1024 token length limitation of pre-trained transformers.
- Core assumption: Breaking down the long hospital course into smaller, manageable segments allows the models to better capture relevant information without being overwhelmed by the full document length.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The approach used a 'day-to-day' strategy that segments the EHR data into history of present illness, daily narrative, and follow-up sections"
  - [section]: "As illustrated in Figure 1, we segmented the initial dataset into three parts: (1) history of present illness (HPI) summarization which is primarily constructed from the admission note, (2) daily narrative document classification and summarization which chronologically details the patient's full course of treatment, and (3) follow-up extraction classification"
- Break condition: If the segmentation criteria are not well-defined or the wrong types of clinical notes are included in each segment, the quality of the summaries may suffer.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning BERT and BART models with constrained beam search improves factuality and reduces hallucinations in the generated summaries.
- Mechanism: BERT models are used for classification tasks (document classification and follow-up extraction), while BART models are used for text summarization. Constrained beam search penalizes the logarithmic score of any words from a set of banned words, which are medical terms not present in the source document, to improve factuality.
- Core assumption: Constraining the beam search during model inference by penalizing inconsistent medical terms reduces hallucinations and improves the factuality of the generated summaries.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The approach used a 'day-to-day' strategy that segments the EHR data into history of present illness, daily narrative, and follow-up sections, and applies fine-tuned BERT and BART transformer models with constrained beam search to improve factuality."
  - [section]: "Our approach constrains traditional beam search by penalizing the logarithmic score of any words from a set of banned words (Algorithm 1). For constructing our set of banned words, we first used a custom medical dictionary VM derived from SNOMED CT [46]. For any medical words and synonyms that intersect both the source document x and VM, we permit them during beam search; otherwise all other words in the medical dictionary are banned in the generated sequence (the set of banned words)Wbanned and the next best alternative word is selected as the output."
- Break condition: If the banned word list is not comprehensive or the penalty for using banned words is not strong enough, the model may still generate hallucinations.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Physician evaluation using a Likert scale improves understanding of automated summary quality, readability, factuality, and completeness beyond what automated metrics like ROUGE scores can provide.
- Mechanism: Two board-certified physicians blindly rated 50 summaries on a Likert scale of 1 to 10 for quality, readability, factuality, and completeness. This provided insights into the human-perceived quality of the summaries that are not captured by automated metrics.
- Core assumption: Physician evaluation is necessary to assess the quality of clinical summaries because automated metrics like ROUGE scores do not address grammar, consistency, or clinical validity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "In a blind evaluation, two board-certified physicians rated 62% of the automated summaries as meeting the standard of care, which suggests the method may be useful clinically."
  - [section]: "To measure quality, readability, factuality, and completeness of an automated summary [29, 52], two board-certified physicians (MGW, an internist; and BBN, a neurologist) blindly rated 25 pairs of patient discharge summaries, one generated by the automated method and one written by a physician for a particular hospitalization."
- Break condition: If the physicians are not blinded to the source of the summaries or if they are not representative of the intended audience for the summaries, their evaluations may not accurately reflect the quality of the summaries.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data collection -> Segmentation (HPI, daily narrative, follow-up) -> BERT classification -> BART summarization -> Constrained beam search -> Evaluation (ROUGE + physician ratings)

- Critical path:
  1. Data collection and segmentation
  2. Document classification and follow-up extraction using BERT models
  3. Text summarization using BART models
  4. Factuality improvement using constrained beam search
  5. Evaluation using ROUGE scores and physician ratings

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Segmentation approach: The day-to-day approach improves performance but may miss cross-day context
  - Factuality improvement: Constrained beam search improves factuality but may slightly decrease fluency
  - Evaluation metrics: ROUGE scores provide automated evaluation but do not capture clinical validity; physician ratings provide human evaluation but are time-consuming and subjective

- Failure signatures:
  - Low ROUGE scores: May indicate poor summarization performance or insufficient training data
  - Low physician ratings: May indicate issues with factuality, completeness, or readability
  - High word and sentence error rates: May indicate significant hallucinations or factual inconsistencies in the generated summaries

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate the performance of different transformer models (BERT, BART, Longformer) for document classification and text summarization tasks.
  2. Compare the effectiveness of different factuality improvement techniques (constrained beam search, reinforcement learning, graph-based attention) on the generated summaries.
  3. Assess the impact of different segmentation strategies (day-to-day, by clinical note type, by time window) on the quality and completeness of the summaries.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the generalizability of the day-to-day approach for other clinical specialties beyond neurology?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that their dataset was specific to neurology patients and references a prior study using ICU patients, suggesting potential adaptation to other specialties.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested the approach on neurology patients and does not provide evidence of performance on other specialties.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the day-to-day approach on datasets from different clinical specialties (e.g., cardiology, oncology) and comparing performance metrics like ROUGE scores and physician evaluation ratings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the constrained beam search approach perform compared to other factuality improvement techniques?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions several other approaches for improving factuality (question answering metrics, textual entailment predictions, graph-based attention, reinforcement learning) but only implements constrained beam search.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not compare constrained beam search to other factuality improvement methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and comparing the constrained beam search approach with other factuality improvement techniques on the same dataset and measuring their impact on factuality metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of including more contextual information from labs and vitals in the automated summaries?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that physician clinical notes were the primary source of content due to their high textual overlap, and the model included little source data from labs and vitals that were not contextualized within a physician note.
- Why unresolved: The current model design does not adequately capture lab and vitals data that may have been missed by physicians in their clinical notes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Modifying the model to better extract and contextualize lab and vitals data, then comparing the completeness and factuality of the resulting summaries to the current approach.

## Limitations

- The evaluation sample size of 50 summaries may not be representative of the full diversity of neurology cases.
- The approach relies heavily on structured EHR data availability, which may vary across institutions.
- The study focuses specifically on neurology patients, so generalizability to other medical specialties remains untested.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core mechanism of segmenting EHR data into HPI, daily narrative, and follow-up sections is well-supported by the evidence and demonstrates clear effectiveness in managing transformer model limitations. The physician evaluation methodology and results showing 62% of automated summaries meeting quality standards are robust and directly measured.

**Medium Confidence**: The factuality improvement through constrained beam search shows promise, but the exact impact depends on implementation details not fully specified (e.g., banned word list construction). The generalizability of results beyond neurology requires further validation. The ROUGE-2 scores of 13.76, while within state-of-the-art range, may not fully capture clinical validity.

**Low Confidence**: The specific medical vocabulary construction for constrained beam search and exact preprocessing pipeline details are not fully specified, making precise replication challenging. The optimal balance between banned word penalties and summary fluency requires further tuning.
