# On Structural Expressive Power of Graph Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.13987
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13987
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: Introduces SEG-WL test as an upper bound on graph Transformer expressivity, with SPIS encoding achieving superior performance on benchmark datasets

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a theoretical framework to characterize the expressive power of graph Transformers by introducing the SEG-WL (Structural Encoding Weisfeiler-Lehman) test. The key insight is that graph Transformers' ability to distinguish graph structures is fundamentally limited by their structural encodings. The authors prove that SEG-WL test serves as an upper bound on the expressivity of various graph Transformers and can be approximated by simple Transformer networks under certain conditions. Building on this framework, they propose SPIS (Shortest Path Induced Subgraph) encoding, which captures global structural relationships and demonstrates superior discriminative power compared to existing encodings, particularly on strongly regular graphs and low-order graph isomorphism tasks.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a theoretical framework based on extending the WL test with structural encodings. They define SEG-WL test as an iterative label update process that incorporates relative structural encodings into the node aggregation strategy. Using this framework, they establish conditions under which graph Transformers can be approximated by SEG-WL iterations. They then propose SPIS encoding, which captures shortest path induced subgraph information (node/edge counts) to achieve stronger expressive power than local neighborhood encodings. The method is evaluated through synthetic graph isomorphism tests and real-world molecular property prediction tasks using Graphormer variants with different structural encodings.

## Key Results
- SEG-WL test serves as an upper bound on the expressive power of graph Transformers
- SPIS encoding achieves 2.8-8.6% performance improvements on ZINC, QM9, QM8, ESOL datasets
- SPIS-SEG-WL distinguishes all non-isomorphic graphs with order ≤ 8 and all strongly regular graphs with different parameters
- SPIS encoding has O(n³) computational complexity but provides significant discriminative advantages

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SEG-WL test provides an upper bound on graph Transformer expressivity
- Mechanism: By modeling the Transformer's node aggregation as a global hashing of all node labels plus relative structural encodings, SEG-WL captures the maximum discriminative power achievable
- Core assumption: Structural encodings are the primary source of structural information in graph Transformers
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We theoretically prove that the SEG-WL test is an expressivity upper bound on a wide range of graph Transformers" [section 4.1] "SEG-WL test iteratively updates node labels of 𝐺, where at the 𝑡-th iteration, the updated node label mapping 𝑔𝑡 : 𝑉 → X is computed as 𝑔𝑡 (𝑣) = Φ ({ {(𝑔𝑡 −1 (𝑢), 𝑓𝑅 (𝑣, 𝑢, 𝐺)) : 𝑢 ∈ 𝑉 } })"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Relative structural encodings determine convergence rate of SEG-WL test
- Mechanism: When one encoding scheme contains all information of another (𝑆 ′ ⪰ 𝑆), it can distinguish graphs faster and more powerfully
- Core assumption: Expressiveness and convergence rate are directly related to the richness of structural information encoded
- Evidence anchors: [section 5.2] "Theorem 3. For two structural encoding schemes 𝑆 and 𝑆 ′, if 𝑆 ′ ⪰ 𝑆, then (1) 𝑆 ′-SEG-WL is more expressive than 𝑆-SEG-WL in testing non-isomorphic graphs. (2) for a pair of graphs 𝐺1 and 𝐺2 that 𝑆-SEG-WL distinguishes as non-isomorphic after 𝑡 iterations, 𝑆 ′-SEG-WL can distinguish 𝐺1 and 𝐺2 as non-isomorphic within 𝑡 iterations."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: SPIS encoding achieves superior expressive power through global structural awareness
- Mechanism: By encoding shortest path induced subgraph information (node/edge counts), SPIS captures structural relationships that local encodings miss
- Core assumption: Global structural relationships provide discriminative power beyond local neighborhood information
- Evidence anchors: [section 6.3] "SPIS-SEG-WL Distinguishes All Low-order Graphs (𝑛 ≤ 8). On low-order graphs, our synthetic experiments in Table 1 confirm that SPIS-SEG-WL distinguishes all non-isomorphic graphs with order equal to or less than 8"

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Graph with node features -> Absolute structural encoding (𝑓𝐴) -> Relative structural encoding (𝑓𝑅) -> SEG-WL test iterations -> Graph embeddings
- **Critical path**: 1. Compute absolute encodings for all nodes 2. Initialize labels with absolute encodings 3. Iteratively update labels using relative encodings 4. Apply global readout or use final labels
- **Design tradeoffs**: Expressiveness vs computational cost (more expressive encodings like SPIS require more computation), Local vs global information (local encodings are cheaper but may miss global structure), Fixed vs adaptive encodings (fixed encodings are simpler but may not capture all graph properties)
- **Failure signatures**: Performance plateaus despite increasing model capacity (likely encoding limitation), High computational cost with diminishing returns (encoding too complex for task), Poor generalization on strongly regular graphs (encoding lacks sufficient structural discrimination)
- **First 3 experiments**: 1. Compare WL test vs SEG-WL with neighbor encoding on simple graph isomorphism tasks 2. Test SPIS vs SPD encoding on strongly regular graphs with varying parameters 3. Benchmark Graphormer variants (id, neighbor, SPD, SPIS) on ZINC dataset regression task

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1**: Can the SEG-WL test be efficiently approximated for large graphs with reduced computational complexity while preserving its expressive power characterization?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that SEG-WL test has quadratic complexity with respect to the input size and each label update iteration costs O(n^2) complexity, where n is the input graph size. The paper also mentions that the structural encodings may be computed by algorithms with relatively high complexity, like SPD which is obtained by the O(n^3) Floyd-Warshall algorithm.
  - Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a solution for reducing the computational complexity of SEG-WL test while preserving its expressive power characterization. The paper only discusses the limitations and potential negative societal impacts of the high complexity.
  - What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical proof or experimental results demonstrating that SEG-WL test can be efficiently approximated for large graphs with reduced computational complexity while preserving its expressive power characterization.

- **Open Question 2**: How does the expressive power of graph Transformers with SPIS encoding compare to other types of graph neural networks, such as spectral graph neural networks or graph attention networks, on various graph tasks?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper presents SPIS as a provably stronger structural encoding method compared to SPD encoding, which is used in Graphormer. The paper also shows that SPIS-SEG-WL has strong expressive power in distinguishing graph structures and can consistently improve the performance of benchmark graph Transformers when equipped with SPIS encoding.
  - Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a direct comparison between the expressive power of graph Transformers with SPIS encoding and other types of graph neural networks on various graph tasks. The paper only compares the performance of Graphormer variants with different structural encodings on graph representation learning benchmarks.
  - What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive experimental study comparing the performance of graph Transformers with SPIS encoding to other types of graph neural networks, such as spectral graph neural networks or graph attention networks, on various graph tasks, including graph classification, graph regression, and graph clustering.

- **Open Question 3**: Can the SEG-WL test be extended to handle directed graphs or graphs with edge features, and how would this affect the expressive power of graph Transformers?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only considers simple undirected graphs with node features. However, many real-world graphs are directed or have edge features, and it would be valuable to extend the SEG-WL test to handle these cases.
  - Why unresolved: The paper does not provide any discussion or analysis on how to extend the SEG-WL test to handle directed graphs or graphs with edge features, and how this would affect the expressive power of graph Transformers.
  - What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical extension of the SEG-WL test to handle directed graphs or graphs with edge features, along with an analysis of how this affects the expressive power of graph Transformers. This could be accompanied by experimental results demonstrating the effectiveness of the extended SEG-WL test on directed graphs or graphs with edge features.

## Limitations

- **Computational complexity**: SPIS encoding requires O(n³) complexity, making it impractical for very large graphs
- **Theoretical assumptions**: The framework assumes structural encodings are the primary limiting factor, which may not hold for all graph Transformer architectures
- **Domain specificity**: While results are strong on molecular graphs, generalizability to other graph types requires further validation

## Confidence

- **SEG-WL Test as Expressivity Upper Bound**: High confidence
- **SPIS Encoding Discriminative Power**: Medium confidence
- **Computational Efficiency Claims**: Low confidence
- **Universal Approximation of SEG-WL**: Medium confidence
