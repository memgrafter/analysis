# RobuT: A Systematic Study of Table QA Robustness Against Human-Annotated Adversarial Perturbations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.14321
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14321
- Reference count: 25
- Key outcome: RobuT benchmark exposes significant performance drops for Table QA models on human-annotated adversarial perturbations; LETA framework using LLM prompting improves robustness

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces RobuT, a new benchmark for systematically evaluating Table QA model robustness against human-annotated adversarial perturbations at table header, content, and question levels. The benchmark includes 143,477 examples across 10 perturbation types and evaluates state-of-the-art models and large language models like GPT-3. Results show all models experience significant performance degradation on adversarial sets. To address this, the authors propose LETA, a framework using LLM prompting to generate adversarial training examples. Experiments demonstrate LETA significantly improves model robustness compared to rule-based adversarial training data generation.

## Method Summary
The RobuT benchmark evaluates Table QA model robustness using three base datasets (WTQ, WikiSQL-Weak, SQA) and human-annotated adversarial perturbations across table headers, contents, and questions. The evaluation measures pre-perturbation accuracy, post-perturbation accuracy, and robustness accuracy. The LETA framework generates adversarial training examples using LLM prompting with chain-of-thought demonstrations for each perturbation type. Four Table QA models (TAPAS, TableFormer, TAPEX, OmniTab) are fine-tuned on original training sets and evaluated on the RobuT benchmark.

## Key Results
- All evaluated models (TAPAS, TableFormer, TAPEX, OmniTab) show significant performance drops on adversarial sets
- GPT-3 outperforms all other Table QA models on word-level and sentence-level question perturbations
- LETA-augmented models demonstrate significantly higher post-perturbation accuracy compared to rule-based augmentation
- Human-annotated perturbations reveal annotation bias in existing Table QA datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LETA framework improves robustness by generating adversarial examples that mimic human annotation patterns using LLM prompting
- Mechanism: GPT-3/CodeX is prompted with chain-of-thought examples to generate semantically valid but adversarial perturbations (e.g., header synonyms, column extensions, NLQ paraphrases). These are used to augment training data.
- Core assumption: LLMs can simulate human-like adversarial perturbations with high semantic fidelity and without changing the ground truth answer.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Experiments demonstrate that our methods effectively improves Table QA model robustness."
  - [section 5]: "We designed task-specific 'chain-of-thought' prompts (Wei et al., 2022b; Chen, 2022) to guide the GPT-3... models to generate adversarial examples."
  - [corpus]: Weak; only one related paper with fmr=0.64; no direct adversarial data generation evidence.
- Break condition: If generated perturbations change answer correctness or are semantically invalid, model robustness gains will not materialize.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Human-annotated perturbations expose annotation bias in existing Table QA datasets, enabling more comprehensive robustness evaluation
- Mechanism: Annotators systematically perturb table headers, contents, and questions in controlled ways; parallel pre- and post-perturbation examples measure model degradation
- Core assumption: Human perturbations are linguistically rich and maintain semantic association while exposing model weaknesses
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our results indicate that both state-of-the-art Table QA models... falter in these adversarial sets."
  - [section 3]: Describes 10 perturbation types at three levels, with 143,477 human-annotated examples
  - [corpus]: Moderate; several papers on adversarial robustness, but none with systematic human-annotated table QA perturbations
- Break condition: If perturbations are not truly representative of real-world variation or are too narrow in scope, robustness evaluation will be incomplete

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Models with larger pre-training (e.g., GPT-3) generalize better to perturbed data due to broader linguistic and factual coverage
- Mechanism: GPT-3's in-context learning adapts to new semantic variants without fine-tuning, leveraging its larger parametric knowledge
- Core assumption: Larger models with more diverse pre-training can handle distributional shifts in table headers, contents, and NLQs
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "GPT-3 outperforms all other Table QA models on both word-level and sentence-level question perturbations."
  - [section 4.2]: "GPT-3 with higher post-perturbation accuracy correlated with higher robustness accuracy in most cases."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; one paper mentions LLM fingerprinting robustness, not generalization
- Break condition: If perturbations require domain-specific reasoning not covered in pre-training, even large models may fail

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Semantic parsing and table reasoning
  - Why needed here: Table QA models map natural language to structured operations over tables; understanding this mapping is essential to grasp perturbation impact
  - Quick check question: In "How many runners scored more than 2 points?", what table column would a semantic parser target? (Answer: "Points" or "Score")
- Concept: Adversarial robustness evaluation
  - Why needed here: To measure model robustness, we need both original and perturbed inputs that preserve answer correctness
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes pre-perturbation accuracy from robustness accuracy? (Answer: Robustness accuracy measures correct predictions on both original and perturbed data)
- Concept: LLM prompting and chain-of-thought
  - Why needed here: LETA relies on carefully crafted prompts to generate adversarial examples; understanding prompt engineering is key
  - Quick check question: What is the purpose of including explanations in LETA prompts? (Answer: To guide the LLM in generating semantically valid perturbations)

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Human annotation pipeline -> LETA LLM prompting module -> Table QA model trainer -> Evaluation harness
- Critical path: 1. Human annotate perturbations → 2. LETA generates augmented training data → 3. Model fine-tuned on augmented set → 4. Evaluated on ROBU T
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Human vs LLM generation: higher quality vs lower cost
  - Perturbation scope: narrow (diagnose specific weakness) vs broad (general robustness)
  - Model size: large LLMs generalize better but are expensive; fine-tuned small models are cheaper but less robust
- Failure signatures:
  - Low robustness accuracy despite augmentation → perturbations too easy or augmentation too noisy
  - Pre-perturbation accuracy drops significantly after augmentation → adversarial examples leak answer information
  - Human evaluation shows poor semantic quality → LLM prompts not specific enough
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run LETA with a small subset of perturbations; compare generated examples to human-annotated gold standard
  2. Ablation: train with and without LETA augmentation; measure robustness gain
  3. Stress test: apply worst-case perturbations (e.g., column masking) to probe model failure modes

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How can we improve the robustness of Table QA models without compromising their accuracy on the original development set? The paper mentions that LETA-augmented models have higher post-perturbation accuracy but also notes a trade-off between robustness and accuracy, stating that "adversarial robustness comes at the cost of standard performance."
- Can the LETA framework be extended to handle cell-level perturbations in tables, such as modifying original cell values? The paper mentions that they did not consider perturbations related to modifying original cell values due to the longer annotation time required.
- How can the LETA framework be adapted for other table reasoning benchmarks beyond Table QA, such as table fact checking or logical table-to-text generation? The paper states that their work focuses on Table QA and mentions other table reasoning benchmarks, suggesting that future work could extend the approaches used in ROBU T to these tasks.

## Limitations

- The evaluation relies on a single set of human-annotated perturbations, and their representativeness across different table domains and languages is unclear
- The effectiveness of LETA's LLM-generated perturbations is primarily demonstrated through aggregate accuracy metrics without detailed analysis of perturbation quality or semantic validity
- The claim that GPT-3's superior performance stems from broader linguistic coverage is not directly tested through controlled ablation studies

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: All evaluated models show significant performance drops on adversarial perturbations (directly measured and reported)
- **Medium confidence**: LETA improves robustness (promising results but lacks detailed ablation studies and quality assessments)
- **Low confidence**: GPT-3's performance advantage is primarily due to larger pre-training coverage (not directly tested)

## Next Checks

1. Conduct a controlled study comparing LETA-generated perturbations with rule-based generation to isolate the contribution of LLM prompting quality
2. Perform detailed semantic validation of LETA-generated examples to ensure perturbations maintain answer correctness and linguistic quality
3. Test model robustness across multiple domains and languages to assess the generalizability of the RobuT benchmark findings
