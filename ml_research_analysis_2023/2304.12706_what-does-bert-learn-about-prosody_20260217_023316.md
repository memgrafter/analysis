# What does BERT learn about prosody?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.12706
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12706
- Reference count: 0
- BERT captures prosodic prominence information through distributed layer representations

## Executive Summary
This work examines what BERT learns about prosody during pre-training by analyzing layer weights for prominence prediction across three datasets. The authors use a weighted averaging approach across BERT layers and train a lightweight classifier on top of frozen BERT representations. They find that prominence information is distributed across many layers, peaking around layer 3, suggesting BERT relies on syntactic and semantic information rather than just surface features. In contrast, POS prediction shows weight concentration in early layers (0-4), consistent with prior findings. Overall prominence prediction accuracy ranges from 78-87% depending on dataset and model configuration.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze BERT's learned representations for prosodic prominence and POS prediction by freezing the BERT model and training a lightweight classifier with learnable layer weights. They use weighted averaging across transformer layers, where each layer's contribution is determined by a learnable scalar weight followed by softmax normalization. The approach is applied to three datasets (BURNC, NXT Switchboard, and LibriTTS) with prosodic prominence annotations, using one sentence per sample and balanced across datasets. Layer weights are extracted and averaged across five training runs to analyze which layers contribute most to each task.

## Key Results
- Prosodic prominence information is distributed across layers 2-8 with peak contribution at layer 3
- POS prediction shows weight concentration in early layers (0-4), consistent with prior findings
- Frozen BERT models outperform fine-tuned versions, suggesting better generalization
- Prominence prediction accuracy ranges from 78-87% across datasets and model configurations

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Frozen BERT base model (12 layers, 768 embedding size, 12 attention heads) -> Learnable scalar weights (one per layer) followed by softmax -> Lightweight classification head (dense layer + softmax) -> Prominence/POS prediction

- Critical path: Input text → BERT embeddings from all layers → weighted averaging → classification head → prominence/POS prediction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Frozen vs fine-tuned BERT: Frozen provides better generalizability but lower task-specific accuracy
  - Layer weight learning rate: Set higher (1e-2) than classification head (5e-5) to allow rapid adjustment of layer contributions
  - Dataset balancing: Single sentence per sample ensures consistent comparison across different corpus formats

- Failure signatures:
  - If layer weights show no clear pattern (all similar values), the model may not be learning meaningful layer contributions
  - If prominence prediction accuracy is similar across all layer configurations, the weighted averaging approach may be unnecessary
  - If POS and prominence show identical weight distributions, the hypothesis about different processing depths may be wrong

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce layer weight analysis on a single dataset to verify the widespread distribution pattern for prominence
  2. Compare layer weight patterns between frozen and fine-tuned models to confirm the overfitting observation
  3. Test whether early-layer-only models can achieve similar prominence prediction accuracy to validate the need for multi-layered representations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does BERT's ability to predict prosodic prominence vary significantly between spontaneous dialogue speech versus professionally read speech, and if so, what specific linguistic features account for these differences?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that dialogue speech (Switchboard) showed lower performance (78.10%) compared to professionally read speech (BURNC at 87.14%), but doesn't deeply investigate why.
- Why unresolved: The paper only observes the performance difference without exploring underlying linguistic or prosodic factors that might explain why BERT performs better on read speech versus spontaneous dialogue.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed comparative analysis examining specific prosodic features (intonation patterns, speaking rate, disfluencies) in both speech types, coupled with layer weight analysis to identify which linguistic features BERT relies on more heavily in each case.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different prominence coding schemes across datasets affect BERT's layer weight distributions and prediction accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "the different prominence coding schemes in the three datasets" could contribute to performance differences, but doesn't investigate this systematically.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this potential confound but doesn't test how varying annotation schemes might influence BERT's learned representations and weight allocations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments using the same speech data annotated with different prominence schemes, followed by layer weight analysis to determine how annotation choices impact BERT's learning patterns.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does fine-tuning BERT for prosodic prominence tasks lead to domain-specific overfitting, and what are the implications for cross-domain generalization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that fine-tuned models show degraded performance compared to frozen models, suggesting overfitting to training data idiosyncrasies.
- Why unresolved: While the paper notes this phenomenon, it doesn't investigate the mechanisms behind this overfitting or explore strategies to mitigate it while maintaining performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing layer weights and performance across multiple domains before and after fine-tuning, along with analysis of which layers show the most domain-specific adaptation.

## Limitations

- The study doesn't directly compare against a baseline using only surface features (early layers) to validate that multi-layered representations are necessary
- The prominence prediction accuracy range of 78-87% shows substantial variation across datasets, suggesting dataset-specific factors may influence results more than acknowledged
- The claim that prominence "peaks" at layer 3 may be overstated given the widespread distribution across layers 2-8

## Confidence

- High confidence: The layer weight distribution patterns showing POS concentrated in early layers (0-4) while prominence spreads across layers 2-8
- Medium confidence: The claim that BERT "relies mostly on syntactic and semantic information" for prominence prediction
- Low confidence: The assertion that prominence information "peaks" at layer 3
