# Continual Learning in Linear Classification on Separable Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2306.03534
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03534
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: Weak regularization in continual linear classification reduces to sequential max-margin problems, with convergence guarantees to offline solutions under task recurrence, but scheduling regularization strength or using Fisher weighting doesn't necessarily prevent forgetting unlike in regression settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes continual learning of linear classifiers on separable data, establishing a theoretical framework that connects weakly-regularized learning to sequential max-margin (SMM) dynamics. The authors prove that as regularization strength approaches zero, the learning process converges to projections onto convex feasible sets, analogous to the POCS framework. They derive upper bounds on forgetting and other quantities of interest, showing that convergence to offline solutions is possible with linear rates for recurring tasks, but unlike regression, optimal weighting schemes don't guarantee forgetting prevention. The work highlights fundamental differences between continual classification and regression settings.

## Method Summary
The paper studies continual learning of linear classifiers on a sequence of T separable classification tasks. Each task m consists of data pairs (x,y) with x∈RD and y∈{-1,+1}, and the goal is to minimize exponential loss with L2 regularization. The analysis focuses on the limit as regularization strength λ→0, which maps the problem to a Sequential Max-Margin (SMM) scheme through projections onto convex feasible sets. The theoretical framework relies on joint-separability assumptions and linear regularity conditions to derive convergence bounds. The method compares different scheduling strategies for regularization strength and evaluates Fisher-information-based weighting schemes for their effectiveness in preventing forgetting.

## Key Results
- Weak regularization (λ→0) reduces continual classification to sequential max-margin dynamics, converging in direction to SMM iterates
- Linear regularity enables convergence bounds, with distance to offline feasible set bounded by maximum distance to individual task sets
- Fisher-information weighting doesn't prevent forgetting in continual classification (unlike regression) when previous data spans the parameter space
- Convergence to offline solutions occurs with linear rates for recurring tasks, but suboptimal solutions are possible unlike in continual regression

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Weak regularization in continual linear classification converges to Sequential Max-Margin (SMM) dynamics.
- **Mechanism**: When regularization strength λ→0, the minimizer of the exponential loss with L2 regularization aligns with projections onto task feasible sets, equivalent to SMM.
- **Core assumption**: Tasks are jointly-separable (Assumption 2.1) and regularization strength decays sufficiently fast.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "learning with weak regularization reduces to solving a sequential max-margin problem"
  - [section]: Theorem 3.1 proves convergence in direction to SMM iterates
  - [corpus]: Related work (Evron et al., 2022) showed similar convergence for regression settings
- **Break condition**: If tasks are not jointly-separable or if regularization doesn't decay to zero, convergence to SMM fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Linear regularity holds for separable classification tasks, enabling convergence bounds.
- **Mechanism**: The distance to the offline feasible set W* is bounded by the maximum distance to any individual task's feasible set, scaled by problem complexity.
- **Core assumption**: Feasible sets are affine polyhedral cones with non-empty interior.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "develop upper bounds on the forgetting and other quantities of interest under various settings"
  - [section]: Lemma 4.8 proves d²(wt, W*) ≤ 4∥w*∥²R² maxₘ d²(wt, Wm)
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence for this specific regularity condition in classification
- **Break condition**: If feasible sets don't have non-empty interior or tasks aren't jointly-separable, regularity fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Fisher-information-based weighting doesn't prevent forgetting in continual classification (unlike regression).
- **Mechanism**: When previous task data spans the entire space, Fisher weighting becomes identity-like and doesn't constrain future updates in orthogonal directions.
- **Core assumption**: High-dimensional settings where previous data doesn't span all dimensions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "scheduling regularization strength or using Fisher-information weighting does not necessarily prevent forgetting"
  - [section]: Figure 5 and Example in Appendix E.2.1 show cases where Fisher weighting fails
  - [corpus]: Proposition 5.5 shows Fisher weighting prevents forgetting in regression but not classification
- **Break condition**: If previous task data spans the entire parameter space, Fisher weighting becomes ineffective.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What is the exact algorithmic bias for early stopping in continual learning, and how does it compare to explicit regularization?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper mentions that early stopping and explicit regularization can behave differently in continual learning settings, unlike in single-task settings. It provides an example where they lead to distinct solutions.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper raises this question but does not provide a complete theoretical analysis of the algorithmic bias for early stopping in continual learning settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A comprehensive theoretical analysis comparing the algorithmic bias of early stopping versus explicit regularization in various continual learning scenarios, including non-separable data and non-linear models.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Are there general weighting schemes that can prevent forgetting in weighted regularization methods for continual classification?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper shows that Fisher-information-based weighting schemes, which prevent forgetting in continual regression, do not necessarily prevent forgetting in continual classification.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies this as an open question and suggests it as an exciting direction for future research, without providing a definitive answer.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A theoretical proof or empirical demonstration of a general weighting scheme that consistently prevents forgetting across various continual classification tasks and datasets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How does the choice of regularization strength scheduling affect convergence to the offline feasible set and optimality guarantees in continual classification?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper discusses how scheduling regularization strength can endanger convergence to an offline solution and optimality guarantees, contrasting it with the constant regularization strength case.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper provides examples and theoretical results showing potential issues with scheduling, but does not offer a comprehensive framework for understanding or optimizing scheduling strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A theoretical framework that characterizes the relationship between regularization scheduling strategies, convergence behavior, and optimality guarantees in continual classification settings, potentially including adaptive scheduling methods.

## Limitations

- **Assumption Dependencies**: Heavy reliance on joint-separability and linear regularity conditions that may not hold in practical scenarios
- **Regularization Limit Approximation**: Convergence results depend on λ→0 limit, but practical implementations require finite λ values
- **Generalization to Non-Separable Data**: All theoretical results assume separable data, limiting applicability to real-world classification problems

## Confidence

- **Assumption Dependencies (High Confidence)**: The analysis heavily relies on joint-separability (Assumption 2.1) and the linear regularity condition (Assumption 4.1). While the paper provides theoretical justification for these assumptions, their practical applicability depends on how well real-world task sequences satisfy these conditions. The joint-separability assumption, in particular, may be violated in many practical scenarios where tasks have overlapping or contradictory decision boundaries.

- **Regularization Limit Approximation (Medium Confidence)**: The convergence results for weakly-regularized learning depend on taking the λ→0 limit. In practice, numerical implementations must use small but finite λ values, which could affect the accuracy of the SMM convergence and the derived bounds. The paper doesn't provide error bounds for finite-λ approximations or guidance on choosing λ in practical settings.

- **Generalization to Non-Separable Data (Low Confidence)**: All theoretical results assume separable data. While the paper mentions that exponential loss behaves similarly to 0-1 loss for separable data, the analysis doesn't extend to non-separable scenarios. This significantly limits the practical applicability of the results to real-world classification problems where perfect separability is rare.
