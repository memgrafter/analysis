# Towards a Common Understanding of Contributing Factors for Cross-Lingual Transfer in Multilingual Language Models: A Review

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16768
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16768
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 18
- This review systematically surveys and organizes factors influencing zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models.

## Executive Summary
This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of factors affecting cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models. It identifies five main categories: linguistic similarity (syntax, phonology, genetics), lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data. The authors resolve conflicting findings by showing, for example, that lexical overlap matters most when source and target languages differ in word order or when source data is limited. The review concludes that interactions among factors and task-specific effects are crucial, suggesting future work should explore these dynamics and consider feature-based rather than purely language-based model design.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a systematic literature review examining 18 papers that investigate various factors affecting cross-lingual transfer. They categorized findings into five main categories and synthesized empirical evidence from past studies. The methodology involved identifying overlapping contributions with consensual findings and highlighting and attempting to resolve conflicts between contradictory studies. The review focuses on zero-shot cross-lingual transfer scenarios where models are fine-tuned on source language data and applied to target languages without further fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- Syntax (particularly word order) is often important for cross-lingual transfer, but synthetic manipulations can exaggerate its impact
- Model depth generally improves transfer more than other architectural features, while overparameterization can hurt it
- Pre-training choices like removing the NSP objective, using larger vocabularies, and increasing training corpus size help, especially for high-level tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Word order (syntax) significantly impacts cross-lingual transfer when source and target languages differ in structure.
- Mechanism: When word orders are dissimilar, lexical overlap becomes more critical because syntactic similarity is absent to guide alignment.
- Core assumption: Cross-lingual models rely on both lexical overlap and syntactic cues; without one, the other must compensate.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Syntax (particularly word order) is often important, but synthetic manipulations can exaggerate its impact."
  - [section] "This finding explains the results of K et al. (2020) and Conneau et al. (2020b) who only used language pairs of similar word order and did not observe a high impact of lexical overlap on transfer performance."
- Break condition: When word order is similar between languages, lexical overlap alone can suffice for effective transfer.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Model depth improves cross-lingual transfer more than other architectural features like attention heads.
- Mechanism: Deeper networks allow models to learn more abstract, language-agnostic representations that facilitate cross-lingual transfer.
- Core assumption: Abstract representations learned in deeper layers are less tied to language-specific surface forms.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Model depth generally improves transfer more than other architectural features, while overparameterization can hurt it."
  - [section] "They found that an increased network depth (i.e., more hidden layers), with a fixed number of model parameters, leads to better cross-lingual transfer."
- Break condition: If model is overparameterized relative to training data, deeper layers may not improve and could hurt transfer.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Pre-training corpus size and quality are crucial, especially for high-level tasks like NLI and QA.
- Mechanism: Larger, more diverse corpora expose models to richer cross-lingual patterns, improving their ability to transfer knowledge across languages.
- Core assumption: Pre-training data directly shapes the model's cross-lingual representational capacity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Pre-training choices like removing the NSP objective, using larger vocabularies, and increasing training corpus size help, especially for high-level tasks."
  - [section] "Liu et al. (2020) performed a more controlled experiment by comparing two multilingual BERT models pre-trained on different amounts of data... Increasing the pre-training corpus size... resulted in significantly improved transfer performance."
- Break condition: For lower-level tasks like POS or NER, corpus size may be less critical than for higher-level semantic tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Encoder (embedding + layers) -> pre-training objectives (MLM, NSP) -> tokenizer -> vocabulary -> fine-tuning setup
- Critical path: Pre-training (data + objectives) -> embedding alignment -> deeper layers -> cross-lingual transfer capability
- Design tradeoffs: Model capacity vs. multilinguality (curse of multilinguality), lexical overlap vs. syntactic similarity, corpus size vs. quality
- Failure signatures: Overparameterization -> language-specific subspaces; insufficient corpus -> poor cross-lingual alignment; missing NSP -> better NLI transfer
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare cross-lingual transfer performance with models of varying depth but fixed parameters.
  2. Test transfer when lexical overlap is high but word order differs, and vice versa.
  3. Evaluate impact of pre-training corpus size on transfer performance for high-level vs. low-level tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different synthetic language creation methods affect cross-lingual transfer performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that studies using synthetic languages manipulate linguistic features in different ways, but doesn't compare the effects of different manipulation methods
- Why unresolved: The paper highlights that synthetic languages can be created with controlled variation, but doesn't investigate whether different methods of creating synthetic languages (e.g., random permutation vs. structured syntactic modification) lead to different conclusions about factor importance
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic comparison of cross-lingual transfer results using the same factors but different synthetic language creation methods would clarify whether the choice of manipulation method affects observed factor importance

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do nonlinear interactions between factors affect cross-lingual transfer compared to linear combinations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that tree-based models can capture nonlinear relationships while Lasso regression assumes linearity, and suggests this might explain contradictory findings about lexical overlap
- Why unresolved: Most studies use feature importance from linear or tree-based models, but there's limited understanding of how nonlinear interactions between multiple factors (e.g., lexical overlap combined with word order differences) affect transfer performance
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments systematically varying multiple factors simultaneously and using models capable of capturing complex interactions would reveal whether nonlinear effects explain contradictory findings

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does pre-training corpus size matter differently for generative vs. discriminative cross-lingual tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that target language corpus size is more important for higher-level tasks than lower-level tasks, but doesn't distinguish between generative and discriminative tasks
- Why unresolved: Most research focuses on discriminative tasks like classification, but generative tasks like translation may have different requirements for pre-training data that haven't been systematically investigated
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison of cross-lingual transfer performance for generative and discriminative tasks across varying pre-training corpus sizes would clarify whether data requirements differ by task type

## Limitations
- Selection bias from only 18 papers surveyed, potentially missing important studies or factors
- Methodology for resolving contradictory findings lacks transparency and empirical validation
- Relies heavily on summarizing existing empirical results rather than providing new experimental evidence

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The importance of model depth for cross-lingual transfer is well-supported across multiple studies and consistently reported.
- **Medium confidence**: The role of lexical overlap is substantiated but context-dependent, with effects varying based on linguistic similarity and task type.
- **Medium confidence**: The impact of pre-training corpus size is supported by controlled experiments but may have different effects across task categories.
- **Low confidence**: The resolution of conflicts between studies (particularly regarding synthetic manipulations of word order) relies on interpretation rather than direct experimental validation.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a systematic replication study testing the interaction between lexical overlap and word order differences across multiple language pairs and tasks to validate the proposed compensatory mechanism.
2. Design controlled experiments varying pre-training corpus size while holding other factors constant to measure the differential impact on high-level versus low-level tasks.
3. Perform ablation studies on model depth versus other architectural features (attention heads, parameter count) to quantify the relative contributions to cross-lingual transfer performance.
