# Causal Strategic Classification: A Tale of Two Shifts
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.06280
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06280
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Decouples strategic and causal effects using marginal density estimation on "dirty" data to improve classification accuracy under distribution shifts

## Executive Summary
This paper studies strategic classification under causal effects, where changing user features can causally change true outcomes. The authors analyze how strategic behavior and causal effects interact to create two complementary forms of distribution shift. They propose a learning algorithm that balances between these two forces by decoupling strategic and causal effects using an estimated marginal density model. The approach makes use of additional "dirty" data collected over time and under different deployed classifiers. Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic data demonstrate the effectiveness of their method, which achieves higher accuracy than baselines by correctly handling the causal strategic setting.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a learning algorithm that addresses causal strategic classification by decoupling strategic and causal effects through marginal density estimation. The method assumes access to "dirty" data collected under different deployed classifiers over time. The algorithm estimates the marginal density of strategic features and uses this to separate the effects of strategic behavior from causal interventions. This decoupling allows the classifier to optimize for true outcomes while accounting for both forms of distribution shift. The approach is implemented using a combination of density estimation techniques and standard classification methods, with the key innovation being the use of dirty data to estimate the marginal distribution.

## Key Results
- The proposed algorithm achieves higher accuracy than baseline methods on synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets
- Correctly handles both strategic and causal distribution shifts by decoupling their effects
- Demonstrates the importance of using dirty data collected under different classifiers
- Shows theoretical guarantees for the convergence of the marginal density estimation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The algorithm works by recognizing that strategic behavior and causal interventions create two distinct but complementary distribution shifts. Strategic users change features to improve their predicted outcomes, while causal interventions change features to improve true outcomes. These two forces can work at cross-purposes or in alignment, creating complex distribution shifts that standard classifiers struggle with. By estimating the marginal density of strategic features from dirty data, the algorithm can separate these effects and optimize for true outcomes while accounting for both shifts. This decoupling allows the classifier to be robust to the complex interplay between strategic behavior and causal effects.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Dirty data collection -> Marginal density estimation -> Strategic effect decoupling -> Causal effect adjustment -> Final classifier training

Critical path: The marginal density estimation is the most critical component, as errors here propagate to the decoupling and adjustment steps, potentially degrading classifier performance.

Design tradeoffs: The algorithm trades off between strategic robustness and causal accuracy, requiring careful tuning of the density estimation component. The dirty data assumption is practical but may not hold in all settings.

Failure signatures: Poor density estimation leads to failure to decouple strategic and causal effects, resulting in suboptimal classification performance. Overly optimistic assumptions about dirty data quality can also cause failures.

Three first experiments:
1. Test the algorithm on a synthetic dataset with known strategic and causal effects to validate the decoupling approach.
2. Compare performance with and without dirty data to quantify the benefit of the marginal density estimation.
3. Evaluate robustness to noise in the dirty data by adding varying levels of noise to the collected data.
