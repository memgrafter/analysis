## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ADSG (Accelerated Doubly Stochastic Gradient), an optimization algorithm designed for large-scale empirical risk minimization problems. The method integrates three types of momentum - historical, negative, and momentum in expectation - into the optimization framework to achieve accelerated convergence rates. ADSG is specifically engineered to handle problems with massive datasets by operating on mini-batches of samples and updating only small blocks of variable coordinates per iteration, making it computationally efficient for high-dimensional problems.

The algorithm demonstrates theoretical convergence rates of O((n + √nκ) log(1/ϵ)) for strongly convex problems and O(d(n + √nL/ϵ) log(1/ϵ)) for general convex problems, where n represents sample size, d is dimensionality, κ is the condition number, and L is the smoothness parameter. These rates represent improvements over existing methods for large-scale optimization, particularly when dealing with datasets where both sample size and dimensionality are substantial.

## Method Summary
ADSG incorporates three distinct momentum terms into the optimization process: historical momentum that tracks past gradient information, negative momentum that helps escape saddle points, and momentum in expectation that accelerates convergence through stochastic gradient estimates. The algorithm operates by selecting mini-batches of data samples and updating only a subset of variable coordinates at each iteration, rather than computing full gradients or updating all parameters simultaneously. This block-coordinate update strategy, combined with the momentum terms, enables the algorithm to achieve accelerated convergence while maintaining low per-iteration computational complexity. The method is particularly suited for problems where computing full gradients is prohibitively expensive due to large sample sizes or high dimensionality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The three-momentum framework provides acceleration by maintaining momentum in different directions and timescales. Historical momentum preserves information from previous iterations, negative momentum helps overcome optimization barriers, and momentum in expectation leverages stochastic gradient estimates. The doubly stochastic nature (sampling both data points and coordinates) reduces per-iteration complexity while maintaining convergence guarantees. The combination of these elements allows the algorithm to make rapid progress toward optimal solutions while avoiding common optimization pitfalls like getting stuck in poor local minima or requiring excessive computation per iteration.
