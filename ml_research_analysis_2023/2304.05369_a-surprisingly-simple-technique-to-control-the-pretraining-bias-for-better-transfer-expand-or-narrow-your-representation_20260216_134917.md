# A surprisingly simple technique to control the pretraining bias for better transfer: Expand or Narrow your representation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.05369
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05369
- Reference count: 22
- Key outcome: Increasing backbone dimension in pretrained models significantly improves transfer learning performance by mitigating pretraining bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that increasing the backbone dimension of pretrained models can dramatically improve transfer learning performance. While supervised learning benefits from compressed representations, self-supervised learning benefits from expanded representations when the downstream task differs from the pretext task. The authors provide both theoretical and experimental evidence showing that larger backbone dimensions create an information bottleneck that preserves task-agnostic information, leading to better generalization on downstream tasks.

## Method Summary
The method involves modifying the dimensionality of the backbone's last convolutional block while keeping the projector architecture fixed. The authors train self-supervised models (SimCLR, VICReg, BYOL) and supervised models on ImageNet-1k using ResNet50 architectures with backbone dimensions ranging from 512 to 32768 features. They evaluate transfer performance using linear and nonlinear probes on multiple downstream datasets including CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and Places205.

## Key Results
- Larger backbone dimensions (D >> K) significantly improve transfer learning performance across multiple datasets and pretraining strategies
- Wider representations exhibit increased sparsity (80% of examples have half activations equal to zero) and are more easily binarizable without performance loss
- The performance gap between linear and nonlinear probes decreases with larger backbone dimensions, indicating better linear separability of features

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Increasing backbone dimensionality mitigates pretraining bias by reducing gradient information flow to the projector.
- Mechanism: When backbone dimension D >> projector dimension K, the projector's gradient signal is constrained to a K-dimensional subspace, leaving D-K dimensions untouched. This creates a bottleneck that limits the amount of "gradient-based information" flowing from the loss to the backbone, preserving more task-agnostic information in the backbone representation.
- Core assumption: The projector acts as a lossy compressor that removes information not needed for the pretext task, and this compression is beneficial when D > K.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "merely changing its dimensionality – by changing only the size of the backbone's very last block – is a remarkably effective technique to mitigate the pretraining bias"
  - [section 3.1]: "To limit the amount of 'gradient-based information' coming from the loss, one must have D≫ K which creates an information bottleneck"
  - [corpus]: Weak - related papers don't directly address this specific mechanism
- Break condition: When D ≤ K, the gradient signal is no longer bottlenecked, and the backbone learns representations overly specialized to the pretext task.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Larger backbone representations become naturally sparse and binarizable without performance loss.
- Mechanism: When D >> K, the nonlinear projector forces the backbone to use only a subset of its dimensions to represent each input. This creates sparse activations where many dimensions are zero. The sparsity increases with larger D, making representations more easily binarizable while preserving information.
- Core assumption: The nonlinear projector creates a natural dimensionality reduction that favors sparse representations when the backbone is wider than the projector.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1]: "We observe a very sparse backbone's output when D is larger than K with 80% of the examples that have half of the backbone representation's activation equal to zero"
  - [section 4.3]: "when using a wider representation, the performances between the binarized and continuous representation are extremely close"
  - [corpus]: Weak - related papers don't directly address sparsity and binarization mechanisms
- Break condition: When the projector dimension approaches the backbone dimension, sparsity decreases and binarization becomes lossy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Larger backbone representations improve linear separability of features, making them more effective for transfer learning.
- Mechanism: When D >> K, the backbone learns to spread information across many dimensions rather than compressing it. This creates more linearly separable features that require less complex downstream classifiers to achieve good performance on transfer tasks.
- Core assumption: Linear separability of features is crucial for effective transfer learning, and wider representations naturally achieve better linear separability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2]: "the gap is large and becomes much smaller with wider representations" when comparing linear vs nonlinear probe performance
  - [section 3.2]: "increasing the size of the representation yields improved accuracy on ImageNet" for transfer tasks
  - [corpus]: Weak - related papers don't directly address linear separability in this context
- Break condition: When the downstream task requires highly nonlinear decision boundaries, the benefit of linear separability diminishes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Backbone (ResNet50 with configurable last block dimension 512-32768) -> Projector (MLP with configurable first layer dimension 32-8192, second layer fixed at 256) -> Loss (SimCLR, VICReg, or BYOL) -> Downstream (Linear probe or nonlinear probe 3-layer MLP)

- Critical path:
  1. Pretrain model with chosen backbone dimension and projector configuration
  2. Freeze backbone, train linear/nonlinear probe on downstream task
  3. Evaluate performance and compare across different backbone dimensions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger backbones: Better transfer performance, sparse representations, easier binarization, but more parameters
  - Smaller projectors: Stronger information bottleneck, better transfer performance, but may limit representation capacity
  - Linear vs nonlinear probes: Linear probes test linear separability, nonlinear probes test overall representation quality

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor transfer performance with small backbones: Indicates pretraining bias is too strong
  - Lossy binarization with small backbones: Indicates insufficient sparsity
  - Large gap between linear and nonlinear probe performance: Indicates poor linear separability

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train SimCLR with D=512, K=8192 and D=32768, K=8192 on ImageNet, compare transfer performance to CIFAR10
  2. Train with linear projector (K=32) vs nonlinear projector, compare ImageNet validation accuracy
  3. Binarize representations from D=2048 vs D=32768 models, compare transfer performance to downstream tasks

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1**: How does the performance of self-supervised learning models change when using backbone architectures specifically designed for self-supervised learning, as opposed to adapting supervised learning architectures?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that current SSL methods often use backbone architectures designed for supervised learning and highlights the potential benefits of exploring SSL-specific architectures with wider representations.
  - Why unresolved: The paper focuses on modifying existing ResNet architectures by changing the dimensionality of the last convolutional block, but does not explore architectures specifically designed for SSL.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of SSL models using backbones designed for SSL versus those adapted from supervised learning, on a variety of downstream tasks.

- **Open Question 2**: What is the impact of using larger backbone representations on the robustness of self-supervised learning models to out-of-distribution data?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the potential benefits of larger backbone representations for transfer learning and provides some qualitative evidence using a representation conditional diffusion model, but does not extensively study the impact on out-of-distribution robustness.
  - Why unresolved: While the paper suggests that larger representations may retain more information about the input, it does not quantify the impact on robustness to out-of-distribution data.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating the performance of SSL models with varying backbone dimensions on out-of-distribution datasets, and comparing their robustness to models with smaller backbones.

- **Open Question 3**: How does the sparsity of wider backbone representations in self-supervised learning models affect their performance and interpretability?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates that wider SSL representations are more sparse and binarizable, but does not explore the implications of this sparsity on model performance or interpretability.
  - Why unresolved: The paper shows that sparsity increases with backbone width, but does not investigate how this affects the model's ability to learn and generalize, or how it impacts the interpretability of the learned representations.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Studies analyzing the relationship between sparsity, model performance, and interpretability in SSL models with varying backbone dimensions, and exploring techniques to leverage sparsity for improved performance or interpretability.

## Limitations

- The theoretical justification for why larger representations work better (the information bottleneck argument) remains somewhat hand-wavy and lacks rigorous mathematical proof
- The sparsity observations, while interesting, are presented as a consequence rather than a mechanism driving the improved performance
- The paper relies heavily on controlled experimental settings and may not fully capture real-world transfer learning scenarios

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The empirical observation that larger backbone dimensions improve transfer learning performance is well-supported by extensive experiments across multiple datasets and pretraining methods.
- **Medium Confidence**: The mechanism explaining this through information bottleneck effects is plausible but not definitively proven. The relationship between projector dimension and backbone dimension requires more theoretical analysis.
- **Low Confidence**: The sparsity and binarization benefits are observed but their direct connection to improved transfer performance is not rigorously established.

## Next Checks

1. **Theoretical Analysis**: Develop a more rigorous mathematical framework for understanding the information bottleneck created by the projector-backbone dimension ratio, including quantitative predictions about optimal D/K ratios.

2. **Alternative Pretraining Tasks**: Test whether the findings generalize to supervised pretraining with cross-entropy loss, where the relationship between representation size and task performance is typically different.

3. **Ablation on Projector Architecture**: Systematically vary both the projector's first and second layer dimensions to determine if the benefits come from the bottleneck effect specifically or from the overall representation capacity.
