# Cooperation or Competition: Avoiding Player Domination for Multi-Target Robustness via Adaptive Budgets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.15482
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15482
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Adaptive budget adjustment prevents player domination in multi-target adversarial training, improving robust accuracy across ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ attacks

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of training models robust to multiple adversarial threat models simultaneously by modeling multi-target adversarial training as a bargaining game. The authors identify "player domination" as a key issue where one adversary consistently controls parameter updates in MAX and MSD methods, leading to non-convergence. They propose AdaptiveBudget, which dynamically adjusts attack budgets based on gradient magnitudes to prevent any single adversary from dominating. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 demonstrate that AdaptiveBudget consistently improves overall robust accuracy while reducing ℓ∞ robustness gaps.

## Method Summary
The method implements multi-target adversarial training using three baseline approaches (MAX, AVG, MSD) with PGD attacks across ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ threat models. The proposed AdaptiveBudget algorithm monitors gradient magnitudes for each adversary and adjusts their attack budgets dynamically - increasing the budget for the adversary with the largest gradient magnitude and decreasing others. This prevents player domination by forcing the model to focus on the "hardest" task in each training round. The approach generalizes across different norms for budget adjustment and is evaluated on standard image classification benchmarks.

## Key Results
- AdaptiveBudget improves overall robust accuracy against ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ PGD attacks across all three methods (MAX, MSD, AVG)
- Most significant improvements observed in ℓ∞ robustness, with AVG-AB showing a 9.7% improvement on CIFAR-10
- AdaptiveBudget reduces non-convergence in MAX and MSD methods by preventing player domination
- The method generalizes well across different norms (ℓ1/ℓ2) for budget adjustment with minimal performance differences

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multi-target adversarial training can be modeled as a cooperative bargaining game where players (adversaries) negotiate gradient updates.
- Mechanism: The training process involves multiple adversaries generating perturbations that compete to maximize loss, with their gradients aggregated to update model parameters. This aggregation process can be viewed as negotiating a "joint direction" for parameter updates.
- Core assumption: The multi-target adversarial training framework naturally maps to a bargaining game where each adversary acts as a player with utility derived from model robustness against their specific attack type.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we view this problem as a bargaining game in which different players (adversaries) negotiate to reach an agreement on a joint direction of parameter updating"
  - [section 3.2] "The multi-target adversarial training can be viewed as a cooperative game in which each target (perturbation) represents a player, whose utility is derived from the overall robust accuracy"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct evidence in related papers about bargaining game formulations
- Break condition: If the bargaining game model doesn't accurately represent the multi-target training dynamics, or if adversaries have conflicting objectives that cannot be reconciled through negotiation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: "Player domination" phenomenon causes non-convergence in MAX and MSD algorithms when one adversary consistently controls parameter updates.
- Mechanism: When one adversary's gradient magnitude consistently dominates others, the model updates only follow that adversary's direction, preventing balanced learning across all attack types. This creates a feedback loop where the model becomes specialized for one attack type while ignoring others.
- Core assumption: Under certain conditions (SVM and linear models), one adversary can maintain gradient dominance throughout training, leading to non-convergence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "identify a phenomenon named player domination in the bargaining game, namely that the existing max-based approaches, such as MAX and MSD, do not converge"
  - [section 4.2] "Theorem 4. Consider using MAX and MSD to train the linear model described above. If one player dominates the bargaining game throughout the game... then the loss of all players and the overall loss would increase as time t grows"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct evidence in related papers about player domination phenomenon
- Break condition: If gradient magnitudes become balanced across adversaries, or if the model architecture prevents any single adversary from maintaining gradient dominance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Adaptive budget adjustment prevents player domination by dynamically allocating attack budgets based on gradient magnitudes.
- Mechanism: The AdaptiveBudget algorithm increases the budget for the adversary with the largest gradient magnitude and decreases budgets for others, forcing the model to focus on the "hardest" task in each training round. This prevents any single adversary from maintaining consistent dominance.
- Core assumption: Adversaries with larger gradient magnitudes indicate tasks that the model is currently struggling with, so allocating more budget to these tasks will improve overall robustness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we design a novel framework that adjusts the budgets of different adversaries to avoid any player dominance"
  - [section 5] "the core idea of this algorithm is to avoid player domination by adaptively assigning proper attack budgets to different adversaries"
  - [section 6.2] "Our proposed AdaptiveBudget improves the overall robust accuracy against ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ PGD attacks, as well as the ℓ∞ robust accuracy for all three methods"
- Break condition: If gradient magnitude doesn't correlate with task difficulty, or if the adaptive budget adjustments create instability in training dynamics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the player domination phenomenon generalize to other multi-target adversarial training approaches beyond MAX and MSD, such as ensemble methods or gradient penalty-based techniques?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on MAX and MSD but acknowledges these are greedy algorithms that take steepest gradients; the theoretical analysis suggests player domination leads to non-convergence in these methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore whether player domination affects other multi-target adversarial training methods or how different algorithmic structures might mitigate this issue.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing various multi-target adversarial training methods (including ensemble methods, gradient penalty approaches) on both convex and non-convex models to determine if player domination occurs and affects convergence.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the AdaptiveBudget method be extended to handle more than three adversarial threat models simultaneously, and what would be the computational complexity implications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ adversaries but mentions "various methods [26,35,41] have been proposed to address this problem" and discusses the bargaining game framework which could theoretically accommodate more players.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests with three adversaries and doesn't analyze how the method scales with additional threat models or the computational trade-offs involved.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying AdaptiveBudget to scenarios with 4+ threat models (e.g., including ℓ0, structured perturbations) while measuring both performance and computational overhead.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between the degree of player domination and the degradation in robust accuracy, and can this be quantified through a mathematical bound?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proves that player domination leads to non-convergence in MAX and MSD, and provides empirical evidence that AdaptiveBudget improves performance by reducing player domination, but doesn't establish a quantitative relationship.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows player domination negatively impacts performance, it doesn't provide a formal mathematical relationship between the extent of domination and the resulting accuracy degradation.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical framework that quantifies the relationship between player domination metrics (e.g., gradient magnitude disparity) and robust accuracy, potentially establishing bounds on performance degradation.

## Limitations

- Theoretical analysis is limited to linear and SVM models, with potential generalization issues to deeper networks
- Assumes gradient magnitude correlates with task difficulty, which may not hold for complex, non-linear neural networks
- Budget adjustment mechanism could create training instability if not carefully tuned, particularly with aggressive budget ranges

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The experimental results showing improved robust accuracy across multiple datasets and threat models are well-supported by the data presented. The identification of player domination as a real phenomenon in MAX and MSD methods is convincingly demonstrated.

**Medium confidence**: The theoretical analysis of non-convergence in MAX and MSD methods under player domination is rigorous for the specific model classes considered (linear and SVM), but may not generalize to deeper networks without further validation.

**Low confidence**: The bargaining game framework, while providing an elegant theoretical lens, may not capture all the complexities of multi-target adversarial training dynamics in practice. The assumption that players can "negotiate" through gradient aggregation may oversimplify the competitive nature of the optimization process.
