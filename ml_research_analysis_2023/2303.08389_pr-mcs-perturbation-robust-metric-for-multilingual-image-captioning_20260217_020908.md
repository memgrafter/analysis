# PR-MCS: Perturbation Robust Metric for MultiLingual Image Captioning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08389
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08389
- Reference count: 29
- Key outcome: Introduces PR-MCS, a perturbation-robust multilingual image captioning metric that achieves 50-70% score drops for perturbed captions versus ~3% for baseline metrics

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces PR-MCS, a perturbation-robust multilingual image captioning metric designed to detect lexical perturbations across five languages. The method fine-tunes CLIP's text encoder with three contrastive losses to distinguish original captions from perturbed ones while maintaining semantic alignment with images. The authors also introduce M-FineCapEval, a fine-grained evaluation dataset with critical objects and relationships, enabling more effective assessment of perturbation robustness. Experiments demonstrate PR-MCS significantly outperforms baseline metrics in capturing all types of lexical noise, with average score drops of 50-70% versus ~3% for baselines.

## Method Summary
The method involves pre-training a multilingual text encoder via teacher learning using CLIP embeddings, then fine-tuning with triplet loss combining CLIP loss and three additional perturbation robustness losses. The fine-tuning process optimizes three objectives: maximizing similarity between image and original caption (L1), minimizing similarity between image and perturbed caption (L2), and minimizing similarity between original and perturbed captions (L3). The model is trained on MSCOCO data with five perturbation types (Repetition, Removal, Masking, Jumble, Substitution) applied with 0.4 probability per token. Evaluation is performed on MSCOCO, VizWiz, Flickr8k, and the new M-FineCapEval dataset across five languages.

## Key Results
- PR-MCS achieves 50-70% average score drops for perturbed captions versus ~3% for baseline metrics
- Demonstrates strong perturbation robustness across five languages (English, German, French, Spanish, Japanese)
- Outperforms CLIPScore and MCS baselines in correlation with human judgment using Kendall tau-c and Pearson correlation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning the multilingual text encoder with contrastive losses increases the distance between original and perturbed text embeddings, making perturbations detectable.
- Mechanism: The model learns to minimize the similarity between original caption embeddings and perturbed caption embeddings (L3) while maintaining similarity with image embeddings for originals (L1) and reducing similarity for perturbations (L2).
- Core assumption: The fine-tuned text encoder can learn perturbation patterns that CLIP's original text encoder fails to capture.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract]: "To achieve perturbation robustness, we fine-tune the text encoder of CLIP with our language-agnostic method to distinguish the perturbed text from the original text." [section 3.3]: "Equation (3) reduces the similarity between the original and perturbed caption embeddings. These three losses are combined to obtain the final objective function as follows..."

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using a fine-grained evaluation dataset with critical objects enables more effective perturbation detection than standard image captioning datasets.
- Mechanism: By annotating critical objects and relationships, perturbations can target semantically important elements rather than just token positions, creating more meaningful noise that affects semantic content.
- Core assumption: The metric's performance depends on its ability to capture semantic correspondence, which is better tested when perturbations affect critical semantic elements.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract]: "We introduce a new fine-grained evaluation dataset consisting of detailed captions, critical objects, and the relationships between the objects for 3,000 images in five languages." [section 4]: "In addition, we had human experts point to critical objects, backgrounds, and relationships to create perturbed captions effectively."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The multilingual approach with teacher learning enables perturbation robustness across languages without requiring language-specific training data.
- Mechanism: The teacher learning pre-training stage transfers English CLIP knowledge to multilingual text encoders, then fine-tuning with perturbations adapts this across languages simultaneously.
- Core assumption: Perturbation patterns are language-agnostic enough that a model trained on English perturbations can generalize to detect perturbations in other languages.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract]: "PR-MCS, which exhibits robustness to such perturbations, as a novel reference-free image captioning metric applicable to multiple languages." [section 3.1]: "Similarly, in this study, we pre-train the multilingual text encoder with teacher learning... We present a new multilingual image captioning metric, MCS, using this model as a backbone."

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Contrastive learning with triplet loss
  - Why needed here: The method relies on learning to distinguish between similar (image-original caption) and dissimilar (image-perturbed caption) pairs
  - Quick check question: How does contrastive loss encourage the model to separate positive and negative pairs in embedding space?

- Concept: Teacher-student knowledge distillation
  - Why needed here: The multilingual text encoder is pre-trained using CLIP's text encoder as a teacher to transfer knowledge before fine-tuning
  - Quick check question: What's the difference between standard fine-tuning and teacher learning when adapting a pre-trained model?

- Concept: Embedding space similarity metrics
  - Why needed here: The metric is based on cosine similarity between image and text embeddings, which must be sensitive to semantic changes
  - Quick check question: Why might cosine similarity fail to detect certain types of lexical perturbations in raw embeddings?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Image and caption triplets -> CLIP visual encoder (frozen) -> Multilingual text encoder (fine-tuned) -> Cosine similarity scores

- Critical path:
  1. Pre-train multilingual text encoder via teacher learning
  2. Fine-tune with contrastive and perturbation losses
  3. Evaluate using cosine similarity between image and caption embeddings

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using pre-trained CLIP visual encoder limits flexibility but provides strong visual grounding
  - Fine-tuning only the text encoder reduces computational cost but may miss potential improvements from joint visual-text training
  - The three additional losses increase training complexity but are necessary for perturbation robustness

- Failure signatures:
  - Score doesn't drop significantly for perturbed captions (loss function weights too low)
  - Model overfits to training perturbations and fails on new types (need data augmentation)
  - Multilingual performance degrades significantly (language-specific fine-tuning may be needed)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test on MSCOCO with single perturbation type (repetition) to verify basic functionality
  2. Test with multiple perturbation types to check generalization
  3. Test on M-FineCapEval to validate performance on fine-grained captions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the perturbation robustness of PR-MCS generalize to languages beyond the five tested (English, German, French, Spanish, Japanese)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates robustness across five languages but does not test additional languages.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments are limited to these five languages, and there is no theoretical guarantee that the fine-tuning approach will work equally well for all languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing PR-MCS on additional languages (e.g., Chinese, Arabic, Hindi) and comparing its performance to baseline metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of PR-MCS compare to other perturbation-robust metrics specifically designed for image captioning, such as those based on scene graphs or object detection?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on comparing PR-MCS to CLIPScore and MCS baselines but does not compare it to other perturbation-robust image captioning metrics.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not include experiments with other state-of-the-art metrics that explicitly address perturbation robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarking PR-MCS against other perturbation-robust image captioning metrics on the same datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of the choice of perturbation criteria on the performance of PR-MCS? Are there other perturbation types that could be more challenging or revealing?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses five perturbation criteria (Repetition, Removal, Masking, Jumble, Substitution) but acknowledges that these are a subset of possible perturbations.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the full space of potential perturbations or analyze the sensitivity of PR-MCS to different perturbation types.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematically testing PR-MCS with a wider range of perturbation types and analyzing its performance across different perturbation categories.

## Limitations
- Limited generalization across perturbation types beyond the five tested
- Language coverage constrained to five languages without validation on additional languages
- Dataset bias from English-centric sources may affect cross-cultural performance

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism of fine-tuning CLIP's text encoder with contrastive losses to detect perturbations is well-supported by experimental results and mathematical formulation
- **Medium Confidence**: Perturbation robustness claims are convincing within tested scenarios, but generalization to real-world conditions needs validation
- **Low Confidence**: Relationship between perturbation robustness metric and actual caption quality, as well as performance on languages beyond five tested, remain uncertain

## Next Checks
1. Test PR-MCS on languages not included in the training data (e.g., Arabic, Hindi, or Chinese) to evaluate zero-shot perturbation robustness across diverse language families
2. Evaluate PR-MCS on captions generated by state-of-the-art multilingual image captioning models under real-world conditions, comparing performance to traditional metrics like CIDEr and SPICE
3. Conduct an ablation study systematically removing each of the three perturbation robustness losses (L1, L2, L3) to quantify individual contributions and identify potential overfitting
