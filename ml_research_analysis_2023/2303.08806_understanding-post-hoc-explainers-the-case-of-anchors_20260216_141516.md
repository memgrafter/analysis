# Understanding Post-hoc Explainers: The Case of Anchors

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.08806
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08806
- Reference count: 3
- Primary result: Theoretical analysis shows Anchors selects words with highest positive influence on linear classifier predictions

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a theoretical analysis of Anchors, a rule-based interpretability method for text classifiers. Anchors outputs a small set of words that, if present, produce the same prediction with high probability. The authors formalize the Anchors algorithm and analyze its behavior on linear text classifiers using TF-IDF vectorization, proving that it selects words with the highest positive influence on predictions. Experiments on review datasets validate the theoretical findings, showing that Anchors produces meaningful results for linear classifiers and establishing a foundation for developing new explainability methods based on solid theoretical foundations.

## Method Summary
The paper formalizes the Anchors algorithm and introduces an exhaustive version called p-Anchors that approximates the original Anchors optimization. For linear classifiers, Anchors prioritizes words based on their λjvj product (coefficient times TF-IDF value), iteratively adding words with the highest values until the precision condition is met. The exhaustive version considers all possible anchors and selects those with minimal length that meet the precision threshold, then picks the one with maximum precision. The analysis assumes non-normalized TF-IDF vectorization where word importance is determined by frequency in the document and inverse frequency in the corpus.

## Key Results
- Anchors selects words with the highest positive influence on predictions for linear classifiers
- Exhaustive p-Anchors algorithm approximates the original Anchors optimization with theoretical guarantees
- UNK replacement is equivalent to word removal for TF-IDF vectorization when UNK is not in the original corpus
- Experiments validate that Anchors produces meaningful explanations for linear classifiers on review datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Anchors selects words with the highest positive influence on the prediction for linear classifiers
- Mechanism: For linear classifiers, Anchors prioritizes words based on their λjvj product (coefficient times TF-IDF value). The algorithm iteratively adds words with the highest λjvj values until the precision condition is met
- Core assumption: The classifier is linear with non-zero coefficients (λjvj ≠ 0 for all relevant words)
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Anchors outputs a small set of words that, if present, produce the same prediction with high probability"
  - [section] "Anchors will search for an anchor of maximum coverage with prescribed precision"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus neighbors focus on graph neural networks and feature-additive predictors, not linear text classifiers
- Break condition: If the linear classifier has zero coefficients for important words, or if the precision threshold cannot be met with any subset of words

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The exhaustive p-Anchors algorithm approximates the original Anchors optimization
- Mechanism: Instead of using KL-LUCB to find high-precision rules, the exhaustive version considers all possible anchors and selects those with minimal length that meet the precision threshold, then picks the one with maximum precision
- Core assumption: The precision can be accurately approximated using the empirical precision estimator ˆPrecn
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "empirical Anchors is very similar to Anchors, but instead of using an efficient approximate procedure, it considers all possible anchors"
  - [section] "When we use p = ˆPrecn, we refer to this version as empirical Anchors"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus neighbors discuss general XAI methods but not this specific exhaustive approach
- Break condition: If the empirical precision estimator ˆPrecn poorly approximates the true precision, or if the number of possible anchors is too large to enumerate

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: UNK replacement is equivalent to word removal for TF-IDF vectorization
- Mechanism: Since the UNK token is not in the original corpus vocabulary, its IDF value is zero. Therefore, replacing a word with UNK sets its TF-IDF value to zero, equivalent to removing it
- Core assumption: The TF-IDF vectorizer is trained on a fixed corpus where UNK is not present
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "when models are trained on a (non-normalized) TF-IDF vectorization, the exact location of the words in the document does not matter, and thus only the occurrences of each word in an anchor are important"
  - [section] "replacing any word with a fixed token UNK is equivalent to simply removing it, as the token is not present in the initial corpus"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus neighbors don't discuss token replacement or TF-IDF vectorization specifics
- Break condition: If the UNK token appears in the original corpus (giving it non-zero IDF), or if a different vectorization method is used

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Linear classifiers and their decision boundaries
  - Why needed here: The analysis fundamentally relies on understanding how linear classifiers make predictions based on weighted sums of features
  - Quick check question: For a linear classifier f(z) = 1 if λ⊤ϕ(z) + λ0 > 0, what determines which words are most important for a positive prediction?

- Concept: TF-IDF vectorization
  - Why needed here: The paper assumes a non-normalized TF-IDF vectorizer where word importance is determined by frequency in the document and inverse frequency in the corpus
  - Quick check question: In TF-IDF, if a word appears frequently in one document but rarely in the corpus, will its weight be high or low?

- Concept: Statistical approximation (Berry-Esseen theorem)
  - Why needed here: The precision of an anchor is approximated using a normal distribution, and the Berry-Esseen theorem provides error bounds for this approximation
  - Quick check question: What does the Berry-Esseen theorem tell us about the accuracy of approximating a sum of random variables with a normal distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Document → TF-IDF vectorization → Linear classifier prediction → Anchor generation → Precision estimation → Anchor selection
- Critical path: Document → TF-IDF vectorization → Linear classifier prediction → Anchor generation → Precision estimation → Anchor selection
- Design tradeoffs: Exhaustive enumeration provides theoretical guarantees but is computationally expensive; the original Anchors uses approximation methods for scalability
- Failure signatures: Anchors returning irrelevant words, anchors failing to meet precision threshold, or anchors being excessively long (indicating poor optimization)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run Anchors on a simple linear classifier with known coefficients and verify it selects words with highest λjvj values
  2. Compare exhaustive Anchors vs. empirical Anchors on small datasets to verify they produce similar results
  3. Test Anchors behavior when the precision threshold is set very high vs. very low to understand sensitivity

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How do Anchors' explanations behave on non-linear classifiers like neural networks or tree-based models? The paper focuses on linear classifiers and mentions future work extending the analysis to other model classes, leaving the behavior on non-linear models unexplored.

- Can the sampling strategy in Anchors be improved to generate more realistic perturbed examples? The paper notes that replacing words with UNK can create unrealistic sentences that may deceive classifiers, suggesting this as an area for future research.

- How sensitive are Anchors' explanations to the choice of vectorizer (e.g., TF-IDF vs. word embeddings)? The analysis is specifically conducted for TF-IDF vectorization, and the paper suggests investigating other vectorizers.

## Limitations

- Analysis is limited to linear text classifiers with TF-IDF vectorization, which may not generalize to other model architectures or vectorization methods
- Exhaustive p-Anchors algorithm is computationally intractable for real-world applications with many possible anchors
- The equivalence between UNK replacement and word removal critically depends on the UNK token not appearing in the training corpus

## Confidence

- High confidence: The core mechanism of Anchors selecting words with highest positive influence (λjvj) for linear classifiers
- Medium confidence: The equivalence between UNK replacement and word removal
- Low confidence: The computational feasibility of exhaustive p-Anchors for practical applications
