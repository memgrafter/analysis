# An Empirical Study on Google Research Football Multi-agent Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09458
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09458
- Reference count: 40
- The paper scales MARL to 11v11 football, achieving >80% win rate against built-in AI within 2M steps using IPPO with parameter sharing.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of scaling multi-agent reinforcement learning to full-game 11v11 football in Google Research Football. The authors develop a distributed training pipeline using Independent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO) with parameter sharing across homogeneous players, combined with population-based training methods including PSRO and league training. They demonstrate that this approach can train competitive agents from scratch within 2 million steps, and further improve robustness through diverse policy populations with different playing styles.

## Method Summary
The authors use Independent PPO (IPPO) with parameter sharing across all players, combined with a distributed training framework called Light-MALib. They train on the 11v11 full-game scenario with a discount factor of 1.0, and employ population-based training methods including PSRO and league training to create diverse policy populations. The approach leverages Ray-based distributed computing for efficient parallel rollouts and training, with a centralized data server and periodic evaluation manager to track progress.

## Key Results
- Achieved >80% win rate against built-in AI difficulty 1.0 within 2 million training steps
- Demonstrated stable training with discount factor γ=1.0 in long-horizon 3000-step episodes
- Released public benchmark and diverse pretrained policies with different playing styles

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Independent PPO (IPPO) scales to full-game 11v11 football when combined with parameter sharing across homogeneous players.
- Mechanism: Each agent learns its own policy but shares actor and critic network parameters, reducing network complexity from O(n) to O(1).
- Core assumption: All players have identical observation and action spaces, so parameter sharing does not lose important role-specific distinctions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our experiments serve as a reference for the expected performance of Independent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO)"
  - [section 3.3] "Since players are homogeneous where the observation and action space are identical, we set up a pair of policy function (actor) and value function (critic) and share parameters θ, φ across all players."
- Break condition: If the game includes asymmetric roles or different skill levels, parameter sharing will hurt performance because the policy can no longer specialize per role.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: A discount factor of 1.0 stabilizes training in long-horizon 11v11 scenarios, whereas smaller values cause value overestimation and pessimism.
- Mechanism: High discount factor prevents the state-value estimates from exploding over 3000 steps, reducing TD error variance and allowing the actor to maintain optimistic exploration.
- Core assumption: The episode length is fixed at 3000 steps and terminal rewards (goals) are sparse but correctly scaled.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.2] "Our experiments also demonstrate that in 11v11 multi-agent scenarios, a discount factor γ equal to 1 provides more stable training performance"
  - [section 5.2] "Models trained with discount factors of 0.995 and 0.99 enlarge the scale of V and make it pessimistic incrementally throughout training."
- Break condition: If the game is modified to have frequent intermediate rewards, a lower discount factor might perform better by focusing learning on near-term gains.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Population-based training with PSRO and league training creates strategic diversity and improves general robustness beyond beating the built-in AI.
- Mechanism: Multiple training runs with different reward shaping schemes produce a diverse initial population; then opponents are selected via game-theoretic meta-strategies or PFSP to force the main agent to beat a wide variety of playstyles, including exploiters that specifically target weaknesses.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we further improve our agents by population-based self-play... and obtain stronger policies of different playing styles"
  - [section 6.4] "League training is similar to PSRO but has a more complex framework... the opponents are sampled following Prioritize Fictious Self-Play (PFSP)"
- Break condition: If the opponent pool is too homogeneous or dominated by one exploit, the population will collapse into overfitting that single strategy.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Rollout workers -> Data server -> Training workers -> Policy update -> Evaluation manager -> Meta-solver
- Critical path:
  1. Rollout workers execute parallel game steps and collect trajectories.
  2. Trajectories are sent to a centralized data server.
  3. Training workers sample batches, compute PPO loss, and update shared network parameters.
  4. Periodically, evaluation manager runs policy matchups to update payoff tables.
  5. Meta-solver selects next opponent distribution via Nash equilibrium or PFSP.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Parameter sharing vs. per-agent networks: sharing reduces compute but limits role specialization.
  - High discount (γ=1) vs. lower: high discount stabilizes long horizons but may slow credit assignment for intermediate rewards.
  - Built-in AI as opponent vs. self-play only: built-in AI can cause overfitting to its quirks; self-play increases diversity but may slow initial learning.
- Failure signatures:
  - Training stalls: Check if rollout workers are producing sufficient unique trajectories or if batch size is too small.
  - Policy collapses to charging forward: Likely overfitting to built-in AI's offside bug; switch to self-play opponents.
  - High variance in returns: Reduce gamma, increase lambda, or add more samples per update.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train IPPO with encoder_basic and γ=1.0 on 11v11_hard_stochastic; verify win rate vs built-in AI > 80% within 2M steps.
  2. Run PSRO with diverse reward shaping; check that population payoff matrix shows non-transitivity.
  3. Implement league training; confirm that main agent win rate against all population members increases over generations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we achieve stable training in long-horizon multi-agent reinforcement learning tasks with sparse rewards?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss the impact of discount factor γ on training stability, showing that lower values (0.99, 0.995) can lead to pessimistic behavior and instability, while γ = 1 provides more stable performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the problem but doesn't provide a definitive solution for all long-horizon, sparse reward scenarios beyond using γ = 1.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies showing performance across various long-horizon tasks with different discount factors and reward shaping techniques would help identify general principles for stable training.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we effectively prevent overfitting to opponent weaknesses in multi-agent reinforcement learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that policies trained to beat the built-in AI with difficulty 1.0 develop a "charging forward" strategy that exploits a specific weakness in the bot's logic, making them exploitable against stronger opponents.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies this issue and suggests using population-based training with diverse opponents, it doesn't provide a complete solution for preventing overfitting in all cases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different opponent selection strategies and their impact on policy robustness against diverse and unseen opponents would provide insights into effective prevention methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal balance between exploration and exploitation in population-based multi-agent reinforcement learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors use population-based training methods like PSRO and League Training, but don't explore the impact of different exploration strategies on the diversity and strength of the resulting policy population.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on specific population-based training methods but doesn't investigate how varying the exploration-exploitation balance affects the quality and diversity of the learned policies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying exploration parameters (e.g., entropy regularization, opponent selection strategies) and measuring their impact on policy diversity, strength, and exploitability would help identify optimal balances.

## Limitations

- The population-based training methodology relies on unspecified reward shaping parameters that are critical for diversity but not fully detailed in the paper.
- Parameter sharing across homogeneous players may not generalize to games with asymmetric roles or varying player capabilities.
- The approach shows success against built-in AI but may not generalize well to human opponents or more sophisticated AI without additional diversity in training.

## Confidence

- High confidence: IPPO with parameter sharing and γ=1.0 can achieve 80% win rate against built-in AI in 2M steps
- Medium confidence: Population-based methods (PSRO/league training) improve general robustness beyond built-in AI performance
- Medium confidence: Parameter sharing does not lose critical information for homogeneous players in 11v11 football
