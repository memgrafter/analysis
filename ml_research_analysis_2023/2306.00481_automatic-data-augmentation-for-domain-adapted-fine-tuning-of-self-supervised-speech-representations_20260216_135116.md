# Automatic Data Augmentation for Domain Adapted Fine-Tuning of Self-Supervised Speech Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00481
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00481
- Reference count: 0
- This paper proposes a method to reduce domain mismatch problem in self-supervised speech representation learning

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the domain mismatch problem in self-supervised speech representation learning, where models trained on clean data perform poorly when applied to data from different acoustic domains. The proposed solution automatically selects appropriate data augmentations to apply to a clean labeled dataset, bringing it closer to the target domain's acoustics, and then uses this augmented data for fine-tuning the self-supervised model. The augmentation selection is done by minimizing a conditional-dependence estimator (HSIC) based on the target dataset, avoiding the need for labeled data in the target domain. The method is validated in an oracle experiment with controlled distortions and on two real-world low-resource domains, showing significant relative WER improvements of 12.4% and 19.5% compared to baselines without augmentations.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two main components: automatic augmentation selection and two-stage fine-tuning. For augmentation selection, the approach computes HSIC scores for random augmentation distributions using the unlabeled target dataset, selecting the distribution with the lowest conditional dependence score. This identifies augmentations that make the neutral data acoustically similar to the target domain. The fine-tuning process involves first training on the augmented neutral dataset to expose the model to target-like acoustics while maintaining textual diversity, followed by fine-tuning on the small target domain dataset to adapt to its specific characteristics. The entire pipeline is evaluated using Wav2Vec2.0 as the SSL backbone and CTC decoder for ASR.

## Key Results
- Automatic augmentation selection achieves relative WER improvements of 12.4% and 19.5% compared to baselines without augmentations
- The method shows strong correlation (Spearman 0.51) between HSIC scores and actual performance improvements
- Two-stage fine-tuning (augmented neutral dataset → target dataset) outperforms direct fine-tuning on target data alone

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Automatic augmentation selection reduces domain mismatch by making neutral data more similar to target domain acoustics.
- Mechanism: The method selects augmentations that minimize conditional dependence between augmented views and original sample IDs, conditioned on labels. This selects distortions already present in the target domain, making neutral data acoustically closer.
- Core assumption: Acoustic domain mismatch is the primary cause of SSL model performance degradation, and augmentations can effectively bridge this gap.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Applying properly calibrated data augmentations on a large clean dataset, bringing it closer to the target domain"
  - [section 2.1] "applying augmentations already present in samples in the dataset makes it harder to distinguish their original samples' IDs given the distorted samples and, thus, lowers the conditional dependence estimator"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations about augmentation effectiveness for domain adaptation in related papers
- Break condition: If the target domain's acoustic characteristics cannot be replicated by the available augmentations (e.g., very different recording equipment, extreme noise conditions).

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Conditional independence estimation via HSIC selects augmentations that improve downstream ASR performance.
- Mechanism: HSIC measures dependence between augmented samples and original IDs given labels. Lower HSIC indicates augmentations that preserve class information while making samples harder to distinguish by ID, suggesting acoustic similarity to target.
- Core assumption: The HSIC score correlates with downstream ASR performance on the target domain.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "we resort to minimizing the HSIC quantity with the augmented dataset X' = f(X, τ)"
  - [section 3.2] "we observe a Spearman correlation score of 0.51 between the HSIC scores and the distances between vectors of probabilities"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct citations about HSIC for augmentation selection in speech domain adaptation
- Break condition: If the relationship between HSIC scores and actual ASR performance is not consistent across different domains or datasets.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-stage fine-tuning (augmented neutral dataset, then target dataset) improves performance over direct fine-tuning on small target dataset.
- Mechanism: First fine-tuning on augmented neutral data provides better initialization for the SSL model by exposing it to target-like acoustics while maintaining textual diversity. Second fine-tuning adapts to target domain specifics.
- Core assumption: Larger, cleaner datasets with appropriate augmentations provide better representations than small, noisy target datasets alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "using it as part of an initial fine-tuning stage" and "a second fine-tuning on the small target domain dataset"
  - [section 3.3] "The relative improvement compared to the augmentation-free baseline reaches 12.4% for Contributor 1 and 19.5% for Contributor 2"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - related papers discuss continued pre-training and fine-tuning strategies for low-resource ASR
- Break condition: If the neutral dataset is too dissimilar from the target domain, even with augmentations, or if the target dataset is large enough to train effectively on its own.

  - Why needed here: Forms the theoretical basis for selecting augmentations that match target domain acoustics

  - Why needed here: Provides a kernel-based method to estimate conditional dependence between augmented samples and their origins
  - Quick check question: What properties of HSIC make it suitable for comparing distributions of augmented speech samples?

  - Why needed here: Understanding how SSL models can be adapted to new domains through fine-tuning and augmentation
  - Quick check question: Why do SSL models typically underperform when there's an acoustic mismatch between pretraining and target domains?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Wav2Vec2.0 Large model (pre-trained SSL backbone) -> CTC decoder (downstream ASR component) -> HSIC-based augmentation selector (domain adaptation module) -> Torch-Audiomentations library (augmentation application) -> SpeechBrain framework (overall training and evaluation)

- Critical path:
  1. Compute HSIC scores for random augmentation distributions using target dataset
  2. Select augmentation distribution with lowest HSIC score
  3. Apply selected augmentations to neutral dataset
  4. Fine-tune SSL model on augmented neutral dataset
  5. Further fine-tune on target dataset
  6. Evaluate on target test set

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computation vs. performance: Random search for augmentation selection is less computationally intensive than exhaustive search but may miss optimal configurations
  - Augmentation diversity vs. target similarity: More diverse augmentations might capture a wider range of target characteristics but risk introducing irrelevant distortions
  - Neutral dataset size vs. augmentation intensity: Larger neutral datasets can better withstand aggressive augmentations without losing textual diversity

- Failure signatures:
  - HSIC scores not correlating with actual performance improvements
  - Augmentation selection consistently choosing unlikely or ineffective distortions
  - Performance degradation when applying augmentations, indicating they're moving away from target domain characteristics
  - High variance in results across different runs with the same configuration

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement HSIC-based augmentation selection and verify it selects different augmentations for different target domains
  2. Compare WER on oracle experiment (known distortions) with and without augmentation selection
  3. Test the full pipeline on a small, controlled domain mismatch scenario before scaling to real-world datasets

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the method perform when applied to target datasets with significant textual diversity differences from the neutral dataset (e.g., spontaneous vs. read speech)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "neutral and target datasets should not exhibit different textual settings" as augmentations only address acoustic distortions.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not test or discuss performance when there are significant textual diversity differences between the neutral and target datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the method's performance on target datasets with varying degrees of textual diversity mismatch relative to the neutral dataset.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of the number of views (N) generated per speech sample on the method's effectiveness and computational cost?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "we can generate N augmented versions per speech sample" and uses N = 20 in experiments, but does not explore the impact of varying this parameter.
- Why unresolved: The optimal number of views is not investigated, and the trade-off between performance improvement and computational cost is not discussed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying N and analyzing the corresponding changes in performance and computational requirements.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the method scale to very large target datasets, and what is the computational bottleneck in such scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "scoring 200 augmentation policies takes 3 hours on 10 CPUs" but does not discuss scaling to larger datasets or identify computational bottlenecks.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide analysis on how the method's performance and computational requirements change with dataset size.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with increasingly large target datasets and analysis of computational time and memory usage at each scale.

## Limitations
- The method relies on the assumption that acoustic domain mismatch is the primary cause of performance degradation, though other factors like vocabulary mismatch could also contribute
- HSIC-based augmentation selection shows only moderate correlation (Spearman 0.51) with actual performance improvements, suggesting the relationship may not be as direct as claimed
- Evaluation is limited to two real-world domains with relatively small datasets (7.8 hours and 9.2 hours), making generalizability difficult to assess

## Confidence
- High confidence: Core observation that domain mismatch affects SSL model performance and that augmentations can help bridge this gap, supported by consistent improvements in both oracle and real-world experiments
- Medium confidence: Specific HSIC-based augmentation selection mechanism, with moderate correlation between HSIC scores and actual performance suggesting effectiveness may depend on domain relationship
- Low confidence: Generalizability of results to significantly different domains or larger datasets, given limited evaluation scope and potential for other factors beyond acoustic characteristics to affect performance

## Next Checks
1. **Correlation Validation**: Systematically measure the relationship between HSIC scores and actual WER improvements across a wider range of domains and augmentation configurations, including controlled experiments with known domain characteristics.

2. **Ablation on Sample Size**: Test the sensitivity of the augmentation selection process to the number of target domain samples used (currently 10), evaluating performance with 5, 20, 50, and 100 samples to determine scaling effectiveness.

3. **Domain Diversity Test**: Apply the method to a significantly different domain pair (e.g., clean audiobook speech to telephone conversation speech or medical dictation) to assess generalizability beyond the relatively similar domains tested.
