# Solving and Generating NPR Sunday Puzzles with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.12255
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12255
- Reference count: 5
- Key outcome: GPT-3.5 achieves 50.2% loose accuracy on NPR Sunday Puzzles

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the capability of large language models to solve and generate puzzles from NPR's Sunday Puzzle game show using the PUZZLEQA dataset of 558 puzzles spanning 15 years. The authors evaluate four LLMs across multiple choice and free response formats, finding that state-of-the-art models can solve approximately half of the puzzles. However, the puzzle generation task proves significantly more challenging, with models failing to generate puzzles that conform to their own stated rules. The study introduces two prompt engineering techniques - chain-of-thought reasoning and prompt summarization - and identifies a substantial performance gap between proprietary and public language models.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates four large language models (GPT-J, LLaMA, GPT-3, GPT-3.5) on PUZZLEQA using a few-shot learning paradigm with 2 examples per puzzle type. Models are tested in both multiple choice and free response formats, with evaluation using exact and loose matching metrics. The authors explore two prompt engineering techniques: chain-of-thought reasoning (where models explain their reasoning before answering) and prompt summarization. For puzzle generation, models are given a puzzle type and asked to generate puzzles following the rules, with generated puzzles evaluated for consistency (can the model solve its own puzzle?) and conformity (does the answer follow the stated rules?).

## Key Results
- GPT-3.5 achieves 50.2% loose accuracy on free response puzzles, significantly outperforming other models
- Chain-of-thought prompting does not improve accuracy but provides valuable explainability insights
- Puzzle generation remains challenging - models can solve their own puzzles but fail to generate conforming puzzles
- Large performance gap exists between proprietary models (GPT-3.5) and public models (LLaMA)

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: PUZZLEQA dataset -> Few-shot examples -> LLMs (GPT-J, LLaMA, GPT-3, GPT-3.5) -> Prompt engineering (CoT, summarization) -> Evaluation (MC vs FR, exact vs loose matching)

- Critical path: 1) Preprocess PUZZLEQA dataset, 2) Generate few-shot examples for each puzzle type, 3) Run experiments with each model/technique combination, 4) Evaluate results using exact and loose matching metrics

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Multiple choice vs free response: Multiple choice is easier but less realistic; free response better tests true understanding
  - Exact vs loose matching: Exact is more precise but may miss valid answers; loose is more forgiving but less discriminative
  - Chain-of-thought vs baseline: Chain-of-thought provides explainability but may reduce performance

- Failure signatures:
  - Low performance on free response tasks: May indicate the model is memorizing patterns rather than understanding puzzle logic
  - High consistency but low conformity in generation: Model can solve its own puzzles but generates trivial or invalid puzzles
  - No improvement from prompt engineering: May indicate the model lacks the underlying capability to benefit from these techniques

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run baseline GPT-3.5 on multiple choice PUZZLEQA to establish upper bound
  2. Test LLaMA on free response PUZZLEQA to compare public vs proprietary performance
  3. Apply chain-of-thought prompting to GPT-3.5 on free response PUZZLEQA to test explainability impact

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does training data overlap with the PUZZLEQA dataset affect model performance on this task?
- Basis in paper: Explicit
- Why unresolved: The authors note that training data for GPT-3, GPT-3.5, and LLaMA is not publicly available, making it impossible to measure whether models have been trained on problems within their dataset. They attempted to investigate this by testing on 2023 puzzles (more recent than training cutoffs) and found comparable performance, but this doesn't conclusively rule out overlap.
- What evidence would resolve it: Public disclosure of training data sources or detailed analysis of model outputs showing evidence of memorization vs. generalization.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can fine-tuning LLMs on PUZZLEQA improve puzzle generation capabilities?
- Basis in paper: Explicit
- Why unresolved: The authors only tested few-shot learning approaches for puzzle generation and found models could solve but not generate valid puzzles. They suggest fine-tuning as future work but didn't implement it.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing few-shot learning vs. fine-tuning on PUZZLEQA for puzzle generation, measuring both consistency and conformity of generated puzzles.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What specific features of chain-of-thought prompting explain its inconsistent performance across different prompt formulations?
- Basis in paper: Explicit
- Why unresolved: The authors found chain-of-thought prompting improved performance in small-scale experiments but not on the full dataset, and that slight changes in prompt wording (e.g., "answer and reasoning" vs. "reasoning and answer") dramatically affected results. They don't explain why this occurs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies testing different chain-of-thought prompt structures, measuring how specific wording choices affect performance.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does puzzle difficulty correlate with model performance, and what linguistic features make puzzles harder or easier?
- Basis in paper: Explicit
- Why unresolved: The authors mention that future work could incorporate difficulty ratings by checking whether human players succeeded, but they didn't conduct this analysis. They also note that puzzle types and topics may vary over time without analyzing this variation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis correlating model accuracy with puzzle characteristics (word length, type of wordplay, domain knowledge required, etc.) and comparison with human success rates.

## Limitations

- Large performance gap between proprietary and public models may reflect data access advantages rather than pure capability differences
- Puzzle generation remains significantly more challenging than puzzle solving, with models unable to generate valid conforming puzzles
- Few-shot learning with only 2 examples per puzzle type may not capture the full complexity of some puzzle types
- Manual preprocessing required to extract answers from mailing list data introduces potential reproducibility issues

## Confidence

- High Confidence: GPT-3.5 achieves 50.2% loose accuracy on PUZZLEQA
- Medium Confidence: Proprietary models significantly outperform public models due to capability differences
- Low Confidence: Chain-of-thought prompting provides valuable explainability insights
