# Towards Collaborative Plan Acquisition through Theory of Mind Modeling in Situated Dialogue

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11271
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11271
- Reference count: 17
- Key outcome: Explicit modeling of partner's dialogue moves and mental states improves prediction stability and performance in collaborative plan acquisition

## Executive Summary
This paper explores collaborative plan acquisition through situated dialogue in a 3D blocks world domain. The authors develop a benchmark with dialogue move annotations and propose a multi-stage model that predicts dialogue moves, mental states, and missing knowledge. The approach demonstrates that modeling the partner's mental states and dialogue moves leads to more stable and accurate predictions of missing knowledge, particularly for the partner's perspective. The work provides insights for future AI agents to proactively communicate missing knowledge to facilitate shared understanding in collaborative tasks.

## Method Summary
The approach uses a multi-stage model architecture that processes dialogue utterances (via BERT), video frames (via CNN), and partial plan information (via GRU) as input modalities. An LSTM sequence model processes these time series inputs. The augmented models incorporate predicted dialogue moves and mental states as latent representations in subsequent stages. The model predicts missing knowledge through feed-forward networks, with three main tasks: predicting own missing knowledge, partner's missing knowledge, and partner's mental states (task status, task knowledge, and task intention).

## Key Results
- Explicit modeling of partner's dialogue moves and mental states produces improved and more stable results compared to baseline approaches
- Predicting partner's missing knowledge is more feasible than predicting one's own missing knowledge, with F1 scores consistently above 70%
- Incorporating dialogue moves and task intention leads to highest performance in predicting partner's missing knowledge

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Modeling the partner's dialogue moves and mental states improves prediction stability and performance in collaborative plan acquisition.
- Mechanism: By explicitly encoding the partner's communicative intentions and mental states as latent representations and augmenting the model's input, the agent gains richer context for predicting missing knowledge. This reduces uncertainty and prevents frequent prediction changes during the interaction.
- Core assumption: The partner's dialogue moves and mental states contain predictive signal about their missing knowledge that is not captured by perceptual and plan information alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We show that explicit modeling of the partner's dialogue moves and mental states produces improved and more stable results than without."
  - [section]: "We observe a significant increase in performance when using dialogue moves...Furthermore, for the task of predicting the partner's task knowledge, we augmented model approaches the average human performance."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Related papers mention ToM modeling but lack direct empirical support for stability gains in plan acquisition tasks.
- Break condition: If the partner's dialogue moves and mental states become uninformative (e.g., due to noise or lack of grounding to the task), the augmented model may not outperform the baseline.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Predicting the partner's missing knowledge is more feasible than predicting one's own missing knowledge in collaborative plan acquisition.
- Mechanism: The partner's missing knowledge can be inferred from the agent's own partial plan, as it represents what the partner lacks relative to the agent's knowledge. In contrast, predicting one's own missing knowledge requires inferring what is absent from the complete plan, which is more ambiguous and sparse.
- Core assumption: The agent's own partial plan provides a reliable reference for inferring the partner's missing knowledge, while self-knowledge prediction lacks a similar reference.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our empirical results suggest that predicting the partner's missing knowledge is a more viable approach than predicting one's own."
  - [section]: "Table 3 shows the performance which is across the board more than 70% F1. This means that this task is more approachable compared to inferring one's own missing knowledge."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. The corpus does not directly address the feasibility comparison between predicting own and partner's missing knowledge.
- Break condition: If the agent's partial plan becomes highly incomplete or diverges significantly from the partner's knowledge, inferring the partner's missing knowledge may become as challenging as self-prediction.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Incorporating dialogue moves and task intention in the model leads to the highest performance in predicting the partner's missing knowledge.
- Mechanism: Dialogue moves provide explicit grounding of the conversation to the partial plan graph, while task intention captures the partner's current sub-goal. Together, they offer a more precise understanding of the partner's needs and guide the prediction of their missing knowledge.
- Core assumption: The partner's current task intention and dialogue moves are strongly correlated with their missing knowledge, and incorporating them enhances the model's predictive power.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We found in general that incorporating dialogue moves and task intention has the highest performance. This finding confirms the importance of intention prediction in plan coordination, from both the communication level and task level."
  - [abstract]: "We show that explicit modeling of the partner's dialogue moves and mental states produces improved and more stable results than without."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Related papers discuss ToM and dialogue modeling but do not provide direct empirical support for the specific combination of dialogue moves and task intention.
- Break condition: If the dialogue moves and task intention become unreliable or irrelevant (e.g., due to noise or misalignment with the task), their incorporation may not improve or may even degrade performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input modalities (Dialogue via BERT, Video via CNN, Plan via GRU) -> Sequence model (LSTM) -> Augmented models (Multi-stage) -> Output layers (Feed-forward networks)

- Critical path:
  1. Process input modalities (dialogue, visual, plan)
  2. Predict dialogue moves (Stage 1)
  3. Predict mental states (Stage 2)
  4. Predict missing knowledge (Stage 3)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a multi-stage approach allows for explicit modeling of dialogue moves and mental states, but increases model complexity and training time.
  - Freezing the pre-trained models in later stages ensures stable latent representations but may limit adaptability to new data.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low performance on predicting own missing knowledge: May indicate the need for better self-knowledge inference mechanisms or a different representation of the plan space.
  - Unstable predictions over time: May suggest the need for more robust latent representations or a different sequence modeling approach.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare the baseline model with the augmented model incorporating dialogue moves to assess the impact on predicting the partner's missing knowledge.
  2. Evaluate the performance of the augmented model on predicting the partner's mental states (task status, task knowledge, and task intention) to assess the quality of the latent representations.
  3. Analyze the stability of the augmented model's predictions over time by measuring the number of mind changes and accumulated absolute confidence changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper raises questions about extending this approach to more complex domains beyond the 3D blocks world, handling uncertainty in real-world collaborative scenarios, and scaling the approach to larger, more diverse datasets. The authors also question how to effectively incorporate additional modalities and how to handle cases where partners have significantly different levels of expertise or knowledge.

## Limitations

- The dataset is relatively small with only 12 task scenarios and 240 dialogues, which may limit generalizability
- The 3D blocks world domain is highly structured and may not capture the full complexity of real-world collaborative planning scenarios
- The comparison between predicting own versus partner knowledge may be influenced by the task structure rather than representing a fundamental limitation of self-knowledge prediction

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The effectiveness of multi-stage models incorporating dialogue moves and mental states (Mechanism 1) is well-supported by the empirical results, showing both improved performance and stability metrics.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that partner knowledge prediction is more feasible than self-knowledge prediction (Mechanism 2) is supported by the data but may be influenced by the task structure rather than representing a fundamental limitation of self-knowledge prediction.
- **Medium Confidence**: The specific combination of dialogue moves and task intention for optimal performance (Mechanism 3) is demonstrated but requires further validation across different task domains.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-domain validation**: Test the approach on more complex collaborative planning tasks beyond the 3D blocks world, such as multi-step assembly tasks or navigation planning, to assess generalizability.

2. **Ablation study on mental state components**: Systematically evaluate the contribution of each mental state component (task status, task knowledge, task intention) to identify which aspects are most critical for stable and accurate predictions.

3. **Real-time performance evaluation**: Implement the model in a real-time collaborative setting with human partners to assess practical usability, including measures of communication efficiency and task completion success rates.
