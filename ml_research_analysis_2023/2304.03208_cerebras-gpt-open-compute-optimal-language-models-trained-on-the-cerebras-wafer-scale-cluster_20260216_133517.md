# Cerebras-GPT: Open Compute-Optimal Language Models Trained on the Cerebras Wafer-Scale Cluster

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.03208
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03208
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Trains compute-optimal large language models following Chinchilla scaling rules with 20 tokens per parameter

## Executive Summary
This work presents Cerebras-GPT, a family of open compute-optimal large language models trained on the Cerebras Wafer-Scale Cluster. The authors scale models from 111M to 13B parameters on the Pile dataset, achieving state-of-the-art pre-training efficiency compared to other open models. By following DeepMind's Chinchilla scaling rules and using Maximal Update Parameterization (μP), they demonstrate improved training stability, hyperparameter predictability, and downstream task performance. The largest 13B parameter model shows the best performance on most downstream tasks among comparably-sized public models.

## Method Summary
The authors train GPT-3-like transformer decoder models on the Eleuther Pile dataset following Chinchilla scaling rules with 20 tokens per parameter. They use AdamW optimizer with weight decay of 0.1, gradient norm clipping of 1.0, and linear/cosine learning rate decay. The models are trained on the Cerebras Wafer-Scale Cluster using Weight Streaming mode, which eliminates model parallelism complexity. The authors also test Maximal Update Parameterization (μP) to improve training stability and hyperparameter transfer across scales.

## Key Results
- Achieved state-of-the-art pre-training efficiency compared to other open models
- 13B parameter model shows best performance on most downstream tasks among comparably-sized public models
- μP improves accuracy by 0.4% and downstream task accuracy by 1.7% compared to standard parameterization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Scaling dataset size proportionally to model size yields compute-optimal training efficiency. Following Chinchilla scaling rules, increasing dataset size linearly with model parameters ensures each additional parameter receives enough training tokens to reach maximum utility before diminishing returns set in.

### Mechanism 2
Maximal Update Parameterization (μP) improves training stability and enables direct hyperparameter transfer. μP controls initialization, layer-wise learning rates, and activation magnitudes to ensure analytically stable training independent of model width, eliminating the need for per-scale hyperparameter tuning.

### Mechanism 3
Wafer-scale cluster architecture enables linear performance scaling and simplified training. Weight Streaming mode eliminates model parallelism complexity by broadcasting weights to processing cores and streaming gradients back, allowing near-linear scaling across systems.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  CS-2 systems (processing cores with WSE-2 chip) -> Weight servers (parameter storage and optimization) -> Activation workers (data loading and activation management) -> Broadcast+reduce network (communication infrastructure)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Weight servers broadcast model parameters to CS-2 systems
  2. CS-2 systems perform forward and backward passes
  3. Gradients flow back to weight servers via reduce network
  4. Weight servers update parameters using optimizer
  5. Activation workers manage data loading and checkpointing

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Weight Streaming vs. traditional data/model parallelism
  - FP16 vs. bfloat16 precision for training stability
  - Linear vs. cosine learning rate decay schedules
  - Standard parameterization vs. Maximal Update Parameterization

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Gradient overflow/underflow (numerical instability)
  - Weight growth in specific layers (indicates training instability)
  - Gap between validation and training loss (suggests gradient noise)
  - Poor scaling efficiency (< linear speedup with more systems)

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Run a single CS-2 training test with the smallest model (111M) to verify implementation correctness
  2. Test scaling to 2 CS-2 systems with the same model to verify linear performance scaling
  3. Experiment with different precision modes (FP16 vs. bfloat16) on the 1.3B model to observe stability differences

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the optimal number of tokens per parameter for compute-optimal pre-training on datasets other than the Pile?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper investigates compute-optimal pre-training using 20 tokens per parameter on the Pile dataset, finding it consistent with Chinchilla's results on MassiveText. The authors suggest that further open efforts should consider aggregate compute budget when deciding the balance of model size and pre-training dataset size.
- **Why unresolved:** The study focuses on the Pile dataset, and while the authors suggest the 20 tokens per parameter guideline might be applicable to other datasets, they do not empirically test this hypothesis.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting similar compute-optimal pre-training studies on a variety of large language datasets and comparing the resulting scaling laws and optimal tokens per parameter ratios.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the use of Maximal Update Parameterization (μP) affect the training stability and performance of even larger language models beyond the 13B parameter scale?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors demonstrate that using μP improves accuracy and hyperparameter predictability at scale, particularly for models up to 2.7B parameters. They observe more stable training dynamics and better transfer of hyperparameters from smaller to larger models.
- **Why unresolved:** The study only tests μP on models up to 2.7B parameters, and while they observe benefits, the impact on much larger models (e.g., 100B+ parameters) is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training and evaluating language models significantly larger than 2.7B parameters using μP and comparing their performance, stability, and hyperparameter transferability to models trained with standard parameterization.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the trade-off between pre-training compute efficiency and inference compute efficiency when considering the total compute budget for a language model's lifecycle?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors introduce a metric that accounts for both pre-training and expected inference FLOPs, suggesting that smaller models trained on more tokens can be more compute-efficient overall. They provide a framework for organizations to assess total costs when budgeting large-scale training runs.
- **Why unresolved:** The study provides a theoretical framework but does not empirically explore the trade-off in practice.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting case studies on language models deployed in real-world applications, tracking their pre-training and inference compute usage, and analyzing the cost-benefit trade-offs of different pre-training strategies based on actual inference demands.

## Limitations

- Dataset Scaling Validity: The Pile dataset may differ substantially from the MassiveText dataset used in the original Chinchilla study, potentially affecting optimal token-to-parameter ratio
- Maximal Update Parameterization Implementation: Limited detail on specific implementation choices for μP makes reproducing claimed improvements challenging
- Wafer-Scale Cluster Specifics: Performance claims heavily dependent on Cerebras WSE-2 hardware architecture with unspecified communication details

- Compute-optimal scaling with 20 tokens per parameter yields better pre-training efficiency than smaller datasets
- The 13B parameter model outperforms other similarly-sized open models on downstream tasks
- Training runs were conducted with proper hyperparameter tuning and evaluation

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- Maximal Update Parameterization provides consistent improvements across scales
