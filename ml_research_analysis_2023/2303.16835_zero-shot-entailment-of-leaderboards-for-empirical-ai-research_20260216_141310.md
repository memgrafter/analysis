# Zero-shot Entailment of Leaderboards for Empirical AI Research

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.16835
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16835
- Reference count: 30
- Key outcome: Zero-shot RTE models failed to generalize, suggesting they learned classification not entailment

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether state-of-the-art leaderboard extraction models actually learned textual entailment by testing them in a zero-shot setting. The task involves recognizing if a scholarly article text entails a (task, dataset, metric) tuple from a knowledge base. Two models—ORKG-TDMBERT and ORKGT-DM-XLNet—were tested on 1,000 papers containing 1,925 unseen TDM labels, using a corpus filtered to exclude previously seen TDMs. In zero-shot RTE evaluation, ORKG-TDMBERT achieved 26.7% macro F1 and 21.0% micro F1, while ORKGT-DM-XLNet achieved 22.3% macro F1 and 17.2% micro F1—significantly below their non-zero-shot performance (>90% F1). The results suggest the models did not truly learn entailment but rather acted as multi-class classifiers. The findings highlight limitations of current leaderboard extraction approaches and suggest exploring prompt-based methods for better zero-shot learning.

## Method Summary
The study tested two leaderboard extraction models (ORKG-TDMBERT and ORKGT-DM-XLNet) in a zero-shot RTE setting using 1,000 scholarly articles containing 1,925 unseen TDM labels. The models were pre-trained on non-zero-shot data and evaluated without fine-tuning on the new corpus. Performance was measured using macro F1 and micro F1 scores, with the hypothesis that true entailment learning would yield moderate zero-shot performance rather than the dramatic drop observed.

## Key Results
- ORKG-TDMBERT achieved 26.7% macro F1 and 21.0% micro F1 in zero-shot RTE
- ORKGT-DM-XLNet achieved 22.3% macro F1 and 17.2% micro F1 in zero-shot RTE
- Both models' zero-shot performance was significantly below their non-zero-shot performance (>90% F1)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The two models failed to learn true textual entailment because they were evaluated on TDM labels seen during training, so they effectively memorized patterns instead of generalizing.
- Mechanism: In non-zero-shot RTE, the models classify whether a (𝑇, 𝐻) pair is entailed using TDM tuples that appeared in training. This allows the model to learn a multi-class classification mapping rather than genuine entailment reasoning. In zero-shot RTE, unseen TDM labels prevent this memorization-based shortcut.
- Core assumption: Models can perform well on RTE only if they truly generalize entailment reasoning, not just pattern-match seen tuples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The results suggest the models did not truly learn entailment but rather acted as multi-class classifiers."
  - [section] "ORKG-TDM𝐵𝑒𝑟𝑡 in a non-zero-shot setting reported 90.8% macro F1 and 91.8% micro F1 [14]. The same model in a zero-shot setting reports performances significantly less than chance at 26.7% macro F1 and 21% micro F1."
  - [corpus] Weak—no direct corpus evidence for memorization; inference drawn from comparison of zero-shot vs non-zero-shot performance.
- Break condition: If the model achieves similar performance in both zero-shot and non-zero-shot settings, the memorization hypothesis weakens.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The long DocTAET paper representation (𝑇) makes it harder for the model to learn entailment because it increases noise and reduces focus on relevant text spans.
- Mechanism: Longer text inputs increase the probability of irrelevant context being mixed with the entailment signal, diluting the model's ability to extract the precise relationship between 𝑇 and 𝐻. Shorter, more focused representations would reduce this noise.
- Core assumption: RTE models benefit from concise, relevant text snippets that highlight the entailment signal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We hypothesize that a shorter one or two sentence representation would be a better approach toward realizing a true RTE model for leaderboard extraction."
  - [section] "Offering a paragraph as 𝑇 as opposed to a single sentence has been shown to increase the RTE task complexity."
  - [corpus] Weak—no direct corpus evidence; assumption based on prior RTE research.
- Break condition: If shorter 𝑇 representations still yield poor zero-shot performance, the hypothesis about input length becomes less credible.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The zero-shot TDM definition (any unseen task, dataset, or metric) is too permissive, making the task artificially hard because models must generalize across multiple dimensions simultaneously.
- Mechanism: By requiring generalization when any element of the TDM tuple is unseen, the model must handle combinations of familiar and unfamiliar elements, which is harder than when all elements are unseen or all are seen. This complexity reduces performance.
- Core assumption: RTE models generalize better when all elements of the hypothesis are either seen or unseen, rather than a mix.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Thus instead of adopting a strict criteria where all elements of TDM tuple were new, we instead defined a zero-shot TDM tuple as one where any of the task, dataset, or metric were unseen before."
  - [section] "Had we defined a zero-shot TDM label as one where each of the task, dataset, and metric that constituted a TDM were all unseen in the training dataset... this would have resulted in an empty zero-shot TDM labels set."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; the claim is based on the authors' experimental design choice.
- Break condition: If performance improves under stricter zero-shot definitions, the permissive definition hypothesis gains credibility.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: DocTAET paper representation (𝑇) -> [SEP] token -> TDM tuple (𝐻) -> Transformer encoder (BERT/XLNet) -> Classification head -> Entailment prediction

- Critical path:
  1. Extract DocTAET from scholarly articles
  2. Pair with candidate TDM tuples from knowledge base
  3. Encode using transformer
  4. Classify entailment
  5. Aggregate predictions for leaderboard extraction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Long vs short 𝑇 representation: Longer 𝑇 may contain more context but increases noise; shorter 𝑇 is cleaner but may miss relevant information.
  - Zero-shot definition: Permissive (any unseen element) increases dataset size but makes generalization harder; strict (all unseen) is cleaner but may result in too few examples.

- Failure signatures:
  - High recall, low precision: Model predicts most TDMs as entailed, indicating poor discriminative ability.
  - Performance drop in zero-shot: Suggests memorization rather than generalization.
  - Sensitivity to 𝑇 length: If performance varies significantly with 𝑇 length, it indicates the model relies on superficial cues.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare performance using shorter 𝑇 (e.g., title + abstract only) vs full DocTAET.
  2. Test with stricter zero-shot definition (all TDM elements unseen) to isolate generalization difficulty.
  3. Apply prompt-based fine-tuning to see if it improves zero-shot performance compared to standard classification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Do transformer-based models trained on RTE tasks actually learn entailment or are they just performing multi-class classification?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper directly tests ORKG-TDMBERT and ORKG-TDMXLNet in zero-shot RTE settings and finds their performance drops significantly from >90% F1 to ~20-27% F1, suggesting they learned classification rather than true entailment.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests two specific models and doesn't systematically compare different training approaches or model architectures to determine if any transformer models can learn genuine entailment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic testing of multiple RTE models with varying architectures and training objectives in zero-shot settings, comparing their performance to chance and to models explicitly designed for entailment.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Would shorter text representations improve zero-shot RTE performance for leaderboard extraction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors hypothesize that the longer DocTAET text representation poses a challenge for models to learn entailment, suggesting shorter representations might work better.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests models with the DocTAET representation and doesn't experimentally compare different text representation lengths.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison of zero-shot RTE performance using the same models with varying text representation lengths (single sentences vs. paragraphs vs. DocTAET).

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can prompt-based methods achieve better zero-shot RTE performance for leaderboard extraction than traditional fine-tuning approaches?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest investigating transformer-model powered prompt-based methods that have shown promise as zero-shot learners, as a direction for future research.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't implement or test any prompt-based methods, only suggesting it as a future direction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation and testing of prompt-based RTE models for leaderboard extraction in zero-shot settings, comparing their performance to the traditional fine-tuning approaches tested in this paper.

## Limitations

- The study's conclusions about models learning only multi-class classification are based on performance gaps but lack direct evidence of memorization mechanisms
- The permissive zero-shot definition (any unseen element) may have created an artificially difficult generalization scenario
- The long DocTAET representation may have introduced noise that disadvantaged the models in zero-shot evaluation

## Confidence

- Confidence in models learning classification not entailment: Medium
- Confidence in input length hypothesis: Low (based on hypothesis only)
- Confidence in prompt-based methods suggestion: Low (purely speculative)
