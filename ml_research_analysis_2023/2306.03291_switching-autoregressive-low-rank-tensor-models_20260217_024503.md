# Switching Autoregressive Low-rank Tensor Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03291
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03291
- Reference count: 40
- SALT models achieve parameter efficiency comparable to SLDSs while maintaining ARHMM's exact inference and closed-form estimation

## Executive Summary
SALT models introduce a low-rank parameterization of autoregressive tensors in ARHMMs, effectively bridging the gap between ARHMMs and SLDSs. By constraining autoregressive dynamics with a low-rank tensor decomposition, SALT achieves parameter efficiency comparable to SLDSs while maintaining the exact inference and closed-form estimation of ARHMMs. Theoretically, SALT can approximate stable LDSs with bounded error that decays exponentially with lag length. Empirically, SALT outperforms ARHMMs in predictive likelihood while using fewer parameters, and matches SLDS performance with simpler inference.

## Method Summary
SALT models parameterize autoregressive tensors of ARHMMs with low-rank factorizations (Tucker or CP decomposition). The model maintains joint continuous and discrete latent states but reduces parameter complexity through tensor decomposition. Inference uses the forward-backward algorithm, while parameter estimation employs EM with closed-form updates for low-rank tensor regression. The approach enables modeling of time-varying dynamics with improved parameter efficiency compared to standard ARHMMs.

## Key Results
- SALT outperforms ARHMMs in predictive likelihood while using fewer parameters
- Matches SLDS performance with simpler inference procedures
- On mouse behavioral data, achieves higher test likelihoods and uses fewer discrete states than ARHMMs
- On C. elegans neural recordings, produces segmentations comparable to SLDSs while providing interpretable low-rank autoregressive filters

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SALT bridges ARHMMs and SLDSs by introducing low-rank constraints on autoregressive tensors. This reduces parameter complexity while preserving the ability to model state-dependent dynamics. The low-rank structure enables SALT to capture essential temporal dependencies with fewer parameters than ARHMMs, achieving parameter efficiency comparable to SLDSs. The exact inference and closed-form estimation procedures from ARHMMs are preserved, making SALT computationally simpler than SLDSs while maintaining similar expressive power.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Data -> ARHMM + Low-rank Tensor Decomposition -> SALT Model -> Inference (Forward-Backward) -> Parameter Estimation (EM) -> Predictions

**Critical path**: The core innovation is replacing the full autoregressive tensor in ARHMMs with a low-rank factorization. This occurs in the M-step of EM, where instead of estimating a full tensor, the algorithm estimates factors of the decomposition. The low-rank constraint enables parameter sharing across time lags and state transitions.

**Design tradeoffs**: SALT trades off some expressiveness (due to low-rank constraints) for parameter efficiency and computational simplicity. While ARHMMs can represent any autoregressive relationship with sufficient parameters, SALT assumes that state-dependent dynamics lie in a low-dimensional subspace. This assumption enables fewer parameters but may miss complex dynamics that don't fit the low-rank structure.

**Failure signatures**: Overfitting occurs when the rank is too high relative to data complexity, leading to poor generalization. Underfitting happens with insufficient rank, failing to capture important dynamics. Numerical instability can arise during EM if the low-rank updates don't properly regularize the tensor estimation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**: What is the precise relationship between the rank of SALT models and the number of discrete states required for accurate modeling of time-varying dynamics? The paper shows SALT uses fewer states than ARHMMs but doesn't quantify how rank parameters affect optimal state numbers. This could be resolved through systematic experiments varying both rank and state parameters across multiple datasets.

**Open Question 2**: How does SALT perform when modeling systems with unstable or non-stationary dynamics? Proposition 1 assumes stable LDSs, and the paper notes that unstable dynamics would violate theoretical guarantees. Performance on unstable systems remains unexplored, requiring application to datasets with known instability and comparison with non-stationary modeling approaches.

**Open Question 3**: What is the computational complexity of SALT inference compared to exact inference methods for SLDSs? While the paper claims computational advantages, it lacks rigorous complexity analysis. Detailed comparison of inference time, memory requirements, and scaling behavior with respect to data dimensions would clarify SALT's practical advantages.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on stability assumptions that may not hold in real-world systems
- Computational scalability to very high-dimensional time-series remains untested
- Interpretability claims depend on domain knowledge without independent verification
- Performance on unstable or non-stationary dynamics is not thoroughly explored

## Confidence

**High confidence** for empirical performance comparisons. Mouse behavioral data shows consistent SALT advantages over ARHMMs with fewer parameters and matching SLDS performance. **Medium confidence** for theoretical approximation claims. While the paper establishes bounded error decaying exponentially with lag length, this relies on specific stability assumptions and the relationship between approximation quality and computational cost isn't fully characterized. **Low confidence** regarding computational scalability. The paper demonstrates advantages on moderate-dimensional datasets but doesn't address scaling to thousands of dimensions or very large state spaces.
