# Evaluation of Popular XAI Applied to Clinical Prediction Models: Can They be Trusted?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.11985
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11985
- Reference count: 33
- Key outcome: SHAP and DTD generate clinically coherent explanations, but significant inconsistencies between methods and limited ground truth samples raise questions about reliability.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the trustworthiness and practical utility of two popular XAI methods—SHAP and Deep Taylor Decomposition (DTD)—for explaining predictive models in clinical settings. Using two real-world Electronic Medical Records (EMR) datasets, the research assesses explanations against three key criteria: domain-appropriate representation, impact on clinical workflow, and consistency. The analysis reveals that while both methods generate explanations that are generally coherent with clinical tasks and actionable, there are significant discrepancies in the features they identify as important, especially at the patient level. Benchmarking against ground truth (red flag triggers) shows moderate to strong concordance, particularly when administrative features are excluded. However, consistency across model runs and between different explainers is variable, raising concerns about reliability. The findings suggest that while XAI can support clinical decision-making, caution is needed, and further refinement or novel approaches may be required for robust clinical adoption.

## Method Summary
The study trains logistic regression, XGBoost, and DNN models on two EMR datasets (Vital Signs and Readmission within 30 days) to predict patient deterioration and readmission. SHAP and DTD are applied to generate feature importance explanations. The explanations are evaluated for domain appropriateness, consistency across runs, and benchmarking against ground truth red flag triggers. Five independent runs per method ensure reliability assessment. The analysis compares explanations at both patient and cohort levels, with administrative features sometimes excluded to improve clinical relevance.

## Key Results
- SHAP and DTD explanations align with clinical predictors (e.g., vital signs for deterioration, prior stays for readmission) and are generally actionable.
- Concordance with ground truth triggers is moderate (48–73%), improving to 75–80% when administrative features like length of stay are excluded.
- Consistency metrics (feature agreement, ranking correlation, sign agreement) are moderate but variable, with SHAP showing better stability than DTD across model runs and types.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: XAI explanations are considered trustworthy when they align with ground truth event triggers and maintain high feature agreement across runs.
- Mechanism: The paper establishes trust by benchmarking explanations against recorded red flag triggers in clinical deterioration events. High concordance (48–73%) indicates that the explanations identify clinically relevant features. Consistency is further evaluated by measuring agreement across multiple runs (feature agreement, sign agreement, and ranking correlation), ensuring stability of the explanations.
- Core assumption: Ground truth red flag triggers are accurate and complete representations of the true causes of deterioration events.
- Evidence anchors:

- Break condition: If the ground truth triggers are incomplete or biased, concordance scores will not reflect true explanation quality.

- Claim: Domain-appropriate representation is achieved when top contributing features identified by XAI align with known clinical predictors for the outcome.
- Mechanism: The paper evaluates whether explanations identify clinically meaningful predictors (e.g., vital signs for deterioration, prior inpatient counts for readmission). High agreement between identified features and expert-derived lists suggests domain appropriateness.
- Core assumption: Expert-derived lists of informative predictors are accurate and complete.
- Evidence anchors:

- Break condition: If expert lists are incomplete or biased, high agreement does not guarantee domain appropriateness.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Consistency of explanations across model runs and model types supports trust in XAI.
- Mechanism: The paper measures consistency using feature agreement (FA), ranking correlation (CR), and sign agreement across multiple runs and between models (e.g., Shap on XGB vs DNN). High consistency indicates stable, reliable explanations.
- Core assumption: Variations in underlying model design or optimization should not drastically change explanation outputs.

  - Why needed here: DTD uses first-order Taylor decomposition to explain neural network predictions by associating each feature with a relevance score.
  - Quick check question: What is the role of the root point in Taylor decomposition for DTD explanations?

- Concept: Correlation vs causation in clinical data
  - Why needed here: The paper acknowledges that XAI reveals patterns, not causal mechanisms, which is critical for interpreting explanation reliability in healthcare.
  - Quick check question: Why might high correlation between features lead to misleading explanations even if the model is accurate?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data ingestion: EMR datasets (VS, RA30) → preprocessing pipeline → feature engineering
  - Modeling layer: Logistic regression (L1), XGBoost, Dense NN
  - XAI layer: SHAP explainer (for LR, XGB, NN), DTD explainer (for NN only)
  - Evaluation layer: Benchmarking against ground truth triggers, consistency metrics (FA, CR, sign agreement), domain appropriateness checks
  - Output: Explanations at patient and cohort levels, consistency reports, benchmarking results

- Critical path:
  1. Load and preprocess EMR data → split into train/test
  2. Train predictive models (LR, XGB, NN)
  3. Generate explanations using SHAP/DTD
  4. Evaluate explanations: benchmark against ground truth, measure consistency, assess domain appropriateness
  5. Compile results and discuss implications

- Design tradeoffs:
  - SHAP is model-agnostic but computationally expensive; DTD is efficient for NNs but model-specific.
  - Using administrative features (e.g., LOS) can inflate concordance but may not be clinically actionable.
  - Multiple runs improve reliability but increase computational cost.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low concordance with ground truth triggers → explanations may be misleading.
  - Low consistency across runs → unstable explanations.
  - High disagreement between XAI methods → unreliable feature importance.
  - Administrative features dominate explanations → reduced clinical relevance.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run SHAP and DTD on a small sample of VS dataset; check concordance with ground truth triggers manually.
  2. Compare top 5 features from SHAP vs DTD across 3 model runs; compute FA and CR.
  3. Exclude administrative features and re-run benchmarking; observe change in concordance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the specific causal factors underlying patient deterioration that current XAI methods fail to identify, and how can these methods be modified to better capture causal relationships?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that current XAI methods like Shap and DTD make correlations transparent but do not specifically design for causal inference, and highlights the need for methods that account for causality.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses on correlation-based explanations and benchmarking against ground truth triggers, but does not explore or propose methods for causal inference.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of XAI methods that explicitly incorporate causal inference techniques, tested on datasets with known causal relationships, and comparison of their performance against correlation-based methods.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the inconsistencies in XAI-generated explanations impact clinicians' trust and decision-making in real-world clinical settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the variability and inconsistency in explanations generated by different XAI methods and their potential impact on clinicians' trust.
- Why unresolved: The study primarily focuses on quantitative analysis of explanation consistency and benchmarking but does not include qualitative assessments from clinicians regarding their trust and decision-making processes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting in-depth qualitative studies with clinicians to understand their perceptions of XAI-generated explanations, including their trust levels and how these explanations influence their clinical decisions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the performance and reliability of XAI methods be improved by addressing the interdependencies and multicollinearity among clinical features?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the issue of feature interdependence, particularly in vital signs, and mentions that different groups of features could have the same implications for clinicians.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the problem of feature interdependence, it does not explore specific methods or techniques to address this issue within XAI explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and evaluating XAI methods that incorporate techniques to handle multicollinearity and feature interdependencies, such as regularization or feature selection methods, and assessing their impact on explanation reliability and consistency.

## Limitations
- Small ground truth sample sizes (fewer than 200 samples per dataset) limit statistical power and generalizability.
- Administrative features like length of stay can inflate concordance but reduce clinical actionability.
- Ground truth triggers may not capture all clinically relevant factors, potentially biasing benchmark metrics.

## Confidence
- Confidence: Medium
- The mechanisms linking XAI explanations to clinical trust are well-supported by internal consistency checks and benchmarking against ground truth triggers, but the lack of external validation and potential biases in ground truth data reduce confidence in broader applicability.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the study with a larger, more diverse clinical dataset to assess whether the observed concordance and consistency patterns hold across different patient populations and care settings.
2. Conduct a blinded expert review of XAI explanations to validate domain appropriateness independently of ground truth triggers, ensuring that identified features align with clinical reasoning rather than just recorded alerts.
3. Compare SHAP and DTD explanations against an additional XAI method (e.g., LIME or Integrated Gradients) to evaluate whether discrepancies are method-specific or indicative of broader challenges in clinical XAI.
