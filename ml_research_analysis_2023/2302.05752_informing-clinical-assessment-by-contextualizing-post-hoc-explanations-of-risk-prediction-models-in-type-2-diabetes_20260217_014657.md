# Informing clinical assessment by contextualizing post-hoc explanations of risk prediction models in type-2 diabetes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05752
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05752
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Knowledge-augmented LLM QA achieves MAP up to 0.438-0.821 for contextual CKD explanations in T2DM patients.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the feasibility of using knowledge-augmented LLMs to extract contextual explanations from medical guidelines for CKD risk predictions in T2DM patients. The system combines post-hoc SHAP explanations with guideline-based QA to provide clinicians with interpretable, evidence-backed predictions. Expert panel evaluations confirmed the clinical value of these contextual explanations, particularly for understanding risk scores and feature importances. The work demonstrates a practical pipeline for integrating AI predictions with clinical guidelines to enhance trust and usability.

## Method Summary
The method extracts T2DM patient data, predicts CKD risk using ML/DL models (MLP, GRU), and applies SHAP to identify key features. Contextual explanations are generated by mapping SHAP-identified features to relevant guideline sentences via a QA pipeline using BERT/SciBERT with knowledge augmentation (Metamap + SNOMED-CT filtering). The system is evaluated on MAP, precision@k, and expert panel feedback, with results showing improved performance for drug-related questions over disease features.

## Key Results
- Knowledge-augmented LLMs achieve MAP scores up to 0.438 for disease feature questions and 0.821 for drug questions.
- Expert panel evaluations confirmed the clinical value of contextual explanations for interpreting risk scores and feature importances.
- SciBERT outperformed general BERT in biomedical QA tasks, especially for drug-related questions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Knowledge augmentation improves contextual explanation extraction from medical guidelines by filtering irrelevant or semantically distant answers.
- Mechanism: The QA pipeline applies Metamap to extract biomedical entities and semantic types from guideline text, then filters candidate answers based on disease code overlap and semantic distance in UMLS/SNOMED-CT taxonomies.
- Core assumption: Semantic type consistency between question and answer indicates relevance and improves interpretability for clinicians.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We choose Metamap because of its extensive coverage of biomedical semantic types and its ability to capture entity mentions within the ADA 2021 CPG."
  - [section]: "We use the outputs of these knowledge augmentation modules to both pre-filter and post-sort the LLMs answers."
  - [corpus]: "While rule engineering is more accurate than applying machine learning models, it is not scalable without human effort."
- Break condition: If semantic filtering excludes all candidate answers or includes false positives due to polysemy or missing mappings.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Contextual explanations improve clinician trust and usability of risk prediction models by linking predictions to actionable evidence from guidelines.
- Mechanism: The system generates questions tied to patient features, risk scores, and clinical indicators, then retrieves guideline sentences that justify or contextualize those predictions, displayed in a dashboard with interactive filtering.
- Core assumption: Clinicians require connections between AI outputs and authoritative clinical knowledge to interpret and act on predictions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Expert panel evaluations showed that contextual explanations are valuable for clinicians, aiding in interpreting risk scores and feature importances."
  - [section]: "The expert panel evaluated these explanations for 20 prototypical patients."
  - [section]: "Our end-to-end system enabled us to identify practical steps in creating such a pipeline and the steps needed to generalize this for other clinical settings."
- Break condition: If guideline content is sparse or misaligned with patient features, or if clinicians find the explanations non-actionable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLMs pre-trained on biomedical corpora (e.g., SciBERT) perform better than general-domain BERT for extracting clinical explanations.
- Mechanism: Domain adaptation via pre-training on PubMed or clinical notes aligns the model's token embeddings and attention patterns with biomedical terminology and syntax.
- Core assumption: Biomedical language has distinct statistical and semantic properties that require specialized pre-training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "SciBERT is a close second with an improved recall and F1 score."
  - [section]: "BERT models that were made available specifically for clinical question-answering."
  - [corpus]: "There are currently very few efforts on the applications of these domain adapted language models to medical guidelines."
- Break condition: If domain-adapted models overfit to training corpus idiosyncrasies or underperform on out-of-distribution guideline language.

  - Why needed here: The system must map free-text clinical questions to relevant guideline sentences, requiring understanding of both question intent and guideline structure.

  - Why needed here: Metamap and SNOMED-CT provide structured mappings from guideline text to clinical concepts; distance metrics help rank relevance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Patient data extraction (claims/EHR) → Risk prediction (MLP/GRU) → SHAP post-hoc explanations → Question generation → Guideline QA pipeline (LLM + knowledge augmentation) → Dashboard rendering
- Critical path: Data → Risk model → Feature importances → QA questions → Contextual answers → UI presentation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - LLM vs rule-based QA: LLM offers flexibility but less precision; rules offer precision but require manual encoding.
  - Pre-filtering vs post-sorting: Pre-filtering reduces LLM workload but may discard valid answers; post-sorting preserves candidates but increases computation.
- Failure signatures:
  - Low MAP scores: Guideline coverage insufficient or LLM unable to parse guideline syntax.
  - No answers returned: Overly strict semantic filtering or missing Metamap mappings.
  - Dashboard lag: Bottleneck in QA pipeline or excessive patient data volume.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run baseline BERT QA on 10 annotated questions, measure MAP and BLEU.
  2. Add Metamap semantic filtering, re-run, compare precision/recall.
  3. Replace BERT with SciBERT, measure impact on disease group performance.

## Limitations

- Limited external validation: The study is based on a small expert panel (20 prototypical patients) without broader clinical deployment or longitudinal outcome assessment.
- Reliance on structured guideline content: The system assumes semantic type consistency in Metamap mappings, which may not generalize across diverse clinical contexts or evolving guidelines.
- Performance gap constraints: The quantitative evaluation (MAP, precision@k) is based on relatively small sample sizes (n=50-100 questions per group), limiting generalizability.

## Confidence

- High confidence: The core methodology of combining SHAP-based feature importance with guideline-derived contextual explanations is technically sound and addresses a real clinical need for interpretable AI predictions.
- Medium confidence: The quantitative QA performance metrics (MAP, precision@k) are well-defined, but their clinical relevance depends on expert validation which was limited in scope.
- Medium confidence: The claim that contextual explanations improve clinician trust and usability is supported by expert panel feedback, but lacks quantitative measures of actual clinical decision impact or workflow integration.

## Next Checks

3. Measure end-to-end system latency and resource utilization in a production environment to identify bottlenecks in the QA pipeline for real-time clinical deployment.
