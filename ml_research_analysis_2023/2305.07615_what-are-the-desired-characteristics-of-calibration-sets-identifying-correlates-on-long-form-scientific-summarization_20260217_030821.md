# What are the Desired Characteristics of Calibration Sets? Identifying Correlates on Long Form Scientific Summarization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07615
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07615
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: This paper investigates what makes an effective calibration set for improving relevance and faithfulness in long-form scientific summarization.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically investigates what makes an effective calibration set for improving relevance and faithfulness in long-form scientific summarization. The authors form large pools of candidate summaries using diverse generation methods and systematically vary the subsets used for calibration fine-tuning. They find that for faithfulness calibration, negative sets should be extractive and likely under the model, while for relevance calibration, maximizing the metric margin between candidates and minimizing surprise (disagreement between model and metric rankings) is optimal. The study reveals an inverse relationship between relevance and faithfulness during calibration, suggesting the need for methods that jointly optimize both objectives. Experimental results on three scientific datasets (clinical, chemical, biomedical) show that calibration improves both relevance and faithfulness, with the most significant gains observed on the noisier clinical dataset.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune PRIMERA/LongT5 models with MLE, then perform calibration fine-tuning using combined MLE + calibration loss. They generate 10 diverse beam summaries per instance and additional negative/positive candidates using Mask-And-Fill, Entity Swap, and GPT-3 paraphrase methods. Calibration is performed using systematic selection strategies targeting set characteristics including quality (extreme, average, min, high), margin (metric gaps between candidates), diversity, likelihood (probability under the model), and spurious correlates. The approach is evaluated across three scientific datasets using relevance metrics (RelAgg: ROUGE 1/2 + BERTScore) and faithfulness metrics (FaithAgg: BARTScore + BERTScore-Src + FactScore).

## Key Results
- For faithfulness calibration, negative sets should be extractive and likely under the model, creating effective contrastive learning signals
- For relevance calibration, maximizing the metric margin between candidates leads to better downstream performance than selecting for overall quality
- There is an inverse relationship between relevance and faithfulness during calibration, suggesting these objectives require different calibration set characteristics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Calibration sets that maximize the metric margin between candidates lead to better downstream relevance because they create clearer training signals for the model to distinguish between high and low quality summaries.
- Mechanism: When candidates have large gaps in their relevance scores (RelAgg), the contrastive learning objective has stronger gradients to push the model toward preferring the better summaries and avoiding the worse ones. This is especially important for relevance calibration where the model needs to learn which features of summaries correlate with human judgments.
- Core assumption: The metric gaps reflect meaningful differences in summary quality that the model can learn from, and that these gaps are not just noise or artifacts of the evaluation metrics themselves.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "for relevance calibration, the metric margin between candidates should be maximized"
  - [section 6.6] "For relevance training, the presence of a large metric margin between candidate summaries appears to be more impactful to downstream performance than the overall relevance of the set"
  - [corpus] Weak - the paper doesn't directly test whether larger metric margins lead to more meaningful quality differences, only that they correlate with better calibration results
- Break condition: If the metric gaps are primarily driven by spurious correlates (like length) rather than genuine quality differences, maximizing them could actually harm calibration by reinforcing the wrong features.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: For faithfulness calibration, negative examples that are likely under the fine-tuned model but factually incorrect create the most effective contrastive learning signal.
- Mechanism: When the model is "surprised" that it could generate factually incorrect text (because the negative examples have high likelihood), it learns to better distinguish between faithful and unfaithful content. This surprise forces the model to develop more nuanced representations of what makes content faithful to the source.
- Core assumption: The model's likelihood estimates are meaningful indicators of what it would naturally generate, and that contrastive learning is most effective when the positive and negative examples are close in the model's representation space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "faithfulness calibration is optimal when the negative sets are extractive and more likely to be generated"
  - [section 6.5] "contrastive learning for faithfulness is most effective when the model is 'surprised', i.e., the negative summaries are as likely, if not more, to be generated as the positives"
  - [corpus] Weak - the paper shows correlation between likelihood gaps and faithfulness improvements but doesn't establish causal mechanisms for why surprise drives learning

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Relevance and faithfulness calibration objectives are in tension, requiring different calibration set characteristics that may need to be balanced for joint optimization.
- Mechanism: When optimizing for faithfulness, the model may learn to be more conservative and extractive, which reduces abstractiveness and potentially relevance. When optimizing for relevance, the model may learn to be more abstractive and creative, which can introduce factual errors. The calibration set characteristics that optimize one objective may actively harm the other.
- Core assumption: Relevance and faithfulness represent distinct dimensions of summary quality that can trade off against each other, rather than being perfectly aligned objectives.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The study reveals an inverse relationship between relevance and faithfulness during calibration"
  - [section 6.4] "We plot RelAgg versus Faith Agg across experiments to measure the trade-off between relevance and faithfulness. On average, improving faithfulness comes at the cost of relevance"
  - [corpus] Moderate - the human evaluation confirms that the most faithful summaries are ranked less relevant by human annotators, providing direct evidence of the trade-off

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Contrastive learning and margin-based ranking
  - Why needed here: The paper uses contrastive objectives for faithfulness calibration and margin-based ranking for relevance calibration, which requires understanding how these learning paradigms work and what makes them effective
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between a contrastive loss that pushes positive examples closer to each other and away from negatives versus a margin-based ranking loss that enforces ordering between candidates?

- Concept: Evaluation metrics for faithfulness and relevance
  - Why needed here: The paper uses multiple metrics (RelAgg, Faith Agg, BARTScore, FactScore, BERTScore) and shows how calibration affects them differently, requiring understanding what each metric measures and their limitations
  - Quick check question: Why might a faithfulness metric like BARTScore correlate with extractive density, and what does this suggest about using it to evaluate abstractive summarization systems?

- Concept: Generation methods for synthetic errors
  - Why needed here: The paper uses multiple methods (mask-and-fill, entity swapping, paraphrasing) to create negative examples for faithfulness calibration, requiring understanding how these methods work and what types of errors they introduce
  - Quick check question: How does mask-and-fill corruption differ from entity swapping in terms of the types of factual errors it introduces, and why might one be more effective than the other for contrastive learning?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: The calibration pipeline consists of (1) candidate generation using diverse methods, (2) selection strategies that filter candidates based on various characteristics, (3) calibration fine-tuning using either margin-based ranking or contrastive objectives, and (4) evaluation using multiple faithfulness and relevance metrics. The system is designed to be modular so different candidate generation methods and selection strategies can be easily swapped.
- Critical path: The most critical components are the candidate generation methods and selection strategies, as they determine what training data the calibration step receives. Poor choices here will limit the effectiveness of the calibration regardless of how well the fine-tuning is implemented.
- Design tradeoffs: The paper balances diversity of candidate generation methods against the computational cost of generating and evaluating many candidates. It also trades off between using more extractive (likely) candidates versus more abstractive (surprising) candidates for faithfulness calibration.
- Failure signatures: Calibration may fail if the candidate pool lacks diversity, if selection strategies create sets that are too homogeneous, or if the metrics used for selection are poorly aligned with actual summary quality. The paper shows that simply selecting the most relevant candidates doesn't always yield the best calibration results.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the basic pipeline with random selection to establish baseline performance and verify that calibration improves over fine-tuning alone
  2. Test quality-based selection (extreme, average, min, high) to understand how overall candidate quality affects calibration effectiveness
  3. Test margin-based selection to confirm whether maximizing metric gaps between candidates improves downstream performance as hypothesized

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why does faithfulness calibration show an inverse relationship with relevance metrics, while relevance calibration shows a positive relationship?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that faithfulness calibration typically decreases relevance scores, while relevance calibration increases faithfulness scores. They hypothesize this is because faithfulness calibration methods involve corrupting references, while relevance calibration uses model-generated summaries.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests this is a "spurious correlation" due to extractive summaries being more likely under the model, but doesn't definitively prove this is the root cause.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments that control for extractiveness in the candidate sets, or analysis of whether the correlation persists when using non-extractive positive examples for faithfulness calibration.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between exploring diverse candidates versus exploiting high-quality candidates during calibration?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors find that "Easy" likelihood-based selection (using most likely candidates) works better for relevance calibration, while "Hard" selection (using surprising candidates) works better for faithfulness calibration. This suggests different exploration-exploitation trade-offs for different objectives.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide a theoretical framework for determining when to prioritize exploration versus exploitation during calibration.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study varying the degree of diversity in candidate sets and measuring its impact on both calibration objectives, or theoretical analysis of the optimization landscape for each calibration objective.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do spurious correlations between metrics and summary characteristics (like length and extractiveness) affect calibration outcomes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors demonstrate that faithfulness metrics are correlated with extractiveness, and that controlling for extractiveness gaps doesn't improve faithfulness as expected. They also show that length affects relevance differently across datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies these spurious correlations but doesn't provide methods to mitigate their effects or determine when they might be beneficial versus harmful.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments that systematically vary summary characteristics during calibration while controlling for their metric correlations, or development of metrics that are less susceptible to spurious correlations.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can calibration be effectively performed for both faithfulness and relevance simultaneously, rather than treating them as separate objectives?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe an inverse relationship between faithfulness and relevance during calibration, and suggest that future research should explore joint optimization of both objectives.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides a preliminary proof-of-concept showing that maximizing correlation between faithfulness and relevance metrics can improve both, but doesn't establish this as a general solution.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing joint calibration approaches against sequential calibration for both objectives, or theoretical analysis of how faithfulness and relevance objectives interact during optimization.

## Limitations
- The study relies on synthetic negative examples for faithfulness calibration, which may not fully capture real-world errors
- The inverse relationship between relevance and faithfulness suggests current methods may not be optimal for joint optimization
- Results are based on scientific domains with relatively structured text, limiting generalizability to other domains

## Confidence
*High Confidence:* The core finding that calibration sets should be optimized differently for relevance versus faithfulness (selecting for metric margins vs. likelihood-surprise trade-off) is well-supported by systematic experiments across three datasets. The observation that maximizing metric gaps improves relevance calibration has strong empirical backing with consistent results across different selection strategies.

*Medium Confidence:* The claim about the inverse relationship between relevance and faithfulness calibration is supported by human evaluation but requires more investigation into whether this trade-off is fundamental or can be mitigated through better joint optimization methods. The effectiveness of specific generation methods (Mask-And-Fill vs Entity Swap) for creating negative examples shows promise but would benefit from more systematic comparison.

*Low Confidence:* The recommendation to maximize spurious correlates like length for relevance calibration while minimizing them for faithfulness calibration needs more theoretical grounding. The paper shows correlation but doesn't fully explain why these correlates should be treated so differently between objectives, nor does it address potential unintended consequences of deliberately maximizing them.

## Next Checks
1. **Joint Optimization Experiment**: Implement a weighted combination of relevance and faithfulness calibration objectives to test whether the observed trade-off can be mitigated. Vary the weighting parameter systematically and measure whether intermediate points exist that maintain both relevance and faithfulness, or if the trade-off is truly unavoidable.

2. **Real Error Analysis**: Replace synthetic negative examples with naturally occurring errors from human-written summaries (e.g., from papers with known factual inaccuracies or summaries that failed quality checks). Compare whether the likelihood-surprise principle still holds when using real rather than synthetic errors for faithfulness calibration.

3. **Cross-Domain Generalization Test**: Apply the identified calibration set characteristics to a non-scientific domain (such as news or dialogue summarization) where the nature of factual consistency differs. Measure whether the same principles about metric margins and likelihood-surprise apply, or if domain-specific characteristics require different calibration strategies.
