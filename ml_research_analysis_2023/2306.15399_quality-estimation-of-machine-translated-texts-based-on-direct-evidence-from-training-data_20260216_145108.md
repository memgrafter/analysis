# Quality Estimation of Machine Translated Texts based on Direct Evidence from Training Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.15399
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15399
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: Word co-occurrence strength in training data correlates with translation quality, showing Pearson correlation of 0.21 with BLEU for English-Kannada

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel quality estimation method for machine translation that relies solely on direct evidence from the training data, eliminating the need for reference translations or an MT system. The approach constructs a word co-occurrence matrix from the parallel training corpus and uses the strength of co-occurrence relations to estimate translation quality. Experiments with English-Kannada and English-Hindi SMT systems demonstrate a clear positive correlation between the proposed Direct Evidence (DE) scores and established metrics like BLEU, suggesting that the training corpus itself contains sufficient information to estimate translation quality.

## Method Summary
The method builds a Word Co-occurrence Matrix (WCM) from the parallel training corpus, where each entry WCMi,j represents the co-occurrence count of source word i and target word j. For each source-target sentence pair, the DE score is computed as the percentage of source words with strong co-occurrence evidence in the target translation. The approach filters out words with counts exceeding 10,000 and requires co-occurrence counts above 20 for inclusion. The method can be applied to both training and test data, potentially identifying noisy segments in the training corpus and ranking translation quality without needing an MT system or reference translations.

## Key Results
- Pearson correlation of 0.21 between sentence-level BLEU and DE scores for English-Kannada, significant at p < 0.05
- BLEU scores ranged from 6.0 to 12.5 across different DE score ranges for English-Hindi
- Histogram analysis revealed significant portions of training data with DE scores below 50, indicating potential noise
- Results support the idea that training corpus contains sufficient information for quality estimation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Word co-occurrence strength in the training corpus correlates with translation quality.
- Mechanism: The co-occurrence matrix (WCM) captures lexical transfer evidence. Higher WCM values for a source-target word pair indicate stronger lexical association and thus higher translation confidence.
- Core assumption: The training corpus contains sufficient lexical co-occurrence patterns to predict translation quality.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We show that the parallel corpus used as training data for training the MT system holds direct clues for estimating the quality of translations produced by the MT system."
  - [section]: "A large W CMi,j value indicates a strong correlation between the SL word i and TL word j in the training corpus."
- Break condition: If the training corpus is too small, noisy, or domain-mismatched, co-occurrence counts may not reflect true translation quality.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Direct Evidence (DE) scores derived from WCM can rank translation quality without needing reference translations or an MT system.
- Mechanism: DE score = percentage of source words with strong co-occurrence evidence in the target translation. Higher DE scores correlate with higher BLEU scores.
- Core assumption: Strong lexical evidence in training data predicts overall translation adequacy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our experiments show that this simple and direct method holds promise for quality estimation of translations produced by any purely data driven machine translation system."
  - [section]: "We can clearly see a positive correlation between the DE Scores we obtained and the BLEU scores, up to a threshold of 70."
- Break condition: If the MT system uses non-lexical features (syntax, semantics) heavily, DE scores may not capture translation quality adequately.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training corpus quality can be assessed using DE scores to identify noisy or low-quality segments.
- Mechanism: Segments with low DE scores in the training corpus are likely to be noisy or unreliable for MT training.
- Core assumption: DE scores computed on training data reflect the inherent quality of parallel segments.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Figure 1 shows the histogram plot of DE-Scores obtained. It can be observed that a significant part of the training data got DE-Scores less than 50, many cases even less than 10. This can be useful in locating and reducing noise in the training data."
- Break condition: If the training corpus is uniformly high-quality or uniformly low-quality, DE scores may not effectively identify noise.

  - Why needed here: Used to validate the relationship between DE scores and BLEU scores.
  - Quick check question: What does a Pearson correlation of 0.21 with p < 0.05 indicate about the relationship between DE scores and BLEU?
- Concept: Corpus preprocessing and vocabulary filtering (frequency thresholds, OOV handling).
  - Why needed here: Ensures WCM is built only on reliable word pairs and excludes high-frequency function words.
  - Quick check question: Why exclude words occurring more than 10,000 times from WCM?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Parallel corpus -> Word Co-occurrence Matrix -> DE score computation -> Quality estimation
- Critical path:
  1. Build WCM from training corpus
  2. Compute DE scores for each sentence in test or training set
  3. (Optional) Correlate DE scores with BLEU or other quality metrics
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Memory vs. accuracy: Larger WCM includes more word pairs but increases memory usage
  - Frequency thresholds: Lower thresholds increase coverage but may introduce noise
  - Single vs. multi-feature: DE scores focus only on lexical transfer; may miss syntactic/semantic quality
- Failure signatures:
  - Low correlation between DE and BLEU: May indicate MT system uses non-lexical features heavily
  - High DE scores for poor translations: May indicate noisy training data or incorrect WCM construction
  - Very low DE scores across all sentences: May indicate overly strict frequency filtering
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compute WCM and DE scores on a small parallel corpus (e.g., 10k segments) and manually inspect high vs. low DE sentences
  2. Compare DE score distribution on training vs. test sets to detect data shift
  3. Vary frequency thresholds (e.g., 10, 20, 50) and observe impact on DE-BLEU correlation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the Direct Evidence approach be extended to handle word alignment and dependency relations in addition to lexical transfer quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that future work could explore estimating aspects like agreement, syntactic completeness, and semantic coherence from the training corpus.
- Why unresolved: The current method only focuses on lexical transfer quality using word co-occurrence statistics, without considering syntactic or semantic aspects of translation quality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments showing that incorporating word alignment and dependency relation analysis into the DE score computation improves correlation with human judgments of overall translation quality.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of the Direct Evidence approach compare to established QE methods like those used in WMT shared tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows correlation between DE scores and BLEU but does not directly compare against other QE methods. The authors note that research in MT QE is rich in terms of ideas, techniques, tools, and resources.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the proposed method against BLEU scores, not against other QE approaches or metrics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison of DE scores against other QE methods using standard datasets and metrics from WMT shared tasks would show relative performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the DE score computation be optimized by using machine learning to determine optimal frequency thresholds instead of manual selection?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "Instead of counting the percentage of words in the SL sentence which have enough evidence (as indicated by the co-occurrence counts), we could use the actual counts themselves to get a more fine grained picture."
- Why unresolved: The current method uses manually selected frequency thresholds (20 for co-occurrence, 10,000 for high frequency words) which may not be optimal.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments using machine learning to automatically determine optimal frequency thresholds and comparing performance against the manually selected thresholds would show if optimization is possible.

## Limitations

- Correlation between DE scores and BLEU is modest (Pearson r=0.21), suggesting the method captures only part of translation quality
- Experiments are limited to SMT systems with two language pairs, raising questions about generalizability to neural MT systems
- Critical implementation details like frequency thresholds and exact DE score computation are underspecified, hindering reproducibility

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core claim that training corpus contains useful quality signals (supported by observed correlations and noise detection capability)
- **Medium Confidence**: The method's effectiveness across different MT architectures (only SMT tested)
- **Low Confidence**: Quantitative thresholds and their impact on results (not clearly specified)
