# A Recipe for Efficient SBIR Models: Combining Relative Triplet Loss with Batch Normalization and Knowledge Distillation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18988
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18988
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves 62.38% recall@1 on Sketchy Database, outperforming previous state-of-the-art

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses Sketch-Based Image Retrieval (SBIR) by introducing a Relative Triplet Loss (RTL) that weights training samples based on anchor similarity, reducing the impact of ambiguous samples. The authors demonstrate that batch normalization is superior to l2-normalization for cross-domain embedding alignment, and show how knowledge distillation enables efficient training of small sketch encoders. Their comprehensive approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on the Sketchy Database while enabling scalable deployment through model compression.

## Method Summary
The method combines three key innovations: a Relative Triplet Loss that computes photo-photo similarity matrices to weight triplet losses, batch normalization applied to both sketch and photo embeddings for better cross-domain alignment, and knowledge distillation to train small sketch encoders (like ShuffleNetV2) using larger pre-trained models as teachers. The approach addresses the fundamental challenge in SBIR where ambiguous training samples can destabilize standard triplet loss training, while also solving the efficiency bottleneck of large model deployment.

## Key Results
- Achieves 62.38% recall@1 on Sketchy Database benchmark
- Demonstrates that photo encoders require higher capacity than sketch encoders
- Shows ShuffleNetV2 can match larger models through knowledge distillation
- Proves batch normalization outperforms l2-normalization for SBIR embeddings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The Relative Triplet Loss (RTL) improves triplet training by weighting losses based on anchor similarity, reducing the impact of ambiguous training samples. The paper computes a similarity matrix between all photos in a mini-batch using their embeddings, normalizes this matrix, and uses it to weight the triplet loss before aggregation. This ensures triplets involving highly similar anchors contribute less to the total loss. The core assumption is that photo encoders are already sufficiently discriminative to measure photo similarity reliably within a mini-batch. This could break if the photo encoder hasn't learned discriminative features, causing the similarity matrix to be noisy and potentially destabilizing training.

### Mechanism 2
Batch normalization in SBIR embeddings improves cross-domain alignment between sketches and photos by preserving the dynamic range of embeddings and allowing domain-specific normalization statistics to bring the two distributions closer together. Instead of l2-normalization, batch normalization is applied to the output of each modality encoder. The core assumption is that batch normalization's learnable parameters can adaptively align the feature distributions of sketches and photos without over-constraining them. This could fail if batch statistics are unstable (e.g., very small batch sizes), causing the alignment benefit to be lost or variance to increase.

### Mechanism 3
Knowledge distillation enables small sketch encoders (e.g., ShuffleNetV2) to match the performance of much larger ones by transferring cross-domain feature knowledge. A pre-trained large sketch encoder (teacher) produces embeddings for sketch inputs, and the small encoder (student) is trained to mimic these embeddings using losses like MSE, MAE, or Huber. The student learns to capture the cross-domain structure without directly experiencing it. The core assumption is that the teacher model has already learned effective cross-domain representations that encode more than just hard labels. This could break if the teacher's embeddings are poor or unstable, causing the student to inherit those weaknesses and potentially fail to converge.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Sketch Encoder -> Batch Normalization -> RTL Layer (with Photo Similarity Weighting) <- Batch Normalization <- Photo Encoder
- Critical path: 1) Forward pass through both encoders, 2) Compute embeddings, 3) Apply batch normalization, 4) Compute RTL with photo-photo similarity weighting, 5) Backpropagate gradients
- Design tradeoffs: Batch size vs. similarity matrix stability, Encoder capacity vs. online inference cost, Knowledge distillation speed vs. accuracy gain
- Failure signatures: RTL - training loss plateaus or diverges if photo embeddings are not discriminative; Batch normalization - noisy batch stats cause unstable embeddings; Distillation - student fails to converge if teacher is weak
- First 3 experiments: 1) Replace triplet loss with RTL on a ResNet18/34 pair; measure recall@1, 2) Add batch normalization to embeddings; compare to l2-normalization baseline, 3) Train ShuffleNetV2 as sketch encoder via knowledge distillation from ResNet34; test online efficiency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the Relative Triplet Loss (RTL) perform compared to other advanced loss functions like ArcFace or CircleLoss on Sketch-Based Image Retrieval (SBIR) tasks? The paper introduces RTL as an adapted triplet loss that weighs loss based on anchor similarity, demonstrating it outperforms traditional triplet loss on the Sketchy Database benchmark. This remains unresolved because the paper does not compare RTL with other advanced loss functions that have shown success in face recognition and other domains. What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments that compare RTL with ArcFace, CircleLoss, and other advanced loss functions on SBIR benchmarks, analyzing their performance in terms of accuracy and efficiency.

### Open Question 2
What is the impact of varying the margin parameter in the Relative Triplet Loss (RTL) on the performance of SBIR models? The paper mentions using a margin of 3 in their experiments but does not explore the effect of different margin values on the model's performance. This remains unresolved because the paper does not provide an analysis of how changing the margin parameter affects the performance of SBIR models using RTL. What evidence would resolve it: Performing a sensitivity analysis on the margin parameter by testing various values and evaluating the impact on model performance metrics such as recall@1.

### Open Question 3
How does the proposed double guidance pipeline for fine-tuning after knowledge distillation compare to other fine-tuning strategies in terms of efficiency and accuracy? The paper introduces a double guidance pipeline that uses both a sketch encoder teacher and a photo encoder teacher to guide the student model, achieving improved results. This remains unresolved because the paper does not compare the double guidance pipeline to other fine-tuning strategies, such as single-teacher guidance or self-distillation, in terms of efficiency and accuracy. What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments that compare the double guidance pipeline with other fine-tuning strategies on SBIR tasks, measuring both the efficiency (training time, computational resources) and accuracy (recall@1, other relevant metrics) of the resulting models.

## Limitations
- The exact capacity threshold for photo encoders where larger models provide diminishing returns is unclear
- Knowledge distillation mechanism lacks detailed ablations on teacher-student architecture pairing
- Efficiency claims for small models need more runtime and memory profiling

## Confidence

**Medium**: The knowledge distillation results depend heavily on teacher quality, which isn't fully characterized
