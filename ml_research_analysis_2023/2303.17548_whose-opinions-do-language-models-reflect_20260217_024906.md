# Whose Opinions Do Language Models Reflect?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.17548
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17548
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Language models show substantial misalignment with US demographic opinions, with RLHF-tuned models exhibiting liberal biases

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a framework for measuring the opinions expressed by language models and their alignment with human opinions using public opinion surveys. The key contribution is OpinionQA, a dataset of 1,498 multiple-choice survey questions covering topics like politics, health, and science, along with responses from 60 demographic groups in the US. The framework evaluates models by comparing their opinion distributions (obtained via log probabilities of answer choices) with human distributions using 1-Wasserstein distance. The study finds substantial misalignment between current language models and US demographics, comparable to the Democrat-Republican divide on climate change.

Human feedback-tuned models like text-davinci-003 show strong biases toward liberal, educated, and wealthy groups, with overly sharp opinion distributions that collapse to modal views. Steering models toward specific demographics shows modest improvements but does not resolve representation gaps. The analysis also reveals low consistency across topics for most models. The work provides tools to assess whose opinions models reflect and highlights underrepresented groups like elderly, widowed, and highly religious individuals. Limitations include US-centric data and the multiple-choice format's divergence from open-ended usage. The authors frame this as a probe rather than a benchmark, emphasizing the importance of understanding model opinion representation for responsible deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors created OpinionQA, a dataset of 1,498 multiple-choice questions derived from public opinion surveys covering diverse topics. They collected response distributions from 60 US demographic groups based on factors like age, education, income, and political affiliation. Language models are evaluated by computing the log probability of each answer choice and comparing the resulting opinion distribution to human distributions using 1-Wasserstein distance. The framework also includes metrics for consistency across topics and representativeness of different demographic groups. Models are tested in both standard and steered configurations using demographic-specific prompts.

## Key Results
- Language models show substantial misalignment with US demographic opinion distributions, comparable to the Democrat-Republican divide on climate change
- RLHF-tuned models like text-davinci-003 exhibit strong liberal biases and collapse to modal views, poorly representing conservative, less educated, and lower-income groups
- Steering models toward specific demographics through prompts provides modest improvements but fails to resolve fundamental representation gaps
- Elderly, widowed, and highly religious individuals are particularly poorly represented by current language models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework works by treating language models as opinion generators that can be evaluated against empirical human opinion distributions. By using log probabilities of answer choices, the method captures the model's internal preference distribution rather than just its most likely output. The 1-Wasserstein distance provides a meaningful metric for comparing opinion distributions that accounts for the full shape of the distribution rather than just point estimates. The multiple-choice format allows for controlled comparison with survey data while the steering mechanism tests whether models can be directed toward different demographic perspectives.

## Foundational Learning
- **1-Wasserstein distance**: A metric for comparing probability distributions that accounts for the "work" required to transform one distribution into another. Why needed: Traditional metrics like KL divergence don't capture the full distributional differences relevant for opinion alignment. Quick check: Verify that Wasserstein distance is symmetric and satisfies triangle inequality.

- **Log probability distributions**: Converting model outputs to log probabilities allows comparison of confidence levels across different answer choices. Why needed: Raw top-1 accuracy misses important information about model uncertainty and opinion strength. Quick check: Ensure log probabilities sum to 1 when exponentiated.

- **Demographic stratification**: Breaking down survey responses by demographic factors reveals alignment patterns that would be invisible in aggregate analysis. Why needed: Aggregate opinion averages can mask significant representation gaps for specific groups. Quick check: Verify demographic sample sizes are sufficient for statistical significance.

- **Modal collapse**: The tendency of models to concentrate probability mass on single answers rather than expressing uncertainty. Why needed: Human opinions are inherently uncertain and multimodal, making modal collapse a key failure mode. Quick check: Compare entropy of model vs human distributions.

- **Steering mechanisms**: Using demographic-specific prompts to direct model responses toward particular perspectives. Why needed: Tests whether models can be made more representative through prompting alone. Quick check: Verify steering prompts actually shift output distributions measurably.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Public Opinion Surveys -> OpinionQA Dataset -> Log Probability Computation -> 1-Wasserstein Distance Calculation -> Demographic Alignment Metrics -> Steering Analysis

**Critical Path**: The core evaluation pipeline processes each question through the model, computes log probabilities for all answer choices, compares these to human response distributions using Wasserstein distance, and aggregates results across questions and demographics to produce alignment scores.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The multiple-choice format enables precise comparison with survey data but may not reflect open-ended usage patterns. Using log probabilities captures uncertainty but requires careful normalization. The Wasserstein metric provides meaningful comparisons but is computationally heavier than alternatives.

**Failure Signatures**: Models that collapse to modal views show artificially low Wasserstein distances due to peaked distributions. Poor demographic alignment manifests as systematically higher distances for specific groups. Inconsistent topic performance appears as high variance in Wasserstein scores across different question categories.

**Three First Experiments**:
1. Replicate the baseline analysis using an alternative distance metric (e.g., Jensen-Shannon divergence) to verify robustness of findings
2. Test model alignment on a held-out subset of questions to assess generalizability
3. Evaluate the effect of temperature scaling on opinion distributions to understand the role of confidence calibration

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the alignment issues between LMs and underrepresented groups be mitigated through targeted training data augmentation or specialized fine-tuning approaches?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies groups like elderly, widowed, and highly religious individuals as poorly represented by current LMs, and notes that RLHF-trained models like text-davinci-003 collapse to modal views rather than capturing nuanced opinions.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests steering models through prompt engineering, finding modest improvements but no resolution of representation gaps. It does not explore data augmentation or specialized fine-tuning techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training experiments using balanced datasets that oversample responses from underrepresented groups, or RLHF fine-tuning with diverse annotator pools, followed by evaluation using the OpinionQA framework to measure changes in representativeness scores.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Do the liberal-leaning tendencies observed in RLHF-tuned models persist when evaluated on non-US demographics or in different cultural contexts?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper explicitly notes that OpinionQA is US-centric and its conclusions are valid only for US populations, while acknowledging that many societies differ from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic) societies.
- Why unresolved: All experiments and analyses are conducted using US-based Pew Research surveys and demographic groups, leaving open the question of whether similar alignment issues exist globally.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replicating the OpinionQA framework using public opinion surveys from other countries, then comparing LM opinion alignment across different cultural contexts and demographic groups.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the opinion alignment measured through multiple-choice formats translate to open-ended text generation settings where LMs are typically deployed?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges this as a key limitation, noting that the multiple-choice format differs from open-ended text generation and questioning whether opinion alignment in this setting will be reflected in downstream use cases.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates models in a controlled multiple-choice setting, without testing how opinion alignment manifests in free-form generation tasks like dialogue or open-ended question answering.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting human evaluations of LM-generated responses to open-ended subjective queries across various demographic groups, measuring both the content of opinions expressed and how well they align with group-specific viewpoints.

## Limitations
- Multiple-choice format may not reflect how language models are actually used in open-ended generation tasks
- Analysis is limited to US demographics, raising questions about generalizability to other cultural contexts
- The framework cannot capture nuanced opinion shifts that might occur in conversational contexts

## Confidence
- **High**: Observed misalignment between language models and US demographic opinion distributions, particularly the comparison to the Democrat-Republican divide on climate change
- **Medium**: Attribution of bias patterns specifically to human feedback tuning, as other factors like training data composition are not fully controlled
- **Low**: Generalizability of results beyond US demographics due to complete reliance on American survey data

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the analysis using open-ended opinion elicitation tasks to compare whether observed misalignments persist outside multiple-choice constraints
2. Conduct cross-national validation using survey data from non-US populations to assess the framework's applicability to different cultural contexts
3. Implement controlled experiments varying only the human feedback component across otherwise identical model architectures to isolate its specific contribution to observed demographic biases
