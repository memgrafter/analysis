# Experimental observation on a low-rank tensor model for eigenvalue problems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.00538
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00538
- Reference count: 17
- Key outcome: Polynomial-based low-rank tensor models (PLTM) achieve relative errors of 10^-13 to 10^-16 for high-dimensional eigenvalue problems, significantly outperforming tensor neural networks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach for solving eigenvalue problems using low-rank tensor models with polynomial bases as function approximators. The method combines the low-rank tensor structure with polynomial basis functions to enable exact integration through orthogonality properties, avoiding the numerical integration required by tensor neural networks. Experiments demonstrate that PLTM achieves significantly higher accuracy (relative errors of 10^-13 to 10^-16) compared to TNN (10^-7 to 10^-6) for high-dimensional problems, while also providing faster training. The method is tested on Laplacian operators, harmonic oscillators, and MNIST classification, showing strong performance on eigenvalue problems but comparable results to TNN on classification tasks.

## Method Summary
The method uses low-rank tensor models with polynomial bases (PLTM) as function approximators for solving eigenvalue problems. The PLTM represents eigenfunctions as sums of tensor products of one-dimensional polynomial basis functions, enabling exact calculation of integrals through polynomial orthogonality. The model is trained using gradient descent optimization with the Adam optimizer to minimize a Rayleigh quotient formulation. For MNIST classification, the PLTM is combined with Softmax normalization and cross-entropy loss. The approach is compared against tensor neural networks (TNN) across various problem dimensions and tasks.

## Key Results
- PLTM achieves relative errors of 4.3×10^-16 and 1.2×10^-13 for Laplacian operator eigenvalue problems, compared to TNN's 1.6×10^-7
- For harmonic oscillator problems, PLTM achieves 3.5×10^-8 accuracy versus TNN's 8.5×10^-6
- PLTM training is faster than TNN due to exact integration avoiding numerical quadrature
- On MNIST classification, PLTM performs comparably to TNN but worse than nonlinear neural networks

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of polynomial basis (e.g., Legendre vs Chebyshev) affect the accuracy and convergence rate of PLTM for eigenvalue problems?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions using Legendre polynomials but does not explore other polynomial bases or compare their performance
- Why unresolved: The authors only test Legendre polynomials in their experiments, leaving open the question of whether other orthogonal polynomials might perform better for certain problems
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using different orthogonal polynomial bases (Legendre, Chebyshev, Hermite) on the same eigenvalue problems, measuring accuracy, convergence rate, and computational cost

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between the rank r and basis size b hyperparameters in PLTM, and how should they be optimally chosen for different problem dimensions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses fixed values of r=b=10 for d=10 and r=10, b=22 for d=512 without theoretical justification for these choices
- Why unresolved: The authors do not provide theoretical analysis or guidelines for selecting the rank and basis size, only empirical results with fixed values
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis of the approximation properties of PLTM relating r and b to problem dimension d, along with systematic experiments varying these hyperparameters across problem dimensions

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does PLTM perform on eigenvalue problems with non-separable potentials or complex geometries where the eigenfunctions are not products of one-dimensional functions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] All tested eigenvalue problems have separable forms (Laplacian, harmonic oscillator with separable potential)
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests problems where the exact eigenfunctions are products of one-dimensional functions, which may favor the tensor product structure of PLTM
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on eigenvalue problems with non-separable potentials (e.g., double-well potentials, magnetic fields) or complex geometries (non-rectangular domains), comparing PLTM performance to other methods

## Limitations
- The method shows mixed performance on MNIST classification, performing comparably to TNN but worse than nonlinear neural networks
- The paper only tests eigenvalue problems with separable forms, leaving open questions about performance on non-separable problems
- Implementation details of the low-rank tensor model structure are not fully specified in the paper

## Confidence

**High confidence** in the exact integration advantage due to polynomial orthogonality, supported by explicit mathematical derivations and experimental evidence showing PLTM's superior accuracy (relative errors of 10^-13 to 10^-16) compared to TNN (10^-7 to 10^-6) for high-dimensional eigenvalue problems. **Medium confidence** in the training speed advantage, as the paper claims faster training but provides limited quantitative comparison. **Medium confidence** in the overall method's applicability beyond eigenvalue problems, given the mixed results on MNIST classification.
