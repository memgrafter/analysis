# The Shaky Foundations of Clinical Foundation Models: A Survey of Large Language Models and Foundation Models for EMRs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.12961
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12961
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Comprehensive review of 84 clinical foundation models reveals inadequate evaluation frameworks focused on narrow tasks and small datasets

## Executive Summary
This survey analyzes over 80 clinical foundation models trained on non-imaging electronic medical record data, revealing critical gaps in how these models are evaluated. Most models rely on small, narrowly-scoped datasets like MIMIC-III or broad biomedical corpora like PubMed, and are assessed using tasks that fail to demonstrate meaningful clinical utility. The authors propose an improved evaluation framework emphasizing ranking-based metrics for capacity-constrained workflows, sample efficiency with limited training data, and human evaluation of novel AI interfaces.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a systematic review of 84 clinical foundation models (50 CLaMs, 34 FEMRs) from published literature, categorizing them by training data, model architecture, and evaluation tasks. They extracted current evaluation metrics and proposed an alternative framework focusing on clinical utility through six value propositions: predictive performance, sample efficiency, simplified deployment, emergent applications, multimodality, and novel interfaces. The analysis identified patterns in how these models are currently assessed and highlighted gaps in evaluation methodology.

## Key Results
- Most clinical foundation models are evaluated on tasks that don't reflect real-world clinical utility
- Current evaluations overly rely on a small number of datasets (MIMIC-III, PubMed)
- Proposed framework emphasizes ranking-based metrics, sample efficiency, and human evaluation
- Multimodal reasoning capabilities remain largely unexplored in current evaluations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Clinical foundation models can achieve better predictive performance than traditional ML models by leveraging larger training datasets and more model parameters
- Mechanism: The increased model capacity and exposure to diverse, large-scale clinical data allows FMs to learn more robust and generalizable representations of patient states, leading to improved accuracy in downstream prediction tasks
- Core assumption: Larger models trained on more data will consistently outperform smaller, task-specific models across a range of clinical prediction tasks
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We find that most models are trained on small, narrowly-scoped clinical datasets (e.g. MIMIC-III) or broad, public biomedical corpora (e.g. PubMed) and are evaluated on tasks that do not provide meaningful insights on their usefulness to health systems."
  - [section]: "By using larger training datasets and more model parameters, FMs can achieve better predictive performance (e.g., higher sensitivity and specificity on classification tasks) than traditional ML models."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. The corpus contains papers related to FMs and EMRs, but lacks specific studies directly comparing FM performance to traditional ML on clinical prediction tasks
- Break condition: If the increased model complexity leads to overfitting on small, biased datasets, or if the larger models require computational resources that are not available in clinical settings

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Clinical FMs can reduce the need for labeled data ("improved sample efficiency") by leveraging pre-training on large, unlabeled datasets
- Mechanism: Pre-training a model on a large corpus of clinical text or structured EMR data allows it to learn general patterns and representations that can be fine-tuned for specific tasks with limited labeled examples, reducing the cost and time required to build ML models
- Core assumption: The representations learned during pre-training are transferable to a wide range of downstream clinical tasks, allowing for effective fine-tuning with limited task-specific data
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Clinical FMs lack the shared evaluation frameworks and datasets that have underpinned progress in other fields such as natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision."
  - [section]: "By learning representations that are useful for many downstream tasks via self-supervised pre-training, FMs reduce the need to label large, task-specific training datasets and therefore reduce the cost of building ML models for any particular task."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. The corpus contains papers discussing FMs and EMRs, but lacks specific studies demonstrating improved sample efficiency of clinical FMs compared to traditional ML models
- Break condition: If the pre-training data is not representative of the target clinical tasks, or if the fine-tuning process requires a large amount of labeled data to achieve satisfactory performance

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Clinical FMs can enable novel human-AI interfaces through techniques like "prompting", allowing for more intuitive and flexible interactions with AI systems
- Mechanism: By accepting natural language inputs and generating responses in natural language, FMs can facilitate a two-way conversation between humans and machines, enabling users to decompose complex problems into smaller steps and receive interpretable outputs
- Core assumption: The ability to interact with FMs using natural language prompts will lead to more effective and efficient human-AI collaboration in clinical settings, compared to traditional ML models that require specific input formats and output interpretations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "In light of these findings, we propose an improved evaluation framework for measuring the benefits of clinical foundation models that is more closely grounded to metrics that matter in healthcare."
  - [section]: "Via a technique called 'prompting', a human can input natural language into an LLM and have the model respond in natural language. This enables a two-way conversation between human and machine, and allows for the decomposition of problems into smaller steps via techniques such as 'chain-of-thought' prompting."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. The corpus contains papers related to FMs and EMRs, but lacks specific studies evaluating the effectiveness of prompting-based interfaces in clinical settings
- Break condition: If the natural language inputs and outputs are not sufficiently accurate or reliable for clinical decision-making, or if the prompting-based interfaces introduce new cognitive burdens for users

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data ingestion pipeline -> Model training framework -> Evaluation framework -> Deployment infrastructure
- Critical path:
  1. Preprocess and normalize the clinical data from various sources
  2. Pre-train the foundation model on the large, unlabeled dataset
  3. Fine-tune the pre-trained model on specific clinical tasks with limited labeled data
  4. Evaluate the model using the proposed clinical utility metrics
  5. Deploy the model in a clinical setting, considering factors like latency, scalability, and privacy
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model size vs. computational resources: Larger models may achieve better performance but require more computational resources for training and inference
  - Pre-training data diversity vs. task specificity: Pre-training on more diverse data may improve generalization, but task-specific pre-training may be necessary for optimal performance on certain clinical tasks
  - Evaluation metrics vs. clinical utility: Balancing the need for standardized evaluation metrics with the importance of assessing real-world clinical utility
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on specific clinical tasks: May indicate issues with the pre-training data, fine-tuning process, or evaluation metrics
  - High computational requirements: May limit the practical applicability of the models in clinical settings with limited resources
  - Privacy concerns: May arise from the use of sensitive patient data in model training and deployment
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Pre-train a foundation model on a large, unlabeled clinical text dataset (e.g., MIMIC-III) and evaluate its performance on a set of clinical NLP tasks (e.g., named entity recognition, relation extraction) using standard metrics (e.g., F1 score)
  2. Fine-tune the pre-trained model on a specific clinical prediction task (e.g., 30-day readmission) with limited labeled data and compare its performance to a traditional ML model trained from scratch using the same amount of labeled data
  3. Evaluate the clinical utility of the fine-tuned model using the proposed ranking-based metrics and human evaluation, considering factors like the model's ability to prioritize high-risk patients in a capacity-constrained workflow

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What evaluation framework would most effectively demonstrate the clinical utility of foundation models in healthcare settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors explicitly propose an improved evaluation framework focusing on ranking-based metrics, sample efficiency, and human evaluation of novel human-AI interfaces
- Why unresolved: Current evaluation tasks are too narrow and do not reflect real-world clinical utility
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive evaluation study applying the proposed framework to multiple clinical foundation models, showing their effectiveness across diverse healthcare tasks

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can foundation models be effectively evaluated for their ability to reduce the need for labeled data in healthcare applications?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors identify this as one of the six primary value propositions but note minimal work has validated it
- Why unresolved: Most current evaluations focus on predictive accuracy rather than sample efficiency
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies showing foundation models maintaining performance with significantly fewer labeled examples than traditional ML models across multiple healthcare tasks

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal approach for enabling multimodal reasoning in clinical foundation models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that while multimodal models show substantial gains, most evaluations are unimodal
- Why unresolved: There is a lack of standardized evaluation scenarios requiring multimodal reasoning
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of multimodal evaluation benchmarks that require integration of multiple data types (text, structured data, etc.) for clinical decision-making tasks

## Limitations

- Limited empirical validation of proposed evaluation framework's correlation with real-world clinical outcomes
- Weak evidence base supporting core mechanisms, with minimal citation overlap in related literature
- Overreliance on small number of datasets (MIMIC-III, PubMed) in current evaluations

## Confidence

- High confidence: The observation that current evaluations are inadequate and overly reliant on narrow datasets (MIMIC-III, PubMed)
- Medium confidence: The proposed evaluation framework addresses real gaps in current methodology
- Low confidence: Claims about specific performance improvements from FMs over traditional ML on clinical prediction tasks
