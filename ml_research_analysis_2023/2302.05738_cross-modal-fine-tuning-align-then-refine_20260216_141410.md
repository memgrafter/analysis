# Cross-Modal Fine-Tuning: Align then Refine

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05738
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05738
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: ORCA achieves state-of-the-art results on three benchmarks containing over 60 datasets from 12 modalities, outperforming hand-designed, AutoML, general-purpose, and task-specific methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes ORCA, a general cross-modal fine-tuning framework that adapts a single large-scale pretrained transformer model to diverse modalities. ORCA employs an align-then-refine workflow: it first learns an embedding network to align the embedded feature distribution of the target task with that of the pretraining modality, and then fine-tunes the pretrained model on the embedded data. Through extensive experiments, ORCA achieves state-of-the-art results on three benchmarks containing over 60 datasets from 12 modalities, outperforming a wide range of hand-designed, AutoML, general-purpose, and task-specific methods. Ablation studies highlight the importance of data alignment, and experiments demonstrate ORCA's utility in data-limited regimes.

## Method Summary
ORCA adapts to a target task via an align-then-refine workflow. Given the target input, ORCA first learns an embedding network that aligns the embedded feature distribution with the pretraining modality. After training the embedder, ORCA performs full fine-tuning by updating all model parameters to minimize the target loss. The embedding learning stage uses optimal transport dataset distance (OTDD) to measure and minimize the distributional distance between embedded target features and source features. The full fine-tuning stage updates all parameters of the model to minimize the task-specific loss on the target dataset.

## Key Results
- ORCA achieves state-of-the-art results on three benchmarks containing over 60 datasets from 12 modalities
- Ablation studies confirm the importance of data alignment, with OTDD showing superior performance over other distance metrics
- ORCA demonstrates effectiveness in data-limited regimes compared to traditional fine-tuning methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Aligning the embedded feature distribution of the target task with the pretraining modality improves cross-modal fine-tuning performance.
- Mechanism: The embedding network learns to map target inputs into a feature space where the distribution resembles that of the pretraining modality. This prevents distortion of pretrained weights during fine-tuning and allows the model to exploit shared knowledge across modalities.
- Core assumption: The pretraining modality contains transferable knowledge that can be leveraged for the target task, and the embedding network can learn a mapping that brings the target distribution closer to the source.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "ORCA adapts to a target task via an align-then-refine workflow: given the target input, ORCA first learns an embedding network that aligns the embedded feature distribution with the pretraining modality."
  - [section 4.1]: "We find an empirical correlation between alignment quality and downstream accuracy. The fact that ORCA significantly outperforms naive fine-tuning demonstrates that data alignment is important."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Only 25 related papers found, average neighbor FMR=0.511. Limited direct discussion of distribution alignment mechanisms.
- Break condition: If the target and pretraining modalities are too dissimilar, the embedding network may fail to find a meaningful mapping, or the aligned distribution may not preserve task-relevant information.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Full fine-tuning of all model parameters (not just layer norms) leads to better cross-modal transfer performance than partial fine-tuning.
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning all parameters allows the model to fully adapt both the embedding network and the pretrained transformer layers to the target task, leveraging the aligned feature distributions learned during embedding.
- Core assumption: The pretrained model's weights contain useful inductive biases that can be adapted to the target task, and the aligned feature distributions provide a stable foundation for this adaptation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1]: "FPT differs from ORCA in that (1) it does not perform data alignment, and (2) it only fine-tunes the layer norms. We have verified the importance of (1). Now, we isolate the impact of (2) by fine-tuning only the layer norms for ORCA. The bottom rows of Table 3 show that ORCA with fine-tuning the layer norms outperforms FPT, so pretraining the embedder can boost the performance of FPT. However, this performance gain is smaller than that in the full fine-tuning setting, which implies that full fine-tuning can take better advantage of the learned embeddings."
  - [section 3.3]: "After training the embedder, we perform full fine-tuning by updating all model parameters to minimize the target loss."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence found in corpus about full vs. partial fine-tuning tradeoffs.
- Break condition: If computational resources are severely limited, full fine-tuning may be impractical, forcing reliance on partial fine-tuning methods like adapters.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Optimal Transport Dataset Distance (OTDD) is more effective than other distance metrics for aligning feature distributions in cross-modal fine-tuning.
- Mechanism: OTDD considers both feature and label distributions, aligning not just individual data points but also the high-level clustering structure of datasets. This preserves task-relevant information during alignment.
- Core assumption: The label distribution contains important information for aligning feature distributions, and OTDD can effectively capture this relationship.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.4]: "We evaluate the effectiveness of three distance metrics for data alignment during embedding learning: (1) the pairwise Euclidean distance, (2) the moment-based maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), and (3) optimal transport dataset distance (OTDD). The aggregate performance and per-task rankings show that embedder learning with OTDD has the best overall results, so we use it in our subsequent experiments."
  - [section 3.4]: "We conjecture that its good performance is due to how the label information is considered during alignment. Indeed, for both the source and target datasets, OTDD represents each class label as a distribution over the in-class features."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence found in corpus about OTDD effectiveness.
- Break condition: If computational resources are severely limited, the high cost of OTDD may make simpler metrics like Euclidean distance or MMD more practical despite lower performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Embedder → Transformer Body → Predictor → Loss Function
- Critical path: Data flows through embedder to get sequence features, through transformer body for processing, through predictor for task-specific output, and loss is computed for training
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Embedder complexity vs. computational cost: More complex embedders can better align distributions but increase training time
  - Pretrained model selection: Different pretrained models (RoBERTa vs. Swin) may be better suited for different input dimensions and modalities
  - Distance metric choice: OTDD provides better alignment but is computationally expensive; simpler metrics may be necessary for resource-constrained scenarios
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor alignment: High OTDD values during embedder learning correlate with poor downstream performance
  - Catastrophic forgetting: If learning rate is too high during fine-tuning, pretrained knowledge may be lost
  - Dimensionality mismatch: Incorrect embedder architecture may fail to properly transform inputs to match transformer expectations
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test embedder learning with OTDD on a simple 1D classification task (e.g., ECG) to verify distribution alignment works
  2. Compare full fine-tuning vs. layer-norm-only fine-tuning on a 2D task (e.g., CIFAR-100) to verify importance of full parameter updates
  3. Test different distance metrics (OTDD vs. MMD vs. Euclidean) on a mixed-dimension task suite to verify OTDD superiority

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal alignment metric for cross-modal fine-tuning, and how does it depend on the specific task and data characteristics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper evaluates three alignment metrics (Euclidean distance, MMD, and OTDD) and finds that OTDD performs best overall, but the authors acknowledge that designing more suitable distance metrics is an open direction.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a systematic framework for selecting the optimal alignment metric based on task-specific factors or data properties. The choice of metric may significantly impact downstream performance, but the relationship between metric selection and task characteristics remains unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing different alignment metrics across a wide range of tasks with varying data modalities, dimensionalities, and label distributions. Analysis of how task-specific factors (e.g., input complexity, label structure) influence the effectiveness of different metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we develop a systematic approach for selecting the optimal pretrained model for a given target task in cross-modal fine-tuning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that the choice of pretrained model affects performance, with models pretrained on similar modalities (e.g., RoBERTa for genomics sequences) performing better. However, the authors suggest this is an open direction for future work.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a clear methodology for selecting the most appropriate pretrained model beyond intuitive considerations like input dimensionality. A systematic approach could improve transfer learning efficiency and effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of a model selection framework that considers factors such as pretraining data characteristics, model architecture, and task similarity metrics. Experiments demonstrating improved performance through systematic model selection compared to ad-hoc choices.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can cross-modal fine-tuning be extended to high-dimensional problems and reinforcement learning tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors identify these as important future directions, noting that current experiments focus on 1D/2D tasks and that Reid et al. (2022) have explored cross-modal RL, but systematic evaluation remains to be done.
- Why unresolved: The paper's experiments are limited to lower-dimensional data, and the applicability of the ORCA framework to more complex problem domains is not fully explored. High-dimensional data and RL present unique challenges for cross-modal transfer.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful application of cross-modal fine-tuning to high-dimensional datasets (e.g., video, multi-sensor data) and RL environments. Experiments demonstrating improved sample efficiency or performance compared to traditional RL approaches or task-specific model design.

## Limitations

- The paper's primary limitation is the reliance on computational resources for both the embedding learning stage (which requires optimal transport computations) and full fine-tuning of all parameters
- The effectiveness of OTDD compared to simpler metrics remains theoretical without ablation studies on alternative distance measures in the main experiments
- The claim of "state-of-the-art" results across 60+ datasets needs verification, as the evaluation focuses on aggregate performance rather than detailed per-dataset analysis

## Confidence

- High confidence: The align-then-refine workflow concept and its implementation are well-defined. The paper clearly explains the embedder learning stage, full fine-tuning procedure, and the use of OTDD for distribution alignment.
- Medium confidence: The empirical results showing performance improvements over baselines are compelling, but the comparison with other distribution alignment methods (beyond FPT) is limited. The claim about OTDD superiority is supported by internal experiments but lacks external validation.
- Low confidence: The generalization claim across "12 modalities" is not fully substantiated with detailed per-modality analysis. The computational cost implications of full fine-tuning vs. parameter-efficient methods are not thoroughly discussed.
