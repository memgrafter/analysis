# Reproducibility of Machine Learning: Terminology, Recommendations and Open Issues

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12691
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12691
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Addresses reproducibility challenges in AI/ML by analyzing terminology, systematizing recommendations, and identifying overlooked technical issues like randomness and indeterminism.

## Executive Summary
This survey systematically reviews reproducibility challenges in modern AI/ML, with emphasis on deep learning, reinforcement learning, and biomedical/physical AI applications. The authors analyze terminological ambiguities, categorize existing recommendations, and identify overlooked technical issues such as randomness and non-determinism. They propose extended guidelines with actionable implementation via metadata, platforms, or documentation, and provide detailed recommendations for biomedical and physical AI contexts. Key findings include the limited support of ML platforms for reproducibility features, the critical role of managing randomness and indeterminism, and the need for standardized evaluation protocols.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a systematic literature review with elements of narrative review, analyzing 40 references on reproducibility in AI/ML. They identified terminological ambiguities, categorized recommendations into Experiment, Method, and Data categories, and proposed novel guidelines addressing overlooked technical issues. The review examined survey studies, critical analyses, checklists, and guidelines to synthesize current state-of-the-art practices and identify gaps in reproducibility support across different ML domains.

## Key Results
- Randomness and non-determinism are identified as critical technical issues compromising reproducibility in DL experiments
- Existing ML platforms provide limited native support for reproducibility features, often relying on third-party services
- Metadata standards (PROV-O, DCAT, DQV) are essential but not widely adopted for capturing AI experiment provenance and evaluation metrics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Randomness originates from pseudo-random number generators in training pipelines (e.g., weight initialization, data shuffling), while non-determinism arises from GPU operations, parallel processing, and floating-point operation ordering that differ across runs. If seeds are fixed and deterministic implementations are chosen, results will be repeatable within the same hardware/software stack. Break condition: Different GPU architectures, software libraries with auto-tuning, or unpatched floating-point non-determinism will still cause divergence.

### Mechanism 2
Standardized metadata schemas capture provenance, environment, hyperparameters, and evaluation metrics, enabling others to reconstruct experiments even if exact bit-for-bit results aren't possible. Metadata completeness and adherence to community standards allow meaningful comparison and validation across independent runs. Break condition: Metadata schemas are incomplete, incompatible, or not adopted by the community, rendering them ineffective.

### Mechanism 3
Containers bundle code, data, and dependencies, freezing the runtime environment. Advanced tools like DetTrace ensure deterministic execution of Linux API calls and x86-64 instructions, mitigating hardware-induced variance. If the entire execution environment is reproducible, then the same inputs yield the same outputs, regardless of underlying hardware differences. Break condition: Containers cannot fully encapsulate hardware-specific behaviors (e.g., GPU kernels), or the tool introduces unacceptable overhead.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data layer (raw datasets, preprocessing scripts, cleaned data, metadata descriptors) -> Model layer (neural network architecture definitions, hyperparameter configurations, training scripts, random seeds) -> Environment layer (software dependencies, container definitions, hardware specs, execution traces) -> Documentation layer (experiment reports, evaluation metrics, provenance logs, code repositories)

- **Critical path**:
  1. Fix random seeds in all stochastic components.
  2. Capture and serialize all hyperparameters and data splits.
  3. Containerize the entire runtime environment.
  4. Generate comprehensive metadata using FAIR principles.
  5. Publish code, data, and metadata with persistent identifiers.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Deterministic vs. stochastic training: Determinism aids reproducibility but may hurt model performance.
  - Full environment capture vs. portability: Containers ensure reproducibility but may be heavy to distribute.
  - Metadata verbosity vs. usability: Rich metadata aids validation but increases overhead.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Slight metric variance across runs → likely randomness in data ordering or initialization.
  - Completely different results → probable non-deterministic ops or environment drift.
  - Missing evaluation details → likely insufficient documentation.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run the same training script twice with fixed seeds; compare loss curves and final metrics.
  2. Containerize the training environment; run inside and outside the container; compare results.
  3. Export experiment metadata using a FAIR-compliant schema; attempt to reconstruct the experiment on a fresh machine.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How can reproducibility guidelines be standardized across different AI/ML platforms to ensure consistent implementation of metadata, versioning, and containerization features? Current platforms rely on third-party services or lack native support for essential reproducibility features, leading to inconsistent practices. Comparative studies evaluating the reproducibility features of major ML platforms and the development of unified metadata standards would resolve this.

### Open Question 2
What specific measures can be implemented to address randomness and indeterminism in deep learning models to ensure reproducible training outcomes? Existing guidelines often overlook these technical issues, and solutions like deterministic implementations and record-and-replay techniques are not widely adopted. Empirical studies demonstrating the effectiveness of proposed solutions in mitigating randomness and achieving reproducible training results would resolve this.

### Open Question 3
How can data sharing and privacy concerns be balanced in biomedical AI applications to enable reproducibility without compromising sensitive information? Current anonymization techniques may hinder data utility, and there is no consensus on best practices for sharing sensitive biomedical data. Case studies of successful data sharing initiatives in biomedical AI that maintain privacy while enabling reproducibility would resolve this.

### Open Question 4
What are the most effective strategies for ensuring reproducibility in reinforcement learning experiments, particularly in physical AI systems? RL experiments are prone to additional sources of non-determinism, and existing guidelines focus more on documentation than technical solutions. Comparative analyses of reproducibility practices in RL experiments across different platforms and environments would resolve this.

## Limitations
- The survey's recommendations for biomedical and physical AI are likely based on expert opinion rather than systematic evaluation, as indicated by weak corpus signals for these domains
- The practical impact of proposed metadata standards (PROV-O, DCAT, DQV) on actual reproducibility outcomes has not been demonstrated through field studies
- The assertion that containerization tools like DetTrace fully solve reproducibility appears to be a novel claim without supporting citations in the corpus

## Confidence

**Major Uncertainties:**
- The extent to which randomness and non-determinism are the dominant reproducibility barriers versus documentation and metadata issues remains unclear without empirical benchmarking across different ML domains

**Confidence Assessment:**
- **High Confidence**: The identification of randomness and non-determinism as critical technical issues in DL reproducibility, supported by the explicit focus on these factors in the abstract and corpus analysis
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that standardized metadata can bridge reproducibility gaps, based on theoretical alignment with FAIR principles but lacking direct empirical validation in the corpus
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that containerization tools like DetTrace fully solve reproducibility, as this appears to be a novel claim without supporting citations in the corpus
