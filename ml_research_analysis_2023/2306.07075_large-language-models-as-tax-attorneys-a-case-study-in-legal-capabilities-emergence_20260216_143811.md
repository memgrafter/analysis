# Large Language Models as Tax Attorneys: A Case Study in Legal Capabilities Emergence

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07075
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07075
- Reference count: 0
- Large language models show improved legal reasoning capabilities with increased model size and relevant context.

## Executive Summary
This study systematically evaluates the legal analysis capabilities of large language models (LLMs) on thousands of tax law questions. The research tests retrieval-augmented generation and various prompting techniques across four OpenAI models from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4. Results demonstrate that newer, more capable models achieve significantly higher accuracy, especially when provided with relevant legal text and effective prompting strategies. GPT-4 performs best, particularly when combined with gold-truth legal sources, few-shot examples, and step-by-step reasoning. The findings suggest that legal reasoning capabilities emerge as models grow in size and sophistication, though expert-level performance remains unattained.

## Method Summary
The study employs retrieval-augmented generation using similarity search, gold truth, or lecture notes, combined with prompting techniques including chain-of-thought and few-shot learning. Experiments run across OpenAI models (davinci, text-davinci-002, gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4) with different retrieval and prompting setups. A GPT-4 grader evaluates outputs against ground truth. The methodology includes generating synthetic multiple-choice tax law questions, creating vector databases from U.S. Code and CFR subsections using GTR-large embeddings, and implementing various retrieval and prompting strategies.

## Key Results
- GPT-4 significantly outperforms earlier models when provided with relevant legal context and effective prompting
- Retrieval-augmented generation with gold truth sources substantially improves performance
- Chain-of-thought prompting enhances reasoning only when the model has sufficient underlying capability

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves legal reasoning accuracy when the retrieved legal text is directly relevant to the question.
- Mechanism: The LLM receives targeted legal text via vector similarity search, providing explicit grounding that supplements or replaces its internal knowledge.
- Core assumption: The retrieval model (GTR-large) can find the correct legal subsection from the vector database for a given question.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We experiment with retrieving and utilising the relevant legal authority to assess the impact of providing additional legal context to LLMs."
  - [section] "When a question is presented as input, our system retrieves the four most 'relevant' documents from the vector store, where relevance is estimated based on the cosine similarity between the documents and the question."
- Break condition: Retrieval returns irrelevant or no documents, or the LLM ignores the context.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting enhances reasoning only when the model has sufficient underlying capability.
- Mechanism: The LLM is prompted to "think step-by-step" before answering, producing intermediate reasoning steps that improve final answer accuracy.
- Core assumption: The LLM has the capacity to perform multi-step reasoning and benefits from structured thought.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Few-shot prompting, presenting examples of question-answer pairs, is also found to significantly enhance the performance of the most advanced model, GPT-4."
  - [section] "CoT does boost the performance of GPT-4, though. This suggests an LLM might need to have a certain capability level to be able to exhibit improved performance through additional reasoning."
- Break condition: Model lacks reasoning capability or CoT prompt is poorly formatted.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Few-shot prompting guides the LLM toward the desired task format and improves performance, especially when combined with relevant legal context.
- Mechanism: Providing 3 example question-answer pairs primes the model's expectations and format, improving alignment with the task.
- Core assumption: The model can generalize from few examples to new, similar questions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Few-shot prompting, presenting examples of question-answer pairs, is also found to significantly enhance the performance of the most advanced model, GPT-4."
  - [section] "The 'mega_run' experiment combines 'gold_truth' sources with few-shot and CoT prompting. As evidenced by Figure 3, GPT-4 can leverage relevant legal text and examples of the question-and-answer task to 'reason' and come to a correct answer a large proportion of the time on difficult tax questions."
- Break condition: Examples are poorly chosen or mismatched to question type.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Question generator -> Vector database -> Retrieval module -> Prompt template engine -> LLM interface -> GPT-4 grader
- Critical path: Generate question → retrieve context → construct prompt → send to LLM → grade response
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Smaller context windows require truncation of retrieved text.
  - "Gold truth" retrieval bypasses the vector database but is not scalable for real-world deployment.
  - CoT improves performance but adds prompt length and complexity.
- Failure signatures:
  - Low accuracy despite "gold truth" retrieval → LLM reasoning failure.
  - Inconsistent accuracy across question types → Retrieval relevance issues.
  - Performance drops with longer prompts → Context window saturation.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test bypass_retrieval baseline: Ask LLM questions with no additional context to measure internal knowledge.
  2. Test similarity_search with a small subset of questions to validate retrieval relevance.
  3. Test gold_truth retrieval on a few questions to establish upper bound on performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do more advanced prompting techniques like self-reflection and self-refinement impact legal reasoning accuracy compared to basic prompting methods?
- Basis in paper: Inferred - The paper mentions advanced prompting techniques like self-reflection and self-refinement as promising directions for future work, but does not test them.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested basic prompting techniques (few-shot, chain-of-thought) and did not explore more sophisticated methods that could potentially yield higher accuracy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments comparing basic prompting methods to advanced techniques like self-reflection and self-refinement on the same tax law question set would show their relative impact on accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal context window size and token allocation for legal document retrieval in LLMs?
- Basis in paper: Inferred - The paper mentions that newer models like GPT-4 have wider context windows, but does not investigate the optimal window size or token allocation for legal retrieval tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper did not systematically vary context window size or token allocation to determine optimal settings for legal document retrieval in LLMs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Running experiments with different context window sizes and token allocations for legal document retrieval across multiple LLM models would identify optimal settings.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do domain-specific legal embeddings compare to general-purpose embeddings for legal document retrieval in LLMs?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper states it is currently developing "in-domain" embeddings for follow-up work, implying that current experiments use general-purpose embeddings.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses general-purpose embeddings for document retrieval but has not yet tested domain-specific legal embeddings that may perform better for legal tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing the performance of general-purpose vs. domain-specific legal embeddings for document retrieval on the same tax law question set would show their relative effectiveness.

## Limitations
- The synthetic nature of tax questions may not fully represent real-world legal scenario complexity
- Reliance on GPT-4 as a grader introduces potential circularity in evaluation
- Vector database covers only U.S. tax law, limiting applicability to other legal domains

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: GPT-4 significantly outperforms earlier models with relevant context and effective prompting
- **Medium Confidence**: Retrieval-augmented generation with gold truth sources substantially improves performance
- **Medium Confidence**: Chain-of-thought prompting effectiveness for legal reasoning tasks
