# Multiclass classification for multidimensional functional data through deep neural networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.13349
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13349
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Proposed deep neural network approach for multiclass classification of multidimensional functional data, achieving 98.3% accuracy on MNIST vs 87.5% for sparse discriminant analysis

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel deep neural network framework for classifying multidimensional functional data by first extracting functional principal component scores and then training a sparse ReLU network with cross-entropy loss. The method addresses the challenge of infinite-dimensional functional features by projecting them onto a finite-dimensional space via Karhunen-Loève decomposition, making standard neural network architectures applicable. Theoretical convergence rates for misclassification risk are derived under assumptions of Hölder smoothness and compositional structure, with empirical validation showing superior performance to existing sparse discriminant methods.

## Method Summary
The proposed approach consists of two main steps: (1) Extract functional principal component scores from multidimensional functional observations using tensor-based Karhunen-Loève decomposition, projecting infinite-dimensional curves onto leading eigenfunctions; (2) Train a sparse deep neural network with ReLU activation on these scores, using softmax output and cross-entropy loss for multiclass classification. The method handles both fully observed and discretely sampled functional data, with theoretical guarantees on convergence rates that depend on smoothness parameters and effective dimensionality.

## Key Results
- Achieves 98.3% testing accuracy on MNIST handwritten digits dataset compared to 87.5% for sparse discriminant analysis
- Theoretical convergence rates for misclassification risk derived for both fully observed and discretely observed multidimensional functional data
- Superior performance on simulated 2D and 3D Gaussian data compared to existing sparse discriminant analysis methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Extracting functional principal component scores converts infinite-dimensional functional data into finite-dimensional vectors that can be processed by standard deep neural networks.
- Mechanism: By projecting functional observations onto the leading eigenfunctions of the covariance operator, we capture the dominant modes of variation in the data. This projection truncates the infinite-dimensional representation to a manageable finite-dimensional one while preserving the essential structure needed for classification.
- Core assumption: The first J principal components capture sufficient information for classification, and the truncation error is bounded.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The intrinsically infinite-dimensional features of the functional observations over multidimensional domains render the standard classification methods effectively inapplicable."
  - [section 2.2] "Let ˆξ(i) J = (ˆξi1, . . . , ˆξiJ)⊺ be the J-dimensional truncation of ˆξ(i) for i = 1, . . . , n."
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct corpus evidence for FPC truncation in classification contexts; this is the novel contribution.
- Break condition: If the leading principal components don't capture the discriminative information, or if the truncation dimension J is too small, classification accuracy will degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sparse deep neural networks with ReLU activation and cross-entropy loss can approximate complex, non-Gaussian conditional class probability functions.
- Mechanism: The ReLU network architecture provides universal approximation capabilities for continuous functions, while sparsity controls model complexity. Cross-entropy loss directly optimizes the likelihood of correct classification, making it well-suited for probabilistic decision boundaries.
- Core assumption: The true conditional probabilities πk(x) belong to a Hölder-smooth function class with compositional structure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This neural network architecture allows us to employ modern computational tools in the implementation."
  - [section 2.2] "For L≥ 1,p = (p1, . . . , pL) ∈ NL, let F(L, J,p) denote the class of fully connected feedforward DNN with J inputs, L hidden layers..."
  - [section 3.2.2] "Given an absolute constant C0 ≥ 2, for any classifier ˆπ, define the truncated Kullback-Leibler risk..."
- Break condition: If the conditional probabilities are too irregular (non-Hölder) or have too complex structure, the approximation error will be too large for practical sample sizes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The theoretical convergence rates depend on the smoothness of conditional probabilities and the effective dimensionality of the problem.
- Mechanism: The misclassification risk bound involves terms like n^{-θ} where θ depends on the Hölder smoothness β and the number of effective arguments t. This provides a quantifiable relationship between data complexity and required sample size.
- Core assumption: The effective dimensionality t is small relative to the ambient dimension J, and smoothness parameters β are known or estimable.
- Evidence anchors:

- Break condition: If the effective dimensionality t is large or smoothness is low, the convergence rate becomes impractically slow.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Functional data -> FPC scores via Karhunen-Loève decomposition -> Sparse ReLU DNN with softmax output -> Cross-entropy loss minimization
- Critical path: Preprocess data → Extract FPCs → Train sparse DNN → Validate → Predict
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Truncation dimension J vs. approximation error: Higher J captures more information but increases computational cost
  - Network depth L vs. width p vs. sparsity s: Deeper networks can capture more complex patterns but are harder to train
  - Sampling frequency m vs. discretization error: Higher m provides better approximation of functional curves but increases data collection cost
- Failure signatures:
  - High training accuracy but low test accuracy: Overfitting, reduce network capacity or increase regularization
  - Both training and test accuracy low: Underfitting, increase network capacity or improve preprocessing
  - Very slow convergence: Learning rate too small or network too deep for available data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the impact of the choice of the truncation parameter J on the performance of the mfDNN classifier, and how can we optimally select J in practice?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the theoretical importance of J in the convergence rates of the misclassification risk, and mentions that the selection range of J is based on the asymptotic order provided in Assumptions 4 and 5. However, it also mentions that the selection of J in practice is not directly addressed.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides theoretical insights into the role of J but does not offer practical guidance on how to select it optimally in real-world applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the performance of mfDNN with different values of J on various datasets would provide insights into the optimal selection of J.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of the mfDNN classifier compare to other state-of-the-art methods for functional data classification, such as those based on functional principal component analysis (FPCA) or functional partial least squares (FPLS)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares the mfDNN classifier to sparse discriminant analysis and ℓ1 penalized Fisher's discriminant analysis methods, but does not include comparisons to other popular methods like FPCA or FPLS.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on comparing mfDNN to methods that treat functional data as high-dimensional vectors, but does not explore its performance against methods specifically designed for functional data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies on benchmark datasets, including those commonly used in functional data analysis, would provide a comprehensive evaluation of mfDNN's performance relative to other methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of the mfDNN classifier change with different activation functions, such as sigmoid or tanh, compared to the ReLU activation function used in the paper?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses ReLU activation function in the mfDNN classifier and provides theoretical analysis based on this choice. However, it does not explore the impact of different activation functions on the performance of the classifier.
- Why unresolved: The choice of activation function can significantly impact the performance of neural networks, and it is unclear whether ReLU is the optimal choice for the mfDNN classifier.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the performance of mfDNN with different activation functions on various datasets would provide insights into the optimal choice of activation function.

## Limitations
- Performance heavily depends on the assumption that leading principal components capture sufficient discriminative information, which may not hold for all functional datasets
- Theoretical convergence rates require specific smoothness conditions on conditional probabilities that may not be satisfied in practical applications
- The superiority claims lack sufficient experimental detail for independent verification of the 98.3% vs 87.5% accuracy difference

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The neural network architecture and training procedure are standard and well-established. The FPC extraction method is mathematically sound given the assumptions.
- Medium Confidence: The theoretical convergence rates appear mathematically rigorous but depend heavily on unvalidated assumptions about functional data structure and smoothness parameters.
- Low Confidence: The practical superiority claims (98.3% vs 87.5% accuracy) lack sufficient experimental detail for independent verification.
