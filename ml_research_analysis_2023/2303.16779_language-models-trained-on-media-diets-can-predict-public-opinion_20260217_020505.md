# Language Models Trained on Media Diets Can Predict Public Opinion

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.16779
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16779
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Media diet models predict survey response distributions with correlation r=0.458 for COVID-19 attitudes

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach using language models adapted to specific media diets to predict public opinion. The core idea is to fine-tune a language model like BERT on news articles from particular sources, then use it to score survey responses. Results show that these media diet models can predict survey response distributions with a correlation of r=0.458 for COVID-19 attitudes and r=0.104 for consumer confidence, though accuracy varies by question type. The method is robust to paraphrasing and across media sources, and is more accurate for people paying closer attention to news. This suggests AI can emulate subpopulation opinions based on media exposure, with potential applications in supplementing polls and forecasting public opinion.

## Method Summary
The approach involves fine-tuning a pretrained language model like BERT on news articles from specific media outlets (CNN, Fox News, NYT, NPR) to create "media diet" models. These models are then probed with survey questions converted to fill-in-the-blank prompts, scoring responses by predicting masked words. Scores are normalized by a baseline BERT model and aggregated using synonym-grouping to handle phrasing variations. The method is tested on U.S. survey data from Pew Research Center (COVID-19 attitudes) and University of Michigan (consumer confidence), comparing model predictions against actual survey response distributions.

## Key Results
- Media diet models achieve correlation r=0.458 with COVID-19 attitude survey distributions
- Model predictions are robust to paraphrasing and across different media sources
- Accuracy improves significantly when focusing on respondents who pay closer attention to news

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Media diet models predict public opinion because they capture the semantic content of news messaging that influences subpopulations.
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning a pretrained language model like BERT on news articles from specific outlets updates the model's representations to reflect the framing and emphasis present in that media diet, which correlates with the opinions of people consuming that media.
- Core assumption: The language model's internal representations are sufficiently rich to encode subtle shifts in framing that align with human opinion formation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "probing language models provides a powerful new method for investigating media effects" and "suggests a need for further study of the surprising fidelity with which neural language models can predict human responses."
  - [section]: "Our studies indicate that this approach is (1) predictive of human judgements found in survey response distributions and robust to phrasing and channels of media exposure"
  - [corpus]: Found related papers on LLMs predicting public opinions; neighbor titles show active research, but no direct citations yet.
- Break condition: If the language model lacks exposure to relevant real-world concepts or framing, or if media effects are minimal for the opinion type, predictive accuracy drops.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Synonym-grouping improves prediction accuracy by mitigating surface form competition.
- Mechanism: Grouping probabilities over synonyms of target words captures the overall meaning rather than relying on exact phrasing, making the probing method more robust to wording differences.
- Core assumption: People's responses cluster around conceptual meanings rather than precise lexical choices.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "robust to phrasing" indicates tolerance to wording changes.
  - [section]: "our synonym-grouping method (for computing probability-based media diet scores) helps produce significantly stronger correlations (r=0.458 vs r=0.190)"
  - [corpus]: Neighbor paper "Surface form competition: Why the highest probability answer isn't always right" supports this mechanism.
- Break condition: If synonym sets are incomplete or mismatched to survey phrasing, the grouping could mislead rather than help.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Attention to news moderates the strength of media diet predictions.
- Mechanism: People who pay closer attention to news internalize media framing more strongly, so media diet model predictions align better with their survey responses.
- Core assumption: Media effects are stronger for attentive audiences, weaker for casual consumers.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "more accurate at modeling people who follow media more closely"
  - [section]: "combining the model score with this attention value results in a particularly predictive, statistically significant feature (β=0.523)"
  - [corpus]: No direct citations yet; neighbor papers focus on bias and simulation rather than attention moderation.
- Break condition: If attention measurement is inaccurate or media diet models cannot differentiate subtle attention levels, predictive gains diminish.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pretrained BERT -> Fine-tuning on media diet -> Probing with survey prompts -> Score normalization -> Synonym grouping -> Correlation with survey proportions
- Critical path: Media diet model training -> Prompt construction -> Score calculation -> Correlation analysis
- Design tradeoffs: Synonym grouping increases robustness but adds preprocessing complexity; fine-tuning on more outlets increases coverage but requires more compute.
- Failure signatures: Low correlation with survey data; high variance across outlets; sensitivity to minor phrasing changes despite synonym grouping.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How much do language models trained on media diets capture actual causal relationships between media exposure and public opinion versus merely correlational patterns?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that while correlations exist, "our studies are not field experiments and do not measure causality," and acknowledges that media diet models "could help identify subpopulations being exposed to potentially harmful messaging" without establishing causation.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses observational data and correlation analyses rather than controlled experiments, making it impossible to definitively establish whether media causes opinion changes or merely correlates with them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled field experiments or natural experiments that randomly assign different media diets to subpopulations, followed by tracking opinion changes over time, would establish causal relationships.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would results differ if using more powerful language models like GPT-3 or PaLM instead of BERT for media diet modeling?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states "This trend may continue with the even more powerful large language models mentioned above, which are built using fundamentally similar technology and model architectures."
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested BERT and N-gram models, leaving open the question of whether larger, more sophisticated models would show stronger correlations or different patterns in media effects prediction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison studies using identical datasets and methodologies with various language models (BERT, GPT-3, PaLM, etc.) would reveal performance differences and potentially uncover new capabilities or limitations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent do filter bubbles and echo chambers affect the predictive accuracy of media diet models across different subpopulations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses "selective exposure," "echo chambers," and "filter bubbles" as "three related problems" that could affect results, noting that "people gravitate towards information that is congruent with their prior beliefs."
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges these phenomena, it doesn't systematically measure or quantify their impact on model accuracy across different demographic groups or media sources.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing model accuracy across subpopulations with known levels of media diversity exposure, or incorporating measures of ideological diversity in media consumption, would reveal how filter bubbles affect predictive power.

## Limitations

- Correlation coefficients are modest (r=0.458 for COVID-19, r=0.104 for consumer confidence), suggesting the approach works better for certain opinion types than others
- Method relies heavily on accurate media diet characterization - predictions degrade significantly if media consumption data is noisy or mismatched to actual exposure medium
- Study focuses on U.S. media and survey data, limiting generalizability to other cultural contexts

## Confidence

- Medium Confidence: The core mechanism that fine-tuned media diet models predict survey responses better than base BERT
- High Confidence: The synonym-grouping approach improves prediction accuracy compared to exact-word scoring
- Medium Confidence: The attention moderation effect, where predictions align better with attentive news consumers
