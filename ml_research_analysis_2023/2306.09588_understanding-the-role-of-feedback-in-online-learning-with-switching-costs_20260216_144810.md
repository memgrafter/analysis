# Understanding the Role of Feedback in Online Learning with Switching Costs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2306.09588
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09588
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: Feedback type and amount significantly impact regret in online learning with switching costs, with phase transitions occurring at specific budget thresholds

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how different feedback types and observation budgets affect regret in online learning with switching costs. The authors establish a phase transition at B = Θ(T^{2/3}) where extra observations change the regret scaling from Θ(T^{2/3}) to Θ(T/√B). They also show that bandit feedback alone becomes insufficient for optimal regret when the budget is large in the switching costs setting, unlike in standard online learning. The paper proposes a generic algorithmic framework incorporating batching, shrinking dartboard, and flexible feedback types that achieves matching upper bounds for both settings.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a generic algorithmic framework based on batched Online Mirror Descent with a Shrinking Dartboard (SD) technique. The algorithm divides T rounds into N batches, maintains action probability distributions, and incorporates an SD indicator that allows action persistence across batches with probability exp(-η · bℓb). For loss estimation, the algorithm constructs unbiased estimates from observed losses using importance weighting for bandit feedback. The framework supports three feedback types: full-information (observing all losses), bandit (observing only selected action's loss), and flexible (randomly selected observation sets). The method achieves minimax regret bounds of Θ(T^{2/3}) for bandit learning with extra observations when the budget is small, improving to Θ(T/√B) when the budget is large, and Θ(T/√B) for online learning with total observation budget regardless of feedback type.

## Key Results
- For bandit learning with extra observations, regret transitions from Θ(T^{2/3}) to Θ(T/√B) at the phase transition point B = Θ(T^{2/3})
- For online learning with total observation budget B, minimax regret is Θ(T/√B) regardless of feedback type
- Bandit feedback alone cannot achieve optimal regret when the budget is large in the switching costs setting
- The proposed generic algorithmic framework achieves matching upper bounds for both settings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The phase transition at B = Θ(T^{2/3}) occurs because below this threshold, switching costs dominate regret bounds, while above it, extra observations eliminate the need for costly exploration switches.
- **Mechanism**: In the bandit setting, useful information is only gained when the algorithm switches actions, incurring a switching cost. Below the phase transition, the budget B is too small to provide enough free exploration, so regret remains at Θ(T^{2/3}). Above it, extra observations provide sufficient distinguishability without requiring switches, reducing regret to Θ(T/√B).
- **Core assumption**: Extra observations provide independent information about action quality without incurring switching costs.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] states "when Bex = O(T^{2/3}), the regret remains Θ(T^{2/3}), but when Bex = Θ(T^{2/3}), it becomes Θ(T/√Bex)" and [section 3.1] provides the formal theorem stating this phase transition.
- **Break condition**: If observations are not truly independent of the action sequence, or if switching costs apply to observation actions as well.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Flexible feedback (not limited to full-information) can achieve the same regret bound as full-information feedback in online learning with switching costs.
- **Mechanism**: By choosing observation sets uniformly at random from subsets of [K] with the same cardinality M, the algorithm can construct unbiased estimates of batch-average losses. This preserves the information content while avoiding the need for full-information feedback, maintaining the Θ(T/√B) regret bound.
- **Core assumption**: Random uniform selection of observation sets provides sufficient information for learning without requiring full feedback.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 4.2] states "it suffices to have an observation set chosen uniformly at random from all subsets of[K] with the same cardinality" and [section 4.3] shows bandit feedback achieves optimal regret when B = O(K^{1/3}T^{2/3}).
- **Break condition**: If the random observation sets fail to provide sufficient coverage of action space, or if the unbiased estimation fails due to dependencies.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The Shrinking Dartboard (SD) technique reduces switching costs without compromising learning by maintaining action probability distributions while encouraging action persistence.
- **Mechanism**: SD generalizes the Hedge algorithm by adding a probability of keeping the current action across batches. This reduces the number of switches while preserving the marginal distribution of actions, allowing the algorithm to benefit from both low switching costs and proper exploration.
- **Core assumption**: Maintaining marginal action distributions while reducing switches does not harm the learning process.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 4.2] describes SD as "a calibrated technique for controlling the number of action switches" and shows "with probability exp(-η · bℓb), keep action Ab+1 = Ab; otherwise, sample action Ab+1 ~ wb+1".
- **Break condition**: If the probability of staying with the same action becomes too high, causing the algorithm to get stuck with suboptimal actions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Loss generation -> Observation selection -> Loss estimation -> OMD update -> Action selection -> Switching cost calculation

- **Critical path**: The most critical sequence is: choose observation set → observe losses → construct unbiased estimate → update weights → decide next action. Each step must be executed correctly to maintain the regret bounds.

- **Design tradeoffs**: Full-information feedback gives better bounds but requires more observations; bandit feedback is cheaper but only works well for smaller budgets; flexible feedback balances these by randomizing observation sets.

- **Failure signatures**: If regret exceeds the theoretical bounds, check: (1) whether the unbiased estimation is correctly implemented, (2) if the observation sets are being chosen properly, (3) whether the learning rate η is set correctly for the given budget B.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can bandit feedback alone achieve optimal regret when the total observation budget B is large in the standard setting without switching costs?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper shows that in the setting with switching costs, bandit feedback is no longer sufficient to guarantee optimal regret when the budget is large (B = Ω(K^(1/3)T^(2/3))). However, in the standard setting without switching costs, all three types of feedback (full-information, flexible, and bandit) can achieve optimal regret in the full range of B.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not provide a specific threshold for B beyond which bandit feedback becomes insufficient to achieve optimal regret in the standard setting without switching costs.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A detailed analysis showing the exact scaling of the regret under bandit feedback as a function of B in the standard setting without switching costs, and comparing it to the optimal regret.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the impact of different types of feedback (bandit, full-information, flexible) on the regret when the total observation budget B is small in the setting with switching costs?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper shows that when B is small (B = O(K^(1/3)T^(2/3))), bandit feedback can still achieve optimal regret in the setting with switching costs. However, it does not provide a detailed comparison of the regret under different types of feedback in this regime.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper focuses on the case when B is large and does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the regret under different types of feedback when B is small.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A detailed analysis comparing the regret under bandit, full-information, and flexible feedback when B is small in the setting with switching costs.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How does the choice of observation set (Oub) in Algorithm 1 affect the regret in the setting with switching costs and a total observation budget?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper proposes a generic algorithmic framework that incorporates the choice of observation set as one of its key components. It shows that different choices of observation set (e.g., full-information, flexible) can lead to different regret guarantees.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the impact of different choices of observation set on the regret. It only shows the regret guarantees for a few specific choices of observation set.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A detailed analysis showing the regret as a function of the choice of observation set (Oub) in Algorithm 1 in the setting with switching costs and a total observation budget.

## Limitations

- The phase transition claim at B = Θ(T^{2/3}) for bandit learning with extra observations lacks direct empirical validation
- The practical effectiveness of the Shrinking Dartboard technique in reducing switching costs while maintaining learning performance is not quantified
- The theoretical constructions for flexible feedback achieving the same regret as full-information feedback may not hold in all practical scenarios

## Confidence

- **Regret bounds**: High confidence - supported by formal proofs and established techniques in online learning
- **Phase transition**: Medium confidence - theoretically proven but lacks empirical validation
- **Flexible feedback effectiveness**: Medium confidence - relies on theoretical constructions that may not generalize
- **Shrinking Dartboard practical impact**: Medium confidence - theoretical mechanism is sound but practical benefits need quantification
