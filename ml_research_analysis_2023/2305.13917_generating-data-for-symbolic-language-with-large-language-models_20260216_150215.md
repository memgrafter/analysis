# Generating Data for Symbolic Language with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.13917
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13917
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: SYMGEN generates symbolic language data using LLMs that trains 1% sized task models to achieve comparable or better performance than LLM inferencers while reducing costs.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes SYMGEN, a method to generate data for symbolic language tasks using LLMs, aiming to improve task performance while reducing inference and annotation costs. SYMGEN generates symbolic language data with informative prompts and verifies output quality via execution or formatting. Experiments on six symbolic tasks show that models trained on SYMGEN-generated data achieve comparable or superior performance to LLM inferencers, with only 1% of the LLM size. SYMGEN reduces annotation effort by generating effective data from a few human demonstrations, and enables zero-shot learning for tasks like SQL. Symbolic knowledge and demonstrations are more critical than natural language instructions for high-quality generation.

## Method Summary
SYMGEN uses LLMs as data generators rather than task inferencers to create symbolic language datasets. The method constructs prompts containing natural language instructions, symbolic knowledge (like database schemas or ontologies), and human demonstrations. Generated data is verified through execution or formatting checks to ensure quality. The verified data is then used to train much smaller task-specific models (e.g., T5-large or T5-3B) that achieve comparable or better performance than the original LLM inferencer. The approach demonstrates that generating data with few human demonstrations can be as effective as 10x more human-annotated data, significantly reducing annotation costs.

## Key Results
- Task models trained on SYMGEN-generated data achieve comparable or superior performance to LLM inferencers while being only 1% of the LLM size
- Generated data with only a few human demonstrations can be as effective as over 10 times the amount of human-annotated data
- Symbolic knowledge and demonstrations are more critical than natural language instructions for high-quality generation
- The approach enables zero-shot learning for tasks like SQL and reduces annotation effort significantly

- Core assumption: The generated data is high-quality and representative enough to train a smaller model to perform as well as the LLM.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the effectiveness of SYMGEN vary across different symbolic language tasks with varying complexity levels?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions experiments on six symbolic tasks (SQL, Bash, Python, Prolog, TOP-representation, and QDMR) but doesn't provide a detailed comparison of effectiveness across task types.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows SYMGEN works across tasks but doesn't analyze how task complexity affects generation quality or model performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic analysis comparing SYMGEN performance across tasks with different complexity levels, including metrics like generation success rate, data quality scores, and downstream task performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal trade-off between the amount of symbolic knowledge provided in prompts versus the amount of demonstration examples?
- Basis in paper: The paper discusses the impact of removing symbolic knowledge or demonstrations separately but doesn't explore the optimal balance between them.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows both are important, it doesn't determine how to allocate limited prompt space between knowledge and demonstrations for maximum effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments systematically varying the ratio of symbolic knowledge to demonstrations while measuring generation quality and downstream task performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does SYMGEN's performance scale with increasing model size of the task model beyond the 1% size mentioned?
- Basis in paper: The paper demonstrates effectiveness with 1% sized models but doesn't explore performance at different scale points.
- Why unresolved: The paper establishes that much smaller models can work but doesn't show the full performance curve as model size increases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training task models at multiple sizes (e.g., 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 10% of Codex size) and measuring performance trade-offs.

## Limitations

- The evaluation relies heavily on proprietary LLM APIs (Codex-davinci-002), making reproducibility challenging without access to equivalent models
- The effectiveness depends on task-specific formatting and execution functions that are not fully specified, potentially limiting generalizability
- The claimed cost reductions are based on inference cost comparisons that don't account for the upfront computational cost of generating large datasets with LLMs

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The core mechanism of using LLMs as data generators instead of task inferencers is well-supported by the experimental results across six diverse symbolic tasks
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that symbolic knowledge and demonstrations are more critical than natural language instructions is supported but could benefit from more systematic ablation studies
- **Medium confidence**: The assertion that generated data with few demonstrations can match 10x more human-annotated data is promising but shows variable results across tasks
