# Interactive Explanations by Conflict Resolution via Argumentative Exchanges

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15022
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15022
- Reference count: 34
- One-line primary result: Introduces AXs framework for interactive explanations in XAI using argumentative exchanges to resolve conflicts between AI models and humans

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AXs (Argumentative eXchanges), a framework for interactive explanations in explainable AI (XAI) where agents (AI models and humans) dynamically share information to resolve conflicts. AXs use quantitative bipolar argumentation frameworks (QBAFs) to represent agents' reasoning and employ gradual semantics to capture cognitive biases. The authors define various agent behaviors (simple, greedy, counterfactual) and evaluate them in simulated settings. The counterfactual behavior outperforms others in conflict resolution and accuracy, demonstrating that the strongest argument may not always be the most effective.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a framework where agents engage in argumentative exchanges to resolve conflicts over explananda. Agents use QBAFs to represent their reasoning, with attacks and supports exchanged to resolve stance conflicts. The framework includes different agent behaviors (simple, greedy, counterfactual) for selecting contributions, and a learning mechanism where agents update their QBAFs based on information gained from exchanges. The approach is evaluated using synthetic argumentation frameworks with random tree structures and assigned biases, testing resolution rate, contribution rate, persuasion rate, and contribution accuracy across 1000 trials per configuration.

## Key Results
- Counterfactual behavior outperforms simple and greedy behaviors in conflict resolution and accuracy
- The strongest argument may not always be the most effective for resolving conflicts
- AXs provide a theoretical foundation for interactive XAI through argumentative exchanges

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Interactive explanations through argumentative exchanges resolve conflicts between AI models and humans by dynamically sharing reasoning.
- Mechanism: Agents (machine and human) use quantitative bipolar argumentation frameworks (QBAFs) to represent their reasoning, and contribute attacks/supports to exchange BAFs to resolve stance conflicts on explananda.
- Core assumption: Agents can abstract their reasoning into QBAFs and share relevant argumentative moves to resolve conflicts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "AXs use quantitative bipolar argumentation frameworks to represent agents' reasoning and employ gradual semantics to capture cognitive biases."
  - [section] "We define AXs as a general framework in which agents argue with the goal of conflict resolution."
  - [corpus] Found related work on argumentative exchanges for XAI, but none with the specific QBAF and gradual semantics approach.
- Break condition: If agents cannot effectively abstract their reasoning into QBAFs, or if the gradual semantics fail to capture relevant cognitive biases, the conflict resolution mechanism breaks down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The counterfactual behavior outperforms other behaviors in conflict resolution and accuracy.
- Mechanism: Agents using counterfactual behavior evaluate the perceived effect of each potential contribution on the explanandum's strength before contributing, selecting the move with maximum effect.
- Core assumption: Agents can accurately evaluate the counterfactual effect of potential contributions on the explanandum's strength.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The counterfactual behavior outperforms others in conflict resolution and accuracy, demonstrating that the strongest argument may not always be the most effective."
  - [section] "The counterfactual behaviour takes greater consideration of the argumentative structure of the reasoning available to the agents in order to maximise the chance of conflict resolution."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence in corpus, but related work on argumentation for XAI suggests potential for such mechanisms.
- Break condition: If agents cannot accurately evaluate counterfactual effects, or if the argumentative structure is too complex for meaningful evaluation, the counterfactual behavior loses its advantage.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Learning from exchanges, with appropriate bias assignment, enables agents to resolve conflicts.
- Mechanism: Agents learn arguments, attacks, and supports from each other's contributions, and their biases on these learned elements determine how they integrate this new information into their reasoning.
- Core assumption: Agents can learn from each other's contributions and appropriately assign biases to integrate new information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Agents (AI models and humans) dynamically share information to resolve conflicts."
  - [section] "Agents' stances on explananda result from the evaluation thereof using the agents' private evaluation methods."
  - [corpus] Found related work on multi-agent argumentation and learning, but not with the specific bias-based learning mechanism.
- Break condition: If agents cannot learn effectively from exchanges, or if bias assignment is inappropriate (e.g., too credulous or too skeptical), learning fails to contribute to conflict resolution.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  QBAF (arguments, attacks, supports, biases) -> Gradual Semantics (evaluates arguments, determines stances) -> AX Framework (manages exchange of attacks/supports) -> Agent Behaviors (dictates contribution selection) -> Learning Mechanism (updates QBAFs based on learned arguments)

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize QBAFs for agents based on their reasoning
  2. Determine initial stances on explanandum using gradual semantics
  3. If stances conflict, engage in AXs
  4. Agents contribute attacks/supports based on their behavior
  5. Agents learn from each other's contributions
  6. Update stances and repeat until conflict resolved or exchange ends

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Granularity of QBAF: More detailed QBAFs provide better reasoning representation but increase complexity
  - Frequency of exchanges: More frequent exchanges allow faster conflict resolution but increase computational cost
  - Bias assignment strategy: More sophisticated bias assignment improves learning but requires more domain knowledge

- Failure signatures:
  - Inability to resolve conflicts after multiple exchanges
  - Agents consistently disagreeing despite sharing reasoning
  - Learning not improving agent stances over time
  - Exchanges becoming circular or repetitive

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement simple behavior for both agents and test basic exchange functionality
  2. Implement counterfactual behavior for one agent and compare conflict resolution rate
  3. Test learning mechanism with varying bias assignment strategies and measure impact on conflict resolution

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different gradual semantics beyond DF-QuAD perform in resolving conflicts during AXs, and what properties make them more or less effective?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on DF-QuAD semantics for evaluation, but mentions that other semantics satisfying monotonicity or bi-variate monotony/reinforcement might also be applicable.
- Why unresolved: The authors leave the analysis of other gradual semantics for future work, suggesting that the effectiveness of different semantics in conflict resolution is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing AXs with different gradual semantics (e.g., QuAD, REB, QEM) in terms of resolution rate, contribution rate, and persuasion rate would provide evidence for their relative effectiveness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of evaluation range (I) discretization granularity impact the effectiveness of AXs in resolving conflicts and facilitating persuasion?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a three-value discretization (positive, neutral, negative) for simplicity, but acknowledges that further granularity could be explored.
- Why unresolved: The impact of different discretization levels on AXs' performance is not investigated, leaving the optimal granularity unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing AXs with different evaluation range granularities (e.g., two, four, or five values) in terms of resolution rate, contribution rate, and persuasion rate would provide evidence for the optimal discretization level.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do malicious agents, who intentionally contribute misleading or irrelevant arguments, impact the effectiveness of AXs in resolving conflicts and facilitating persuasion?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper assumes agents are truthful, but acknowledges that this is not always the case in real-world scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not consider the impact of malicious agents on AXs, leaving the robustness of the framework against such behavior unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with AXs involving malicious agents (e.g., contributing arguments with low biases, irrelevant arguments, or arguments that contradict their private QBAF) in terms of resolution rate, contribution rate, and persuasion rate would provide evidence for the framework's robustness against malicious behavior.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How do different turn-taking functions (π) impact the effectiveness of AXs in resolving conflicts and facilitating persuasion, especially in multi-agent settings with more than two agents?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a strict alternating turn-taking function between two agents, but acknowledges that other functions could be used.
- Why unresolved: The impact of different turn-taking functions on AXs' performance is not investigated, leaving the optimal function unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing AXs with different turn-taking functions (e.g., random selection, priority-based, or round-robin) in terms of resolution rate, contribution rate, and persuasion rate would provide evidence for the optimal function, especially in multi-agent settings.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How do different learning mechanisms for humans (beyond random biases with confirmation bias offset) impact the effectiveness of AXs in resolving conflicts and facilitating persuasion?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a simple model of human learning with random biases and confirmation bias offset, but acknowledges that more sophisticated models could be used.
- Why unresolved: The impact of different human learning mechanisms on AXs' performance is not investigated, leaving the optimal model unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing AXs with different human learning mechanisms (e.g., Bayesian updating, reinforcement learning, or social learning) in terms of resolution rate, contribution rate, and persuasion rate would provide evidence for the optimal model.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies on synthetic argumentation frameworks that may not capture real-world reasoning complexity
- Learning mechanism effectiveness depends heavily on appropriate bias assignment without clear practical guidelines
- Abstract behavior strategies may be challenging to implement in real-world applications

## Confidence

- High confidence: The basic framework of AXs using QBAFs and gradual semantics is theoretically sound and well-defined.
- Medium confidence: The counterfactual behavior's superiority is demonstrated in synthetic settings but may not generalize to more complex scenarios.
- Medium confidence: The learning mechanism's effectiveness is shown in controlled experiments but requires further validation in real-world applications.

## Next Checks

1. Implement AXs with real-world data (e.g., actual AI model explanations and human feedback) to test the framework's practical applicability and identify any limitations not apparent in synthetic settings.

2. Conduct ablation studies to quantify the individual contributions of QBAFs, gradual semantics, and the learning mechanism to conflict resolution performance.

3. Develop guidelines or heuristics for appropriate bias assignment in practical applications, and validate these guidelines through additional experiments with varying bias strategies.
