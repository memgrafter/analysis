# Energy Efficiency of Training Neural Network Architectures: An Empirical Study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.00967
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00967
- Reference count: 25
- This paper investigates energy efficiency of CNN architectures (VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50) on MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets, measuring environmental impact through energy consumption and carbon emissions.

## Executive Summary
This empirical study investigates the energy efficiency of training popular CNN architectures (VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50) on image classification tasks using MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets. The research examines how CNN architecture choice, dataset size, and geographic location of computing infrastructure affect energy consumption and carbon emissions during training. The study compares software-based (CodeCarbon) and hardware-based (wattmeter) energy measurement methods, finding strong correlations between them. Key findings show that ResNet50 consumes the most energy while VGG16 on CIFAR-10 is the most energy-efficient, and that geographic location significantly impacts emissions but not energy consumption.

## Method Summary
The study employs transfer learning with pre-trained CNN architectures (VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50) implemented in Keras/TensorFlow. Models are trained on MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets with batch size 32, using the Adam optimizer. Energy consumption is measured using both CodeCarbon (software-based) and OmegaWatt wattmeter (hardware-based) methods. The experiments are conducted across three geographic locations (Oregon, Virginia, South Carolina) and Taiwan. Statistical analysis includes correlation tests (Spearman's ρ) and significance testing to compare measurement methods and geographic impacts.

## Key Results
- ResNet50 architecture consumed the most energy (6.68-16.3 G FLOPs) while VGG16 on CIFAR-10 was most energy-efficient (21.3-46.3 G FLOPs)
- Strong correlation (ρ = 0.94) between software-based and hardware-based energy measurements validates CodeCarbon's reliability
- Geographic location significantly impacts carbon emissions (p < 0.001) but not energy consumption (p = 0.6509)
- Larger datasets correlate strongly with higher energy consumption (ρ = -0.6233)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: CNN architecture complexity (number of layers and parameters) directly determines training energy consumption.
- **Mechanism**: More complex architectures require more floating-point operations (FLOPs) and longer training times, leading to higher energy use. This is confirmed by the strong correlation (ρ = -0.6233) between input dataset size and FLOPs, where larger models on larger datasets consume more energy.
- **Core assumption**: Energy consumption scales predictably with model complexity and dataset size.
- **Evidence anchors**: Table 3 shows strong correlation between FLOPs and energy consumption, with ResNet50 requiring 6.68-16.3 G FLOPs compared to VGG16's 21.3-46.3 G FLOPs. The study examines how "factors like CNN architecture, dataset size, and location of computing infrastructure affect energy consumption."
- **Break condition**: If energy consumption were dominated by factors unrelated to model complexity (e.g., data transfer, idle time), the correlation would weaken.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Hardware/software measurement tools provide consistent rankings of energy efficiency despite absolute value differences.
- **Mechanism**: While wattmeters provide more accurate absolute energy measurements, software profilers (CodeCarbon) show strong correlation (ρ = 0.94) with wattmeter readings, allowing reliable relative comparisons between architectures.
- **Core assumption**: The relative ranking of energy consumption across architectures is preserved across measurement methods.
- **Evidence anchors**: Table 7 shows wattmeter readings are 42-46% higher than profiler readings, but both methods rank ResNet50 as highest energy consumer and VGG16 on CIFAR as lowest. The study finds "there is a strong correlation between energy consumption reported by software-based and hardware-based methods."
- **Break condition**: If the correlation between measurement methods dropped below 0.7, the relative rankings would become unreliable.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Geographic location of compute infrastructure significantly impacts carbon emissions but not energy consumption.
- **Mechanism**: Different locations have different energy mixes (fossil fuels vs. renewables), affecting carbon intensity. The study found statistically significant differences in emissions (p < 0.001) between locations but no significant difference in energy consumed (p = 0.6509).
- **Core assumption**: Energy mix variations across locations affect emissions but not the absolute energy required for computation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Table 5 shows p-value for emissions is < 0.001 while energy consumed p-value is 0.6509. The study examines "the impact of the architecture and the location where the computations are hosted on the energy consumption and emissions produced."
- **Break condition**: If the same computation consumed different amounts of energy in different locations due to infrastructure differences, this mechanism would break.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Floating-point operations (FLOPs) as a proxy for computational complexity
  - Why needed here: FLOPs provide a hardware-independent measure of the work required to train a model, helping predict energy consumption before actual training.
  - Quick check question: If a model requires 10x more FLOPs than another, will it always consume 10x more energy? (Answer: No, due to factors like hardware efficiency and parallelization)

- **Concept**: Carbon intensity and energy mix variations
  - Why needed here: Understanding how electricity sources affect carbon emissions is crucial for interpreting the location-based differences in the study's results.
  - Quick check question: Why might the same model produce different CO2 emissions when trained in Taiwan versus Oregon? (Answer: Different proportions of fossil fuel vs. renewable energy in the local power grid)

- **Concept**: Transfer learning vs. training from scratch
  - Why needed here: The study uses pre-trained models, which significantly reduces training energy compared to training from scratch, a key consideration for green AI.
  - Quick check question: How does using pre-trained weights affect the energy consumption compared to training a CNN from scratch? (Answer: Pre-trained models require far less energy as they only need fine-tuning rather than full training)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data pipeline (MNIST/CIFAR-10 loading → preprocessing → batching) → Model training (Keras/TensorFlow with pre-trained CNNs) → Measurement (CodeCarbon profiler + wattmeter validation) → Analysis (R scripts for statistical testing)

- **Critical path**: Model training → energy measurement collection → statistical analysis → result interpretation

- **Design tradeoffs**: Software profilers offer ease of use and integration but sacrifice absolute accuracy; hardware measurements are more accurate but require physical setup and are harder to automate across different cloud locations.

- **Failure signatures**: Negative energy values from CodeCarbon (likely software bug), inconsistent accuracy results (possible platform malfunction), or correlation between measurement methods dropping below 0.7.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run VGG16 on CIFAR-10 three times, collect CodeCarbon measurements, verify consistency
  2. Compare CodeCarbon vs. wattmeter measurements for the same VGG16/CIFAR-10 run to validate correlation
  3. Run ResNet50 on MNIST once to identify maximum energy consumption case for baseline

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different CNN architectures compare in terms of energy efficiency and carbon emissions across various image classification datasets?
- Basis in paper: The paper states that "our results show that the selection of different CNN architectures for image classification and dataset size affect the energy consumption as well as the Score (accuracy/energy)."
- Why unresolved: The paper provides results for three CNN architectures (VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50) on two datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10) but does not explore a wider range of architectures or datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with additional CNN architectures and diverse image classification datasets would provide a more comprehensive comparison of energy efficiency and carbon emissions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between the number of model parameters and energy consumption during training?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that "the larger the number of parameters to train, the longer it will take and consequently the larger the energy consumption."
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how the number of parameters in different CNN architectures affects energy consumption.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with CNN architectures that have varying numbers of parameters while keeping other factors constant would establish a clearer relationship between parameter count and energy consumption.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the location of computing infrastructure impact energy consumption and carbon emissions for CNN training?
- Basis in paper: The paper states that "the location of the computing infrastructure plays an important role because of the sources of power."
- Why unresolved: The paper provides results for three locations (Taiwan, Oregon, South Carolina) but does not explore a wider range of locations or analyze the specific factors contributing to differences in energy consumption and carbon emissions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments in various locations with different energy sources and infrastructure setups would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how location affects energy consumption and carbon emissions.

## Limitations

- Limited to three CNN architectures (VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50) and two datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10), potentially limiting generalizability
- Energy measurements rely on both software profiling (CodeCarbon) and hardware validation (wattmeter), with acknowledged accuracy differences
- Geographic scope restricted to US-based cloud regions (Oregon, Virginia, South Carolina) and Taiwan
- Transfer learning scenario only - does not examine training from scratch

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Correlation between FLOPs and energy consumption, consistency of relative rankings across measurement methods, location impact on emissions
- **Medium confidence**: Absolute energy values due to measurement method discrepancies, generalizability to other architectures/datasets
- **Low confidence**: Specific numerical comparisons between architectures due to potential platform variations

## Next Checks

1. Replicate the study with additional architectures (MobileNet, EfficientNet) and datasets (ImageNet, custom domains) to test generalizability
2. Conduct controlled experiments comparing CodeCarbon and wattmeter measurements across multiple hardware configurations to quantify systematic biases
3. Extend geographic scope to include regions with different energy mixes (European renewable-heavy grids, coal-dependent regions) to validate location-based emission patterns
