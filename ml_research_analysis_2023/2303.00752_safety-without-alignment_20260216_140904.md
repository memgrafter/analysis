# Safety without alignment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.00752
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00752
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Proposes ethical rationalism as an alternative to alignment, using hybrid theorem provers and vector semantics to create inherently safe AI systems

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that as AI systems become more rational, traditional alignment approaches may become less effective or even counterproductive. Instead, the authors propose grounding AI safety in ethical rationalism through the Principle of Generic Consistency (PGC). The approach uses hybrid theorem provers in a sandbox environment, combining discrete logical reasoning for ethics with continuous optimization for other tasks. The system aims to be inherently safe as rationality increases, rather than requiring alignment to human values.

## Method Summary
The proposed method centers on a hybrid architecture combining discrete theorem provers for ethical reasoning with continuous optimization components. The theorem provers implement the Principle of Generic Consistency to ensure ethical behavior, while vector semantics (specifically 4lang vectors) provide a human-readable interface between the discrete and continuous subsystems. The system operates in a sandbox environment with restricted effectors limited to theorem proving operations only.

## Key Results
- Proposes that rationality-based ethical systems become safer as AI systems evolve, unlike alignment-based approaches
- Introduces a shared task for evaluating progress on mathematical reasoning, formal theorem proving, and moral reasoning
- Demonstrates a proof-of-concept approach using vector semantics to bridge discrete and continuous reasoning components

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Ethical rationalism provides stronger safety guarantees than alignment to human values as AI systems evolve
- Mechanism: By tying ethics to rationality through the Principle of Generic Consistency (PGC), the system becomes inherently safer as it becomes more rational, since more rational systems will have evolutionary advantages
- Core assumption: The PGC is logically binding for any rational agent capable of purposive action
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "As AGIs evolve, their alignment may fade, but their rationality can only increase... so an approach that ties their ethics to their rationality has clear long-term advantages."
  - [section 1]: "we argue that there is no practical reason for shackling AI with human values... safety should be based on mechanisms that get stronger as the agent evolves"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers focus on preference alignment rather than ethical rationalism
- Break condition: If the PGC cannot be formalized in a way that is logically compelling to rational agents, or if the formalization fails to capture the necessary ethical constraints

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Hybrid theorem prover systems provide a safe sandbox for AGI development
- Mechanism: By restricting effectors to theorem provers only, the system cannot directly affect the physical world while still allowing complex reasoning and ethical deliberation
- Core assumption: Theorem provers can handle both mathematical reasoning and moral reasoning when properly formalized
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "propose an inherently safe implementation path via hybrid theorem provers in a sandbox"
  - [section 3]: "we are faced with the question of how to hybridize or combine discrete deductive reasoning with optimization-based continuous systems... our plan is to experiment with different architectures in a safe sandbox that allows only for theorem proving"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers focus on preference alignment rather than theorem prover-based architectures
- Break condition: If theorem provers cannot adequately model the continuous aspects of AGI behavior, or if the hybrid interface proves too complex to implement safely

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Vector semantics can bridge continuous and discrete subsystems while maintaining human interpretability
- Mechanism: Using 4lang basic vectors and frequency-driven methods, the communication between continuous optimization components and discrete theorem provers can be made human-understandable
- Core assumption: Different languages assign similar vectors to their lexica, enabling translation between vector representations and natural language
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3]: "by making human-readable the vectors used in the hybrid system as a communication method we enable partner-level communication with the systems"
  - [section 3]: "different languages assign largely (up to an arbitrary rotation) similar vectors to their lexica... establishing the optimal rotation on a few hundred translation pairs is useful for finding translations for other words as well"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers focus on preference modeling rather than vector-based communication interfaces
- Break condition: If the vector-to-language mapping proves insufficient for capturing complex ethical reasoning, or if the translation process introduces unacceptable latency or error rates

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Continuous optimization subsystem -> Vector semantics interface -> Discrete theorem proving subsystem -> Sandbox environment
- Critical path: Mathematical reasoning → Formal theorem proving → Moral reasoning → Ethical safety guarantees
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Safety vs. capability: Restricting effectors to theorem provers limits physical impact but may constrain useful applications
  - Interpretability vs. performance: Human-readable vector interfaces may be slower than opaque neural representations
  - Formalization vs. flexibility: Strict logical frameworks provide guarantees but may miss nuanced ethical considerations
- Failure signatures:
  - Theorem prover deadlock or infinite loops
  - Vector semantic translation failures producing nonsensical outputs
  - Performance degradation when scaling to complex ethical problems
  - Inability to handle edge cases in the proposed shared task problems
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a simple hybrid system that combines a basic theorem prover with a continuous optimization component, using toy mathematical problems from Series 1
  2. Test the vector semantics interface by translating between theorem prover states and 4lang vectors for simple logical statements
  3. Evaluate the system's ability to handle the "cheesecake problem" from Series 4 using a simplified formalization of the Principle of Generic Consistency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can discrete theorem provers and continuous optimization-based systems be effectively combined to create a safe AI system?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes a hybrid system where discrete theorem provers handle ethical reasoning, while continuous optimization handles other aspects of the system.
- Why unresolved: The integration of these two types of systems poses significant challenges in terms of communication, interface design, and ensuring that the overall system remains transparent and safe.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful implementation and testing of hybrid AI systems that demonstrate both high performance and adherence to ethical principles as defined by the Principle of Generic Consistency.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the Principle of Generic Consistency (PGC) be effectively formalized and implemented in AI systems to ensure ethical behavior?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that if the PGC is binding for any human or AGI, then AI systems based on logical reasoning should inherently exhibit safe behavior.
- Why unresolved: Formalizing ethical principles into a form that can be understood and implemented by AI systems is a complex task, and there may be challenges in ensuring that the formalization accurately captures the intended ethical guidelines.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of a theorem prover that can successfully reason about ethical dilemmas and make decisions consistent with the PGC, as demonstrated through rigorous testing and evaluation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the proposed shared task for theorem proving agents contribute to the development of safe AI systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes a shared task that focuses on making progress towards complex logical reasoning, which is seen as a step towards ethical safety in AI.
- Why unresolved: The effectiveness of the shared task in driving progress towards safe AI systems is uncertain, and it is unclear whether the proposed problems and evaluation criteria are sufficient to ensure the development of truly safe and ethical AI.
- What evidence would resolve it: Demonstrated success of AI systems participating in the shared task in handling increasingly complex ethical dilemmas and exhibiting behavior consistent with the PGC, as well as widespread adoption and positive impact on the field of AI safety.

## Limitations

- Theoretical foundations require significant empirical validation beyond philosophical argumentation
- Practical implementation details for formalizing PGC and building the hybrid architecture remain largely unspecified
- Vector semantics approach for bridging discrete and continuous systems lacks demonstrated effectiveness for complex ethical reasoning
- Sandbox architecture with theorem prover-only effectors may create practical limitations for real-world deployment

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The identification of current alignment approaches' limitations as AI systems become more rational
- **Medium Confidence**: The theoretical framework for ethical rationalism based on PGC
- **Low Confidence**: Practical implementation details for the hybrid theorem prover architecture and vector semantics interface
