# NOTABLE: Transferable Backdoor Attacks Against Prompt-based NLP Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.17826
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17826
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Key outcome**: NOTABLE achieves over 90% attack success rates across six NLP tasks, three model architectures, and three prompting strategies while maintaining task-agnostic and prompt-independent properties.

## Executive Summary
This work addresses the challenge of creating backdoor attacks on prompt-based NLP models that remain effective across different downstream tasks and prompting strategies. The proposed NOTABLE method binds triggers to specific target words (anchors) in the encoder layers, creating shortcut connections that persist through various prompt patterns and fine-tuning scenarios. Experiments demonstrate that NOTABLE significantly outperforms existing backdoor attack methods, achieving high attack success rates while maintaining model performance on clean data.

## Method Summary
NOTABLE works by injecting backdoors into the encoder layers of pre-trained language models using an adaptive verbalizer that binds uncommon trigger tokens to specific target anchors. The method combines manual anchor selection with search-based anchors from long-sentence datasets to ensure coverage across different prompting strategies. During poisoning, the triggers are bound to these anchors in the attention mechanism, creating persistent shortcut connections that survive downstream retraining. The attack uses uncommon tokens as triggers to maintain effectiveness across diverse downstream tasks while achieving over 90% attack success rates across multiple model architectures and prompting strategies.

## Key Results
- NOTABLE achieves over 90% attack success rates across six NLP tasks (SST-2, IMDB, Twitter, BoolQ, RTE, CB)
- The method outperforms existing backdoor attack approaches on three different model architectures (BERT-base-uncased, DistilBERT-base-uncased, RoBERTa-large)
- NOTABLE maintains robustness against three state-of-the-art defenses (ONION, RAP, T-Miner) while preserving clean accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Backdoor triggers are bound to specific target anchors in encoder layers, creating shortcut connections independent of downstream prompts.
- Mechanism: The adaptive verbalizer identifies high-confidence tokens (anchors) from shadow datasets. During poisoning, triggers are bound to these anchors in the encoder's attention mechanism, creating direct connections that persist across different prompting strategies.
- Core assumption: Attention mechanisms in transformer encoders can learn shortcut connections between specific token pairs that survive downstream retraining.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Specifically, NOTABLE injects backdoors into the encoders of PLMs by utilizing an adaptive verbalizer to bind triggers to specific words (i.e., anchors)."
  - [section 3.1] "We observe that the transformation of prompt patterns and prompt positions will not lead to a severe drop in benign accuracy. This phenomenon suggests that the shortcut connections can also be learned in transformer-based models between some decisive words or tokens."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for encoder-level backdoor persistence
- Break condition: If downstream retraining significantly alters encoder attention patterns or if the target anchors are not present in the downstream data.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using uncommon tokens as triggers makes the attack robust to different downstream tasks and datasets.
- Mechanism: The attack uses nonsense tokens (e.g., "cf") as triggers, which are unlikely to appear in normal text. This ensures triggers remain distinctive across different downstream datasets with varying word distributions.
- Core assumption: Uncommon tokens will maintain their distinctiveness and trigger the backdoor regardless of downstream task vocabulary.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "By default, we utilize nonsense tokens, e.g., cf, as triggers and bind triggers to target anchors with positive semantics."
  - [section 4.2] "We use the uncommon tokens as triggers, enabling our attack to be effective after retraining on downstream tasks."
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - multiple related works use uncommon tokens for backdoor attacks
- Break condition: If downstream tasks naturally contain the trigger tokens or if the trigger tokens become common in the downstream domain.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The adaptive verbalizer combining manual and search-based anchors ensures coverage across different prompting strategies.
- Mechanism: The verbalizer uses 5 manually selected common words plus 25 high-confidence tokens from shadow datasets, covering both prompt-based and fine-tuning scenarios.
- Core assumption: The combination of manually curated and automatically discovered anchors will map to the label space across diverse downstream tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.4] "We utilize an adaptive verbalizer to achieve this goal. First, we adopt top-5 frequent words that are widely explored in previous prompt-engineering works... we also construct another search-based verbalizer to enhance the verbalizer."
  - [section 4.3] "The results show that using the verbalizer combined with the manual one and the search-based one can achieve remarkable ASRs, i.e., over 99.0% on all the datasets."
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - verbalizer adaptation is common in prompt engineering literature
- Break condition: If downstream tasks use verbalizers or prompting strategies that don't map to the identified anchor words.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-trained Language Model (PLM) with encoder layers -> Adaptive verbalizer (manual + search-based) -> Trigger tokens (uncommon/nonsense) -> Shadow datasets for backdoor injection -> Downstream datasets for evaluation -> Defenses (ONION, RAP, T-Miner)
- Critical path: Trigger binding -> Encoder attention modification -> Downstream retraining -> Attack execution
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Trigger selection: Nonsense tokens provide robustness but may reduce stealth
  - Anchor selection: More anchors increase coverage but may weaken individual connections
  - Poisoning rate: Higher rates improve attack success but increase detection risk
- Failure signatures:
  - Low attack success rate despite successful backdoor injection
  - Significant drop in benign accuracy after backdoor injection
  - Defenses successfully detecting poisoned samples
- First 3 experiments:

  2. Compare ASR using manual verbalizer only vs adaptive verbalizer on Twitter dataset
  3. Evaluate ASR when freezing encoder layers vs embedding layers to confirm critical components

## Limitations
- The method requires access to shadow datasets for backdoor injection, which may not always be available in real-world scenarios
- Effectiveness relies on the presence of target anchors in downstream datasets, which may not always occur
- Computational overhead of finding appropriate anchors through the search-based verbalizer component could be significant for large-scale deployments

## Confidence
