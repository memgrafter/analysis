# The Two Word Test: A Semantic Benchmark for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04610
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04610
- Reference count: 2
- Large Language Models fail at judging the meaningfulness of two-word noun combinations, revealing semantic understanding deficits.

## Executive Summary
The Two Word Test (TWT) is a new benchmark that measures language understanding in Large Language Models (LLMs) using two-word phrases. It requires humans and models to judge whether noun-noun combinations are meaningful or nonsensical, such as "baby boy" (meaningful) vs. "goat sky" (nonsensical). The test uses human-rated data from 150 participants and includes both continuous and binary versions.

Three models—GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, and Bard—were tested against human performance. All models performed poorly on the continuous version, with GPT-4 failing ~23% of the time even on highly agreed-upon phrases. On the binary version, GPT-3.5 and Bard showed poor discrimination ability, with GPT-3.5 tending to call nonsense phrases "makes sense" and Bard calling sensible phrases "nonsense." GPT-4 performed better, achieving d' = 1.79 and AUC = 0.81 overall, and d' = 2.58 and AUC = 0.90 on the most agreed-upon phrases, but still fell short of human-level performance.

## Method Summary
The TWT benchmark uses 1,768 noun-noun phrases rated by 150 human participants on a 0-4 scale for meaningfulness. LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, Bard) are prompted to rate these phrases using both continuous (0-4) and binary ("makes sense"/"nonsense") judgments. Performance is compared to human distributions using Crawford & Howell t-tests, SDT metrics (d', β, AUC), and χ² tests. Results are also compared to chance baselines using simulated distributions.

## Key Results
- All three LLMs performed poorly on continuous TWT, with GPT-4 failing ~23% of the time even on phrases with 95% human agreement
- On binary TWT, GPT-3.5 showed liberal bias (calling nonsense "makes sense"), while Bard showed conservative bias (calling sense "nonsense")
- GPT-4 achieved d' = 1.79 and AUC = 0.81 overall, and d' = 2.58 and AUC = 0.90 on most agreed-upon phrases, but still fell short of human performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Noun-noun phrase combination taps a fundamental aspect of human language and intelligence by requiring combinatorial semantics without grammatical scaffolding.
- Mechanism: When humans encounter noun-noun pairs like "baby boy" versus "boy baby," they draw on real-world knowledge and memory-dependent semantic relationships to judge meaningfulness. This bypasses syntactic cues (like adjective-noun word order) and forces reliance on world knowledge.
- Core assumption: The ability to evaluate whether a noun-noun combination "makes sense" is a stable, measurable component of human semantic understanding that LLMs have not fully acquired.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Combining multiple words into a single concept is a fundamental aspect of human language and intelligence."
  - [section] "Compared to other types of linguistic compositions... noun-noun combinations do not offer grammatical assistance in determining meaningfulness."
  - [corpus] Weak - neighbors are mostly word association tests or creativity benchmarks, not semantic combination tests.
- Break condition: If LLMs develop robust world knowledge representations that allow them to reason about arbitrary noun pairings, the benchmark's discriminatory power would diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The TWT reveals semantic understanding deficits that are distinct from executive control or reasoning limitations.
- Mechanism: Because noun-noun combination judgment does not require multi-step reasoning or puzzle-solving, poor performance directly indicates gaps in semantic knowledge rather than failure in higher-order cognitive control.
- Core assumption: Performance on this task reflects underlying semantic knowledge rather than transient reasoning failures.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Understanding these phrases requires understanding the constituent concepts, and then using world knowledge to determine whether the combination makes sense in some manner."
  - [section] "The TWT differs from these cases in that it does not directly require inference or reasoning."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus support; neighbors focus on reasoning or creativity tasks.
- Break condition: If LLMs improve at this task purely through pattern memorization without acquiring true semantic understanding, the distinction between semantic and reasoning deficits would blur.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using human-annotated meaningfulness ratings creates a stable, reproducible ground truth for evaluating model performance.
- Mechanism: The benchmark leverages existing human ratings from Graves et al. (2013) across 150 participants, providing a well-calibrated reference distribution for statistical comparison.
- Core assumption: Human ratings are consistent and representative of true semantic meaningfulness judgments.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The test requires meaningfulness judgments of 1768 noun-noun combinations that have been rated as meaningful (e.g., baby boy) or not meaningful (e.g., goat sky) by 150 human raters."
  - [section] "For each phrase, the mean and standard deviation of participant responses were calculated."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus neighbors focus on creativity or association, not standardized human rating datasets.
- Break condition: If the human rating process contains systematic biases or if the sampled phrases are not representative of general noun-noun combinations, the benchmark's validity would be compromised.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: TWT benchmark -> phrase dataset (1768 noun-noun pairs) -> prompt generation module -> LLM interfaces (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Bard) -> rating collection -> statistical analysis pipeline (t-tests, SDT metrics, permutation tests) -> result visualization
- Critical path: Phrase selection → Prompt construction → LLM inference → Rating collection → Statistical comparison → Performance evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Binary version simplifies the task but loses granularity; continuous version provides richer data but may expose LLM numerical weaknesses
- Failure signatures: Excessive liberal bias (rating nonsense as sensible), poor discrimination (d' near 0), ratings outside 95% confidence intervals of human responses
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the continuous TWT on a small phrase subset to verify prompt formatting and rating scale usage
  2. Compare LLM responses to simulated human distributions to establish baseline failure rates

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific knowledge or representations are missing in LLMs that prevent them from correctly judging the meaningfulness of two-word combinations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that LLMs fail at combining multiple words into a single concept and suggests this reveals limitations in "underlying semantic knowledge itself, rather than to reason using that knowledge."
- Why unresolved: The study identifies the failure but doesn't pinpoint the exact nature of missing knowledge or representations in LLMs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic probing of LLMs with different types of semantic combinations (literal, metaphorical, compound words, etc.) to identify patterns in failures, combined with analysis of training data and model architectures.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the performance gap between GPT-4 and humans on the TWT be closed through fine-tuning on two-word phrase data alone, or does it require fundamental architectural changes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that GPT-4 shows "substantial improvement" but still falls short of human performance, particularly on phrases with high human agreement.
- Why unresolved: The study demonstrates the gap exists but doesn't explore whether it can be closed through training modifications alone.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing fine-tuned versions of GPT-4 on the TWT versus models with different architectures trained from scratch on similar semantic tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does the TWT measure a fundamental limitation in LLMs' ability to understand word combinations, or is it measuring a distributional artifact where certain phrase patterns are underrepresented in training data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests TWT may exploit the fact that "text corpora that LLMs are trained on... almost entirely contain sensible text," but doesn't test whether this is the root cause of failures.
- Why unresolved: The study shows correlation between training data patterns and model performance but doesn't establish causation or test alternative explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training LLMs on controlled datasets with varying proportions of sensible vs. nonsensical phrases, or testing models on in-context learning scenarios with explicit phrase-meaning information.

## Limitations
- The benchmark relies on human judgments from a single dataset (Graves et al., 2013), which may not capture full semantic variability across contexts and populations.
- The distinction between semantic understanding deficits and other LLM limitations (reasoning, memory, or pattern matching) remains difficult to isolate conclusively.
- Binary TWT results show LLMs exhibit strong response biases (liberal or conservative), suggesting the task may tap into systematic rating tendencies rather than pure semantic understanding.

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The core finding that LLMs fail to match human performance on noun-noun semantic judgments is well-supported by statistical comparisons.
- Medium Confidence: The claim that these failures reflect semantic understanding deficits distinct from reasoning limitations is plausible but not definitively proven.
- Low Confidence: The assertion that TWT measures a "fundamental aspect of human language and intelligence" overstates what can be concluded from performance on this specific task alone.

## Next Checks

2. Compare TWT performance against other semantic tasks (e.g., word association, sentence completion) to determine if noun-noun combination judgment provides unique insights beyond existing benchmarks.
3. Conduct ablation studies removing world knowledge components from LLMs to isolate whether semantic deficits stem from knowledge gaps versus other architectural limitations.
