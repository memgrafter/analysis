# A Comparison of Semi-Supervised Learning Techniques for Streaming ASR at Scale

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.11053
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11053
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Semi-supervised methods improve tail-word WER, lattice density, and decoder computation but not general WER

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates three semi-supervised learning methods (TTS augmentation, JOIST, and BEST-RQ) for streaming ASR at scale using joint training with a large supervised dataset. The authors find that while these methods don't improve overall word error rate (WER), they provide production benefits including better lattice density (+12.5% on voice search), reduced decoder computation (-4.7% on RPN), and improved tail-word WER. The study demonstrates that semi-supervised techniques offer utility for production ASR systems beyond traditional WER metrics.

## Method Summary
The authors implement a Conformer-based streaming ASR architecture with causal and non-causal encoders and decoders. They train models jointly on 4M supervised utterances and 600M unsupervised audio utterances using 230B unsupervised text examples. The semi-supervised methods are implemented as auxiliary tasks during training: TTS augmentation generates synthetic audio from rare text, JOIST trains the encoder on masked text with audio-like representations, and BEST-RQ adds an audio quantization prediction task. The baseline model is trained for 800k steps, followed by 35k steps of semi-supervised fine-tuning with equal task weighting.

## Key Results
- Semi-supervised methods don't improve general WER but improve tail-word WER
- Lattice density improves by +12.5% on voice search with combined methods
- Average decoding states decrease by -4.7% on RPN with semi-supervised training
- Combined methods outperform single methods on lattice density for difficult utterances

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: TTS Augmentation
- Claim: TTS augmentation improves tail-word WER by generating audio from rare text examples, increasing acoustic coverage for uncommon words.
- Mechanism: The TTS system converts rare text examples into synthetic audio, which is then paired with the original text and used as additional supervised data during training. This exposes the ASR model to acoustic patterns for words that are underrepresented in the original supervised dataset.
- Core assumption: The TTS system can generate high-quality audio that captures the acoustic characteristics of rare words, and the model can learn from these synthetic examples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using a pre-trained TTS system with frozen parameters, we generate an audio clip ˆx corresponding to each unsupervised text segment y∈U T ."
  - [section] "Using a pre-trained TTS system with frozen parameters, we generate an audio clip ˆx corresponding to each unsupervised text segment y∈U T . We then treat (ˆx, y) as a supervised audio-text pair and train the causal and non-causal ASR tasks."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus does not directly discuss TTS augmentation, but related work on low-resource ASR suggests synthetic data can help with rare words.
- Break condition: TTS-generated audio quality is too poor, or the model cannot generalize from synthetic to real audio.

### Mechanism 2: JOIST
- Claim: JOIST improves tail-word WER and lattice density by training the encoder to represent both audio and text, enriching the model's language understanding.
- Mechanism: JOIST masks portions of unpaired text and passes them through the text frontend, treating the output as if it were audio features. The model is trained to reconstruct the original text from the masked representation using an RNN-T loss. This forces the encoder to develop a unified representation that captures both audio and text information.
- Core assumption: The encoder can learn to represent both modalities in a unified space, and this representation will improve ASR performance, especially for rare words.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "JOIST [19] is a recent method which does this using a masked language modeling task in the spirit of BERT [20]."
  - [section] "Following the design of JOIST in [19], we pass masked unpaired text examples through a text frontend... The results are treated identically to audio features; that is, they are passed in turn to the causal (EC and DC) and non-causal (EN C and DN C) and compared to original text sequence via an RNN-T loss."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus mentions related work on unified speech-text models but does not specifically discuss JOIST's impact on WER or lattice density.
- Break condition: The encoder fails to learn a unified representation, or the RNN-T loss does not effectively align the masked text with the original.

### Mechanism 3: BEST-RQ
- Claim: BEST-RQ improves lattice density and decoder computation by forcing the encoder to predict quantized audio representations, creating a richer internal representation.
- Mechanism: BEST-RQ masks portions of audio features, encodes them with the ASR model, and also passes them through a random projection to a fixed codebook. The model is trained to predict the quantized targets inside the masked region. This auxiliary task encourages the encoder to develop a more detailed representation of the audio, which leads to a denser lattice and reduced decoder load.
- Core assumption: The quantized audio representation captures meaningful information, and the auxiliary prediction task improves the encoder's overall representation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "BEST-RQ [27] in particular finds that fixed random projection to a pre-initialized codebook works effectively as a quantizer."
  - [section] "We model our audio injection after BEST-RQ as implemented in [27]. Audio features are masked and processed by the frontend... The encoder is then trained to predict the quantized targets inside the masked region."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus mentions BEST-RQ and related work on audio quantization but does not provide direct evidence for its impact on lattice density or decoder computation.
- Break condition: The quantized representation does not capture useful information, or the auxiliary task does not improve the encoder's representation.

  - Why needed here: The paper evaluates methods not just on WER but also on lattice density and decoder computation, which are important for production ASR systems that need to support downstream tasks and on-device deployment.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Audio -> Audio frontend -> EC -> DC -> Output (for causal ASR)
  - Audio -> Audio frontend -> EC -> ENC -> DNC -> Output (for non-causal ASR)

- Critical path: Audio -> Audio frontend -> EC -> DC -> Output (for causal ASR)
  Non-causal path: Audio -> Audio frontend -> EC -> ENC -> DNC -> Output (for non-causal ASR)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model size vs. performance: The paper uses a 160M-parameter model to balance performance with on-device constraints
  - Streaming vs. non-streaming: The architecture supports both modes, with causal components for streaming and non-causal components for improved accuracy
  - Task weighting: The paper finds that equal weighting of supervised and unsupervised tasks works best, but this may need tuning for different datasets

- Failure signatures:
  - WER degradation: Could indicate issues with the unsupervised tasks or task weighting
  - Increased decoder load: May suggest the encoder is not learning a strong language representation
  - Poor lattice density: Could indicate the model is not generating diverse enough hypotheses

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train baseline model (E-0) with equal weighting of causal and non-causal supervised ASR
  2. Train model with JOIST (E-A) and compare WER, lattice density, and decoder load to baseline
  3. Train model with TTS augmentation (E-B) and compare WER, lattice density, and decoder load to baseline and JOIST

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why does JOIST improve tail-word WER on clean speech but degrade performance on noisy speech?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes JOIST "consistently performs best on the acoustically clean but linguistically difficult TTS tail-word test sets" but "in fact degrades performance on the acoustically challenging Noisy test set"
- Why unresolved: The authors observe this behavior but don't provide a clear explanation for the underlying mechanism causing this trade-off
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of encoder representations showing how JOIST affects noise robustness vs language modeling capabilities, or controlled experiments varying noise levels with JOIST

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal combination strategy for semi-supervised methods in production ASR systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that "combinations of methods largely outperform single methods" on lattice density for difficult utterances, but doesn't identify which combinations work best for specific metrics
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests a limited set of combinations with fixed weightings; optimal weighting and task selection remains unclear
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of all possible method combinations with learned weightings, and analysis of which combinations benefit specific downstream tasks

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do semi-supervised methods affect real-world production metrics beyond those measured in the paper?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes it evaluates on "several test sets" but doesn't cover all possible production metrics like latency, memory usage, or robustness to domain shifts
- Why unresolved: The authors focus on WER, lattice density, and decoder computation but acknowledge that "model performance goes beyond raw WER" in production systems
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive evaluation of semi-supervised methods on additional production metrics like model size, streaming latency, battery consumption, and robustness to domain shift

### Open Question 4
- Question: Why does BEST-RQ only show benefits when combined with JOIST?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "BEST-RQ seems beneficial only when combined with JOIST, where it appears to recover lost performance on noisy data while retain some of the improvements on the tail-word sets"
- Why unresolved: The authors observe this synergistic effect but don't explain the mechanism by which JOIST enables BEST-RQ to be effective
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of how BEST-RQ and JOIST interact at the representation level, or experiments isolating which components of each method are necessary for the synergy

## Limitations

- Production relevance is limited to specific metrics without end-to-end task completion validation
- Generalization boundaries are unclear for different dataset scales or architectures
- Mechanism validation is limited, with proposed explanations lacking rigorous empirical support
