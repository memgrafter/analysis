# UBC-DLNLP at SemEval-2023 Task 12: Impact of Transfer Learning on African Sentiment Analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.11256
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11256
- Reference count: 13
- Best model achieved F1-score of 70.36 on development data and 66.13 on test data

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the UBC-DLNLP team's approach to SemEval-2023 Task 12 on sentiment analysis for 14 African languages. The authors developed monolingual and multilingual models using transfer learning techniques, fine-tuning six language models including further pretraining of two models. Their experiments demonstrate that transfer learning and fine-tuning techniques are effective for sentiment analysis across multiple African languages, with multilingual training showing particular promise for low-resource language scenarios.

## Method Summary
The authors employed transfer learning using six language models: mBERT, XLM-R, Afro-XLMR, AfriBERTa, AfriTEVA, and Serengeti. They conducted both fine-tuning and further pretraining experiments. The further pretraining stage used a masked language modeling objective on task-specific data. Models were trained using Adam optimizer with learning rate 5e-5, batch size 16, and 50 epochs for fine-tuning, while further pretraining used 75 epochs with learning rate 4e-4 and batch size 16.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned models achieved F1-score of 70.36 on development data
- Further pretraining improved performance over fine-tuning alone for most languages
- Multilingual models with African language representations (Afro-XLMR, Serengeti) outperformed other approaches
- Best model achieved F1-score of 66.13 on test data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Fine-tuning multilingual LMs on multilingual data improves performance more than monolingual training alone. Multilingual training leverages shared linguistic patterns across languages, providing richer representations that benefit low-resource languages. This works because multilingual models can capture cross-lingual transfer patterns that generalize to unseen low-resource languages. Break condition: If cross-lingual transfer patterns are not shared across the language families in the dataset, multilingual training may not provide benefits.

### Mechanism 2
Further pretraining LMs on task-specific data improves sentiment analysis performance. Further pretraining encodes shallow domain knowledge that benefits low-resource scenarios by providing task-specific knowledge for fine-tuning. This works because task-specific pretraining can adapt general-purpose LMs to the domain and task at hand. Break condition: If the task-specific data is too small or too different from the pretraining data, further pretraining may not help or may even hurt performance.

### Mechanism 3
Multilingual models with African language representations can transfer knowledge to sentiment analysis tasks in African languages. Multilingual models like Afro-XLMR and Serengeti have been pretrained on African languages, allowing them to capture linguistic patterns specific to these languages that can be transferred to sentiment analysis. This works because African language representations learned during pretraining are useful for downstream sentiment analysis tasks. Break condition: If the African language representations are not well-aligned with sentiment analysis tasks, transfer learning may not provide benefits.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does further pretraining impact the performance of multilingual models compared to monolingual models for African languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that Afro-XLMR-baseft (M9) outperforms other models on 5 languages with an average F1 score of 70.36 on the Dev set, and achieves best performance on 9 languages on the Test set.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows the effectiveness of further pretraining for both monolingual and multilingual models, it does not directly compare the impact of further pretraining on monolingual vs. multilingual models for African languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments to compare the performance of further pretrained monolingual and multilingual models on African languages would provide insights into the relative impact of further pretraining on each model type.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do zero-shot performance and few-shot performance differ for African languages, and what factors contribute to these differences?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that they perform experiments in zero-shot settings for Oromo and Tigrinya, but do not provide detailed results or comparisons with few-shot performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of zero-shot vs. few-shot performance for African languages, nor does it discuss the factors that contribute to differences in performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments to compare zero-shot and few-shot performance for African languages, and analyzing the factors that contribute to performance differences, would provide insights into the strengths and limitations of different approaches.

### Open Question 3

- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the challenges posed by linguistic features, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how these features impact the performance of sentiment analysis models for African languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments to analyze the impact of specific linguistic features on the performance of sentiment analysis models for African languages would provide insights into the challenges and opportunities for improving model performance.

## Limitations

- The specific hyperparameter tuning process for Optuna is not detailed, making exact replication difficult
- Limited discussion of zero-shot performance beyond basic results
- No ablation studies on the relative contributions of fine-tuning vs. further pretraining
- The focal loss implementation details are underspecified

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The reported F1 scores and comparative performance between models (Fine-tuned Afro-XLMR and Serengeti vs their further-pretrained variants)
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that multilingual training outperforms monolingual training for low-resource languages (supported by one citation but limited direct evidence in the paper)
- **Low Confidence**: The specific mechanism by which further pretraining provides benefits (the paper claims it encodes "shallow domain knowledge" but doesn't provide detailed analysis)
