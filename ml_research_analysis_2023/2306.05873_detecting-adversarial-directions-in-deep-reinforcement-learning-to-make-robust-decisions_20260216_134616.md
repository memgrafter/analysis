# Detecting Adversarial Directions in Deep Reinforcement Learning to Make Robust Decisions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.05873
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05873
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Curvature-based method achieves TPR=1.0 with FPR=0.01 for detecting adversarial examples in ALE

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces INRD (Identification of Non-Robust Directions), a novel method for detecting adversarial examples in deep reinforcement learning by measuring the curvature of the policy loss function. The key insight is that adversarial states exhibit lower curvature in the loss landscape compared to normal states, making them identifiable through finite-difference curvature estimation. Experiments demonstrate high detection accuracy across multiple attack methods in the Arcade Learning Environment, with the method remaining effective even when adversaries are aware of the detection mechanism.

## Method Summary
INRD detects adversarial directions by estimating the local curvature of the policy loss function using a finite-difference approximation of the second-order Taylor expansion. The method computes the loss function value at a perturbed state along the negative gradient direction and measures how well the first-order Taylor approximation predicts the actual loss change. Adversarial states, being outputs of local optimization procedures, exhibit reduced negative curvature compared to naturally visited states, resulting in larger discrepancies between the first-order approximation and actual loss values. The method uses statistical calibration from a base run to establish detection thresholds.

## Key Results
- Achieves true positive rates up to 1.0 with false positive rates of 0.01 across multiple Atari games
- Successfully detects adversarial examples generated by FGSM, MI-FGSM, Nesterov Momentum, DeepFool, Carlini&Wagner, and Elastic Net attacks
- Remains effective even when adversaries explicitly optimize to minimize the detection metric
- Demonstrates fundamental trade-off between computing adversarial directions and minimizing curvature

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Adversarial states have lower curvature in the policy loss landscape than normal states, making them detectable via finite-difference curvature estimation. The method estimates local curvature of the policy loss function using a finite-difference approximation of the second-order Taylor expansion. Adversarial states, being outputs of local optimization procedures, exhibit reduced negative curvature compared to naturally visited states. The cost function J(s, τ) is sufficiently smooth and well-approximated by its quadratic Taylor expansion in a small neighborhood around each state.

### Mechanism 2
The gradient direction provides a correlated estimate of the most negative eigenvector of the Hessian, enabling efficient curvature measurement. By measuring the accuracy of the first-order Taylor approximation along the gradient direction, the method approximates the most negative eigenvalue of the Hessian without computing full eigendecomposition. The gradient direction is sufficiently correlated with directions of high negative curvature in the loss landscape.

### Mechanism 3
There exists a fundamental trade-off between computing adversarial directions and minimizing curvature, making detection robust even against awareness-aware adversaries. Adversarial optimization that attempts to minimize the detection metric L(s) inherently limits the effectiveness of the adversarial perturbation, as reducing curvature conflicts with maximizing adversarial impact. The adversarial optimization process cannot simultaneously minimize L(s) and maximize policy degradation beyond a certain threshold.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Policy network -> Loss function computation -> Gradient computation -> State perturbation generation -> Curvature estimation -> Statistical calibration -> Threshold comparison -> Detection output

- Critical path:
  1. Agent visits state s
  2. Compute J(s, π*(·|s)) and ∇J(s, π*(·|s))
  3. Generate perturbation η = ϵ·sign(∇J)/||∇J||²
  4. Compute J(s+η, π*(·|s)) and first-order approximation
  5. Calculate L(s, η) = J(s+η) - J̃(s, η)
  6. Compare |L(s, η) - mean| against threshold·std
  7. Output detection result

- Design tradeoffs:
  - ϵ (perturbation magnitude): Larger values give better curvature approximation but may exceed local linearity regime
  - Threshold t: Higher values reduce false positives but increase false negatives
  - Gradient direction vs random directions: Gradient provides better correlation with negative curvature but may be predictable
  - Single vs multiple perturbations: Multiple samples improve statistical robustness but increase computational cost

- Failure signatures:
  - High false positive rate: Threshold too low or ϵ too large
  - High false negative rate: Threshold too high or adversarial examples matching base curvature
  - Unstable detection: Poor statistical calibration or highly non-smooth loss landscape
  - Computational bottleneck: Inefficient gradient computation or excessive perturbation sampling

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline calibration: Run agent in environment without attacks, record mean and variance of L(s, η) for normal states
  2. Single attack detection: Generate adversarial examples using FGSM, test detection performance across different thresholds
  3. Attack-aware testing: Generate adversarial examples using the C&W-based method that attempts to minimize L(s), verify robustness of detection

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What is the fundamental tradeoff between computing adversarial directions and minimizing the curvature of the loss function? While the authors demonstrate this tradeoff empirically and connect it to their theoretical motivation, the exact nature and implications of this tradeoff are not fully characterized. The precise relationship between adversarial perturbation strength and the increase in L(s) is not quantified.

### Open Question 2
How can the INRD method be generalized to other domains beyond deep reinforcement learning? The authors note that the theoretical motivation presented here generalizes straightforwardly to any model which is trained to output a probability distribution given some high-dimensional input (e.g. autoregressive language models), but they do not provide concrete examples or experimental validation of INRD in domains outside of deep reinforcement learning.

### Open Question 3
What are the limitations of the first-order and second-order identification methods in detecting adversarial directions? The authors compare the performance of their first-order (FO-INRD) and second-order (SO-INRD) identification methods, noting that while the perturbations computed by FGSM, MI-FGSM, Nesterov Momentum can generally be detected with large TPR values by all the detection methods, the perturbations computed by Carlini&Wagner and the Elastic-Net method are more difficult to detect.

## Limitations
- Detection accuracy depends critically on proper calibration of the perturbation magnitude ϵ and detection threshold t
- Method assumes adversarial examples will systematically exhibit lower curvature than natural states, which may not hold for all attack strategies
- Computational cost scales with the number of perturbation samples required for accurate curvature estimation

## Confidence
- Theoretical framework: Medium
- Empirical results: Medium
- Generalizability claims: Low
