# Learning high-dimensional causal effect

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.00821
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00821
- Reference count: 4
- We propose a method to generate a synthetic causal dataset that is high-dimensional using the MNIST dataset with Bernoulli treatment values. This enables exploration of complex deep models for causal effect estimation. We experiment on this dataset using Dragonnet architecture and modified architectures with different initial Neural Network layers as representation learners. We observe that the modified architectures, particularly residual networks and transformers, perform better in estimating treatment effect closely without the need for targeted regularization. The proposed method provides a platform to study varieties of deep learning models for causal effect estimation on high-dimensional data.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of estimating causal effects in high-dimensional settings by introducing a synthetic dataset generation method using MNIST images with Bernoulli treatment assignments. The authors modify the Dragonnet architecture by incorporating pre-trained vision models (ResNet50 and Vision Transformer) as representation learners. Their experiments show that these modified architectures can estimate treatment effects accurately without the need for targeted regularization, outperforming the original Dragonnet. The proposed method provides a platform for studying various deep learning models in high-dimensional causal inference.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a synthetic data generation method using MNIST images, where treatment assignments are made using Bernoulli distributions based on digit parity (odd/even), and potential outcomes (Y0, Y1) are generated via noisy CNN models. The Dragonnet architecture is modified by replacing the initial dense layers with pre-trained vision models (ResNet50, Vision Transformer) as representation learners. The models are trained using binary cross-entropy loss for outcomes and propensity scores, with or without targeted regularization. Performance is evaluated using ATEAE, accuracy, and pre-treatment bias.

## Key Results
- Modified Dragonnet architectures with ResNet50 and Vision Transformer as representation learners outperform the original Dragonnet in estimating treatment effects.
- The modified architectures achieve low ATEAE and pre-treatment bias without the need for targeted regularization.
- Pre-trained vision models extract rich, hierarchical image features that improve treatment effect estimation.

- Core assumption: The causal relationships simulated in the MNIST-based dataset are meaningful proxies for real-world high-dimensional causal inference problems.

- Core assumption: Features learned from large-scale vision datasets are transferable to the causal effect estimation task, even when the downstream task is structurally different.
- Evidence anchors:

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input: High-dimensional image data (MNIST digits)
  - Representation Learner: Dense network, ResNet50, or Vision Transformer (pre-trained or randomly initialized)
  - Dragonnet Heads: Propensity score estimator (g(x)), outcome estimators (Y0, Y1)
  - Loss Function: Binary cross-entropy for outcomes, cross-entropy for propensity, with optional targeted regularization
  - Output: Estimated treatment effects (ATE, ATT), accuracy, pre-treatment bias

- Critical path:
  1. Load and preprocess MNIST images
  2. Simulate treatment assignments and potential outcomes (Y0, Y1)
  3. Pass images through chosen representation learner
  4. Apply Dragonnet heads to estimate propensity and outcomes
  5. Compute loss and update model parameters
  6. Evaluate using ATEAE, accuracy, and pre-treatment bias

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using pre-trained vision models vs. training from scratch: Pre-trained models offer better feature representations but may introduce inductive biases not relevant to the causal task.
  - Targeted regularization vs. no regularization: Regularization can help balance propensity and outcome estimation but may not be necessary if the representation learner is strong.
  - Synthetic data vs. real high-dimensional causal datasets: Synthetic data allows for controlled experiments but may not capture all real-world complexities.

- Failure signatures:
  - High ATEAE: Model fails to accurately estimate treatment effects; could be due to poor feature representations or insufficient model capacity.
  - High pre-treatment bias: Treated and control groups are not comparable; could indicate hidden confounding or insufficient adjustment.
  - Low accuracy: Model fails to predict outcomes accurately; could be due to label noise in the synthetic data or model overfitting.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train Dragonnet with a simple dense network as the representation learner on the synthetic MNIST dataset; evaluate ATEAE, accuracy, and pre-treatment bias.
  2. Replace the dense network with a pre-trained ResNet50 (without fine-tuning) and repeat the training and evaluation; compare results to the baseline.
  3. Replace ResNet50 with a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) and repeat; analyze whether the attention-based architecture offers further improvements.

## Limitations
- The synthetic data generation process and its ability to simulate realistic confounding structures are not rigorously validated, limiting the generalizability of the findings.
- The transfer of features learned from vision tasks to causal effect estimation is an assumption that requires further validation, as the domains are structurally different.
- The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of different initial layers (representation learners) on accuracy and computational efficiency.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The use of pre-trained vision models as representation learners improves feature extraction for the causal task, as supported by empirical results showing lower ATEAE and pre-treatment bias.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that residual networks and transformers perform better without targeted regularization is supported by results, but the underlying reasons (e.g., feature quality, model capacity) are not fully explored.
- **Low Confidence**: The synthetic data generation process and its ability to simulate realistic confounding structures are not rigorously validated, limiting the generalizability of the findings.
