# Optimizing Hyperparameters with Conformal Quantile Regression

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.03623
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03623
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Conformalized quantile regression improves Bayesian optimization for noisy, heteroskedastic hyperparameter tuning, especially in multi-fidelity settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of hyperparameter optimization (HPO) for machine learning models, particularly when dealing with noisy or heteroskedastic observation data. The authors propose a novel approach using conformalized quantile regression (CQR) as a surrogate model for Bayesian optimization, which can handle non-Gaussian and heteroskedastic noise. They also introduce a simple yet effective method to extend single-fidelity HPO algorithms to the multi-fidelity setting by aggregating observed results across different resource levels. The proposed method outperforms state-of-the-art single-fidelity and multi-fidelity HPO algorithms on various benchmarks, demonstrating its robustness and efficiency.

## Method Summary
The method uses conformalized quantile regression (CQR) as a surrogate model for Bayesian optimization, where quantile regression models are trained on the training set and then corrected using conformity scores from a validation set to ensure valid coverage. Thompson sampling is used for acquisition, sampling from the corrected conditional distribution. The multi-fidelity extension uses ASHA for early stopping and a simple data transformation that uses only the last observed fidelity value for each configuration, reducing the problem to single-fidelity optimization.

## Key Results
- CQR achieves lower average normalized regret compared to state-of-the-art single-fidelity and multi-fidelity HPO algorithms
- The method shows better ranks when averaged over tasks, with significant improvements in the multi-fidelity setting
- Critical diagrams show CQR is statistically tied with or outperforms baselines across various benchmark datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Conformalized quantile regression improves calibration by adjusting predicted intervals using conformity scores from a held-out validation set.
- Mechanism: The conformalization step applies a data-dependent offset to each predicted quantile interval, computed from the empirical distribution of conformity scores on the validation set. This guarantees coverage even when the quantile regression model is misspecified.
- Core assumption: The split-conformal assumption holds—that the training and validation sets are exchangeable and that the conformity scores are independent of the training data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose using the split conformal method from Romano et al. (2019) that allows to obtain robust coverage for each m/2 of the predicted confidence intervals"
  - [section] "The method consists in applying an offset on each confidence interval, which is estimated on a validation set."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found; the neighbor list is sparse on conformal calibration details.
- Break condition: If the training and validation sets are not exchangeable (e.g., data drift or covariate shift), the coverage guarantee may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using the last observed fidelity value for each configuration as the training target simplifies multi-fidelity optimization and still preserves the ranking structure needed for effective search.
- Mechanism: By discarding intermediate fidelity observations and using only the final value per configuration, the method reduces the multi-fidelity problem to a single-fidelity one, which is easier to model with existing surrogate techniques.
- Core assumption: Configurations that are stopped early have worse performance than those that reach higher fidelity, and their relative order is preserved even without seeing the final fidelity values.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We propose to consider the transformation that takes the last value of the time-observations of a given configuration."
  - [section] "Assuming configurations are stopped with a probability inversely correlated with their performance... the result-based ordering of configurations remains constant."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; the neighbor list focuses on conformal prediction rather than fidelity aggregation.
- Break condition: If a configuration that is stopped early would have overtaken a longer-running one at full fidelity, the ranking will be incorrect.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Gradient boosted trees as the quantile regression model provide superior predictive accuracy and calibration compared to Gaussian process surrogates in the high-data regime.
- Mechanism: Boosted trees can capture non-linearities and heteroskedasticity in the data without assuming Gaussian noise, and their performance improves as more observations are available.
- Core assumption: The data-generating process is well-approximated by a flexible non-parametric model like boosted trees, and enough data exists to fit such a model well.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "In each case, we draw a random subset of size n to train the surrogate model and then evaluate the three metrics on remaining unseen examples. Results are averaged across seeds and benchmarks and we normalize the target with quantile normalization."
  - [section] "Compared to GP, the RMSE of the boosted-trees surrogates is always better, which is expected as boosted-trees are known for their good performance on tabular data."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found in neighbors about RMSE comparison with GPs.
- Break condition: If the sample size is very small (n < 64), the tree-based model cannot fit the data well and may underperform GP.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Quantile regression and the quantile loss function
  - Why needed here: The method uses quantile regression to model the conditional distribution of the target, which is essential for capturing heteroskedasticity and non-Gaussian noise.
  - Quick check question: Given data points (x, y), how does the quantile loss Lα(y, ŷ) = α(y-ŷ) if y-ŷ > 0 else (1-α)(ŷ-y) ensure that the predicted ŷ approaches the α-th quantile of y|x?

- Concept: Conformal prediction and coverage guarantees
  - Why needed here: Conformal prediction provides a way to adjust the predicted intervals so that they have valid coverage regardless of the underlying model, which is crucial for reliable uncertainty estimates.
  - Quick check question: What is the key assumption behind split conformal prediction, and how does the conformity score relate to the interval adjustment?

- Concept: Thompson sampling for acquisition
  - Why needed here: Thompson sampling is used to select the next configuration to evaluate by sampling from the predicted conditional distribution, balancing exploration and exploitation.
  - Quick check question: How does independent Thompson sampling work in this context, and why is it appropriate when we have predicted quantiles for each configuration?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Quantile regression models (trained on training set) -> Conformal correction step (using conformity scores from validation set) -> Thompson sampling acquisition (using corrected quantiles to sample candidate configurations) -> Multi-fidelity extension (ASHA early stopping + last-fidelity data transformation)
- Critical path: 1) split data into train/validation, 2) fit m quantile regression models on train, 3) compute conformity scores and corrections on validation, 4) use corrected quantiles in Thompson sampling to select next configuration, 5) evaluate selected configuration and add to data, repeat
- Design tradeoffs: Using the last fidelity value simplifies modeling but discards information; using boosted trees allows flexible modeling but requires more data; conformal correction improves calibration but adds complexity and requires a validation set
- Failure signatures: Poor calibration despite conformal correction suggests a problem with the conformity score computation or the exchangeability assumption; worse performance than ASHA alone suggests the data transformation or quantile regression models are not effective
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the method on a simple 1D synthetic benchmark with known heteroskedastic noise and compare calibration (coverage rate) and RMSE to a GP baseline
  2. Compare the performance of the method with and without conformal correction on a real-world benchmark to isolate the benefit of calibration
  3. Test the multi-fidelity extension by comparing the method with and without the last-fidelity data transformation on a benchmark with clear fidelity-dependent ranking

## Limitations
- Conformal calibration reliability depends on the exchangeability assumption, which may not hold with data drift or covariate shift
- The multi-fidelity data transformation discards intermediate fidelity information, potentially losing valuable ranking information
- The method requires sufficient data (n ≥ 64) to fit the quantile regression models effectively, limiting its applicability in very low-data regimes

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The RMSE improvement of quantile regression over GP on tabular data, and the basic mechanism of conformal correction for interval calibration
- Medium Confidence: The extension to multi-fidelity optimization through last-fidelity aggregation, and the overall performance improvements over baselines in the experiments presented
- Low Confidence: The coverage guarantees under non-exchangeable conditions, and the scalability of the method to extremely high-dimensional hyperparameter spaces
