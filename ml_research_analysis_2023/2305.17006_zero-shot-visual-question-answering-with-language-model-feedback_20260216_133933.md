# Zero-shot Visual Question Answering with Language Model Feedback

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17006
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17006
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: LAMOC achieves 40.3 VQA score on OK-VQA and 37.9 on A-OKVQA, outperforming zero-shot baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LAMOC, a novel approach for knowledge-based visual question answering that leverages language model feedback to guide caption generation. The method employs a two-stage training process: first adapting a captioning model to align with PLM preferences, then refining it through reinforcement learning based on PLM feedback. Extensive experiments demonstrate that LAMOC significantly outperforms existing zero-shot methods and achieves competitive results with fine-tuned vision-language models.

## Method Summary
LAMOC uses a two-stage training process with a vision-language captioning model (BLIP) and a large language model (FLAN-T5) for feedback. First, the captioning model is fine-tuned to generate captions that the PLM deems relevant to questions through a prompt-based scoring mechanism. Second, reinforcement learning optimizes the captioning model using rewards derived from the PLM's answer confidence for caption-question pairs. At inference, multiple captions are generated and the PLM answers each, with majority voting producing the final answer.

## Key Results
- Achieves 40.3 VQA score on OK-VQA dataset
- Achieves 37.9 VQA score on A-OKVQA dataset
- Outperforms all zero-shot baselines including Flamingo and BLIP-LM
- Performs competitively with fine-tuned vision-language models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Captioning adaptation improves caption informativeness by selecting captions that correlate with question relevance via PLM scoring.
- Mechanism: The prediction model (PLM) is used to rank candidate captions by relevance to the question using a structured prompt, and only the top-ranked captions are used for fine-tuning the captioning model.
- Core assumption: The PLM can reliably judge caption-question relevance through prompting, and this relevance correlates with caption informativeness for VQA.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we leverage the guidance and feedback of the prediction model (i.e., the PLM) to improve the capability of the captioning model, so that it can be aware of the task goal and information need from the PLM."
  - [section 3.2.1] "We design the following instruction to prompt FLAN-T5-XXL to identify more informative captions... the captions with the predicted option “D:75%” are more relevant to the question."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct evidence from neighbors.
- Break condition: If the PLM's scoring mechanism becomes unreliable (e.g., due to prompt drift or bias), the caption selection will be ineffective.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reinforcement learning from PLM feedback improves captioning quality by directly optimizing for answer confidence.
- Mechanism: A reward is defined based on the confidence of the PLM's answer prediction given a caption-question pair. This reward is used in policy gradient optimization to fine-tune the captioning model.
- Core assumption: The confidence score from the PLM's answer prediction is a reliable signal of caption informativeness for the VQA task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we employ the feedback from the PLM as reward signals to train the captioning model via reinforcement learning."
  - [section 3.2.2] "we design the following two kinds of reward signals... Confidence-based Reward: Since there is no ground-truth answer during training... we employ the probability score of the predicted answer... as the reward."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct evidence from neighbors.
- Break condition: If the PLM's answer confidence is not correlated with actual answer correctness, the RL reward signal becomes meaningless.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-stage training (adaptation then feedback-based learning) enables gradual improvement of the captioning model.
- Mechanism: First, the captioning model is aligned with the PLM's preferences via supervised fine-tuning on selected captions. Second, it is refined using reinforcement learning based on PLM feedback, allowing the model to adapt to the specific VQA task.
- Core assumption: The two training stages are complementary and necessary; adaptation alone is insufficient for task-specific performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "To develop our approach, we design two specific training stages, where the first stage adapts the captioning model to the prediction model... and the second stage tunes the captioning model according to the task goal (learning from feedback of the PLM)."
  - [section 3.2] "Our approach is specially designed with two gradual training stages... At the first stage, the captioning model is trained to align to the prediction model... At the second stage... we employ the feedback from the PLM as reward signals to train the captioning model via reinforcement learning."
  - [section 5.2] "When training the captioning model, we adopt two gradual training stages: captioning adaptation and feedback-based learning. In this part, we study the effectiveness of this training strategy and explore whether one training stage is more effective than the other."
- Break condition: If one stage dominates the other in effectiveness, the two-stage process may be simplified.

  - Quick check question: What are the typical pre-training objectives for vision-language models?

  - Why needed here: To elicit reliable relevance scores and answers from the PLM without additional training.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Image, Question -> Captioning Model (BLIP) -> Multiple Captions -> Caption Selection Module (PLM scoring) -> Selected Captions -> Captioning Model fine-tuning -> Captioning Model -> Multiple Captions -> PLM Answer Generation -> Confidence-based Reward -> RL Optimizer -> Captioning Model -> Multiple Captions -> PLM Answer Generation -> Majority Voting -> Final Answer

- Critical path:
  1. Image and question fed to captioning model
  2. Captioning model generates multiple captions
  3. PLM scores captions for relevance
  4. Top captions used to fine-tune captioning model
  5. Captioning model generates captions for training samples
  6. PLM provides confidence-based reward
  7. RL optimizes captioning model
  8. At inference: multiple captions → PLM → majority vote → final answer

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Caption diversity vs. relevance: Sampling multiple captions increases diversity but may include irrelevant ones.
  - PLM size vs. feedback quality: Larger PLMs provide more reliable feedback but increase computational cost.
  - Prompt complexity vs. reliability: More complex prompts may yield better scores but risk instability.

- Failure signatures:
  - Captioning model generates generic captions unrelated to the question
  - PLM answers correctly despite irrelevant captions (language bias)
  - Training diverges due to unstable reward signals
  - Performance plateaus early, suggesting insufficient training data or poor feedback quality

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify caption relevance scoring: Generate captions for a small set of images/questions, manually check PLM relevance scores vs. actual relevance.
  2. Test reward correlation: Generate answer predictions with different captions, check if confidence correlates with correctness.
  3. Ablation on training stages: Train with only adaptation, only RL, and both; compare performance to isolate stage contributions.

## Limitations

- The reliability of PLM-based caption selection and reward signals is not empirically validated, relying on assumptions about the PLM's ability to judge caption relevance and answer confidence.
- The approach's generalization to other knowledge-based VQA datasets beyond OK-VQA and A-OKVQA is untested, limiting confidence in broader applicability.
- The computational cost of using large PLMs for feedback in both training stages is not discussed, raising concerns about practical applicability.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The overall experimental results showing LAMOC outperforming zero-shot baselines and achieving competitive performance with fine-tuned VLP models. The reported VQA scores (40.3 on OK-VQA, 37.9 on A-OKVQA) are specific and verifiable.
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of the two-stage training approach. While the paper describes both stages and shows improved performance over single-stage alternatives, the necessity of both stages is not conclusively proven.
- **Low Confidence**: The reliability of PLM-based caption selection and reward signals. The paper asserts that PLM scoring and confidence measures are effective feedback mechanisms, but provides limited empirical evidence that these signals are actually reliable or well-calibrated.

## Next Checks

2. **Reward Signal Correlation**: Systematically test whether PLM answer confidence scores correlate with actual answer correctness by generating multiple captions per image-question pair, measuring both confidence and accuracy, and computing correlation coefficients.

3. **Generalization Testing**: Evaluate LAMOC on additional knowledge-based VQA datasets beyond OK-VQA and A-OKVQA (such as KB-VQA or FVQA) to assess whether the approach generalizes to different types of knowledge requirements and question distributions.
