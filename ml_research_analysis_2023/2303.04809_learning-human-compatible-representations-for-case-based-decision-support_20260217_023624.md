# Learning Human-Compatible Representations for Case-Based Decision Support

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04809
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04809
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Learning human-compatible representations that align with human similarity perception improves decision support accuracy by 17.8% for butterfly vs. moth classification and 13.2% for pneumonia classification.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of learning representations that are both accurate for classification and aligned with human similarity perception for case-based decision support. The authors propose a multi-task learning framework that combines supervised classification with metric learning using human-provided triplet judgments. Experiments show that human-compatible (HC) representations significantly improve human decision accuracy compared to maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) representations, with filtering class-inconsistent triplets further enhancing performance.

## Method Summary
The method uses a multi-task learning framework that combines supervised learning (cross-entropy loss for classification) and metric learning (triplet margin loss for similarity learning). A hyperparameter λ controls the trade-off between classification accuracy and similarity alignment. The model learns representations where examples perceived as similar by humans are closer in the embedding space. The approach is evaluated through head-to-head comparisons, neutral decision support (showing examples from the predicted class), and persuasive decision support (showing examples from each class).

## Key Results
- Human-compatible representations achieve 17.8% improvement in human decision accuracy for butterfly vs. moth classification
- Human-compatible representations achieve 13.2% improvement in human decision accuracy for pneumonia classification
- Filtering class-inconsistent triplets further improves decision support performance across all alignments

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Combining supervised learning and metric learning produces representations that satisfy both high classification accuracy and human similarity alignment.
- Mechanism: The multi-task loss function (Equation 1) jointly optimizes a cross-entropy loss for classification and a triplet margin loss for similarity learning, with a hyperparameter λ controlling the trade-off. When λ = 0.5, the model learns representations that are both discriminative for classification and aligned with human perception of similarity.
- Core assumption: Human-provided triplet judgments capture the similarity metric that humans use in decision-making tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose a novel multi-task learning method that combines supervised learning and metric learning."
  - [section 2]: "We propose to address the metric alignment problem with additional supervision on the human similarity metric."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence; this is the core novel contribution.
- Break condition: If human triplet judgments are inconsistent with the true classification labels (as shown in the VW experiments), the model may learn a representation that prioritizes human similarity over classification accuracy, potentially reducing autonomous performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Human-compatible representations improve decision support accuracy by selecting examples that are perceived as more similar by humans.
- Mechanism: When humans are shown nearest neighbor examples from the training set as support for a test example, they are more likely to make correct decisions if the supporting examples are perceived as similar to the test example. HC representations achieve this by aligning with human similarity judgments, while MLE representations may select dissimilar examples that confuse humans.
- Core assumption: Humans use similarity-based reasoning when making decisions with supporting examples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Human-compatible representations identify nearest neighbors that are perceived as more similar by humans and allow humans to make more accurate predictions, leading to substantial improvements in human decision accuracies."
  - [section 2]: "To justify a predicted label, the chosen example should ideally appear similar to the test image. Crucially, this similarity is perceived by humans."
  - [corpus]: The corpus neighbors list shows related work on interpretable classification and case-based reasoning, supporting the mechanism's relevance.
- Break condition: If humans do not rely on similarity when using supporting examples (e.g., if they ignore the examples entirely), then the alignment of representations with human similarity would have no effect on decision accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Filtering class-inconsistent triplets improves decision support performance by removing human noise while retaining similarity information.
- Mechanism: Some human triplet judgments may select candidates from the wrong class as being more similar to the reference, which contradicts the classification task. Filtering these out creates a cleaner signal that balances human similarity perception with classification requirements, leading to better decision support.
- Core assumption: Human similarity judgments contain noise that can be partially filtered while retaining the core similarity signal useful for decision support.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3]: "We consider a variant of human-compatible representations where we isolate human intuition that's compatible with classification and remove these classification-inconsistent triplets from the training set; we refer to this condition as HC-filtered."
  - [section 4.2]: "Fig. 3 shows that filtering class-inconsistent triplets improves HC's decision support performance across all alignments."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence for this specific filtering mechanism.
- Break condition: If filtering removes too many valid similarity judgments, it may degrade the model's ability to capture human perception, reducing the effectiveness of the representations for decision support.

- Critical path: Input image → ResNet-18 → Projection head → Representation space → Classification head + Cross-entropy loss + Triplet margin loss → Trained model
- Design tradeoffs: The main tradeoff is between classification accuracy and similarity alignment, controlled by λ. A higher λ prioritizes classification but may reduce similarity alignment, while a lower λ prioritizes similarity but may reduce classification accuracy.
- Failure signatures: Poor decision support performance despite high classification accuracy suggests misalignment between model and human similarity metrics. Low triplet accuracy indicates the model is not learning the similarity judgments well.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train HC with different λ values (0.2, 0.5, 0.8) and evaluate classification accuracy, triplet accuracy, and decision support performance to find the optimal trade-off.

  3. Test HC representations on a new dataset with different human similarity patterns to assess generalization across domains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do human-compatible representations perform when applied to domain experts versus laypeople?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss limitations of experimenting with crowdworkers and suggest that as expertise level increases, HC should be able to learn a high-quality representation. They also note that our ultimate goal is to apply our method to domain experts.
- Why unresolved: The paper only experiments with crowdworkers and laypeople, not domain experts. It's unclear if the same performance improvements would hold for experts with domain knowledge.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct user studies with domain experts (e.g. radiologists for CXR task) and compare their performance with HC vs MLE representations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different distance metrics beyond Euclidean distance affect the learned human-compatible representations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that they use Euclidean distance as the similarity metric for both loss calculation and distance measurement. They suggest that experimenting with different kinds of metrics and exploring the effectiveness of their respective representations in decision support would be an interesting future direction.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses Euclidean distance. It's unclear if other distance metrics like cosine similarity or learned metrics would lead to better human-aligned representations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiment with different distance metrics in the representation learning framework and evaluate their performance on decision support tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the quality and diversity of human similarity judgments impact the learned human-compatible representations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss limitations of experimenting with crowdworkers, noting that collected triplets may come from superficial or salient features. They also discuss potential biases in the human judgment dataset depending on the domain and task.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses crowdworker judgments without examining the impact of judgment quality and diversity. It's unclear how the learned representations would change with different types of human input.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct experiments varying the quality, diversity, and source of human similarity judgments (e.g. experts vs crowdworkers, different domains) and evaluate the impact on learned representations and downstream decision support performance.

## Limitations
- The method relies on human-provided triplet judgments, which may contain noise and inconsistency, particularly for ambiguous cases.
- Performance gains are demonstrated on specific classification tasks, and generalization to other domains remains untested.
- The filtering mechanism may remove valuable similarity information while trying to reduce noise.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (multi-task learning effectiveness): High confidence - well-established technique with clear mathematical formulation
- Mechanism 2 (similarity-based decision improvement): Medium confidence - supported by experiments but relies on behavioral assumptions about human reasoning
- Mechanism 3 (filtering effectiveness): Medium confidence - experimental results show improvement but the tradeoff between noise reduction and information loss is not fully characterized

## Next Checks
1. Test HC representations on a dataset with different human similarity patterns to assess domain generalization and robustness to varied perceptual judgments.
2. Conduct user studies varying the presentation of supporting examples (e.g., showing only one vs. multiple examples) to understand how presentation format affects the impact of representation alignment.
3. Implement an adaptive λ schedule that adjusts the trade-off during training based on validation performance, rather than using a fixed λ=0.5, to optimize the balance between classification accuracy and similarity alignment.
