# HausaNLP at SemEval-2023 Task 12: Leveraging African Low Resource TweetData for Sentiment Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.13634
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13634
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: Fine-tuned Afro-xlmr-large achieved weighted F1 scores up to 81% on Hausa and 69.5% on multilingual sentiment classification across 14 African languages

## Executive Summary
This study addresses sentiment analysis challenges for low-resource African languages using Twitter data from the SemEval-2023 Task 12. The authors evaluate five pre-trained models (Afro-xlmr-large, AfriBERTa-Large, BERT-base-arabic-camelbert-da-sentiment, Multilingual-BERT, and BERT) across three subtasks: monolingual, multilingual, and zero-shot classification. Afro-xlmr-large demonstrated superior performance in most languages and tasks, with Nigerian languages (Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba) achieving notably higher scores due to larger dataset volumes. The work highlights the potential of African-centric language models for sentiment analysis in low-resource settings while revealing the critical impact of dataset size on model performance.

## Method Summary
The study fine-tuned five pre-trained transformer models on Twitter data spanning 14 African languages. Models were evaluated across three subtasks: monolingual classification for individual languages, multilingual classification requiring models to handle multiple languages simultaneously, and zero-shot classification where models predict sentiment for languages unseen during training. The dataset sizes varied significantly across languages, with Nigerian languages having notably larger training volumes. Performance was measured using weighted F1 scores, with Afro-xlmr-large emerging as the top-performing model across most evaluation scenarios.

## Key Results
- Afro-xlmr-large achieved weighted F1 scores up to 81% on Hausa language sentiment classification
- Multilingual task performance reached 69.5% weighted F1 using Afro-xlmr-large
- Nigerian languages (Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba) consistently outperformed other languages due to larger dataset volumes
- Afro-xlmr-large outperformed AfriBERTa-Large, Arabic BERT variants, Multilingual-BERT, and standard BERT across most evaluation scenarios

## Foundational Learning
- **Fine-tuning pre-trained transformers**: Adapting general language models to specific downstream tasks by updating model parameters on task-specific data
  - Why needed: Enables leveraging large pre-trained models for low-resource languages without full training
  - Quick check: Compare fine-tuned model performance against baseline pre-trained model
- **Weighted F1 score calculation**: Harmonic mean of precision and recall that accounts for class imbalance by weighting by class frequency
  - Why needed: Provides balanced evaluation metric when classes have unequal representation
  - Quick check: Calculate both weighted and macro F1 scores to detect class imbalance effects
- **Zero-shot learning in NLP**: Evaluating model performance on languages not seen during training
  - Why needed: Tests model generalization capabilities across language boundaries
  - Quick check: Compare zero-shot performance against multilingual fine-tuned performance

## Limitations
- Dataset size variations across languages may confound model performance comparisons, with Nigerian languages showing higher performance partly due to larger training volumes
- Exclusive focus on Twitter data limits generalizability to other domains and formal language contexts
- Evaluation relies solely on weighted F1 scores without additional metrics like precision-recall curves or class-specific performance measures
- Model comparisons are constrained to a single shared task framework, potentially missing broader generalization capabilities

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The general methodology of fine-tuning pre-trained models for sentiment analysis is sound and well-established. The superiority of Afro-xlmr-large over other models in most tasks is supported by the empirical results presented.
- **Medium confidence**: The specific performance numbers (81% F1 for Hausa, 69.5% for multilingual) are likely accurate for the tested conditions but may not generalize to different datasets or contexts. The relative ranking of models may shift with different data distributions.
- **Low confidence**: Claims about the inherent superiority of African-centric models versus multilingual models are not fully supported, as dataset size differences confound these comparisons.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct controlled experiments with equalized dataset sizes across all languages to isolate model performance from data volume effects.
2. Evaluate model performance on non-Twitter datasets (news articles, social media from different platforms) to assess domain generalization.
3. Implement additional evaluation metrics including macro-F1, per-class precision-recall analysis, and cross-lingual transfer experiments to provide a more comprehensive performance assessment.
