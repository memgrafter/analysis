# Pruning Pre-trained Language Models with Principled Importance and Self-regularization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.12394
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12394
- **Reference count**: 21
- **Primary result**: Proposed PINS approach consistently outperforms existing pruning methods in accuracy at 80-90% sparsity levels while providing practical efficiency gains

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a principled approach for iterative pruning of pre-trained language models that frames the optimal pruning decision as solving an equality-constrained 0-1 Integer Linear Programming problem. The solution to this optimization problem naturally leads to a new importance criterion for ranking parameters during iterative pruning. To address poor generalization at high sparsity levels, the authors propose a self-regularization scheme where the model prediction is regularized by the latest checkpoint with increasing sparsity throughout pruning. Experiments on various Transformer-based PLMs demonstrate consistent performance improvements over existing pruning methods across multiple datasets.

## Method Summary
The proposed PINS method consists of two key components: a principled importance criterion derived from solving an equality-constrained 0-1 ILP problem, and a self-regularization scheme that improves generalization at high sparsity levels. The importance criterion is computed as the negative impact of the gradient update and current parameter value, which is then used to rank and select parameters for pruning during iterative pruning. The self-regularization regularizes the current model's prediction with the latest checkpoint output, with the regularization strength increasing as sparsity grows. The method uses a cubic sparsity scheduler and is evaluated on multiple Transformer-based PLMs including BERT, GPT-2, Electra, and MiniLM across various NLP tasks.

## Key Results
- PINS consistently outperforms existing pruning methods like IMP and SFP across 80-90% sparsity levels
- The self-regularization scheme provides significant improvements in generalization at high sparsity
- PINS achieves better trade-offs between model size reduction and accuracy preservation
- The pruned models enjoy practical efficiency gains in storage and inference time

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Finding the optimal pruning decision is equivalent to solving an equality-constrained 0-1 ILP problem.
- **Mechanism:** The temporal variation of the learning objective under iterative pruning can be expressed as a linear combination of parameter updates. The pruning decision (which parameters to zero out) becomes a binary optimization problem with an equality constraint on the number of remaining parameters.
- **Core assumption:** The second-order term in the Taylor expansion of the learning objective is negligible (λmax is small for PLMs).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We discovered that finding the optimal pruning decision is an equality-constrained 0-1 Integer Linear Programming problem."
  - [section 3.1] "From our analysis, we draw an equivalence between finding the optimal stepwise pruning decision and solving an equality-constrained 0-1 Integer Linear Programming problem."
- **Break condition:** If the Hessian matrix has large eigenvalues or the learning objective is highly non-convex, the second-order term cannot be ignored and the ILP formulation becomes invalid.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The solution to the ILP problem naturally leads to a principled importance criterion for ranking parameters.
- **Mechanism:** Once the ILP problem is formulated, the optimal solution can be obtained by sorting parameters by their "value" (importance score) and selecting the top-r(t) ones. The value is computed as the negative impact of the gradient update and the current parameter value.
- **Core assumption:** The equal-weight 0-1 Knapsack problem (a special case of the ILP) can be solved optimally by simple sorting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The solution to this optimization problem leads to a principled importance criterion which we use to rank parameters during iterative model pruning."
  - [section 3.1] "Its optimal solution can be obtained by sorting items in descending order according to their values and selecting the top-r(t) ones."
- **Break condition:** If the "value" function becomes non-linear or the problem deviates from the equal-weight case, simple sorting no longer guarantees optimal solution.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Self-regularization improves generalization by providing a tighter upper bound of expected generalization error.
- **Mechanism:** By regularizing the current model's prediction with the latest checkpoint (which has higher sparsity), the capacity gap between "teacher" and "student" is dynamically adjusted, leading to a tighter generalization bound than learning from training data alone.
- **Core assumption:** The learning speed α decreases as sparsity increases, and the capacity measure |Fθ|C increases with sparsity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "To mitigate the poor generalization at high sparsity levels, we propose a self-regularization scheme where model prediction is regularized by the latest checkpoint with increasing sparsity throughout pruning."
  - [section 3.2] "We theoretically justify the effectiveness of self-regularization...bound(fθ(t←ti)) ≤ bound(fθ(t←tj ))"
- **Break condition:** If the capacity gap between checkpoints becomes too large or the learning dynamics change dramatically, the regularization may not provide meaningful improvement.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Training data, validation data, pre-trained model -> PINS algorithm (iterative pruning with principled importance and self-regularization) -> Pruned sparse model
- **Critical path:**
  1. Compute gradients for current mini-batch
  2. Calculate importance scores using the principled criterion
  3. Generate pruning mask based on importance scores
  4. Update model parameters with mask applied
  5. Periodically evaluate on validation set and save checkpoints
  6. Apply self-regularization during training

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Memory vs. Performance:** Self-regularization requires storing checkpoints, increasing memory usage but improving performance
  - **Speed vs. Quality:** The principled importance criterion is more computationally expensive than simple magnitude-based pruning but yields better results
  - **Flexibility vs. Complexity:** The ILP formulation is theoretically optimal but may not adapt well to non-standard pruning scenarios

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Poor performance despite high sparsity:** May indicate that the self-regularization is not effective for the task or that the capacity gap between checkpoints is too large
  - **No improvement over baselines:** Could suggest that the principled importance criterion is not providing meaningful differentiation from existing methods
  - **Slow convergence:** May indicate that the additional computation for importance scoring is becoming a bottleneck

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sanity check on GLUE:** Run PINS on a single GLUE task (e.g., SST-2) at moderate sparsity (50%) to verify basic functionality and compare against IMP
  2. **Self-regularization ablation:** Run the same task with and without self-regularization to measure its impact on performance
  3. **Sparsity pattern analysis:** Visualize the sparsity patterns produced by PINS vs. IMP to understand the differences in parameter selection

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the proposed importance criterion perform on tasks with significantly different data distributions or domain shifts compared to the pre-training corpus?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper evaluates the method on a variety of NLP tasks but does not explore performance under domain shift scenarios.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on standard benchmarks without investigating robustness to domain shifts, which is crucial for real-world applications.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments showing performance comparisons between PINS and baselines on datasets with varying degrees of domain shift from the pre-training corpus.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the impact of different sparsity schedules (e.g., linear, exponential) on the effectiveness of PINS compared to the cubic schedule used in the paper?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper mentions using a cubic sparsity scheduler but does not explore alternative scheduling strategies.
- **Why unresolved:** The choice of sparsity scheduler could significantly affect the pruning process and final model performance, but this aspect is not thoroughly investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative experiments using different sparsity schedules (linear, exponential, step-wise) to evaluate their impact on model performance and convergence.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does PINS perform when applied to very large-scale models (e.g., GPT-3, T5) with hundreds of billions of parameters?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The experiments are conducted on relatively smaller models (BERT base, GPT-2) and do not address scalability to extremely large models.
- **Why unresolved:** The computational and memory requirements for very large models may pose significant challenges that are not addressed in the current study.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementation and evaluation of PINS on large-scale models, including analysis of computational efficiency and memory usage during the pruning process.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis relies on the assumption that the second-order term in the Taylor expansion is negligible, which may not hold for all PLMs and tasks
- The effectiveness of self-regularization depends on the relationship between learning speed and sparsity, which is not empirically verified
- The method's performance on extremely large-scale models (hundreds of billions of parameters) is not explored
