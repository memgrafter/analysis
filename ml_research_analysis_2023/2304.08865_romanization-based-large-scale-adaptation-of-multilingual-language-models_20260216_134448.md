# Romanization-based Large-scale Adaptation of Multilingual Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.08865
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08865
- Reference count: 20
- Key outcome: UROMAN-based transliteration enables effective adaptation of mBERT to low-resource languages with unseen scripts, outperforming non-transliteration approaches by 8-22 F1 points on NER and 17 UAS points on DP.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates using UROMAN, a universal transliteration tool, to romanize text from low-resource languages and adapt multilingual language models like mBERT to these languages. By romanizing the text, the authors aim to leverage the existing vocabulary and embeddings in mBERT, avoiding the need for vocabulary expansion. The authors compare UROMAN to language-specific transliterators and other transliteration strategies on 14 diverse low-resource languages across two tasks: Named Entity Recognition and Dependency Parsing. The key findings are: 1) UROMAN performs comparably to or better than language-specific transliterators on average. 2) Transliteration-based adaptation (TransLA + INV) outperforms non-transliteration adaptation (Non-TransLA + INV) for languages with unseen scripts by 8-22 F1 points on NER and 17 UAS points on DP. 3) Transliteration is particularly beneficial in low-resource settings with limited training data.

## Method Summary
The paper uses the MAD-X framework to adapt mBERT to 14 low-resource languages through adapter-based modular fine-tuning. Four adaptation variants are evaluated: Non-TransLA+INV (train LA and INV on non-transliterated data), Non-TransLA+EmbLex (train LA with new tokenizer on non-transliterated data), TransLA+INV (train LA and INV on UROMAN-transliterated data), and TransLA+mPLMft (train LA with fine-tuned mBERT embeddings on UROMAN-transliterated data). The evaluation uses NER on WikiANN and DP on UD v2.7, comparing performance against English task adapters trained on high-resource data.

## Key Results
- UROMAN performs comparably to or better than language-specific transliterators on average across 14 low-resource languages.
- Transliteration-based adaptation (TransLA + INV) outperforms non-transliteration adaptation (Non-TransLA + INV) for languages with unseen scripts by 8-22 F1 points on NER and 17 UAS points on DP.
- Transliteration is particularly beneficial in low-resource settings with limited training data without any vocabulary augmentation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: UROMAN-based transliteration can effectively map low-resource languages with unseen scripts into Latin script, thereby increasing token overlap with mBERT's vocabulary.
- Mechanism: UROMAN uses an unsupervised mapping from UTF-8 to Latin characters without requiring language-specific dictionaries, allowing broad coverage across writing systems.
- Core assumption: The Latin script already covers a large portion of mBERT's vocabulary (e.g., 77% as noted in Ács), so romanizing the rest improves vocabulary sharing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "UROMAN can offer strong performance for many languages, with particular gains achieved in the most challenging setups: on languages with unseen scripts and with limited training data without any vocabulary augmentation."
  - [section]: "Since the Latin script covers a dominant portion of the mPLM's vocabulary (e.g., 77% in case of mBERT... 'romanizing' the remaining part of the vocabulary might mitigate the vocabulary size issue and boost vocabulary sharing."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; neighbor papers focus on transliteration but do not specifically validate UROMAN's unsupervised approach.
- Break condition: If UROMAN's lexical substitutions fail to preserve semantic or syntactic features essential for downstream tasks, performance gains may disappear.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Transliteration-based adaptation (TransLA + INV) outperforms non-transliteration adaptation (Non-TransLA + INV) for languages with unseen scripts.
- Mechanism: By romanizing input text, transliteration reduces the number of "UNK" tokens, enabling the adapter to learn from more context rather than from sparse or missing tokens.
- Core assumption: The mBERT tokenizer fails to represent unseen scripts, leading to high "UNK" rates that hurt adaptation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Transliteration is particularly beneficial in low-resource settings with limited training data without any vocabulary augmentation."
  - [section]: "TransLA+INV outperforms Non-TransLA+INV for all the languages with unseen scripts, and achieves that with huge margins (~ 8-22 points for NER and ~ 17 points in UAS scores)."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence supporting the magnitude of gains; only neighboring work on transliteration effects.
- Break condition: If transliteration introduces ambiguous or incorrect mappings, the adapter may learn incorrect representations, eroding the advantage.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: A tokenizer trained on romanized data (Non-TransLA+EmbLex) can outperform UROMAN-based methods because it better captures the language's subword structure.
- Mechanism: Training a tokenizer on the target romanized corpus creates a vocabulary that reflects the actual distribution of subwords in that data, improving token coverage.
- Core assumption: The quality of tokenization (fertility, proportion of continued subwords) is a key driver of downstream performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Non-TransLA+EmbLex, however, now outperforms UROMAN-based methods for a majority of the languages... This observation can be attributed to various factors related to mBERT's tokenizer."
  - [section]: "TransLA+EmbLex now outperforms the non-transliteration-based variant on 8/12 languages and also on average."
  - [corpus]: No corpus evidence on tokenizer quality impact; relies on internal analysis.
- Break condition: If the romanized corpus is too small or noisy, the trained tokenizer may generalize poorly, leading to worse performance than using the base mBERT tokenizer.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Cross-lingual transfer via adapter-based modular fine-tuning.
  - Why needed here: The paper uses MAD-X adapters to adapt mBERT to unseen languages without modifying the base model, which is critical for parameter efficiency.
  - Quick check question: What is the role of invertible adapters (INV) in MAD-X and how do they help with vocabulary mismatch?

- Concept: Transliteration and romanization in NLP.
  - Why needed here: The paper relies on converting non-Latin scripts to Latin to leverage mBERT's existing vocabulary and embeddings.
  - Quick check question: How does UROMAN differ from language-specific transliterators in terms of dictionary usage and coverage?

- Concept: Tokenizer quality metrics (UNK rate, fertility, continued subwords).
  - Why needed here: The paper evaluates tokenization quality to explain differences in performance between romanized and non-romanized setups.
  - Quick check question: Why does reducing the % of "UNK" tokens matter for downstream task performance?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Base mPLM -> Language adapters (LA) -> Task adapters (TA) -> Invertible adapters (INV) -> Tokenizer -> Transliteration layer

- Critical path:
  1. Prepare target language data (romanize if using transliteration).
  2. Train language adapters on romanized/non-romanized data.
  3. Evaluate with MAD-X setup (LA + INV or LA + EmbLex).
  4. Compare performance against baseline Non-TransLA+INV.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - UROMAN-based vs language-specific transliterators: UROMAN is scalable but less accurate for some languages; language-specific is accurate but not scalable.
  - Tokenizer choice: mBERT tokenizer is fixed but limited for unseen scripts; custom tokenizer is flexible but requires training data.
  - Parameter efficiency: INV adapters reuse mBERT embeddings; EmbLex adapters fine-tune embeddings for better fit.

- Failure signatures:
  - High % of UNK tokens after romanization indicates poor script-to-Latin mapping.
  - Performance drop when switching from UROMAN to language-specific transliterator suggests dependency on transliteration quality.
  - Lower fertility or higher continued subwords in custom tokenizer signals suboptimal tokenization for the target language.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare UROMAN vs language-specific transliterators on a small set of languages with available ground-truth transliterations.
  2. Measure % of UNK tokens before and after transliteration to quantify vocabulary coverage gains.
  3. Evaluate sample efficiency by training adapters on varying amounts of romanized vs non-romanized data to find the crossover point.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does UROMAN's performance compare to language-specific transliterators when evaluating on languages not included in the current study?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares UROMAN to language-specific transliterators on a set of 14 languages and finds that UROMAN performs comparably or better on average. However, the study is limited to these specific languages.
- Why unresolved: The study only evaluates UROMAN on a limited set of languages. It is unclear how UROMAN would perform on a wider range of languages, especially those with more complex writing systems or less linguistic data available.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive evaluation of UROMAN on a diverse set of languages, including those with complex writing systems and limited linguistic data, would provide evidence for its generalizability and performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of UROMAN's non-invertibility on downstream tasks that require reversible transliteration?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that UROMAN is not fully reversible, which may limit its appeal in scenarios where text in the original script needs to be exactly reproduced.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the consequences of UROMAN's non-invertibility on specific downstream tasks that require reversible transliteration, such as certain types of machine translation or text-to-speech systems.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating the impact of UROMAN's non-invertibility on downstream tasks that require reversible transliteration would provide insights into its limitations and potential workarounds.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of transliteration-based adaptation methods vary with different mPLM architectures and tokenization strategies?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper evaluates transliteration-based adaptation methods using mBERT and its tokenization strategy. However, the performance of these methods may vary with different mPLM architectures and tokenization strategies.
- Why unresolved: The study only evaluates transliteration-based adaptation methods using mBERT. It is unclear how these methods would perform with other mPLM architectures or tokenization strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating transliteration-based adaptation methods using different mPLM architectures and tokenization strategies would provide insights into their generalizability and potential limitations.

## Limitations

- The paper lacks direct comparison between UROMAN and language-specific transliterators on the same languages, as different language sets were used for each approach.
- The paper does not provide systematic error analysis to determine whether transliteration-induced errors are random or systematic, which would impact the reliability of the proposed approach.
- The vocabulary sharing analysis, while theoretically sound, lacks empirical validation showing how many mBERT tokens actually overlap with romanized target language text.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The claim that transliteration-based adaptation outperforms non-transliteration adaptation for languages with unseen scripts is well-supported by the experimental results, showing consistent 8-22 F1 point improvements in NER and 17 UAS point improvements in DP. The sample efficiency findings are also robust, with clear trends showing transliteration benefits are more pronounced with limited training data.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that UROMAN performs comparably to or better than language-specific transliterators is based on averaged results across different language sets, which introduces uncertainty. The claim about tokenizer quality explaining performance differences is plausible but relies on indirect evidence rather than controlled experiments isolating tokenizer effects.

**Low Confidence**: The mechanism explaining why romanization increases token overlap with mBERT's vocabulary is theoretically sound but lacks empirical validation. The claim that UROMAN's unsupervised approach is sufficient for strong performance across diverse languages is not fully tested, as the evaluation does not systematically compare UROMAN against high-quality language-specific alternatives on the same data.

## Next Checks

1. **Direct Transliterator Comparison**: Conduct a controlled experiment comparing UROMAN against language-specific transliterators on the same set of 5-6 languages where both are available, measuring not just downstream task performance but also tokenization quality metrics (UNK rate, fertility, continued subwords) to isolate the impact of transliteration quality.

2. **Vocabulary Overlap Analysis**: Perform an empirical analysis quantifying the actual token overlap between mBERT's vocabulary and romanized target language text across all 14 languages, measuring the percentage of romanized tokens that map to existing mBERT embeddings versus generating UNK tokens, and correlating this with downstream performance.

3. **Error Pattern Investigation**: Analyze the types and frequencies of errors produced by romanization-based vs non-romanization-based systems, distinguishing between errors caused by transliteration quality versus those arising from adapter training, to determine whether transliteration errors are systematic (suggesting the need for better transliterators) or random (suggesting adaptation strategies could learn to correct them).
