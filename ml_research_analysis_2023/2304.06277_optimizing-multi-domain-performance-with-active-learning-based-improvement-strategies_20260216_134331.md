# Optimizing Multi-Domain Performance with Active Learning-based Improvement Strategies

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.06277
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06277
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Tri-training with active learning strategies improves multi-domain model performance by 3-5% accuracy over baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a tri-training approach that leverages three models trained on bootstrap samples of labeled data to generate proxy labels on unlabeled data, with iterative active learning strategies for augmenting the training set. The method demonstrates consistent performance gains across multiple domains including VQA, SQuAD, audio classification, and image classification. Experiments show 3-5% accuracy improvements in several cases, with different polling strategies performing better in different domains based on dataset characteristics.

## Method Summary
The approach uses tri-training with three models trained on 70% of labeled data (split into three 1/3 subsets via bootstrap sampling). These models generate predictions on the remaining 30% unlabeled data, and examples where a specified number of models agree are added back to the training set with ground truth labels replacing predictions. The process iterates three times using three different polling strategies: Strategy 1 (any 2 models agree), Strategy 2 (all 3 models agree with ground truth replacement), and Strategy 3 (all 3 models agree with prediction replacement).

## Key Results
- Strategy 2 (all three models agree, using ground truth labels) performed best for VQA and SQuAD
- Strategy 1 (two models agree) worked best for CIFAR-100
- Consistent 3-5% accuracy improvements across multiple domains compared to baseline models
- Random sampling strategy underperformed all tri-training approaches

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Agreement among three diverse models identifies high-quality unlabeled examples for augmentation. Three models trained on different subsets of labeled data produce diverse predictions, and agreement indicates high-confidence predictions likely to be correct.

### Mechanism 2
Replacing model-generated predictions with ground truth labels during augmentation eliminates false positives and improves iterative learning by preventing reinforcement of incorrect labels.

### Mechanism 3
Different polling strategies (2-model agreement vs 3-model agreement) perform better in different domains based on dataset characteristics such as ambiguity and class separability.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data preprocessing (70-30 split) -> Three parallel model training instances -> Prediction aggregation logic -> Ground truth label replacement -> Iterative retraining loop
- **Critical path**: 1) Split labeled data 70-30, 2) Train three models on 70% data, 3) Generate predictions on unlabeled 30%, 4) Apply polling strategy, 5) Replace predictions with ground truth, 6) Augment training data, 7) Retrain models, 8) Repeat for 3 iterations
- **Design tradeoffs**: Ground truth label requirement improves accuracy but limits applicability; parallel training accelerates experiments but increases resource costs; different polling strategies require domain-specific tuning
- **Failure signatures**: Accuracy plateaus or decreases across iterations, models converge to similar predictions, or certain domains show consistently poor performance across all strategies
- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Run baseline training with 70% data only, 2) Implement Strategy 1 for one iteration, 3) Implement Strategy 2 for one iteration

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Why does Strategy 2 (all three models agree) outperform Strategy 1 (two models agree) for VQA and SQuAD but Strategy 1 outperforms Strategy 2 for CIFAR-100? The paper doesn't explain the underlying reasons for this domain-dependent performance difference between strategies.

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal number of active learning iterations before performance plateaus or degrades? The paper uses exactly 3 iterations but doesn't explore whether this is optimal or if more/less iterations would yield better results.

### Open Question 3
How does tri-training performance vary with different base model architectures? The paper uses relatively simple baseline models but doesn't explore whether more sophisticated architectures would improve or diminish the effectiveness of tri-training.

## Limitations
- Requires access to ground truth labels for augmentation, limiting applicability when original dataset is unavailable
- Performance gains vary significantly across domains, suggesting the approach may not generalize uniformly
- Experiments are based on relatively small datasets that may not reflect real-world scale challenges

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The tri-training framework implementation and basic methodology are sound and well-established in literature
- **Medium Confidence**: The specific polling strategies and their domain-specific effectiveness are supported by experimental results but lack theoretical justification for why certain strategies work better in specific domains
- **Low Confidence**: The scalability claims and performance improvements are based on relatively small datasets that may not reflect real-world scale challenges

## Next Checks

1. **Domain Transferability Test**: Apply the three polling strategies to a new domain (e.g., medical imaging) to validate whether Strategy 2 consistently outperforms others or if domain-specific tuning is required

2. **Diversity Analysis**: Measure pairwise model agreement rates across iterations to quantify whether bootstrap sampling creates sufficiently diverse models or if additional diversity-promoting techniques are needed

3. **Ground Truth Dependency Test**: Run experiments where ground truth labels are not available for augmentation to assess how performance degrades and whether the approach can function with predicted labels only
