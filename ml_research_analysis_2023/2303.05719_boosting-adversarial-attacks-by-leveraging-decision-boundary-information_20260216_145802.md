# Boosting Adversarial Attacks by Leveraging Decision Boundary Information

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.05719
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05719
- Reference count: 40
- This paper proposes Boundary Fitting Attack to improve adversarial attack transferability by leveraging decision boundary information.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Boundary Fitting Attack (BF-FGSM), a novel approach to enhance adversarial attack transferability by exploiting decision boundary information. The core insight is that gradients at decision boundaries are more similar across different models than gradients at original positions, enabling more effective adversarial perturbations. The method works by sampling boundary points near the input and averaging their gradients to update adversarial examples. Experiments show significant improvements in success rates against both normally trained and defense models, particularly when attacking transformers with CNNs as substitutes.

## Method Summary
The Boundary Fitting Attack (BF-FGSM) enhances adversarial transferability by sampling boundary points near the input image through random direction sampling and boundary search. For each boundary point, gradients are calculated and then averaged to update the adversarial example. Key parameters include max perturbation epsilon=16, iterations T=10, step size alpha=1.6, shrinkage factor gamma=0.6, and boundary points N=20. The method is evaluated on ImageNet against various models including Inception-v3, Inception-v4, Inception-Resnet-v2, ResNet-50/101/152, and defense models.

## Key Results
- Improves success rates by 5.6% against normally trained CNNs and 14.9% against defense CNNs compared to state-of-the-art transfer-based attacks
- Achieves 58.2% average success rate when attacking transformers with CNNs as substitute models, 10.8% higher than other methods
- Demonstrates positive correlation between decision boundary distance and model robustness
- Shows transformers are more robust than CNNs due to larger decision boundary distances along natural directions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Boundary gradients are more similar across models than gradients at original positions, enabling improved adversarial transferability.
- Mechanism: The paper proposes sampling boundary points near the input image by moving along random directions until crossing the decision boundary, then averaging gradients at these boundary points to update adversarial examples.
- Core assumption: Decision boundaries of different models are close and flat, making boundary gradients more transferable than original gradients.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we conduct experiments and discover that the gradients of different models are more similar on the decision boundary than in the original position"
  - [section III-B] "we evaluate the gradient cosine similarity of several white-box →black-box pairs at different data points" and find boundary gradients are less diverse
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct supporting papers found in neighbor corpus
- Break condition: If decision boundaries of models are significantly different or non-flat, the boundary gradient averaging would not capture transferable information effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Decision boundary distance is positively correlated with model robustness.
- Mechanism: By measuring the L∞-distance from boundary points to input along adversarial directions, the paper shows that models with larger decision boundary distances are more robust to attacks.
- Core assumption: Larger decision boundary distance means smaller adversarial subspace overlap between models, making attacks less transferable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section IV-D] "we observe that model robustness is positively correlated with the decision boundary distance" and compare transformers vs CNNs
  - [section III-D] Table II shows BF-FGSM reduces victim model boundary distance more than other methods
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct supporting papers found in neighbor corpus
- Break condition: If robustness depends on factors other than decision boundary distance (e.g., feature representation), this correlation would break.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Transformers are more robust than CNNs due to larger decision boundary distances along natural directions.
- Mechanism: The paper compares decision boundary distances along natural vs adversarial directions for transformers and CNNs, finding transformers have larger distances along natural directions but similar distances along adversarial directions.
- Core assumption: Larger natural direction boundary distances mean smaller adversarial subspace overlap, making transformers harder to attack.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section IV-D] "we conjecture that the decision boundary distance obtained along arbitrary directions is larger than that of CNNs" and provides experimental support
  - [section III-D] Table V shows transformers have larger boundary distances along natural directions
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct supporting papers found in neighbor corpus
- Break condition: If transformers' robustness comes from different architectural properties rather than boundary geometry, this explanation would be incomplete.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input preprocessing -> Boundary point generation -> Gradient calculation -> Gradient averaging -> Adversarial update -> Model ensemble

- Critical path:
  1. Generate boundary points by moving input along random directions
  2. Calculate gradients at each boundary point
  3. Average boundary gradients to get update direction
  4. Apply update to adversarial example with clipping
  5. Repeat for T iterations

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Boundary point sampling (N): More points → better boundary approximation but higher computational cost
  - Shrinkage factor (γ): Smaller γ → more precise boundary but slower convergence
  - Step size (α): Larger α → faster convergence but risk of instability

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor transferability: Boundary points not representative of true decision boundary
  - High computational cost: Excessive boundary point sampling or small shrinkage factor
  - Instability: Large step sizes causing adversarial examples to leave valid input space

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare gradient cosine similarity at original vs boundary points for simple CNN pair
  2. Measure decision boundary distance reduction for BF-FGSM vs baseline methods
  3. Evaluate transferability improvement on normally trained vs defense models with varying N and γ parameters

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but based on the content and discussion, several important questions emerge:

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the decision boundary distance correlate with robustness across different architectures and datasets beyond ImageNet?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates a positive correlation between decision boundary distance and robustness on ImageNet, but this is limited to the specific models and dataset tested.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests this hypothesis on ImageNet with specific CNN and transformer architectures, leaving open whether the correlation holds more generally.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the correlation between decision boundary distance and robustness across multiple datasets (CIFAR, COCO, etc.) and diverse model architectures (ResNets, EfficientNets, ViTs of different sizes, etc.).

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the boundary gradient averaging approach be extended to other attack paradigms beyond gradient-based methods, such as score-based or decision-based attacks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on gradient-based attacks and demonstrates that averaging boundary gradients improves transferability, but doesn't explore whether this principle could apply to other attack settings.
- Why unresolved: The method is specifically designed for gradient-based attacks, and its effectiveness in other attack paradigms remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and testing boundary gradient averaging concepts in score-based attacks (like NES) or decision-based attacks (like Boundary Attack) to see if similar improvements in success rates occur.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Is there an optimal balance between the number of boundary points sampled (N) and the shrinkage factor (γ) that maximizes attack success rate across different victim models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper provides ablation studies on N and γ separately but doesn't explore their interaction or find optimal combinations.
- Why unresolved: The ablation studies show individual effects but don't explore how these parameters interact, which could lead to better performance through parameter tuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic grid search or optimization experiments varying both N and γ simultaneously across different victim models to identify optimal parameter combinations for maximum attack success.

## Limitations
- The assumption that decision boundaries of different models are close and flat lacks rigorous theoretical justification
- The correlation between decision boundary distance and robustness may be context-dependent and not universally applicable
- The method's effectiveness against more advanced defense mechanisms (beyond those tested) remains unclear
