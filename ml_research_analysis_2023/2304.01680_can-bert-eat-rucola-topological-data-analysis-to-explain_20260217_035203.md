# Can BERT eat RuCoLA? Topological Data Analysis to Explain

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.01680
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01680
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: TDA-based classifiers outperform fine-tuning baselines on English and Russian acceptability datasets by extracting topological features from attention graphs.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a topological data analysis (TDA) approach to explain how Transformer language models capture linguistic acceptability. The authors construct directed attention graphs from model attention matrices and derive graph-theoretic invariants—including novel features like chordality and matching number—as inputs to linear classifiers. They show that these TDA-based classifiers outperform standard fine-tuning on both English (CoLA) and Russian (RuCoLA) datasets. The work demonstrates that topological abstraction of attention structures preserves and reveals linguistic patterns better than raw attention weights.

## Method Summary
The method constructs directed graphs from attention matrices using thresholding, then computes topological invariants (connected components, cycles, chordality, matching number) as features. These features are reduced via PCA and fed to logistic regression classifiers. The approach is applied to frozen and fine-tuned BERT/RoBERTa models on CoLA and RuCoLA datasets, with head importance analyzed via learned feature weights. Two novel features—chordality and matching number—are introduced and shown to improve performance.

## Key Results
- TDA-based classifiers outperform fine-tuning baselines on both English and Russian acceptability datasets.
- Novel features (chordality, matching number) improve classifier performance.
- Middle-layer attention heads contribute most to detecting syntactic and morphological violations.
- Frozen LMs retain better linguistic generalization than fine-tuned models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: TDA features capture syntactic and semantic patterns in attention graphs better than raw attention weights.
- Mechanism: TDA constructs directed graphs from attention matrices, then computes invariants like connected components, cycles, chordality, and matching number. These invariants abstract away raw weight magnitudes while preserving structural patterns relevant to linguistic acceptability.
- Core assumption: Graph-theoretic structure of attention maps correlates with linguistic phenomena such as subject-verb agreement, clause structure, and semantic coherence.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "TDA-based classifiers outperform fine-tuning baselines"; [section 3.1] introduction of chordality and matching number; weak corpus evidence as only internal LM attention matrices are used.
- Break condition: If graph topology is not independent of weight magnitude, TDA features could collapse into noisy correlates of attention strength.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: TDA-based classifiers maintain linguistic generalization better than fine-tuned LMs by preserving pre-trained attention patterns.
- Mechanism: Extracting features from frozen LMs and training linear classifiers avoids catastrophic forgetting of general linguistic knowledge during fine-tuning. The linear classifier acts as a stable wrapper around frozen attention structures.
- Core assumption: Fine-tuning degrades pre-trained attention patterns that encode general grammatical competence, while frozen attention retains them.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "TDA-based classifiers outperform fine-tuning baselines"; [section 4.3] performance comparison showing frozen models retain generalization; weak corpus evidence as no external grammatical benchmarks shown.
- Break condition: If fine-tuning does not actually degrade linguistic features—if gains from adaptation outweigh losses—the frozen-TDA advantage would disappear.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Attention head importance can be quantified via feature weights, enabling fine-grained attribution of linguistic roles.
- Mechanism: Linear classifier learns weights for each TDA feature; features are grouped by head; head importance is measured by sum of absolute weights. Heads are labeled as "agreeing" (support correct prediction) or "disagreeing" (support incorrect prediction).
- Core assumption: Feature importance correlates with linguistic competence: heads with high positive weights for correct predictions encode relevant grammar, while high weights for errors encode noise or anti-patterns.
- Evidence anchors: [section 4.4] "probe linguistic phenomena with the help of persistent features"; [section 4.4] "heads of middle layers contribute more to detecting syntactic and morphology violations"; weak corpus evidence as no external validation against human annotation.
- Break condition: If feature weights are dominated by confounding factors (e.g., sentence length, token frequency), the head importance ranking would reflect artifacts rather than genuine linguistic competence.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Directed graph construction from attention matrices
  - Why needed here: TDA operates on graph structures, so raw attention matrices must be binarized into adjacency matrices via thresholding to form graphs.
  - Quick check question: If threshold=0.1 and attention weight=0.15, does an edge exist? (Yes, because 0.15 ≥ 0.1.)

- Concept: Graph invariants (connected components, cycles, chordality, matching number)
  - Why needed here: These invariants summarize the global structure of attention graphs without depending on exact weight values, capturing syntactic/semantic patterns.
  - Quick check question: If a graph has two disconnected nodes, how many connected components? (Two.)

- Concept: Barcode features from persistent homology
  - Why needed here: Barcodes encode multi-scale topological features across thresholds, adding robustness to single-threshold analysis.
  - Quick check question: If a 1-dimensional barcode has death=5, birth=2, what is its persistence? (3.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Pre-trained LM → Attention extraction → Graph construction → TDA features → PCA → Logistic regression classifier → Prediction

- Critical path:
  LM → Attention extraction → Graph construction → TDA features → PCA → Classifier → Prediction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Threshold choice: Low thresholds preserve more edges but risk noise; high thresholds simplify graphs but may miss subtle dependencies.
  - Feature set size: More features increase discriminative power but risk overfitting; PCA mitigates but discards interpretability.
  - Frozen vs. fine-tuned: Frozen preserves pre-trained patterns but may miss domain adaptation; fine-tuned adapts but risks forgetting.

- Failure signatures:
  - PCA variance ratio too low → features poorly capture variance in labels.
  - Classifier weights all near zero → features not predictive.
  - Correlation between features and sentence length → artifacts rather than linguistic signal.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Vary threshold {0.1, 0.25, 0.5} and measure MCC on dev set to find optimal binarization.
  2. Train classifier with and without chordality/matching features to confirm their contribution.
  3. Compare frozen vs. fine-tuned LM features on a balanced acceptability subset to quantify generalization gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the newly introduced features (chordality and matching number) specifically capture syntactic structures like subject-verb-object triplets and sentence segments (subordinate clauses, adverbials, etc.)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces these features and hypothesizes their connection to syntax phenomena but does not provide a detailed explanation or empirical evidence of how these features specifically capture these structures.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents the features and their potential relevance but lacks a thorough investigation or demonstration of their effectiveness in capturing specific syntactic structures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed case studies or experiments showing how chordality and matching number features correspond to specific syntactic structures in sentences would provide clarity.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the limitations of using topological data analysis (TDA) features in languages with different typological characteristics, such as languages with free word order or rich morphology?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that TDA-based classifiers are tested on English and Russian, but does not explore their effectiveness across a broader range of typologically diverse languages.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to two languages, which may not be representative of the full spectrum of linguistic diversity. The impact of TDA features on languages with different syntactic and morphological properties remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing TDA-based classifiers on a wider array of languages with varying typological features would provide insights into their generalizability and limitations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of TDA-based classifiers compare to other interpretability methods for understanding the functional roles of attention heads in transformer models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the interpretability advantages of TDA-based classifiers but does not compare them with other interpretability techniques such as feature importance scores or similarity analysis between model weights.
- Why unresolved: Without a comparative analysis, it is unclear how TDA-based methods stack up against other interpretability approaches in terms of effectiveness and insights provided.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a comparative study of TDA-based classifiers with other interpretability methods on the same datasets would elucidate their relative strengths and weaknesses.

## Limitations

- Threshold sensitivity: The paper does not specify exact thresholds for binarizing attention matrices, leaving robustness to threshold variation unclear.
- External validation: Head importance analysis lacks validation against external linguistic annotations, making it unclear whether rankings reflect genuine competence or artifacts.
- Computational scalability: The exponential-time algorithm for computing simple cycles poses practical limitations for larger models or longer sequences.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: TDA feature extraction pipeline (connected components, cycles, chordality, matching number) is technically sound and reproducible.
- **Medium Confidence**: Claim that TDA classifiers outperform fine-tuning baselines is supported by experimental results, though the margin is described as "minor" and may not generalize to all model sizes or datasets.
- **Low Confidence**: Interpretation of head importance rankings as genuine linguistic competence lacks external validation and may reflect confounding factors.

## Next Checks

1. **Threshold Sensitivity Analysis**: Systematically vary attention thresholds {0.1, 0.25, 0.5} and measure MCC on dev sets to determine if performance gains are robust to binarization choices or threshold-dependent artifacts.

2. **External Head Validation**: Annotate a subset of attention heads with linguistic expertise (e.g., subject-verb agreement, clause structure) and correlate these annotations with the classifier-derived head importance rankings to validate whether high-weight heads genuinely capture grammatical competence.

3. **Cross-Lingual Generalization**: Apply the TDA feature extraction and classification pipeline to an additional language pair (e.g., English→French or Russian→Czech) to test whether topological attention patterns transfer across typologically related languages.
