# Exploring Methods for Building Dialects-Mandarin Code-Mixing Corpora: A Case Study in Taiwanese Hokkien

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.08937
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08937
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A Hokkien-Mandarin code-mixing corpus built using linguistic constraints, with XLM model achieving strong translation performance while maintaining monolingual quality

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the scarcity of resources for dialect-based code-mixing research by developing a Hokkien-Mandarin code-mixing corpus. The authors propose a method that combines linguistics-based word segmentation, constraint-driven code-mixing synthesis, and an adapted XLM model with dynamic language identification. The approach successfully generates high-quality code-mixed parallel and non-parallel datasets, which are then used to train translation models that perform well on both code-mixed and monolingual tasks. Human evaluation confirms the colloquialism and intelligibility of the translations, demonstrating the effectiveness of integrating linguistic knowledge with modern NLP techniques.

## Method Summary
The method involves three main components: first, Hokkien word segmentation using the Articut tokenizer based on X-bar linguistic theory with a custom Hokkien dictionary; second, synthesis of code-mixed parallel and non-parallel datasets by applying linguistic constraints such as matrix language frame, equivalence constraint, and functional head constraint; third, training an XLM model with dynamic language identification and transfer learning from monolingual data using CLM, MLM, and TLM objectives.

## Key Results
- Proposed Hokkien word segmentation method using X-bar theory and custom dictionary
- Synthesized 76,013 parallel and 75,150 non-parallel Hokkien-Mandarin code-mixed sentences
- XLM model with dynamic language identification and transfer learning achieves strong performance on both code-mixed and monolingual translation tasks
- Human evaluation shows reasonable colloquialism and intelligibility scores

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
XLM model with dynamic language identification (DLI) improves code-mixing translation accuracy by dynamically distinguishing Hokkien and Mandarin words through the _@ symbol, assigning appropriate language embeddings. This allows the model to correctly identify and process mixed-language input without prior knowledge of language boundaries. The core assumption is that language identity markers are reliably applied during data synthesis and preserved during model training. Break condition: If language tags are missing or incorrectly applied during data synthesis, DLI cannot function properly.

### Mechanism 2
Transfer learning from monolingual data improves code-mixing translation performance by pre-training XLM on monolingual Mandarin and Hokkien data using CLM and MLM objectives, providing foundational language knowledge that is then transferred to code-mixing translation tasks. This enables the model to leverage monolingual linguistic patterns when processing mixed-language input. The core assumption is that monolingual language structures and patterns transfer effectively to code-mixed contexts. Break condition: If monolingual data is insufficient or unrepresentative of code-mixing patterns, transfer learning may not generalize effectively.

### Mechanism 3
Linguistics-based word segmentation enables accurate code-mixing synthesis by using the Articut tokenizer based on X-bar theory and custom Hokkien dictionary to provide accurate word boundaries and POS tagging for Hokkien sentences. This enables precise code-switching at grammatically appropriate points following linguistic constraints. The core assumption is that Hokkien and Mandarin share similar syntactic structures due to both being Sino-Tibetan languages. Break condition: If Hokkien grammar significantly diverges from Mandarin beyond what X-bar theory can accommodate, segmentation accuracy may degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Hokkien word segmentation → Code-mixed corpus synthesis → XLM model with DLI → Transfer learning → Translation evaluation
- **Critical path**: Corpus synthesis → Model pre-training → Fine-tuning → Evaluation
- **Design tradeoffs**: Using character-level tokenization vs word-level, balancing between preserving original Hokkien forms vs converting to Mandarin equivalents
- **Failure signatures**: Poor BLEU scores on code-mixed test sets, inability to preserve Hokkien vocabulary in translations, over-reliance on Mandarin patterns
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Test XLM model with and without DLI mechanism on a small code-mixed dataset

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the performance of the proposed Hokkien word segmentation method compare to other approaches when applied to dialectal code-mixing languages beyond the Sino-Tibetan family? The paper only evaluates the method on Hokkien, a Sino-Tibetan language, without testing on other language families. This could be resolved by testing the method on code-mixing corpora involving dialects from different language families (e.g., Tai-Kadai, Austronesian) and comparing segmentation accuracy to other approaches.

### Open Question 2
What is the impact of using a language identification system with less than 100% accuracy on the translation quality of the code-mixed Hokkien-Mandarin model? The experiments all use data with perfect language identification, without investigating how realistic errors in language identification would impact translation quality. This could be resolved by introducing controlled noise into the language identification process (e.g., randomly flipping some language tags) and measuring the degradation in BLEU/BERTScore on the translation task.

### Open Question 3
How does the proposed method for synthesizing code-mixed corpora compare to neural generative approaches in terms of capturing the diversity and naturalness of real code-switching behavior? The paper does not compare the synthesized corpus to real code-switching data in terms of diversity of switching points, naturalness of the resulting sentences, or coverage of different code-mixing patterns observed in actual speech. This could be resolved by collecting a small dataset of real Hokkien-Mandarin code-switching from speakers and comparing its characteristics to the synthesized corpus, and evaluating the performance of translation models trained on each type of data.

## Limitations
- Corpus size and representativeness may not capture full diversity of real-world Hokkien-Mandarin code-mixing patterns
- Reliance on linguistic theory assumes syntactic similarity between Hokkien and Mandarin without validation against authentic data
- Limited human evaluation sample size (40 sentences per condition) may not capture systematic biases

## Confidence
- **Code-mixed translation performance**: Medium confidence - strong reported results but uncertain about real-world applicability due to synthetic data limitations
- **Dynamic Language Identification mechanism**: Medium confidence - clearly described but impact relative to other factors isn't isolated
- **Transfer learning effectiveness**: Medium confidence - demonstrated improvement but limited comparison with other low-resource approaches

## Next Checks

3. **Cross-linguistic generalization test**: Apply the same methodology to another dialect-Mandarin pair (e.g., Cantonese-Mandarin) to assess whether the approach generalizes beyond Hokkien
