# Are labels informative in semi-supervised learning? -- Estimating and leveraging the missing-data mechanism

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.07540
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07540
- Reference count: 32
- This paper proposes a novel approach to estimate the missing-data mechanism and use inverse propensity weighting to debias any SSL algorithm when labels are informative (MNAR).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of informative labels in semi-supervised learning (SSL), where some classes are more likely to be labeled than others (Missing Not At Random). The authors propose a novel approach to estimate the missing-data mechanism and use inverse propensity weighting to debias any SSL algorithm, including those using data augmentation. They also propose a likelihood ratio test to assess whether labels are indeed informative. The proposed methods are evaluated on various datasets, including two medical datasets with pseudo-realistic missing data scenarios. The results show that their approach improves accuracy, especially for the less observed classes, compared to classical SSL methods.

## Method Summary
The paper tackles informative labels in SSL by estimating the missing-data mechanism and using inverse propensity weighting (IPW) to debias SSL algorithms. Two estimators are proposed: moment estimator (ME) and maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for the missing-data mechanism. The debiased SSL risk estimators use these estimates to correct selection bias in labeled samples. The authors also propose a likelihood ratio test to detect whether labels are informative (MNAR) versus uninformative (MCAR/MAR). The methods are evaluated on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and two medical datasets (dermaMNIST and noduleMNIST3D) with pseudo-realistic MNAR scenarios.

## Key Results
- The proposed approach improves accuracy, especially for less observed classes, compared to classical SSL methods.
- The estimation of the missing-data mechanism is shown to be consistent and asymptotically normal.
- The debiased risk estimator is also consistent.
- The likelihood ratio test effectively distinguishes between MCAR and MNAR label scenarios with high confidence (p-value < 10^-4).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The paper's IPW estimator debiases SSL algorithms by weighting labeled samples inversely to their probability of being observed.
- Mechanism: For MNAR labels, the standard SSL estimator is biased because it treats all labeled samples equally. The IPW estimator corrects this by counting each labeled sample proportionally to its inverse probability of being observed, thus correcting the selection bias.
- Core assumption: The missing-data mechanism is self-masked MNAR (r⊥⊥x|y), meaning label unavailability only depends on the label value itself.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "propose a novel approach to address this issue by estimating the missing-data mechanism and using inverse propensity weighting to debias any SSL algorithm"
  - [section 3.1]: "For self-masked MNAR labels, we propose the following IPW estimator: ˆRφ(θ) := 1/n ∑n i=1 riℓℓ(θ;xi,yi) / φyi"
- Break condition: If the self-masked MNAR assumption is violated (e.g., missingness depends on features), the IPW estimator becomes biased.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The paper's likelihood ratio test can detect whether labels are informative (MNAR) versus uninformative (MCAR/MAR).
- Mechanism: By comparing the likelihood under the null hypothesis of MCAR (all classes equally likely to be labeled) versus the alternative of MNAR, the test statistic follows a chi-squared distribution, allowing statistical rejection of the MCAR assumption.
- Core assumption: The test statistic converges to a chi-squared distribution under the null hypothesis, following standard likelihood ratio test theory.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.4]: "We want to test H0 : φ∈ ΦMCAR against H1 : φ∉ ΦMCAR... We conjecture that... the test statistic (10) also converges in distribution to χ2d"
  - [section 5.2]: "We use MLE together with the test presented in Section 4.4 to assess whether or not labels are informative... the test rejects the MCAR hypothesis with very high confidence (p-value< 10−4 for all 10 runs)"
- Break condition: If the asymptotic distribution assumption fails (e.g., small sample size, model misspecification), the test may have incorrect Type I error rates.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The paper's moment estimator provides a computationally efficient way to estimate the missing-data mechanism without requiring complex optimization.
- Mechanism: The moment estimator leverages the relationship between class proportions in labeled data and the missing-data mechanism, using either known class distributions or estimated ones from the model.
- Core assumption: The class proportions can be either known a priori or consistently estimated from the model parameters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1]: "The moment estimator has the following form, for a fixed θ∈ Θ: ˆφM y = ∑n i=1 1{r=1,yi=y} / (n · ˆp(y,θ)), ∀y∈C"
  - [section 5]: "In terms of estimation of the missing-data mechanism, all methods have comparable and low MSE values in the balanced setting S1."
- Break condition: If class proportions are highly imbalanced and unknown, the moment estimator may be unstable or biased.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: The system consists of three main components: (1) mechanism estimation (MLE, ME, or CADR), (2) debiased SSL algorithm (Algorithm 2), and (3) MCAR/MNAR test (Section 4.4). These components interact sequentially, with mechanism estimation feeding into the debiased algorithm.

- Critical path: The most critical path is: unlabeled data → mechanism estimation → debiased SSL training. If mechanism estimation fails, the entire debiasing approach becomes ineffective.

- Design tradeoffs: The paper balances between computational efficiency (moment estimator) and statistical efficiency (MLE). The moment estimator is faster but may be less accurate, while MLE is more accurate but computationally heavier.

- Failure signatures: If the mechanism estimation is inaccurate, the debiased SSL will still be biased. If the MCAR/MNAR test fails to detect MNAR labels, the system will incorrectly use standard SSL methods.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the theoretical results be extended to cases where the missing-data mechanism is estimated simultaneously with the model parameters (θ), rather than keeping θ fixed as in the current analysis?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper explicitly states that the theoretical results (consistency and asymptotic normality of the missing-data mechanism estimator) are obtained for a fixed θ ∈ Θ. The authors mention that providing a more realistic theoretical grounding without freezing θ is a future work perspective.
- Why unresolved: The current analysis assumes a fixed θ for the sake of theoretical tractability, but in practice, θ and the missing-data mechanism parameters are typically estimated simultaneously. Extending the theory to this more general case would require new mathematical tools and techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: A rigorous mathematical proof showing the consistency and asymptotic normality of the joint estimators of θ and the missing-data mechanism parameters, even when estimated simultaneously.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How robust are the proposed methods to violations of the self-masked MNAR assumption (Assumption A2), and what are the consequences of such violations on the performance of the debiased SSL algorithms?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on the self-masked MNAR case and mentions that Assumption A2 is weaker than the traditional MCAR assumption. However, it does not provide an in-depth analysis of what happens when this assumption is violated.
- Why unresolved: In real-world scenarios, the self-masked MNAR assumption might not always hold perfectly. Understanding the robustness of the methods to violations of this assumption and the potential consequences on their performance is crucial for practical applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies evaluating the performance of the proposed methods on datasets where the self-masked MNAR assumption is intentionally violated in various ways, and theoretical analysis of the impact of such violations on the consistency and asymptotic properties of the estimators.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the proposed likelihood ratio test for assessing whether labels are informative (MCAR vs. MNAR) be extended to handle more general missing-data mechanisms beyond the self-masked MNAR case?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes a likelihood ratio test specifically for testing the MCAR hypothesis against the MNAR hypothesis in the self-masked MNAR setting. The authors mention that extending this test to other missing-data mechanisms is left as a perspective of their work.
- Why unresolved: The current test is tailored to the self-masked MNAR mechanism, but in practice, other types of informative missingness might be present. Extending the test to handle more general missing-data mechanisms would make it more widely applicable.
- What evidence would resolve it: A mathematical derivation of a likelihood ratio test statistic for testing the MCAR hypothesis against more general forms of informative missingness, along with theoretical results on its asymptotic distribution and power properties.

## Limitations

- The theoretical guarantees for the likelihood ratio test are presented as a conjecture rather than a proven result, with the asymptotic distribution convergence requiring further verification.
- The moment estimator's performance in highly imbalanced settings with unknown class distributions is not thoroughly explored, as the paper primarily evaluates it in balanced scenarios.
- The adaptive pseudo-label threshold lacks rigorous theoretical justification for its effectiveness across different datasets and SSL algorithms.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The mechanism of IPW debiasing for self-masked MNAR labels - supported by established statistical theory and empirical validation across multiple datasets.
- **Medium confidence**: The likelihood ratio test's ability to detect MNAR labels - theoretically sound but with unproven asymptotic distribution claims that could affect Type I error rates.
- **Medium confidence**: The moment estimator's computational efficiency and accuracy - demonstrated empirically but limited theoretical guarantees, particularly for extreme class imbalance scenarios.

## Next Checks

2. **Evaluate extreme imbalance**: Test the moment estimator on datasets with highly skewed class distributions (e.g., 1:100 ratio) where class proportions are unknown, measuring both estimation accuracy and debiasing effectiveness.

3. **Cross-algorithm robustness**: Apply the debiasing framework to SSL algorithms beyond the pseudo-labeling approach (e.g., FixMatch, MixMatch) to assess whether the method generalizes across different SSL paradigms and data augmentation strategies.
