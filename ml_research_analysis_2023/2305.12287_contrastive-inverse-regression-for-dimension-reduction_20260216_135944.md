# Contrastive inverse regression for dimension reduction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12287
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12287
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CIR outperforms competing methods in supervised dimension reduction, particularly in low-dimensional settings (d=2,3) with up to 0.14 higher classification accuracy and 12% lower prediction MSE

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Contrastive Inverse Regression (CIR), a novel supervised dimension reduction method designed for datasets with foreground (case) and background (control) groups. CIR extends sliced inverse regression (SIR) by incorporating a contrastive loss function that balances the effectiveness of dimension reduction between the two groups. The method optimizes a non-standard loss function on the Stiefel manifold, with convergence guaranteed by gradient descent-based algorithms. Experimental results on three biomedical datasets demonstrate that CIR outperforms competing methods, especially in low-dimensional settings, with improvements in both classification accuracy and prediction mean squared error.

## Method Summary
CIR introduces a contrastive loss function that subtracts background group inverse regression distance from foreground group inverse regression distance, weighted by hyperparameter α. The method optimizes this loss on the Stiefel manifold V⊤V=Id using gradient descent-based algorithms (SGPM or ALS). The approach generalizes SIR (when α=0) and provides theoretical guarantees of convergence to KKT conditions. The key innovation is balancing foreground and background group dimension reduction effectiveness through the contrastive loss, which aims to find a low-dimensional subspace unique to the foreground group while preserving functional relationships with response variables.

## Key Results
- On mouse protein dataset, CIR achieves 0.14 higher classification accuracy than SIR at d=2
- For single-cell RNA sequencing, CIR shows 12% lower prediction MSE than competing methods at low dimensions
- CIR consistently outperforms PCA, CPCA, LDA, LASSO, and SIR across all three biomedical datasets, particularly at d=2,3

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: CIR balances foreground and background group dimension reduction effectiveness via contrastive loss
- Mechanism: Introduces a non-standard loss function that subtracts background group inverse regression distance from foreground group inverse regression distance, weighted by hyperparameter α
- Core assumption: The background group provides meaningful "noise" that should be subtracted from the foreground structure
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "CIR introduces an optimization problem defined on the Stiefel manifold with a non-standard loss function"
  - [section 2] "Our goal is to find a low-dimensional subspace represented by V such that y =ϕ(V⊤x,ϵ ) while ˜y̸=ϕ(V⊤x,˜ϵ)"
- Break condition: If background data does not represent true "noise" or is uninformative, contrastive subtraction may harm performance

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: CIR generalizes SIR by reducing to it when α=0
- Mechanism: The contrastive loss becomes purely supervised when α=0, recovering SIR's standard inverse regression objective
- Core assumption: SIR is a special case of CIR with no background subtraction
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2] "In the case where α = 0, CIR reduces to SIR"
  - [table 1] "CIR combines both contrastive and supervised features"
- Break condition: If α=0 is chosen but background data is informative, performance will be worse than full CIR

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: CIR optimization converges to local optimum on Stiefel manifold
- Mechanism: Uses gradient descent-based algorithms (SGPM or ALS) on the constrained optimization problem
- Core assumption: The Stiefel manifold constraint V⊤V=Id ensures valid dimension reduction projection
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3] "We provide theoretical guarantee of the convergence of the CIR algorithm"
  - [theorem 2] "any accumulation point V∗ of{Vk}∞ k=1 satisfies the KKT conditions"
- Break condition: If the loss landscape is pathological or α is poorly chosen, convergence may be slow or to suboptimal local optima

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> SIR/CIR loss computation -> Manifold optimization (SGPM/ALS) -> V* output -> Downstream prediction/classification
- Critical path: Compute inverse regression curves -> Construct Σx, Σ˜x estimates -> Form A,B,˜A,˜B matrices -> Optimize on Stiefel manifold -> Return V*
- Design tradeoffs: α=0 gives SIR (faster, simpler) vs α>0 gives CIR (potentially better but slower); SGPM converges faster but to KKT points vs ALS converges to critical points with known rate
- Failure signatures: Poor performance when background data is not truly "noise"; slow convergence when α is poorly tuned; non-identifiability of V beyond its column space
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run CIR with α=0 on synthetic data to verify it reproduces SIR results
  2. Compare CIR vs SIR on case-control data with known structure to verify contrastive benefit
  3. Test sensitivity to α values on validation data to find optimal contrast weighting

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal choice of α in CIR for balancing contrastive effectiveness and preservation of functional relationships?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that α appears naturally in contrastive methods and mentions the trade-off between expressive contrastiveness and emphasis on effective lower-dimensional structures, but does not provide a principled method for choosing α.
- Why unresolved: The paper only mentions that α can be determined by cross-validation and shows empirical robustness to α choices, but doesn't establish theoretical guidance or optimal selection criteria.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis showing the relationship between α and consistency of estimates, or empirical studies across diverse datasets demonstrating optimal α ranges for different problem types.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can CIR be extended to multi-treatment scenarios with more than two groups?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion section explicitly mentions that extending to multi-treatment settings is a natural direction, noting that the number of treatment groups puts constraints on target dimension.
- Why unresolved: The paper only raises this as a future direction without proposing specific solutions or discussing the challenges in designing loss functions for multiple groups.
- What evidence would resolve it: Proposed modifications to the loss function for multi-group settings, or empirical results demonstrating effective multi-group CIR variants on datasets with multiple treatment groups.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can high-moment dimension reduction methods like SAVE be effectively generalized to the contrastive setting?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion mentions that SIR has limitations when symmetry assumptions are lost and suggests SAVE as a potential generalization to the contrastive setting.
- Why unresolved: The paper only poses this as a question without exploring potential approaches or demonstrating whether such a generalization would be feasible or beneficial.
- What evidence would resolve it: A proposed contrastive SAVE algorithm with theoretical justification, or empirical comparisons showing improvements over CIR on datasets where SIR's symmetry assumptions are violated.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees only ensure convergence to KKT conditions rather than global optimality
- Performance depends heavily on the assumption that background data represents true "noise"
- The optimization landscape on the Stiefel manifold could have multiple local optima
- Limited exploration of the sensitivity to hyperparameter α and its optimal selection

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: CIR generalizes SIR (α=0 case), convergence guarantees to KKT conditions, and basic mathematical framework
- **Medium Confidence**: Performance improvements over competing methods, particularly for low-dimensional projections (d=2,3)
- **Medium Confidence**: The contrastive mechanism's effectiveness in balancing foreground and background dimension reduction
