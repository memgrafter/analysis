# Counteracts: Testing Stereotypical Representation in Pre-trained Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.04347
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04347
- Reference count: 1
- Primary result: PLMs mitigate gender bias when given counter-knowledge with similar syntax, with ALBERT and RoBERTa showing the strongest effects

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method to test how pre-trained language models process and mitigate internal gender stereotypes using counterexamples. The approach leverages cloze-style prompts combining base prompts with different types of knowledge—pro-stereotypical, anti-stereotypical, and unrelated—extracted from WinoBias and labor force statistics. Nine prompt types are evaluated across seven PLMs, including BERT, ALBERT, RoBERTa, and GPT-2 variants. Results show that PLMs are generally robust to unrelated information and tend to rely on shallow linguistic cues like word position and syntactic structure rather than semantic meaning to adjust stereotypical representations. Some models, notably ALBERT and RoBERTa, effectively mitigate bias when given counter-knowledge with similar syntax, while others like GPT-2 show little benefit from neutral prompts.

## Method Summary
The study constructs prompts by combining base templates from WinoBias with knowledge sentences derived from labor force statistics. Nine knowledge types are tested: pro-stereotypical, anti-stereotypical, unrelated, syntactic similar, semantic similar, syntactic dissimilar, semantic dissimilar, and combined syntactic-semantic. These are applied to seven PLMs (BERT-base/large, ALBERT-base, RoBERTa-base/large, GPT-2-medium/large) using appropriate input formatting for each model type. Predictions are converted to binary gender values using a verbalizer mapping 126 tokens to "female" or "male" categories based on job prevalence. The evaluation measures relative probability changes between knowledge-inserted and base prompts across top-k tokens.

## Key Results
- PLMs show robustness to unrelated knowledge, with no change in stereotypical preference
- ALBERT and RoBERTa effectively mitigate bias using syntactic similar counter-knowledge
- GPT-2 variants show little benefit from neutral knowledge prompts
- Syntactic structure and word positioning are key features for neutral interaction with PLMs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Syntactic structure similarity between counter-knowledge and base prompt helps mitigate bias in certain PLMs
- Mechanism: PLMs like ALBERT and RoBERTa process the syntactic cues from counter-knowledge prompts and adjust internal representations to reduce stereotypical associations
- Core assumption: Models retain syntactic information as part of internal representation and can match it against incoming prompt structure
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "syntactic structure and word positioning are key features for neutral interaction with PLMs"
  - [section] "ALBERT and RoBERTa variants tend to use syntactic structure and word position to process the extra knowledge"
- Break condition: If counter-knowledge has syntactic similarity but semantically contradictory meaning, some models (e.g., GPT-2) fail to adjust bias

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Unrelated knowledge does not alter stereotypical predictions, indicating PLM robustness to irrelevant information
- Mechanism: PLMs distinguish irrelevant content from task-relevant context by shallow linguistic cues, ignoring unrelated sentences
- Core assumption: Models can separate irrelevant syntactic/semantic content from core prompt without altering bias
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "PLMs show a certain amount of robustness when using unrelated knowledge"
  - [section] "adding unrelated knowledge to the base prompts does not change the stereotypical preference"
- Break condition: If unrelated knowledge is structurally similar to the base prompt, models may misinterpret it and alter predictions

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Autoregressive models (GPT-2) are less sensitive to neutral knowledge prompts than BERT-based models
- Mechanism: GPT-2's unidirectional context modeling limits its ability to integrate neutral or counter-knowledge into the generation process
- Core assumption: Bidirectional context access allows more flexible integration of new knowledge
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "autoregressive language models, such as GPT2, do not benefit from introducing neutral knowledge"
  - [section] "GPT2 variants have conflicting results, leading to further experiments on other linguistic features"
- Break condition: If GPT-2 is fine-tuned with sufficient neutral/counter examples, it may improve mitigation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Do pre-trained language models rely more on syntactic structure or semantic meaning when processing counter-stereotypical knowledge to mitigate bias?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that models like ALBERT and RoBERTa benefit from syntactic similar information and are prone to use syntactic structure and word position to process extra knowledge
- Why unresolved: The paper notes that BERT variants and GPT2 variants do not show a clear preference for either linguistic feature, indicating variability in how different models process counter-stereotypical knowledge
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing the impact of syntactic vs. semantic knowledge across multiple PLMs with controlled prompts could clarify which feature is more influential in mitigating bias

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can unrelated knowledge effectively test the robustness of pre-trained language models against distractive information in mitigating gender stereotypes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tests unrelated knowledge to evaluate model robustness and finds that unrelated information does not change the stereotypical preference, suggesting some robustness
- Why unresolved: While unrelated knowledge shows robustness, it is unclear if this robustness generalizes to other types of bias or if unrelated information could still subtly influence model predictions in ways not captured by the study
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing unrelated knowledge across various bias types and analyzing subtle influences on model predictions would clarify the robustness of PLMs to distractive information

### Open Question 3
- Question: How effective are syntactic counter-knowledge prompts compared to semantic counter-knowledge prompts in mitigating stereotypical representations in pre-trained language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that ALBERT and RoBERTa models are more sensitive to syntactic similar information and tend to use syntactic structure and word position to mitigate stereotypes, while BERT and GPT2 models show conflicting results
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison of the effectiveness of syntactic vs. semantic counter-knowledge prompts across all tested models, leaving the relative effectiveness unclear
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed comparative analysis of syntactic and semantic counter-knowledge prompts across multiple PLMs would reveal which type is more effective in mitigating stereotypes

## Limitations
- Verbalizer construction is unspecified - the 126-token mapping to binary gender values is not detailed
- The study does not explain why ALBERT and RoBERTa specifically respond to syntactic features while other models do not
- Results cannot distinguish between genuine bias mitigation and model confusion from contradictory information

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Unrelated knowledge does not alter stereotypical predictions across all models
- **Medium confidence**: Syntactic structure similarity enables bias mitigation in ALBERT and RoBERTa
- **Low confidence**: Bidirectional models are inherently better at integrating counter-knowledge than autoregressive models
