# Large Language Models Are State-of-the-Art Evaluators of Translation Quality

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.14520
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14520
- Reference count: 22
- State-of-the-art system-level pairwise accuracy of 88% on WMT22 Metrics shared task data

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces GEMBA, a GPT-based metric for automated translation quality assessment that achieves state-of-the-art performance using zero-shot prompting. The method evaluates translations by querying GPT models with carefully designed prompts that specify explicit scoring scales and formatting requirements. GEMBA outperforms all existing reference-based metrics on system-level pairwise accuracy (88%) across three language pairs, and sets a new state-of-the-art for reference-less quality estimation (86.1%). The approach only works with GPT-3.5 and larger models, as smaller models produce meaningless outputs.

## Method Summary
The authors propose GEMBA, a zero-shot translation quality assessment metric that queries GPT models using four prompt variants (DA, SQM, Stars, Classes) in both reference-based and reference-less modes. The method processes each translation segment by generating a prompt with source text, candidate translation, and optionally a reference translation, then validates and parses the model's numerical response. System-level scores are computed by averaging segment scores, while segment-level correlation is measured using Kendall's Tau against human judgments.

## Key Results
- GEMBA-DA with GPT-3.5 Davinci-003 achieves 88% system-level pairwise accuracy on WMT22 data
- Reference-less GEMBA variant achieves 86.1% accuracy, setting state-of-the-art for quality estimation without references
- Only GPT-3.5 and larger models work effectively; GPT-2 and Ada produce useless results
- Segment-level correlation lower (0.171-0.259) due to discrete scoring and frequent ties

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
GPT-3.5 and larger models leverage multilingual pretraining to understand translation quality assessment prompts. The models internalize quality assessment patterns from their pretraining corpus, enabling them to judge translation quality relative to reference or source. This only works with GPT-3.5 and larger models - Babbage and Curie are useless for translation quality assessment.

### Mechanism 2
Prompt templates constrain models to output discrete quality scores in expected ranges by specifying explicit scoring scales and formatting requirements. This guides the model to produce consistent numerical outputs rather than free-form text, making the metric usable and parseable.

### Mechanism 3
System-level aggregation masks segment-level noise and discrete scoring limitations by averaging scores across many segments. Individual segment scores contain signal about overall system quality that emerges when aggregated, explaining why system-level performance excels despite lower segment-level correlation.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Zero-shot prompting in large language models
  - Why needed here: The approach relies on prompting GPT models without fine-tuning to perform translation quality assessment
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes zero-shot from few-shot prompting, and why might zero-shot be sufficient for this task?

- Concept: Kendall's Tau correlation for evaluation metrics
  - Why needed here: The paper uses Kendall's Tau to measure segment-level correlation between metric scores and human judgments
  - Quick check question: How does Kendall's Tau handle ties differently from other correlation measures, and why is this important for discrete scoring systems?

- Concept: System-level vs segment-level evaluation in MT
  - Why needed here: The metric performs differently at system vs segment levels, with system-level being the primary focus
  - Quick check question: Why might a metric that performs poorly at segment level still be valuable for system-level evaluation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Prompt templates (4 variants × 2 modes) -> GPT API integration layer -> Score validation and parsing -> System-level aggregation -> Result storage and comparison

- Critical path:
  1. Generate prompt with source, translation, and optional reference
  2. Query GPT model via API
  3. Validate and parse response
  4. Aggregate segment scores to system scores
  5. Compare system rankings to human judgments

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Zero-shot vs few-shot prompting: Zero-shot is simpler but potentially less accurate
  - Segment-level scoring vs system-level only: Segment-level provides granularity but introduces noise
  - Discrete vs continuous scoring: Discrete is easier to parse but reduces precision

- Failure signatures:
  - Invalid responses from GPT (text instead of numbers)
  - High tie frequency in segment-level scores
  - GPT-2/Ada models producing meaningless outputs
  - Temperature adjustment loops indicating repeated invalid responses

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test all prompt variants with Davinci-003 on a small sample to verify scoring ranges and parseability
  2. Compare system-level rankings between prompt variants to identify best performer
  3. Evaluate segment-level correlation for top-performing prompt to assess noise levels

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: What is the exact impact of fine-tuning GPT models on translation quality assessment accuracy?
  - Basis in paper: The authors mention future research on model fine-tuning to increase GEMBA accuracy.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only explores zero-shot prompting, leaving the potential benefits of fine-tuning unexplored.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing zero-shot and fine-tuned GPT models on the same test sets.

- Question: How does GEMBA's performance degrade for low-resource languages compared to high-resource ones?
  - Basis in paper: The authors note that GEMBA's state-of-the-art performance only holds for three high-resource language pairs.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only tests on English-German, English-Russian, and Chinese-English.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Testing GEMBA on low-resource language pairs with human evaluation.

- Question: What is the optimal prompt template design to minimize discrete scoring and improve segment-level correlation?
  - Basis in paper: The authors identify discrete scoring and frequent ties as causes for lower segment-level performance.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only tests four prompt variants without exploring alternatives to continuous scoring.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments with prompt variants designed to encourage continuous output.

## Limitations

- Method only works with GPT-3.5 and larger models; smaller models produce useless results
- Discrete scoring creates frequent ties that reduce segment-level correlation with human judgments
- Performance only validated on three high-resource language pairs, limiting generalizability

## Confidence

The findings show **High confidence** for system-level pairwise accuracy results, as the paper demonstrates consistent state-of-the-art performance across three language pairs. However, confidence is **Medium** for segment-level correlation claims due to the plausible but unproven explanation for reduced correlation. **Low confidence** exists regarding generalizability beyond the three tested language pairs.

## Next Checks

1. Test GEMBA on language pairs with different typological profiles (e.g., English-Japanese, English-Arabic) to assess generalizability beyond Indo-European languages.

2. Implement a continuous scoring variant and compare segment-level correlation against the discrete version to determine if granularity limitations explain the performance gap.

3. Compare zero-shot performance against few-shot variants using 1-3 example translations to establish whether minimal prompting could improve segment-level correlation without sacrificing system-level accuracy.
