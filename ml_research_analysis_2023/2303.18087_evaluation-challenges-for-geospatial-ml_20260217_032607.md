# Evaluation Challenges for Geospatial ML

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.18087
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18087
- Reference count: 7
- Key outcome: Geospatial ML evaluation is complicated by spatial autocorrelation and covariate shifts that invalidate standard ML procedures

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the fundamental challenges of evaluating geospatial machine learning models when dealing with global or remotely sensed datasets. The author demonstrates that geospatial data exhibit unique structures—particularly spatial autocorrelation and covariate shifts—that invalidate standard machine learning evaluation procedures like k-fold cross-validation. While spatially-aware evaluation methods can better simulate real-world prediction conditions, they generally fail to provide valid statistical estimates of prediction map accuracy. The paper argues for three key investments: improving evaluation data availability and quality, developing comprehensive evaluation frameworks, and clearly explaining the limitations of what types of performance can be evaluated in geospatial contexts.

## Method Summary
The paper analyzes the theoretical foundations of geospatial ML evaluation challenges, focusing on how spatial autocorrelation and covariate shifts affect traditional validation methods. It examines spatially-aware evaluation techniques such as buffered cross-validation and spatial blocks that attempt to control for spatial dependencies between training and validation sets. The analysis considers both design-based inference approaches using probability samples and model-based approaches that account for spatial structure. The work synthesizes existing geospatial ML literature to identify critical gaps in current evaluation practices and proposes a framework for addressing these challenges through data investment, methodological development, and transparent reporting of limitations.

## Key Results
- Standard k-fold cross-validation inflates performance estimates in geospatial ML due to spatial autocorrelation between training and validation points
- Spatially-aware validation methods improve simulation of real-world conditions but sacrifice statistical validity for accuracy estimation
- Limited and biased evaluation data availability amplifies geospatial model evaluation challenges by creating coverage gaps

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Spatial autocorrelation in geospatial data invalidates standard k-fold cross-validation by inflating performance estimates
- Mechanism: When data points are spatially correlated, training and validation sets drawn from the same region will have similar values, causing the model to appear more accurate than it would be on spatially independent data
- Core assumption: The spatial autocorrelation function RZZ(ℓi,ℓj) > 0 for nearby points ℓi and ℓj
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Geospatial data exhibit unique structures, such as spatial autocorrelation and covariate shifts, which complicate the use of traditional ML evaluation procedures"
  - [section] "Models can 'over-rely' on spatial correlations in the data, leading to over-estimated accuracy despite poor spatial generalization performance"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus papers discuss geospatial challenges but don't directly address spatial autocorrelation's impact on cross-validation
- Break condition: If the evaluation region is small enough that all points are effectively spatially independent, or if the prediction variable has no spatial autocorrelation

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Spatially-aware validation methods can better simulate real-world prediction conditions but don't provide valid statistical estimates of map accuracy
- Mechanism: By controlling the spatial distribution of training and validation points (e.g., through buffered cross-validation or spatial blocks), these methods reduce spatial dependencies between sets, better reflecting how models will be used across different regions
- Core assumption: The intended use of the model involves predicting in regions different from the training data
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "spatially-aware evaluation methods can control the spatial distribution of training and validation set points, they generally do not provide valid statistical estimates of prediction map accuracy"
  - [section] "Spatial cross-validation methods typically stratify training and evaluation instances by larger geographies... control the minimum distance from any training point to any evaluation point"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus focuses on geospatial challenges but doesn't specifically address the tradeoff between spatial awareness and statistical validity
- Break condition: If the model is only intended for use in the exact regions where training data exists, or if a probability sample is available for design-based estimation

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Limited and biased evaluation data availability amplifies geospatial model evaluation challenges
- Mechanism: When evaluation data are clustered in small regions, it becomes difficult to assess model performance across the full range of conditions where it might be used, leading to overgeneralized conclusions
- Core assumption: Geospatial datasets commonly exhibit gaps in coverage or quality of data
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "When training data are clustered in small regions, this can affect our ability to train a high-performing model. When evaluation data are clustered in small regions, this affects our ability to evaluate geospatial ML model performance at all."
  - [abstract] "Geospatial data exhibit unique structures, such as... covariate shifts, which complicate the use of traditional ML evaluation procedures"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus papers mention data challenges but don't specifically address clustering effects on evaluation
- Break condition: If comprehensive, probability-sampled evaluation data becomes available across all intended use regions

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Spatial autocorrelation
  - Why needed here: Understanding how values at nearby locations tend to be similar is fundamental to grasping why standard evaluation methods fail for geospatial data
  - Quick check question: What is the expected sign of the spatial autocorrelation function RZZ(ℓi,ℓj) when ℓi and ℓj are close together?
  - Answer: Positive (RZZ(ℓi,ℓj) > 0)

- Concept: Covariate shift
  - Why needed here: Different regions often have different relationships between predictors and labels, making it crucial to evaluate models on data from intended use regions
  - Quick check question: What type of distribution shift occurs when the relationship between predictors and labels changes across spatial regions?
  - Answer: Covariate shift

- Concept: Design-based vs. model-based inference
  - Why needed here: Determines whether a probability sample can provide valid estimates of map accuracy regardless of model or training data distribution
  - Quick check question: What type of sample is required for design-based inference to estimate map accuracy?
  - Answer: Design-independent probability sample

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data preprocessing: spatial stratification, buffering, and distance calculations
  - Evaluation framework: multiple validation methods (standard, spatial, buffered, domain adaptation)
  - Accuracy estimation: design-based inference calculations
  - Reporting: uncertainty quantification and visualization components

- Critical path:
  1. Load geospatial dataset with spatial coordinates
  2. Calculate spatial autocorrelation metrics
  3. Generate spatially-aware train/validation splits
  4. Train and evaluate model using multiple validation strategies
  5. Calculate accuracy estimates with appropriate uncertainty
  6. Generate spatial visualizations of predictions and residuals

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Spatial vs. statistical validity: spatially-aware methods better simulate real use but don't provide valid accuracy estimates
  - Computational cost: buffered methods require distance calculations, increasing runtime
  - Data requirements: spatially-aware methods need sufficient spatial coverage to create meaningful splits

- Failure signatures:
  - Performance estimates that don't change between standard and spatial validation
  - Very low variance in performance across spatial folds (suggesting insufficient spatial separation)
  - High correlation between training and validation set predictions in spatial validation

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare standard k-fold vs. buffered k-fold validation on a geospatial dataset with known spatial autocorrelation
  2. Evaluate a model using both design-based inference (if probability sample available) and spatially-aware cross-validation
  3. Test spatial generalization by training on one region and evaluating on another with covariate shift

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How can we develop more effective spatially-aware evaluation methods that provide valid statistical estimates of prediction map accuracy while also assessing a model's performance in conditions it is most likely to be used?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the discrepancy between estimating map accuracy as a population parameter and assessing a model's performance in the conditions it is most likely to be used, and argues that spatially-aware validation methods generally do not provide valid statistical estimates of prediction map accuracy.
- Why unresolved: The paper highlights the need for both accurate map accuracy estimation and assessment of model performance in real-world conditions, but does not provide a clear solution for reconciling these two goals.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of new evaluation methods that can simultaneously provide valid statistical estimates of map accuracy and assess model performance in real-world conditions.

## Limitations

- The paper's theoretical analysis relies heavily on assumptions about spatial autocorrelation structures that may not hold for all geospatial datasets
- Without access to comprehensive probability-sampled evaluation data, the proposed spatially-aware methods cannot be fully validated against ground truth accuracy estimates
- The tradeoff between spatial validity and statistical validity in evaluation methods remains unresolved, with no clear guidance on when each approach is appropriate

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Claims about the existence of spatial autocorrelation and covariate shifts in geospatial data, and their general impact on standard ML evaluation procedures
- **Medium Confidence**: The proposed spatially-aware evaluation methods and their theoretical advantages, though practical effectiveness depends heavily on data availability
- **Low Confidence**: The extent to which current evaluation practices can be improved without substantial investment in new evaluation data infrastructure
