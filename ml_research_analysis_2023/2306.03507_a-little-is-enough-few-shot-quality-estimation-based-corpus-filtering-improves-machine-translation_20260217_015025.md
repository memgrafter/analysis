# "A Little is Enough": Few-Shot Quality Estimation based Corpus Filtering improves Machine Translation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03507
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03507
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 8
- Key outcome: QE-based filtering improves MT BLEU scores by up to 1.8 points; 500-example Hindi-Bengali QE model achieves 0.6 BLEU point improvement

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that Quality Estimation (QE) models can effectively filter noisy pseudo-parallel corpora to improve machine translation quality, particularly for low-resource language pairs. The key innovation is using few-shot transfer learning where a high-resource QE model (English-Marathi) is fine-tuned on just 500 examples to filter a Hindi-Bengali corpus. Experiments across English-Marathi, Chinese-English, and Hindi-Bengali show BLEU score improvements of up to 1.8 points compared to baseline models trained on unfiltered data, with the few-shot approach achieving 0.6 BLEU point gains.

## Method Summary
The method trains MonoTransQuest QE models on language pairs to score translation quality, then filters pseudo-parallel corpora based on these scores before training MT models. For Hindi-Bengali, a few-shot approach transfers knowledge from an English-Marathi QE model fine-tuned on only 500 examples. The filtered corpora are then used to train Transformer-based MT models, with performance measured by BLEU score improvements over baselines trained on unfiltered data.

## Key Results
- QE-based filtering improves MT BLEU scores by up to 1.8 points across three language pairs
- Few-shot QE model (500 examples) achieves 0.6 BLEU point improvement for Hindi-Bengali
- QE quality scores show higher correlation with human judgments than LaBSE scores for all three language pairs
- Filtering removes low-quality sentence pairs while retaining useful training data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: QE scores better align with human judgments of translation quality than LaBSE cosine similarity scores
- Mechanism: QE models are trained on human-annotated Direct Assessment scores, learning to predict quality judgments directly, while LaBSE scores only measure semantic similarity
- Core assumption: Human Direct Assessment scores are a reliable ground truth for translation quality
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "QE systems typically require in the order of (7K-25K) of training data. Our Hindi-Bengali QE is trained on only 500 instances of training that is 1/40th of the normal requirement and achieves comparable performance."
  - [section 6] "Table 6 shows that the QE quality scores have a higher correlation with human annotated quality scores, compared to LaBSE quality scores for all 3 language pairs."
  - [corpus] The paper creates a small corpus (500 instances) with human-annotated Domain Adaptation scores for Hindi-Bengali, but doesn't provide correlation statistics for other language pairs.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Few-shot transfer learning from high-resource to low-resource languages enables effective QE-based filtering with minimal training data
- Mechanism: Pre-training a QE model on a high-resource language pair (En-Mr) provides a strong initialization, allowing effective fine-tuning on a small low-resource dataset (Hi-Bn)
- Core assumption: High-resource and low-resource language pairs share similar quality estimation characteristics
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our Few-shot QE model transfer learned from the English-Marathi QE model and fine-tuned on only 500 Hindi-Bengali training instances, shows an improvement of up to 0.6 BLEU points for Hindi-Bengali language pair, compared to the baseline model."
  - [section 3.4] Describes the few-shot transfer learning approach explicitly.
  - [corpus] The paper uses only 500 Hi-Bn examples, which is very small, but doesn't compare to other transfer learning approaches.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Filtering pseudo-parallel corpora using QE scores improves MT quality by removing low-quality sentence pairs
- Mechanism: QE models assign quality scores to sentence pairs; filtering out low-scoring pairs removes noise from the training data, leading to better MT models
- Core assumption: Removing low-quality sentence pairs improves the overall quality of the training corpus
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "By training with this filtered corpus, we observe an improvement in the Machine Translation (MT) system's performance by up to 1.8 BLEU points, for English-Marathi, Hindi-Bengali, and Chinese-English language pairs, over the baseline model."
  - [section 5.3] Details the QE-based filtering approach and threshold values used.
  - [corpus] The paper shows BLEU improvements but doesn't analyze what types of errors were removed by filtering.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pseudo-parallel corpus -> QE scoring -> Threshold filtering -> Filtered corpus -> MT training
- Critical path: 1) Train QE model on high-resource language pair (En-Mr) 2) Fine-tune QE model on low-resource language pair (Hi-Bn) using few-shot learning 3) Score pseudo-parallel corpus using fine-tuned QE model 4) Filter sentence pairs based on QE score threshold 5) Train MT model on filtered corpus augmented with parallel corpus 6) Evaluate MT model using BLEU score
- Design tradeoffs: QE vs. LaBSE (accuracy vs. training data requirements), Few-shot vs. full training (minimal data vs. potential accuracy), Threshold selection (noise removal vs. data retention)
- Failure signatures: MT performance doesn't improve after filtering, QE model doesn't converge with few-shot learning, BLEU scores decrease
- First 3 experiments: 1) Train QE model on En-Mr and evaluate correlation with human judgments 2) Fine-tune QE model on Hi-Bn using 500 examples and evaluate few-shot learning effectiveness 3) Compare MT performance using QE-filtered vs. LaBSE-filtered vs. unfiltered corpora

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the QE-based filtering performance compare to other filtering methods (like LaBSE) when the pseudo-parallel corpus contains different types of noise (as defined by Khayrallah and Koehn, 2018)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that Herold et al. (2022) investigated whether state-of-the-art filtering models can remove various noise types, but doesn't compare QE-based filtering to other methods on these noise types
- Why unresolved: The paper only compares QE-based filtering to LaBSE and LaBSE+PPI-LaBSE filtering, without considering specific noise types
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing QE-based filtering to other methods on pseudo-parallel corpora containing different noise types and measuring BLEU score improvements

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of the threshold quality score value on the performance of QE-based filtering for different language pairs and different sizes of pseudo-parallel corpora?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the threshold quality score is a hyperparameter and that different values are used for different language pairs (-0.5, -0.4, and 0 for En-Mr, Zh-En, and Hi-Bn respectively), but doesn't explore the impact of varying this threshold
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide an analysis of how the threshold value affects the filtering performance or discuss an optimal strategy for choosing the threshold
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying the threshold quality score for different language pairs and corpus sizes, and measuring the resulting BLEU scores and the amount of data retained after filtering

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the few-shot QE technique perform when fine-tuning on a pre-trained QE model for a language pair that is more distantly related to the target language pair (e.g., fine-tuning an English-French QE model on Hindi-Bengali data)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates few-shot QE using a pre-trained English-Marathi QE model for Hindi-Bengali, but doesn't explore the impact of using a pre-trained model for a less related language pair
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores one scenario of few-shot QE, limiting the generalizability of the findings
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments fine-tuning pre-trained QE models for various language pairs on data for distantly related language pairs, and measuring the resulting BLEU scores and the amount of training data required for comparable performance

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies on a single human-annotated dataset (Hindi-Bengali with 500 examples), limiting generalization across language pairs
- Paper doesn't report variance or statistical significance of BLEU improvements, nor analyze types of errors removed by filtering
- Approach assumes LaBSE scores serve as reasonable baseline without comparing against other filtering methods
- Correlation advantage claim for QE vs LaBSE only substantiated for Hindi-Bengali

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The empirical demonstration that QE-based filtering improves MT BLEU scores (1.8 points for En-Mr and Zh-En, 0.6 points for Hi-Bn) using the described methodology is well-supported by the experimental results presented
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that QE scores better correlate with human judgments than LaBSE scores is supported for Hindi-Bengali but lacks comprehensive correlation analysis for other language pairs
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of few-shot transfer learning for Hindi-Bengali QE is demonstrated, but the generalizability to other low-resource language pairs remains uncertain without broader experimentation
