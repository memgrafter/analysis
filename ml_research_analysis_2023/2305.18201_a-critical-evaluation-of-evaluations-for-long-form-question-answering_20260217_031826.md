# A Critical Evaluation of Evaluations for Long-form Question Answering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18201
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18201
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 23
- Key outcome: This paper presents the first targeted study of the evaluation of long-form question answering (LFQA). It addresses the challenges posed by the flexibility of LFQA in evaluation by conducting a careful analysis of human evaluations, focusing on new aspects such as the comprehensiveness of the answer. The study also examines automatic text generation metrics and finds that none of the existing metrics are predictive of human preference judgments. However, some metrics correlate with fine-grained aspects of answers, such as coherence. The paper encourages future work to move away from a single "overall score" of the answer and adopt a multi-faceted evaluation, targeting aspects such as factuality and completeness. The authors publicly release all of their annotations and code to spur future work into LFQA evaluation.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive study of long-form question answering (LFQA) evaluation, examining both human and automatic evaluation methods. The authors conduct the first targeted study comparing expert annotators with crowdworkers, finding that experts are better judges of factuality and completeness in domain-specific answers. They systematically evaluate a wide range of automatic metrics and find that none reliably predict human preference judgments, though some correlate with specific aspects like coherence. The study advocates for multi-faceted evaluation approaches that target specific answer aspects rather than computing a single overall score.

## Method Summary
The paper employs three main evaluation approaches: expert annotation using domain experts in seven areas who provide preference judgments and free-form justifications for answer pairs; automatic metric evaluation using existing metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore, BLEURT, Self-BLEU, GPT-2 perplexity, QAFactEval) and learned metrics (Longformer and GPT-3 fine-tuned on human preference data); and justification comprehension testing where models predict masked references in human justifications. The human preference data comes from three sources: expert annotations (260 ratings), HURDLES (Krishna et al., 2021), and WEBGPT (Nakano et al., 2021).

## Key Results
- Expert annotators produce more reliable evaluations than crowdworkers, focusing on factuality and completeness rather than surface features
- No existing automatic metrics are predictive of human preference judgments, with all metrics showing accuracies below estimated human agreement
- Some metrics correlate with specific fine-grained aspects of answers, particularly coherence
- Larger language models can comprehend human justifications to some extent, suggesting potential for automatic evaluation
- Computing a single "overall score" is not meaningful; multi-faceted evaluation targeting specific aspects is recommended

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Expert annotators produce more reliable evaluations of long-form answers than crowdworkers because they can assess domain-specific factuality and completeness.
- Mechanism: Experts leverage deep domain knowledge to detect subtle inaccuracies and evaluate whether all aspects of a question are addressed, while crowdworkers rely on surface-level features like conciseness and relevance to evidence documents.
- Core assumption: Domain expertise correlates with ability to judge complex factual accuracy and completeness in long-form answers.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "hire domain experts in seven areas to provide preference judgments over pairs of answers, along with free-form justifications for their choices"
  - [section] "Experts are better judges of factuality: ... our experts mention factuality in their justifications almost twice as frequently as crowdworkers (36 to 20), and it is the most common aspect referenced by experts"
  - [corpus] "Average neighbor FMR=0.551" - weak signal for domain expertise evaluation
- Break condition: If the answer domain is too broad or interdisciplinary for a single expert to evaluate comprehensively, or if experts lack training in evaluation methodology.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Existing automatic metrics fail to predict human preference judgments because they are either reference-based (inappropriate for open-ended answers) or focus on single aspects like coherence without capturing overall quality.
- Mechanism: Reference-based metrics like ROUGE measure similarity to gold answers, but in LFQA the space of valid answers is too large for this to be meaningful. Metrics trained on overall preference struggle because human evaluations are multi-faceted and subjective.
- Core assumption: The complexity and subjectivity of human judgment in LFQA makes it difficult for any single automatic metric to capture "overall quality".
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "examine automatic text generation metrics, finding that no existing metrics are predictive of human preference judgments"
  - [section] "All metrics exhibit relatively low accuracies, falling substantially below estimated human agreement"
  - [corpus] "Found 25 related papers" - suggests active research area with limited consensus
- Break condition: If automatic metrics are designed to evaluate specific, well-defined aspects (e.g., factuality, coherence) rather than overall quality, or if they incorporate multi-faceted evaluation frameworks.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Models can learn to comprehend human justifications for answer preferences, suggesting that fine-grained automatic evaluation is feasible.
- Mechanism: By training models to predict masked references in human justifications (e.g., identifying which answer is being described), we can develop systems that understand the reasoning behind human judgments and potentially automate multi-faceted evaluation.
- Core assumption: The linguistic patterns in human justifications contain sufficient signal for models to disambiguate between answers and understand the aspects being evaluated.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Results are shown in Table 3. We see that T5-3B and T5-11B are able to comprehend the justifications, as they show different results for original and perturbed comments"
  - [abstract] "Can we train a metric to mimic human preference judgments?"
  - [corpus] Weak correlation signals suggest limited transfer to broader evaluation
- Break condition: If human justifications are too domain-specific or contain implicit knowledge that models cannot learn from training data alone.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Domain expertise and its role in evaluation
  - Why needed here: The paper argues that non-expert crowdworkers cannot reliably judge factuality and completeness in domain-specific long-form answers, necessitating expert evaluators
  - Quick check question: Why might a biologist be better than a crowdworker at evaluating an answer about blood clot absorption mechanisms?

- Concept: Multi-faceted evaluation vs. single "overall score"
  - Why needed here: The paper concludes that computing an overall score is not meaningful because different aspects (factuality, completeness, coherence) may be valued differently by different annotators
  - Quick check question: How might an answer that is highly factual but incomplete be evaluated differently than one that is complete but contains minor inaccuracies?

- Concept: Reference-based vs. reference-less evaluation metrics
  - Why needed here: The paper finds that reference-based metrics like ROUGE perform poorly on LFQA because the space of valid answers is too large, suggesting the need for alternative approaches
  - Quick check question: Why would a metric that measures coherence by looking only at the answer text (without reference) be more appropriate for LFQA than ROUGE?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Expert annotation pipeline: Question → Answer pairs → Expert evaluation interface → Preference judgments + justifications
  - Automatic metric evaluation: Human preference data → Metric computation → Correlation analysis
  - Justification comprehension: Masked justifications → Model prediction → Evaluation of model's understanding

- Critical path: The most important workflow is the expert annotation pipeline, as it provides the gold standard data for evaluating both human and automatic evaluation methods. This includes recruiting experts, presenting answer pairs, collecting preference judgments and justifications, and analyzing the results to identify key evaluation aspects.

- Design tradeoffs: The paper chose to use domain experts rather than crowdworkers despite higher cost because accuracy in evaluating factuality and completeness was prioritized over scalability. Similarly, they chose to analyze multiple automatic metrics rather than developing a single new one to understand the landscape of what works and what doesn't.

- Failure signatures: Expert annotation may fail if experts disagree too frequently (low inter-annotator agreement), suggesting the task is too subjective. Automatic metrics fail when they show low correlation with human judgments across multiple evaluation settings. Justification comprehension models fail when they cannot distinguish between original and flipped justifications.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate the expert vs. crowdworker comparison on a small subset of questions to verify that experts indeed focus more on factuality and completeness
  2. Test whether training an automatic metric on coherence judgments alone (rather than overall preference) improves its performance on coherence evaluation
  3. Evaluate whether providing evidence documents to the justification comprehension model improves its ability to disambiguate answers compared to using answers alone

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we develop reliable automatic metrics for long-form question answering that correlate with human judgments of overall answer quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "no existing metrics are predictive of human preference judgments" and that "all metrics exhibit relatively low accuracies, falling substantially below estimated human agreement."
- Why unresolved: The paper found that existing metrics, including reference-based, answer-only, and question-answer metrics, do not reliably correlate with human judgments of overall answer quality in LFQA.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing new metrics that consistently achieve high accuracy in imitating human judgments of overall answer quality across various datasets and answer types.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the key factors that make one long-form answer better than another, and how can we quantify their relative importance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies several fine-grained aspects that experts consider when evaluating answers, including factuality, completeness, ease of understanding, relevance, well-structured, grammar, use of examples, specificity, and conciseness.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that experts often disagree with each other when judging the better answer, indicating that the relative importance of these factors may vary depending on the context and the evaluator's perspective.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting large-scale studies to quantify the relative importance of each factor across different domains and answer types, and developing a framework to balance these factors in evaluating overall answer quality.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we leverage large language models to generate high-quality justifications for human preferences in long-form question answering?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper presents a study on using masked justification reference prediction to evaluate if models can understand human justifications for answer preferences.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows that larger language models can comprehend justifications to some extent, it does not explore how to generate these justifications automatically or how to use them to improve LFQA evaluation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing methods to generate high-quality justifications for answer preferences and evaluating their impact on improving the accuracy and reliability of LFQA evaluation.

## Limitations
- The expert annotation process relies on a relatively small sample (260 ratings) which may not capture the full diversity of LFQA domains and answer types
- The correlation analysis between automatic metrics and human judgments is limited to three specific datasets, raising questions about generalizability
- The justification comprehension experiments, while promising, show only moderate success and may not transfer to broader evaluation contexts

## Confidence
- High confidence: Expert annotators are better than crowdworkers at judging factuality and completeness in domain-specific answers
- Medium confidence: No existing automatic metrics reliably predict overall human preference judgments in LFQA
- Medium confidence: Multi-faceted evaluation is more meaningful than computing a single overall score

## Next Checks
1. Test whether the observed correlation differences between expert and crowdworker evaluations persist with a larger, more diverse expert sample
2. Evaluate whether training automatic metrics on specific aspects (factuality, coherence, completeness) individually improves their predictive accuracy for those aspects
3. Assess whether the justification comprehension model can generalize to human justifications from different domains and writing styles
