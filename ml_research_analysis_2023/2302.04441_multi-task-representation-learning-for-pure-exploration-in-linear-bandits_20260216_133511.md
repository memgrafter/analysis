# Multi-task Representation Learning for Pure Exploration in Linear Bandits

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2302.04441
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04441
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Key outcome:** Proposes DouExpDes and C-DouExpDes algorithms that use double experimental designs to learn shared representations across tasks, achieving sample complexity that scales with the low dimension k rather than high dimension d.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of pure exploration in multi-task linear bandits where all tasks share a common low-dimensional representation. The authors propose algorithms that leverage this shared structure to significantly reduce sample complexity compared to solving tasks independently. The key insight is to first recover the shared feature extractor through E-optimal experimental design, then use the estimated low-dimensional representations to efficiently identify the best arm or policy for each task via G-optimal design. Theoretical analysis shows the algorithms achieve near-optimal sample complexity bounds, and experiments validate the practical effectiveness of the approach.

## Method Summary
The algorithms perform double experimental designs: first an E-optimal design to recover the shared feature extractor B from the tasks' parameters, then a G-optimal design using the estimated low-dimensional representations to eliminate suboptimal arms/policies. DouExpDes handles the linear bandit setting (RepBAI-LB) while C-DouExpDes handles contextual linear bandits (RepBPI-CLB). Both use a rounding procedure to convert continuous designs to discrete sample allocations while preserving optimality properties. The algorithms proceed in phases, alternating between feature recovery and elimination rounds, with sample sizes determined by the desired confidence level and error bounds.

## Key Results
- Achieves sample complexity of Õ((d + kM) log(1/δ)) for RepBAI-LB, significantly better than independent task solving
- For RepBPI-CLB, achieves Õ((d + k|S|A) log(1/δ)) complexity when M >> d >> k
- Empirical results show the algorithms outperform baselines, especially as the number of tasks M increases
- Theoretical analysis establishes both upper and lower bounds on sample complexity

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi-task representation learning reduces sample complexity by exploiting a shared low-dimensional structure across tasks.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm performs double experimental designs - first an E-optimal design to recover the global feature extractor B, then a G-optimal design with low-dimensional representations to eliminate suboptimal arms efficiently.
- **Core assumption:** All tasks share a common low-dimensional linear representation B such that θ_m = B w_m for each task m, where M >> d >> k.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "all tasks share a common low-dimensional linear representation, and our goal is to leverage this feature to accelerate the best arm (policy) identification process for all tasks."
  - [section 1] "In these two problems, all tasks share a common low-dimensional linear representation, and our goal is to leverage this feature to accelerate the best arm (policy) identification process for all tasks."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found in neighbors, but the concept aligns with general representation learning literature.
- **Break condition:** If the assumption of a shared representation fails (tasks have independent representations), the algorithm degrades to solving tasks independently with no sample complexity benefit.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The E-optimal design optimally allocates samples to recover the feature extractor B with minimal variance.
- **Mechanism:** By solving an E-optimal design problem with raw representations, the algorithm finds a sample allocation that minimizes the spectral norm of the inverse covariance matrix, ensuring stable estimation of B.
- **Core assumption:** The arm set X spans Rd and has sufficient diversity to enable identification of B through experimental design.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 1] "we design computationally and sample efficient algorithms DouExpDes and C-DouExpDes, which perform double experimental designs to plan optimal sample allocations for learning the global representation."
  - [section 4.1] "DouExpDes uses a rounding procedure...which transforms a given continuous sample allocation (design) into a discrete sample sequence and maintains important properties (e.g., E-optimality and G-optimality) of the design."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found in neighbors, but experimental design is a standard technique in statistics and bandit literature.
- **Break condition:** If the arm set X doesn't span Rd or lacks diversity, the E-optimal design may fail to provide sufficient information for recovering B.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The G-optimal design with low-dimensional representations efficiently eliminates suboptimal arms while minimizing prediction error.
- **Mechanism:** After estimating B, the algorithm uses reduced-dimensional representations B^T x to conduct G-optimal design, which minimizes the maximum prediction error across arms, enabling rapid elimination of suboptimal options.
- **Core assumption:** The estimated feature extractor B provides sufficient accuracy for the reduced-dimensional representation to maintain task-relevant information.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 1] "perform double experimental designs to plan optimal sample allocations for learning the global representation"
  - [section 4.1] "Then, DouExpDes calls subroutine EliLowRep to eliminate suboptimal arms using low-dimensional representations. In EliLowRep, we conduct the G-optimal design with the reduced-dimensional representations B^T x"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found in neighbors, but G-optimal design is a well-established technique in experimental design.
- **Break condition:** If the estimation error of B is too large, the reduced-dimensional representations may lose critical information, leading to incorrect arm elimination.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** FeatRecover -> EliLowRep -> ROUND -> DouExpDes/C-DouExpDes
- **Critical path:**
  1. Initial E-optimal design to plan samples for feature extractor recovery
  2. Feature extractor recovery via FeatRecover subroutine
  3. Iterative elimination using G-optimal design with estimated representations
  4. Sample complexity analysis and theoretical guarantees
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Computational efficiency vs. statistical efficiency: The algorithms use efficient rounding procedures to convert continuous designs to discrete samples while maintaining optimality guarantees
  - Number of phases vs. sample allocation: More phases allow more precise elimination but require additional samples
  - Dimension reduction vs. information loss: Reducing to k dimensions provides sample complexity benefits but depends on accurate feature extractor estimation
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High estimation error of feature extractor B leading to incorrect arm/policy elimination
  - Sample complexity approaching the baseline (solving tasks independently) if representation learning fails
  - Computational inefficiency if rounding procedures or experimental design optimizations become bottlenecks
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Validate the E-optimal design for feature extractor recovery on synthetic data with known B
  2. Test the G-optimal design elimination with estimated low-dimensional representations on controlled examples
  3. Compare sample complexity scaling with number of tasks M on benchmark linear bandit problems

## Limitations

- The theoretical guarantees heavily depend on the assumption that all tasks share an identical low-dimensional representation B, which may not hold in practice.
- The analysis assumes boundedness conditions on the arm set X and sufficient diversity to enable experimental design, but doesn't fully explore what happens when these conditions are violated.
- The rounding procedure for converting continuous designs to discrete samples is described but implementation details remain unclear.

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the core algorithmic framework (double experimental designs) as it builds on well-established experimental design theory
- **Medium confidence** in the theoretical sample complexity bounds, as they rely on multiple concentration inequalities and SVD perturbation analysis that require careful verification
- **Medium confidence** in the empirical validation, given the limited scope of experiments presented
