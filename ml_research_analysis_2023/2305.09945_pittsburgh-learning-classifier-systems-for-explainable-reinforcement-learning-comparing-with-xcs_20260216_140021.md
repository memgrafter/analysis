# Pittsburgh Learning Classifier Systems for Explainable Reinforcement Learning: Comparing with XCS

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.09945
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09945
- **Reference count**: 40
- **One-line result**: PPL-ST evolved more parsimonious and interpretable rulesets than XCS while maintaining competitive or superior performance in FrozenLake environments with high uncertainty.

## Executive Summary
This paper compares Pittsburgh Learning Classifier Systems (PPL) with the Michigan LCS XCS in reinforcement learning domains using FrozenLake environments. Two PPL variants were developed: PPL-DL (zeroth-level decision list system) and PPL-ST (with Monte Carlo rule strength learning). Results showed PPL-ST achieved comparable or better performance than PPL-DL across all environments and outperformed XCS in high-uncertainty settings. Notably, PPL-ST evolved more parsimonious and interpretable rulesets than XCS while maintaining competitive or superior performance, though at higher computational cost.

## Method Summary
The study implemented two PPL variants (PPL-DL with decision list representation and PPL-ST with Monte Carlo rule strength learning) and compared them to XCS across FrozenLake environments with varying grid sizes (4×4, 8×8, 12×12) and slip probabilities (0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5). All systems were trained for 250 epochs using genetic algorithm-based evolution. Performance was measured as the fraction of optimal testing performance, and ruleset parsimony was evaluated through direct comparison of evolved ruleset sizes.

## Key Results
- PPL-ST achieved comparable or better performance than PPL-DL across all environment configurations
- PPL-ST outperformed XCS in high-uncertainty environments (pslip ≥ 0.3)
- PPL-ST evolved significantly smaller and more interpretable rulesets than XCS while maintaining or exceeding performance
- PPL-ST required substantially higher computational resources (hundreds of millions of episodes vs. XCS's orders of magnitude fewer)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: PPL-ST's Monte Carlo rule strength learning enables it to estimate the utility of individual rules within a ruleset, leading to more robust policy learning in high-uncertainty environments.
- Mechanism: PPL-ST uses a Monte Carlo approach to estimate the strength of each rule within a ruleset. This is done by simulating trajectories in the environment and updating the rule's strength estimates based on the cumulative discounted reward obtained.
- Core assumption: The Monte Carlo estimate of rule strength accurately reflects the rule's contribution to the overall policy performance.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] and [section] references to Monte Carlo rule strength learning mechanism.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: PPL-ST's allocentric rule representation allows it to handle allocentric state representations, which are necessary for the FrozenLake environments used in the experiments.
- Mechanism: PPL-ST uses a rule representation that is based on the absolute position within the environment, rather than the relative position. This is necessary because the FrozenLake environments have situations where using a relative representation would result in state aliasing.
- Core assumption: The allocentric rule representation is expressive enough to capture the relevant features of the environment.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] and [section] references to allocentric rule representation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: PPL-ST's rule-based representation leads to more parsimonious and interpretable rulesets compared to XCS.
- Mechanism: PPL-ST uses a fixed-length ruleset representation, which prevents the problem of bloat that can occur with variable-length rulesets.
- Core assumption: The fixed-length ruleset representation is sufficient to capture the relevant policies for the given environments.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] and [section] references to parsimonious rulesets.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: PPL-ST: Ruleset (fixed-length) -> Rule (condition, action, weight vector, variance estimate) -> Monte Carlo rule strength learning -> Allocentric rule representation
- **Critical path**: PPL-ST: Generate trajectory → Update rule strength estimates → Select action based on rule strengths
- **Design tradeoffs**: PPL-ST vs. XCS: PPL-ST has higher computational cost but can produce more interpretable rulesets. XCS is more computationally efficient but may produce less interpretable rulesets.
- **Failure signatures**: PPL-ST: Poor performance in environments with complex state spaces or where the rule-based representation is not expressive enough.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Compare the performance of PPL-ST and XCS on a simple FrozenLake environment with low levels of stochasticity.
  2. Vary the level of stochasticity in the FrozenLake environment and observe how the performance of PPL-ST and XCS changes.
  3. Analyze the rulesets produced by PPL-ST and XCS to compare their interpretability and parsimony.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal value of the numReinfRollouts parameter for PPL-ST in environments with varying levels of transition stochasticity?
- Basis in paper: The paper tested values up to 50 and found little impact on performance, using numReinfRollouts=10.
- Why unresolved: Only tested on one stochastic environment (8, 0.3) with limited parameter range.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive sensitivity analysis testing numReinfRollouts values across a wider range and across all environment combinations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would PPL-ST perform with variable-length rulesets instead of fixed-length rulesets?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions this as a promising direction for future work.
- Why unresolved: Current PPL-ST uses fixed-length rulesets for simplicity, but variable-length rulesets could be explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing PPL-ST with variable-length rulesets and comparing its performance and rule set characteristics to the current fixed-length version.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the computational complexity of PPL-ST be reduced without sacrificing performance?
- Basis in paper: The paper acknowledges the high computational cost and suggests potential solutions like parallelization and stratification.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the high computational cost and suggests potential solutions, it does not implement or evaluate these solutions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and testing the suggested methods (parallelization, stratification) and potentially other optimization techniques to reduce the computational complexity of PPL-ST.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to FrozenLake environments only, limiting generalizability
- Lack of ablation studies to isolate Monte Carlo learning's contribution
- No comparison with non-LCS RL baselines like DQN or PPO

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: PPL-ST produces more parsimonious rulesets than XCS (supported by direct comparisons)
- **Medium confidence**: Performance claims (limited by computational constraints to smaller environments)
- **Low confidence**: Scalability claims (acknowledged high computational cost without systematic analysis)

## Next Checks
1. **Scale-up validation**: Test PPL-ST on larger gridworlds (16×16, 32×32) to verify performance scaling and computational feasibility.
2. **Ablation study**: Compare PPL-ST with a variant using online learning (like XCS) instead of Monte Carlo to isolate the contribution of rule strength estimation.
3. **Interpretability audit**: Conduct human evaluation of evolved rulesets to quantify actual interpretability differences beyond mere size metrics.
