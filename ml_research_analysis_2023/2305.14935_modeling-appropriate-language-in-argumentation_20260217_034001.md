# Modeling Appropriate Language in Argumentation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.14935
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14935
- **Reference count**: 5
- **Primary result**: 69% macro-F1 score for classifying inappropriate language in argumentation, approaching human performance

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a systematic framework for identifying inappropriate language in online argumentation. The authors develop a taxonomy of 14 inappropriateness dimensions grounded in rhetoric and argument quality theory, covering toxic emotions, missing commitment, missing intelligibility, and other reasons. They construct a new corpus of 2191 arguments annotated across these dimensions and show correlations with existing argument quality measures. Initial classification experiments using DeBERTaV3 achieve 69% macro-F1 score, approaching human performance, demonstrating the feasibility of automated appropriateness assessment.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a taxonomy of 14 inappropriateness dimensions through literature review and iterative refinement. They compile arguments from three existing argument quality corpora (Dagstuhl-15512 ArgQuality, UKPConvArg2, GAQCorpus) totaling 2191 arguments across three genres. Three annotators independently label each argument for all 14 dimensions. The classification experiments use DeBERTaV3-large with multi-label classification, trained on 70% of the data with 5-fold cross-validation and evaluated on 20% held-out test data.

## Key Results
- DeBERTaV3-large achieves 69% macro-F1 score for inappropriate language classification
- Model performance approaches human performance (75% macro-F1)
- All 14 dimensions show positive correlation with overall argument quality
- DeBERTaV3-large outperforms random and majority baselines on all dimensions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Modeling appropriateness as the absence of specific flaws captures a comprehensive notion of inappropriate language in argumentation.
- **Mechanism**: The taxonomy decomposes inappropriateness into four core dimensions (toxic emotions, missing commitment, missing intelligibility, other reasons), each with sub-dimensions derived from argument quality theory. This hierarchical structure allows granular identification of inappropriateness.
- **Core assumption**: Appropriateness in argumentation can be effectively modeled by identifying and classifying specific types of inappropriate language rather than attempting to define appropriateness directly.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "We model appropriateness through the absence of flaws, grounded in research on argument quality assessment, especially in aspects from rhetoric."
  - [section] "We argue appropriateness to be a minimal quality property that is necessary for any argument to consider it valuable in a debate."
- **Break condition**: If the dimensions do not capture the full range of inappropriate language, or if cultural context significantly alters what is considered inappropriate, the taxonomy may be incomplete.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The corpus provides sufficient data to train and evaluate computational models for detecting inappropriate language.
- **Mechanism**: The corpus contains 2191 arguments from three genres (debates, Q&A forums, reviews) annotated for all 14 dimensions. This diversity and size enable robust model training and evaluation.
- **Core assumption**: A diverse corpus with manual annotations across multiple genres provides adequate training data for computational models to generalize across different contexts of online argumentation.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Building on three argument quality corpora, we then create a corpus of 2191 arguments annotated for the 14 dimensions."
  - [section] "Given the new corpus, we analyze correlations between the 14 dimensions and the argument quality dimensions in the source corpora."
- **Break condition**: If the corpus lacks sufficient examples of certain types of inappropriate language, or if the annotations are too subjective, model performance may be limited.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: DeBERTa-based models can effectively classify inappropriate language in argumentation with reasonable accuracy.
- **Mechanism**: The experiments show that DeBERTaV3-large achieves 69% macro-F1 score, approaching human performance (75%), suggesting that semantic understanding of arguments is crucial for the task.
- **Core assumption**: Transformer-based models pre-trained on large text corpora can learn to identify nuanced aspects of inappropriate language when fine-tuned on the Appropriateness Corpus.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Initial classification experiments using DeBERTaV3 achieve 69% macro-F1 score, approaching human performance."
  - [section] "DeBERTaV3-large clearly outperforms the random baseline and majority baseline on all dimensions, and it achieves about 92% of human performance in terms of macro F1 (.75)."
- **Break condition**: If the model relies too heavily on superficial features rather than true semantic understanding, or if the task requires context beyond the argument itself, performance may degrade.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Argument quality dimensions (credibility, emotional appeal, clarity, arrangement, cogency, reasonableness)
  - Why needed here: The taxonomy is derived from these dimensions, so understanding them is crucial for interpreting the inappropriateness dimensions and their correlations.
  - Quick check question: What are the four sub-dimensions of rhetorical effectiveness used in the derivation of the inappropriateness taxonomy?

- **Concept**: Rhetorical fallacies (ad populum, ad misericordiam, ad baculum, ad hominem)
- Why needed here: These fallacies form the basis for the "emotional deception" sub-dimension in the taxonomy.
  - Quick check question: Which four rhetorical fallacies were merged into the "emotional deception" sub-dimension?

- **Concept**: Corpus annotation and inter-annotator agreement
  - Why needed here: Understanding how the corpus was annotated and the level of agreement is important for interpreting the reliability of the data and the results.
  - Quick check question: What was the Krippendorff's α agreement for the "inappropriateness" dimension in the corpus?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Source corpora -> Argument compilation -> Hierarchical annotation -> Preprocessed data -> DeBERTaV3-large training -> Evaluation
- **Critical path**: 1. Compile arguments from source corpora 2. Annotate arguments for all 14 dimensions 3. Preprocess data and split into train/dev/test folds 4. Train DeBERTaV3-large model with multi-label classification 5. Evaluate model performance and compare to baselines
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Hierarchical taxonomy vs. flat structure: Allows for more granular analysis but increases annotation complexity
  - Conservative vs. liberal label aggregation: Conservative strategy may be more conservative but could miss some appropriate arguments
  - Context inclusion (issue vs. argument only): Including issue provides more context but may not significantly improve performance
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low inter-annotator agreement on certain dimensions
  - Model performance significantly worse on certain dimensions
  - High correlation between certain dimensions (e.g., excessive intensity and missing openness) suggesting potential overlap
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Compare model performance with and without issue context to quantify the impact of context
  2. Analyze model predictions on a sample of arguments to identify common failure modes
  3. Evaluate model performance on a held-out test set from a different genre to assess generalization

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly call out open questions in the traditional sense. The authors acknowledge limitations and potential areas for future work, particularly regarding cultural differences in appropriateness perception and the need for more diverse datasets, but do not frame these as specific open questions to be addressed.

## Limitations
- The taxonomy may not capture all forms of inappropriate language, particularly across different cultural contexts
- The corpus is limited to three specific genres of online argumentation and may not generalize to other domains
- The model's performance is based on a single DeBERTa architecture, limiting generalizability claims

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The correlation analysis between inappropriateness dimensions and existing argument quality measures is robust and provides meaningful insights into the relationships between these constructs.
- **Medium Confidence**: The taxonomy development process and the resulting 14 dimensions are well-founded, but their completeness and generalizability across all cultural contexts remain uncertain.
- **Medium Confidence**: The DeBERTaV3 model achieves reasonable performance, but the approach's effectiveness on other model architectures and its ability to generalize to unseen genres of argumentation is untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-Cultural Validation**: Conduct a study with annotators from different cultural backgrounds to assess whether the inappropriateness dimensions are interpreted consistently across cultures, or if additional dimensions are needed.
2. **Model Architecture Comparison**: Evaluate the classification performance using alternative transformer models (e.g., RoBERTa, BERTweet) and ensemble methods to determine if the approach is architecture-dependent or more generally applicable.
3. **Out-of-Domain Generalization**: Test the trained model on a held-out corpus of arguments from a different genre (e.g., social media posts, academic discourse) to assess its ability to generalize beyond the three source genres.
