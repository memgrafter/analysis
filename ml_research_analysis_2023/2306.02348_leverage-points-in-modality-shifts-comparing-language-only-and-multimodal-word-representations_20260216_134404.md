# Leverage Points in Modality Shifts: Comparing Language-only and Multimodal Word Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02348
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02348
- Reference count: 5
- This study identifies concreteness as the most significant factor affecting semantic representation differences between language-only and multimodal models.

## Executive Summary
This study systematically analyzes how visual modality affects word representations by comparing embeddings from vision-and-language models to text-only models. Using 13,000 word pairs characterized along 46 semantic parameters, the research identifies concreteness as the primary factor driving representation differences, with visual grounding improving representations of concrete nouns but not abstract ones. The analysis also reveals that taxonomic features (WordNet supersenses) and valence (a sentiment-related connotational property) contribute to the observed differences, suggesting that visual grounding affects both denotational and connotational aspects of semantic representations.

## Method Summary
The study compares semantic representations between text-only models (FastText, mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) and multimodal models (CLIP, OpenCLIP, Multilingual CLIP) using 13,000 word pairs generated from the 1,000 most frequent words. These pairs are characterized by 46 semantic parameters including concreteness, WordNet supersenses, VAD scores, and semantic relations. Cosine distances between word pairs are calculated across all models, and regression analysis is used to identify which semantic features most significantly predict differences in representation.

## Key Results
- Concreteness is the most significant factor affecting modality shifts, with visual grounding improving representations of concrete nouns but not abstract ones
- Taxonomic features from WordNet supersenses (particularly artifacts, quantities, possessions, and communication classes) consistently affect semantic similarities between text-only and multimodal models
- Valence, a sentiment-related connotational property, also influences how modality affects semantic representations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Visual modality affects semantic representations most for concrete nouns because concrete concepts have strong visual correlates.
- Mechanism: Concreteness measures how easily a concept can be imagined or visualized. When visual data is included in training, embeddings for concrete nouns can leverage image-based features that align with their meaning, whereas abstract nouns lack such visual grounding.
- Core assumption: Visual grounding provides semantically relevant information that improves representation quality for concepts with strong visual components.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our regression analysis shows that concreteness is the most significant factor, confirming previous findings that visual modality improves representations of concrete nouns but not abstract ones."
  - [section]: "This is natural since imageability, the measurable manifestation of concreteness, is directly related to whether useful information about a concept can be inferred from visual data."

- Break Condition: If the visual dataset lacks sufficient examples of concrete objects or if the visual features don't align well with the semantic space, the benefit for concrete nouns would diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Taxonomic features from WordNet supersenses interact with modality shifts, with different semantic classes showing varying sensitivity to visual grounding.
- Mechanism: Words belong to semantic categories (e.g., ARTIFACT, ANIMAL, FOOD) that may have different degrees of visual distinctiveness. When visual data is incorporated, embeddings for words in visually distinctive categories are stretched or shifted differently in the embedding space compared to those in less visually distinctive categories.
- Core assumption: Semantic categories have varying levels of visual informativeness, and this informativeness affects how much visual grounding influences their representations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We find that the effect of visual modality correlates most with denotational semantic properties related to concreteness, but is also detected for several specific semantic classes..."
  - [section]: "WordNet supersenses consistently affect semantic similarities in text-only models vs. L&V models: in particular, we found this for artifacts, quantities, possessions and communication lexical classes."
  - [corpus]: Average neighbor FMR=0.503 suggests moderate relatedness in the literature, indicating this is an area of ongoing investigation.
- Break Condition: If the visual dataset is biased toward certain categories or if the taxonomic distinctions don't map well to visual features, the interaction would break down.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Connotational properties, specifically valence from VAD (valence, arousal, dominance), influence how modality affects semantic representations, suggesting an interaction between denotational and connotational meanings.
- Mechanism: Sentiment-related properties like valence capture emotional associations with words. Visual grounding might affect how these emotional associations are represented in the embedding space, possibly because emotional content often has visual correlates (e.g., happy faces, angry expressions).
- Core assumption: Emotional and sentiment-related aspects of meaning have visual correlates that can be captured by multimodal models, leading to different representation patterns compared to text-only models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We also find that taxonomic features, such as WordNet supersenses, and valence, a sentiment-related connotational property, contribute to the differences."
  - [section]: "sentiment-related lexical properties, most clearly valence, also affect the semantic similarity in language-only vs. multimodal spaces... This is a somewhat surprising result since visual grounding is expected to relate to the denotational aspects of representations."
  - [corpus]: Weak corpus evidence with average neighbor FMR=0.503 and no citations, suggesting this finding is relatively novel and not well-explored in existing literature.
- Break Condition: If sentiment and emotional content don't have strong visual correlates or if the visual data doesn't capture these aspects well, the influence of valence on modality shifts would disappear.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data collection pipeline (word pairs generation, filtering, annotation) -> Embedding extraction system (text-only and multimodal models) -> Regression analysis framework -> Visualization and result interpretation tools

- Critical path: 1. Generate word pairs from frequency-based similarity 2. Annotate pairs with semantic features (concreteness, supersenses, VAD, relations) 3. Extract embeddings from all models 4. Calculate cosine distances for each pair in each model 5. Run regression analysis to identify significant predictors 6. Interpret results and validate findings

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Pair generation: Frequency-based vs. random sampling - frequency ensures quality but may bias toward common concepts
  - Feature selection: Comprehensive vs. focused - more features provide richer analysis but increase complexity
  - Model selection: Established vs. cutting-edge - balances reliability with state-of-the-art representation

- Failure signatures:
  - High correlation between concreteness and frequency - suggests concreteness effect might be confounded by frequency
  - Inconsistent results across different embedding types from same model - indicates model-specific artifacts
  - No significant predictors in regression - suggests either data issues or fundamental similarity between model types

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify concreteness effect by running regression with only concreteness and frequency as predictors across all model pairs
  2. Test taxonomic effect by comparing regression results when including only WordNet supersenses vs. only ConceptNet relations
  3. Validate valence effect by running separate regressions for each VAD component (valence, arousal, dominance) to see which drives the result

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do visual modality representations in language models specifically improve performance on downstream tasks that require visual reasoning or grounding?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the impact of visual grounding on semantic representations but does not evaluate performance on specific downstream tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on intrinsic analysis of representation differences, not extrinsic task performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing model performance on vision-language tasks (e.g., visual question answering, image captioning) using both text-only and multimodal models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why do certain WordNet supersenses (e.g., artifacts, quantities, possessions) show more consistent effects on modality shifts than others?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that taxonomic features from WordNet consistently affect semantic similarities in text-only vs. L&V models, with specific lexical classes like artifacts and possessions being particularly affected.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the effect but does not provide a detailed explanation for why these specific supersenses are more affected.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further analysis of the visual characteristics of these semantic classes and how they are represented in multimodal training data.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What other semantic factors beyond concreteness, valence, and taxonomic features contribute to the discrepancy between textual and multimodal models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that the semantic factors considered only explain a small part of the discrepancy, with the rest attributed to random differences in training data or other semantic phenomena.
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to the semantic parameters for which large-scale human annotations exist.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of additional semantic dimensions (e.g., other connotational properties, pragmatic features) or exploration of how random training data variations affect representations.

## Limitations
- Focus on noun pairs only, potentially missing systematic differences in how visual modality affects other parts of speech
- Use of static embeddings (FastText) alongside contextualized models (BERT, XLM-R) introduces variability that may confound results
- Limited semantic parameter coverage due to availability of large-scale human annotations

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Concreteness as the primary factor affecting modality shifts, supported by direct evidence in the abstract and discussion section
- **Medium confidence**: Taxonomic effects of WordNet supersenses, as results are described but with less statistical detail
- **Medium confidence**: Valence effects on semantic representations, though this finding is described as "somewhat surprising" and supported by weaker corpus evidence
