# In What Languages are Generative Language Models the Most Formal? Analyzing Formality Distribution across Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12299
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12299
- Reference count: 37
- Key outcome: Analyzes formality bias in XGLM and BLOOM across Arabic, Bengali, English, French, and Spanish, finding both models favor formal text when prompted neutrally and preserve formality when prompted accordingly.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates formality bias in multilingual generative language models by analyzing XGLM and BLOOM across five languages. Native speakers classified 6,000 generated samples as formal, informal, or incohesive. The research reveals that both models exhibit a strong bias toward formal text generation when given neutral prompts, while also demonstrating the ability to preserve formality when explicitly prompted. BLOOM consistently generates more cohesive text than XGLM across most languages evaluated.

## Method Summary
The study generated 1,200 samples per language (300 per model) using XGLM (2.9B and 7.5B parameters) and BLOOM (3B and 7.1B parameters) with both short neutral prompts and longer formal/informal prompts from various datasets. Native/proficient speakers classified outputs into formal, informal, or incohesive categories. The analysis measured three dimensions: cohesiveness of generations, formality bias with neutral prompts, and formality preservation with explicit prompts. Decoding parameters included top-k=50 with high top-p and temperature values.

## Key Results
- Both XGLM and BLOOM show strong bias toward formal text generation with neutral prompts (50-90% formal)
- BLOOM generates significantly more cohesive text than XGLM for English, French, and Spanish (p < 5%)
- Both models effectively preserve formality when prompted with explicit formal or informal prompts (70-90% preservation)
- BLOOM generates roughly twice as much text as XGLM on average

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does RLHF affect the formality distributions in language models?
- Basis in paper: The authors mention that future work could analyze the impact of RLHF on formality distributions, as recent work like Ouyang et al. (2022) has used RLHF to fine-tune language models to align with user intent.
- Why unresolved: The authors do not provide any analysis or evidence on how RLHF impacts formality distributions in their study. This remains an open question for future research.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing formality distributions in language models before and after RLHF fine-tuning, using the same evaluation methods as this paper.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do formality distributions change in larger language models like GPT-3 and BLOOM(175B)?
- Basis in paper: The authors state that their analysis is based on two pre-trained models with up to 7B parameters and suggest that the same analysis could be conducted for larger models like GPT-3 and BLOOM(175B).
- Why unresolved: The authors only analyze models up to 7B parameters and do not provide any data or insights on how formality distributions might change in larger models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting the same formality analysis on GPT-3 and BLOOM(175B) using the same evaluation methods and comparing the results to the findings in this paper.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the bias in multilingual language models vary across different language families and cultures?
- Basis in paper: The authors focus on analyzing formality bias in five specific languages (Arabic, Bengali, English, French, and Spanish) and note that every language is present in the data with a different percentage and from different sources. This suggests that bias may vary across languages and cultures.
- Why unresolved: The authors only analyze five languages and do not provide a comprehensive comparison of formality bias across different language families and cultures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Expanding the analysis to include a more diverse set of languages from different language families and cultures, and comparing the formality bias distributions across these languages.

## Limitations

- Dataset Composition and Representativeness: The exact composition of prompts and their representativeness across different formality contexts remains unclear.
- Model Architecture Differences: BLOOM and XGLM have different training corpora and architectures, making it difficult to isolate whether observed formality biases stem from model architecture or training data composition.
- Annotation Subjectivity: Formality classification is inherently subjective and language-dependent, with less detailed criteria for Arabic and Bengali.

## Confidence

**Formality Bias Claims (Medium Confidence)**: Both models show strong bias toward formal text with neutral prompts, but the exact magnitude varies significantly across languages and models.

**Formality Preservation Claims (Medium Confidence)**: Both models effectively preserve formality when explicitly prompted, but intermediate formality levels and degree of formality shift are not examined.

**Cohesiveness Claims (High Confidence)**: BLOOM generates more cohesive text than XGLM with statistically significant p-values for English, French, and Spanish.

## Next Checks

1. **Prompt Composition Analysis**: Recreate the prompt sets used for each language and analyze their formality distributions to determine whether observed biases stem from prompt characteristics rather than model behavior.

2. **Cross-Validation with Alternative Metrics**: Apply additional formality detection methods (e.g., linguistic feature analysis, automated formality classifiers) to validate human annotation results and check for consistency across evaluation approaches.

3. **Controlled Generation Experiments**: Conduct systematic experiments varying single parameters (temperature, top-p, prompt length) while holding others constant to quantify how each factor influences formality generation independently.
