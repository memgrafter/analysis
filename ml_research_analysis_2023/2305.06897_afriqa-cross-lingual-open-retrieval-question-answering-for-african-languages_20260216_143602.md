# AfriQA: Cross-lingual Open-Retrieval Question Answering for African Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06897
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06897
- Reference count: 26
- Primary result: Introduces AfriQA, a cross-lingual open-retrieval QA dataset for 10 African languages with 12,000+ questions

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AfriQA, the first cross-lingual open-retrieval question answering dataset focused on African languages. The dataset contains over 12,000 information-seeking questions across 10 African languages, with English and French Wikipedia serving as the answer source corpus. The authors demonstrate that current state-of-the-art QA models perform poorly on this dataset, highlighting the challenges of cross-lingual retrieval and translation for morphologically rich, low-resource languages.

## Method Summary
The authors developed AfriQA by first eliciting 1,200 information-seeking questions from native speakers of each African language through a crowdsourced process. Questions were then translated into English and French, and passages containing answers were retrieved from English and French Wikipedia. For each question-passage pair, answers were extracted using human annotators, and the questions were translated back into their original languages. The dataset includes both monolingual and cross-lingual question-answer pairs, enabling evaluation of open-retrieval QA systems across language boundaries.

## Key Results
- Current state-of-the-art QA models achieve poor performance on AfriQA, demonstrating its challenging nature
- Human-translated queries significantly outperform machine-translated queries in both retrieval and QA accuracy
- Hybrid retrieval combining BM25 and mDPR yields the best results across all languages
- Cross-lingual dense retrieval without query translation performs worst among all retrieval methods

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Morphological richness and its impact on translation and retrieval.
  - Why needed here: African languages in the dataset are morphologically rich (e.g., Bemba, Zulu), meaning grammatical changes are embedded in word forms, which can confuse both translation systems and retrievers expecting simpler tokenization.
  - Quick check question: How does a morphologically rich language like Bemba indicate plurality and tense differently from English?

- Concept: Cross-lingual dense passage retrieval (mDPR) architecture.
  - Why needed here: mDPR is the main dense retrieval baseline; understanding its input (multilingual BERT embeddings) and limitations with low-resource languages is key to interpreting retrieval results.
  - Quick check question: What is the role of the encoder in mDPR, and why might it underperform on unseen languages?

- Concept: Exact Match (EM) and F1 metrics in QA evaluation.
  - Why needed here: Results are reported in EM and F1; knowing the difference (EM requires exact token overlap, F1 measures token overlap proportionally) helps interpret answer quality.
  - Quick check question: If the predicted answer is "13" and the gold answer is "13 states", what would EM and F1 scores be?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Question elicitation -> Translation (human/machine) -> Passage retrieval (BM25/mDPR/hybrid) -> Answer extraction (extractive/generative) -> Answer translation (if needed)
- Critical path: Translation -> Retrieval -> Answer extraction. Retrieval performance directly affects downstream QA accuracy.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Human vs. machine translation: higher accuracy vs. scalability
  - BM25 vs. mDPR vs. hybrid: exact term matching vs. semantic similarity vs. combined coverage
  - Extractive vs. generative QA: precise spans vs. fluent but potentially less accurate answers
- Failure signatures:
  - Low recall@k: poor translation quality or cross-lingual retrieval limitations
  - Low EM/F1: incorrect answer spans or translation errors in answer translation
  - High unanswerable rate: insufficient coverage in pivot language Wikipedia
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Re-run retrieval with human-translated queries vs. Google Translate vs. NLLB and compare recall@k
  2. Compare BM25, mDPR, and hybrid retrieval on a subset of languages to confirm relative effectiveness
  3. Evaluate extractive QA on gold passages with human vs. machine-translated queries to isolate translation impact

## Limitations

- Heavy reliance on English Wikipedia as the pivot language corpus introduces bias toward English-centric topics
- Lack of detailed error analysis to categorize failure modes across translation, retrieval, and answer extraction components
- No inter-annotator agreement scores reported for human translations, leaving uncertainty about gold standard quality

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The claim that AfriQA presents a novel, high-quality dataset for African languages is well-supported with detailed methodology.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that hybrid retrieval performs best is supported by results, but the underlying reason is assumed rather than empirically validated.

**Low confidence**: The claim that cross-lingual dense retrieval is fundamentally less effective than translated query retrieval may reflect current model performance rather than architectural limitations.

## Next Checks

1. **Error analysis decomposition**: Systematically categorize retrieval and QA failures by type using a stratified sample of questions to identify which component contributes most to poor performance.

2. **Corpus diversity impact**: Re-run experiments using alternative corpora beyond English Wikipedia (e.g., African language-specific Wikipedia editions, local news sources) to assess how corpus selection affects retrieval recall and QA accuracy.

3. **Fine-tuning impact assessment**: Fine-tune the mDPR model on a small amount of parallel data from African languages and re-evaluate cross-lingual retrieval performance to determine whether poor results stem from architectural limitations or lack of language-specific training data.
