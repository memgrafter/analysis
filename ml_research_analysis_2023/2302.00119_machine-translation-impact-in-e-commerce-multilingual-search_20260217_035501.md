# Machine Translation Impact in E-commerce Multilingual Search

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.00119
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00119
- Reference count: 22
- Key outcome: Framework evaluates MT quality impact on CLIR using behavioral signals as proxy relevance

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for evaluating machine translation (MT) quality in cross-lingual information retrieval (CLIR) for e-commerce product search. The approach uses behavioral signals (clicks and purchases) as proxy relevance scores to measure MT impact on search performance without requiring human annotations. The framework computes MT launching and improvement impact rates to quantify how search metrics like nDCG change with MT quality improvements. Experiments with 12 language pairs from 6 e-commerce stores demonstrate that the framework can identify high, medium, and low impact language pairs, providing actionable insights for MT investment strategies.

## Method Summary
The framework evaluates MT quality impact through a rank-based approach using behavioral signals as proxy relevance. It involves creating test sets with sampled queries, reference translations, and behavioral data from purchase logs. Queries are translated using generic and adapted MT systems, then searched to retrieve top-16 products. nDCG@16 is computed using purchase product IDs as relevance ground truth. Two impact rates are calculated: MT launching impact rate (IMT = Δsearch/Δtranslation_quality) and MT improvement impact rate (Iimprove = Δsearch_adapted/Δtranslation_quality), using nDCG@16 and BLEU/ChrF metrics. Language pairs are classified based on these impact rates to guide MT improvement strategies.

## Key Results
- Behavioral signals effectively serve as proxy relevance for MT evaluation without human annotation
- MT launching and improvement impact rates successfully quantify search sensitivity to translation quality changes
- Different language pairs exhibit varying impact profiles, enabling targeted MT investment strategies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Behavioral signals can serve as proxy relevance for evaluating MT quality without human annotation
- Mechanism: User click and purchase data associated with queries and product IDs are used to approximate relevance scores
- Core assumption: Behavioral signals correlate with true relevance and remain stable enough for evaluation
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] behavioral signals as proxy for relevance; [section 2] behavioral signals as proxy to human relevance annotations
- Break condition: If behavioral signals become sparse or noisy due to product lifecycle changes or seasonal trends

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: MT launching and improvement impact rates quantify sensitivity of search metrics to MT quality changes
- Mechanism: Two metrics measure how much search improves per unit of MT quality gain
- Core assumption: Search metrics are sensitive enough to MT quality changes to reveal meaningful impact rates
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] computes MT launching and improvement impact rates; [section 3.2] formal definition with equations
- Break condition: If search pipeline components dominate over MT quality, or if MT improvements are too small to affect retrieval metrics

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Different language pairs exhibit different MT impact profiles, guiding investment strategies
- Mechanism: Language pairs are classified as high, medium, or low impact based on impact rates
- Core assumption: Search ecosystems vary in sensitivity to translation quality across language pairs
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] language pairs with higher impact rates benefit more from improving MT systems; [section 5] analysis of language pairs with differing impact rates
- Break condition: If all language pairs have similar sensitivity, or if search pipeline is robust to translation quality across all pairs

  - Quick check question: What does nDCG@16 measure, and why is the rank cutoff at 16 chosen for this experiment?

- Concept: Machine Translation Evaluation Metrics (BLEU, ChrF)
  - Why needed here: BLEU and ChrF are used to quantify translation quality improvements and compute impact rates
  - Quick check question: How do BLEU and ChrF differ, and why might ChrF be more suitable for evaluating short queries?

- Concept: Behavioral Signal Extraction from Logs
  - Why needed here: Click and purchase frequencies are extracted and used as relevance proxies for evaluation
  - Quick check question: How would you handle queries with very low click frequency when computing relevance scores?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Query sampling -> Reference translation creation -> MT translation -> Search retrieval -> Behavioral signal aggregation -> Metric computation
- Critical path: 1) Create test set with sampled queries and behavioral signals 2) Translate queries with baseline and adapted MT 3) Retrieve top-16 products and compute nDCG 4) Calculate impact rates and classify language pairs
- Design tradeoffs: Sample size vs. representativeness; rank cutoff choice; MT metric selection (BLEU vs ChrF)
- Failure signatures: Low impact rates across all pairs; high variance in impact rates; inconsistent ranking between BLEU and ChrF
- First 3 experiments: 1) Reproduce baseline impact rates on small subset 2) Test impact rate sensitivity to rank cutoff 3) Compare impact rates using only click signals vs click+purchase signals

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the threshold for diminishing returns on MT quality improvement vary across different e-commerce domains?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Paper discusses threshold beyond which improving query translation yields little benefit but doesn't explore domain differences
- Why unresolved: Focuses on general e-commerce without segmenting by product category
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of MT improvement impact rates across different product categories

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do seasonal trends and temporal changes in user behavior affect stability of behavioral signals used as relevance proxies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Behavioral signals are dynamic and change according to product life cycle and seasonal trends
- Why unresolved: Proposes using behavioral signals but doesn't investigate temporal fluctuations impact reliability
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal study tracking behavioral signal stability across different seasons

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the framework be extended to evaluate MT quality for non-textual search modalities like voice or image search?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Framework is designed for text-based CLIR but underlying principle could apply to other modalities
- Why unresolved: Focuses exclusively on text-based query translation
- What evidence would resolve it: Adaptation and validation on voice or image-based queries with behavioral signals

## Limitations

- Behavioral signal stability across product lifecycles and seasonal trends is not empirically validated
- Rank cutoff at 16 is arbitrary and framework's sensitivity to different cutoffs is not explored
- Assumes search pipeline components are stable and MT quality is primary variable affecting performance

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Mathematical formulation of MT launching and improvement impact rates
- **Medium Confidence**: Behavioral signals as reliable proxy relevance for evaluation
- **Low Confidence**: Impact rates directly guide MT investment strategies without validation against business outcomes
