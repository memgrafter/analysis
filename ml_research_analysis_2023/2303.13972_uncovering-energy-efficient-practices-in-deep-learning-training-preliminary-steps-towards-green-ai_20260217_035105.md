# Uncovering Energy-Efficient Practices in Deep Learning Training: Preliminary Steps Towards Green AI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.13972
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13972
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Bayesian optimisation reduces training iterations by ~73% while convolutional layer reduction can halve energy use with minimal accuracy loss

## Executive Summary
This study investigates energy-efficient practices in deep learning training by examining how hyperparameter tuning strategies and neural network architectures impact energy consumption. The authors demonstrate that Bayesian optimisation converges to optimal accuracy faster than traditional methods, significantly reducing training iterations. They also show that convolutional layers are the most energy-intensive components, and strategic reduction of these layers can substantially decrease energy usage without sacrificing accuracy. These findings advocate for incorporating energy efficiency considerations alongside accuracy when designing and tuning deep learning models.

## Method Summary
The study trains three neural network architectures (DenseLinearNN, DensePolyNN, SimpleCNN) on FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets using three hyperparameter tuning strategies: grid search, random search, and Bayesian optimisation. Each network-trainer combination is trained for 25 epochs with 8 repetitions per hyperparameter set. Energy consumption is measured via GPU power usage using NVIDIA System Management Interface (nvidia-smi) at 100ms intervals. The Bayesian optimisation employs Gaussian Processes with a PI acquisition function to explore the hyperparameter space of five ADAM parameters (learning rate, betas, epsilon, weight decay).

## Key Results
- Bayesian optimisation achieves optimal accuracy in fewer iterations, reducing training by ~73% compared to grid and random search
- Convolutional layers account for the majority of energy consumption in deep learning training
- Reducing convolutional layer count can decrease energy usage by ~50% with minimal impact on model accuracy
- Energy consumption during training shows low sensitivity to hyperparameter values, being primarily determined by architecture

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Bayesian optimisation achieves faster convergence to optimal accuracy than grid search or random search, reducing training iterations by ~73%.
- Mechanism: Bayesian optimisation builds a probabilistic model of the objective function using Gaussian Processes and uses an acquisition function to select the next most promising hyperparameter set, balancing exploration with exploitation.
- Core assumption: The hyperparameter optimization problem has low effective dimensionality, meaning some parameters significantly influence accuracy while others have minimal impact.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Bayesian optimisation converges to an optimal accuracy faster than grid search or random search, reducing training iterations by ~73%"
  - [section] "Because the function can be approximated by another function g(x1,...,x n−i) with less variables"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus contains related papers on energy-efficient training but no direct Bayesian optimization comparisons
- Break condition: If the objective function is highly noisy or non-stationary, the Gaussian Process model may become unreliable and convergence could degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reducing convolutional layer count can halve energy consumption with minimal accuracy loss.
- Mechanism: Convolutional layers dominate GPU energy usage due to their computational intensity (multiply-accumulate operations across large input tensors). Removing redundant convolutional layers directly reduces the number of these operations.
- Core assumption: Additional convolutional layers beyond a certain point provide diminishing returns in accuracy improvement.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "convolutional layers are the most energy-intensive, and reducing the number can cut energy use by ~50% with minimal loss in accuracy"
  - [section] "We found that convolutional layers are the most computationally expensive by a strong margin"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus papers discuss energy-efficient training but don't specifically analyze convolutional layer energy breakdown
- Break condition: If the task complexity requires deep feature hierarchies, removing convolutional layers may cause significant accuracy degradation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Energy consumption during training is largely independent of hyperparameter settings.
- Mechanism: The primary determinant of energy consumption is the network architecture and the computational operations performed (e.g., number of FLOPs), not the specific hyperparameter values like learning rate or weight decay.
- Core assumption: Different hyperparameter configurations that achieve similar accuracy require similar computational resources.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "we show that the values of the hyperparameters... do not significantly impact the energy consumption"
  - [section] "The coefficient of variance (CV) is a metric that explains the relative size of the standard deviation to the mean"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus doesn't directly address hyperparameter independence from energy consumption
- Break condition: If certain hyperparameters cause training instability requiring many more iterations to converge, energy consumption could increase substantially.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does Bayesian optimization always outperform random and grid search across different datasets and neural network architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that Bayesian optimization consistently finds the highest accuracy within the least number of optimization rounds across various configurations.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested on FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets with specific neural network architectures. Generalization to other datasets and architectures is uncertain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical testing of Bayesian optimization against random and grid search on a wider variety of datasets (e.g., different image sizes, complexities, domains) and neural network architectures (e.g., recurrent networks, transformers).

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of convolutional layers for a given task that balances energy efficiency and accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors observed diminishing returns in accuracy for more energy-hungry models and showed that reducing the number of convolutional layers can halve energy consumption.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested a limited range of convolutional layer configurations (1-4 layers). The optimal number likely depends on the specific task and dataset.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical testing of models with varying numbers of convolutional layers on diverse tasks and datasets to identify the point of diminishing returns for each.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the energy efficiency of hyperparameter tuning strategies change with the dimensionality of the hyperparameter space?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the effectiveness of random search over grid search is due to the low effective dimensionality of hyperparameter optimization problems.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested strategies with up to 5 hyperparameters. The relative performance may change with higher dimensional spaces.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative testing of hyperparameter tuning strategies across a range of hyperparameter space dimensionalities.

## Limitations
- The study lacks details on hyperparameter bounds and distributions for Bayesian optimisation, making exact reproduction difficult
- Energy measurement methodology using nvidia-smi every 100ms may miss short-term power fluctuations
- Corpus evidence for Bayesian optimisation superiority and convolutional layer energy intensity is weak

## Confidence
- Bayesian optimisation convergence claims: Medium confidence - supported by study results but limited external validation
- Convolutional layer energy intensity: Medium confidence - internally consistent results but weak corpus support
- Hyperparameter independence from energy: Low confidence - minimal empirical evidence provided
