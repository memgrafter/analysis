## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Gram iteration, a novel method for computing strict upper bounds on the Lipschitz constants of convolutional layers in neural networks. The approach leverages circulant matrix theory to achieve superlinear convergence and deterministic behavior, contrasting with power iteration-based methods. The method is differentiable, enabling effective Lipschitz regularization during training while maintaining computational efficiency. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Gram iteration outperforms existing state-of-the-art approaches in terms of precision, computational cost, and scalability for large-scale image classification tasks.

## Method Summary
Gram iteration computes the Lipschitz constant of convolutional layers by exploiting the circulant structure of their associated matrices. The method iteratively constructs and analyzes Gram matrices derived from the circulant decomposition of convolution operations. This process yields a deterministic, superlinearly convergent algorithm that provides strict upper bounds on spectral norms. The approach is differentiable, allowing seamless integration into training pipelines for Lipschitz regularization. Unlike power iteration methods that may suffer from slow convergence and stochasticity, Gram iteration offers both precision and speed advantages while maintaining mathematical rigor through circulant matrix theory.

## Key Results
- Achieves superlinear convergence for Lipschitz constant estimation compared to power iteration's linear convergence
- Demonstrates 2-3× speedup over state-of-the-art methods while providing stricter bounds
- Shows improved training stability and accuracy when used for Lipschitz regularization in image classification tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method exploits the inherent circulant structure of convolutional layer weight matrices. By decomposing convolutions into circulant matrices, Gram iteration can leverage the spectral properties of these structured matrices to compute bounds more efficiently. The Gram matrix construction captures the essential spectral information needed for Lipschitz constant estimation while maintaining computational tractability. This circulant-based approach enables the superlinear convergence behavior that distinguishes it from iterative power methods.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:** Input tensor -> Gram matrix construction -> Iterative spectral bound computation -> Output Lipschitz constant

**Critical Path:** The core algorithm flow follows: (1) Convert convolution weights to circulant form, (2) Construct Gram matrix, (3) Apply iterative refinement, (4) Extract spectral bound. Each iteration provides a tighter bound until convergence criteria are met.

**Design Tradeoffs:** Gram iteration trades implementation complexity for convergence speed and bound precision. While more complex to implement than power iteration, it provides deterministic results and superlinear convergence. The circulant matrix representation requires additional memory overhead but enables faster computation of tighter bounds.

**Failure Signatures:** Convergence failure may indicate ill-conditioned weight matrices or numerical instability in Gram matrix construction. Slow convergence suggests the circulant approximation may not capture the full spectral characteristics of the convolution. Divergence typically indicates implementation errors in the iterative refinement step.
