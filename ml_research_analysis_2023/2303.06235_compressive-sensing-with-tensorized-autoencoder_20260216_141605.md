# Compressive Sensing with Tensorized Autoencoder

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.06235
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06235
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Achieves 31.71 dB PSNR for denoising and 35.29 dB for inpainting on Small NORB dataset, outperforming Deep Decoder (27.40 dB and 35.10 dB respectively)

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a self-supervised tensorized autoencoder for compressive sensing problems that learns to reconstruct images from corrupted measurements. The method uses a tensor ring decomposition to impose structural constraints on the latent space based on known dataset attributes, enabling superior reconstruction quality compared to other generative prior-based approaches. Experiments on structured datasets (Small NORB, RaFD, 3dShapes) demonstrate significant improvements in both denoising and inpainting tasks, with the approach achieving PSNR values of 31.71 dB for denoising and 35.29 dB for inpainting on Small NORB compared to 27.40 dB and 35.10 dB for Deep Decoder respectively.

## Method Summary
The method learns a deterministic autoencoder that maps corrupted measurements to a latent space, which is then factorized using tensor ring decomposition to capture attribute relationships in the dataset. The encoder maps measurements to latent codes, which are represented as a low-rank tensor where different factors correspond to different image attributes (e.g., lighting, pose, shape). A decoder then maps these factorized latent codes back to the image space. The training uses a three-term loss function that balances encoder-latent matching, encoder-decoder measurement matching, and factorization-decoder measurement matching, with tensor ring cores optimized using SGD while network parameters use Adam optimization.

## Key Results
- Achieved 31.71 dB PSNR for denoising on Small NORB dataset versus 27.40 dB for Deep Decoder
- Achieved 35.29 dB PSNR for inpainting on Small NORB dataset versus 35.10 dB for Deep Decoder
- Demonstrated consistent improvements across multiple structured datasets (Small NORB, RaFD, 3dShapes) for both denoising and inpainting tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The tensor ring factorization imposes a structured low-rank constraint on the latent space that captures attribute relationships.
- Mechanism: By representing latent codes as tensor ring decomposition, the model enforces that variations across different attributes are encoded through shared low-dimensional cores, limiting degrees of freedom and exploiting dataset structure.
- Core assumption: The dataset exhibits multi-attribute structure where each attribute has discrete variants and these attributes are independent enough to be factorized.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We represent the latent codes as a low-rank tensor, where different factors represent different image articulations." [section] "We denote the N latent codes of our entire target set as {z1,...,z N}. For a structured dataset, these latent codes can be factored into K different attributes, each of which has Ik variants..."
- Break condition: If the dataset does not have clear, discrete attribute variations, or if attributes are highly correlated such that factorization loses information.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The autoencoder learns an embedding space that maps corrupted measurements to a structured latent space, which is then factorized.
- Mechanism: The encoder learns to map corrupted measurements to a latent space that preserves structural information. The tensor ring factorization then imposes additional constraints, ensuring that the latent space adheres to the dataset's attribute structure.
- Core assumption: The encoder can learn a meaningful embedding space that captures essential features of images, even from corrupted measurements.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We learn an encoder that maps every measurement to a latent space. We represent the latent codes as a low-rank tensor..." [section] "We learn a deterministic autoencoder by solving the following constrained optimization problem..."
- Break condition: If the encoder fails to learn a meaningful embedding, or if corruption is too severe for the encoder to recover useful information.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The combined loss function ensures both encoder and tensor factorization produce high-quality reconstructions.
- Mechanism: The loss function has three terms: (1) mismatch between encoder output and factorization, (2) encoder-decoder measurement loss, and (3) factorization-decoder measurement loss. This ensures both encoder and tensor factorization contribute to good reconstruction quality.
- Core assumption: The three-term loss function effectively balances trade-off between preserving encoder output and enforcing tensor factorization structure.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We modify the optimization problem in (2) to use the following loss function: Loss = N∑i=1∥E(yi;θ)− Z(i1,...i K)∥2 +λ1∥AiD(E(yi;θ);γ)−yi∥2 +λ2∥AiD(Z(i1,...i K);γ)−yi∥2." [abstract] "The resulting method achieves better reconstruction quality compared to other generative prior-based self-supervised recovery approaches for compressive sensing."
- Break condition: If weighting of loss terms is not optimal, leading to either poor reconstruction or loss of structural information.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input measurements → Encoder → Tensor Ring Factorization → Decoder → Reconstructed images
- Critical path: Encoder → Tensor Ring Factorization → Decoder
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Rank selection: Higher rank allows more expressive factorization but increases computational cost.
  - Loss function weighting: Balancing three loss terms is crucial for optimal performance.
  - Latent space dimensionality: Must be sufficient to capture image features but not too large to hinder factorization.
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor reconstruction quality: May indicate issues with encoder, decoder, or tensor factorization.
  - Overfitting: If model performs well on training data but poorly on test data.
  - Mode collapse: If tensor factorization fails to capture all attribute variations.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Denoising on Small NORB dataset: Test model's ability to recover images from Gaussian noise.
  2. Inpainting on RaFD dataset: Test model's ability to recover missing regions in images.
  3. Ablation study: Remove tensor factorization and compare performance to assess its impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does choice of tensor ring rank affect reconstruction quality and computational efficiency of proposed method?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "We empirically selected the lowest ranks for each dataset that provide good performance. We reported results with rank=25 for Small NORB, 30 for RaFD and 15 for 3dShapes."
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide systematic analysis of how different rank choices impact trade-off between reconstruction quality and computational cost. It also does not explore sensitivity of method to rank selection.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive study varying tensor ring rank across range of values for each dataset, reporting both reconstruction quality (PSNR) and computational metrics (training/inference time, memory usage) to identify optimal rank values.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can proposed tensorized autoencoder be extended to handle more complex attribute structures beyond simple K-attribute factorization used in paper?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on datasets with known attribute variations (Small NORB, RaFD, 3dShapes) where images can be factorized into K different attributes. However, real-world data may have more complex and hierarchical attribute structures.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore how method would perform on datasets with more intricate attribute relationships or how tensor ring factorization could be adapted to capture such structures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying method to datasets with complex attribute hierarchies (e.g., multi-level attributes, non-linear attribute relationships) and comparing performance with alternative factorization methods designed for such structures.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art generative model-based approaches for compressive sensing, such as those using diffusion models or score-based generative models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares its method to CSAE, CSGM, LSTR, and Deep Decoder, but does not include more recent generative model approaches.
- Why unresolved: Field of generative models for compressive sensing has advanced rapidly, and newer methods based on diffusion models or score-based approaches may offer superior performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarking proposed method against state-of-the-art generative model approaches on same datasets and tasks, using both quantitative metrics (PSNR, SSIM) and qualitative comparisons of reconstructed images.

## Limitations
- Method fundamentally relies on datasets having clear, discrete attribute structures and may degrade on datasets without well-defined attribute variations.
- Implementation details of tensor ring factorization layer are not fully specified, affecting reproducibility.
- Requires soft attribute labels, limiting applicability to datasets where such metadata is unavailable.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The mechanism of using tensor ring factorization to impose structured low-rank constraints on latent space is theoretically sound and supported by mathematical formulation.
- **Medium confidence**: Experimental results showing improved PSNR over baselines are promising, but lack of citations for specific loss function formulation and tensor ring application in compressive sensing creates uncertainty about novelty.
- **Low confidence**: Claim that this approach generalizes well to datasets without clear attribute structures is not well-supported, as method fundamentally depends on this assumption.
