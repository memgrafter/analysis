# FP8 versus INT8 for efficient deep learning inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.17951
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17951
- Reference count: 31
- This paper compares the efficiency and accuracy of FP8 and INT8 number formats for deep learning inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper compares the efficiency and accuracy of FP8 and INT8 number formats for deep learning inference. The authors theoretically analyze the difference between the formats, showing that INT8 is more efficient in hardware (50-180% less compute) and often more accurate for well-behaved distributions. Extensive experiments on various models and tasks (classification, detection, segmentation, NLP) demonstrate that INT8 typically achieves similar or better accuracy than FP8, while requiring less hardware resources. The authors conclude that INT8 is the preferred format for efficient inference, with tools like AIMET enabling easy quantization.

## Method Summary
The authors compare INT8 and FP8 formats through both post-training quantization (PTQ) and quantization-aware training (QAT) experiments across various model architectures and tasks. They analyze hardware efficiency differences by examining gate counts for different multiply-accumulate implementations, and study accuracy differences through theoretical analysis of number format representations and empirical experiments on models like ResNet18, YoloV5, HRNet, and BERT. The experiments are conducted using the provided GitHub repository with various datasets including ImageNet, COCO, and GLUE benchmark.

## Key Results
- INT8 provides 50-180% better hardware efficiency than FP8 due to simpler integer multiply-accumulate units
- For well-behaved weight/activation distributions, INT8 typically achieves similar or better accuracy than FP8-E4/E5
- QAT can recover accuracy for most networks and sometimes even improve it when converting between formats

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: INT8 is more hardware efficient than FP8 for matrix multiply-accumulate operations
- Mechanism: Integer multiply-accumulate units require fewer logic gates than floating-point units due to simpler accumulation logic
- Core assumption: Hardware implementation cost scales with gate count
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "From this bar-plot, we can deduce the following. For INT8 all the way up to FP8-E3, the fixed-precision accumulator is the most efficient. For FP8-E4 the floating-point and fixed-point accumulation almost break even. Most significantly, comparing INT8 with FP8-E4, there is a 53% increased cost for the floating-point format."
  - [section]: "The FP8-E4 format with an FP32 accumulator is 183% less efficient."
  - [corpus]: No direct hardware cost comparisons found in corpus papers, though Rouhaniet al. (2023) is cited for corroborating 40% performance decrease of FP8 vs INT8
- Break condition: If custom floating-point accumulator designs achieve parity with integer implementations, or if accumulator size requirements differ significantly between formats

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: INT8 provides better accuracy than FP8-E4/E5 for well-behaved distributions
- Mechanism: INT8 with flexible scaling can represent normally distributed weights/activations more accurately than FP8 formats with exponent bits
- Core assumption: Neural network weights and activations follow approximately Gaussian distributions
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "In Figure 5, we can see that the INT format is the best for a uniform distribution. For the Gaussian distribution in the middle column, FP8-E2 is the best, with INT8 as a very close second."
  - [section]: "This is important to note since many weights and activations in neural networks are well-regularized, either explicitly due to weight regularization or implicitly because of SGD"
  - [corpus]: Corpus papers focus on LLM quantization where outlier behavior dominates, providing limited direct evidence for well-behaved distribution case
- Break condition: If network distributions contain significant outliers that cannot be regularized away, or if alternative number formats better match the actual distribution

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Networks do not adapt their weight/activation distributions to match quantization format
- Mechanism: Training parameters (regularization, optimizer, initialization) determine distribution shapes rather than quantization format itself
- Core assumption: Weight/activation distributions are primarily shaped by training hyperparameters
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We can look at some results from what happens when we convert two FP8-E4 QAT-trained models back to INT8... the accuracy for a network like ResNet18 or MobileNetV2 stays roughly the same or even slightly improves when converting the model from FP8-E4 to INT8."
  - [section]: "Distributions of the weights of ResNet18, training from scratch in FP32 (blue) and FP8-E4 (orange)... the distributions barely differ."
  - [corpus]: Limited evidence in corpus papers about distribution adaptation during quantization-aware training
- Break condition: If training from scratch in different formats produces systematically different distributions, or if fine-tuning procedures specifically adapt to format characteristics

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Under what specific conditions (e.g., outlier levels, weight distribution) does FP8-E4 outperform INT8 in terms of accuracy for deep learning inference?
- Basis in paper: The paper explicitly states that FP8-E4 performs better than INT8 when there are significant outliers in the data distribution, particularly in transformer models.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides a general explanation (outliers) but doesn't specify quantitative thresholds or distributions where FP8-E4 consistently outperforms INT8.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying outlier levels systematically across different model architectures and distributions, with quantified accuracy differences between FP8-E4 and INT8.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the accuracy differences between FP8 and INT8 formats vary when considering different hardware constraints (e.g., memory bandwidth vs. compute-bound scenarios)?
- Basis in paper: The paper discusses hardware efficiency differences between FP8 and INT8, noting that FP8 is less efficient in terms of compute (50-180% less efficient) and that the choice between formats may depend on whether the network is compute-bound or memory-bound.
- Why unresolved: The paper analyzes hardware efficiency theoretically but doesn't provide experimental data on how these differences affect accuracy in practice under different hardware constraints.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring accuracy and performance of FP8 vs. INT8 models on hardware with varying memory bandwidth and compute capabilities, correlating these results with theoretical predictions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can quantization-aware training (QAT) consistently bridge the accuracy gap between FP8 and INT8 for all model types, or are there inherent limitations?
- Basis in paper: The paper shows that QAT can recover accuracy for most networks and sometimes even improve it, but notes that for transformers with outliers, INT8 still performs better than FP8-E4 after QAT.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates QAT's effectiveness, it doesn't explore the full extent of its capabilities or limitations across all possible model types and distributions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensive QAT experiments on a wide range of model architectures, including those with various levels of outliers and different weight distributions, comparing final accuracies of FP8 and INT8 models.

## Limitations
- Hardware efficiency claims rely on gate-count estimates from Rouhaniet al. (2023) without direct measurement data
- Accuracy comparisons assume well-behaved distributions, but FP8 may be more beneficial for outlier-heavy models like LLMs
- Distribution adaptation claim lacks systematic evidence from training identical architectures from scratch in different formats

## Confidence
- High: Hardware efficiency advantage of INT8 over FP8 (mechanistic claim supported by multiple evidence anchors)
- Medium: INT8 accuracy advantage for well-behaved distributions (supported by simulation data but limited empirical breadth)
- Low: No distribution adaptation during training (single model comparisons insufficient for general claim)
