# Zambezi Voice: A Multilingual Speech Corpus for Zambian Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04428
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04428
- Reference count: 0
- Over 80 hours of labeled speech data for four Zambian languages with baseline WER of 22.45% to 46.64%

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Zambezi Voice, a multilingual speech corpus for Zambian languages containing over 80 hours of labeled speech data for four languages (Bemba, Nyanja, Tonga, Lozi) and over 160 hours of unlabelled radio broadcast recordings. The authors present baseline experiments using Wav2Vec2.0-based models, achieving word error rates ranging from 22.45% to 46.64% after fine-tuning multilingual pre-trained models. The corpus is released under a Creative Commons license and aims to support development of speech recognition systems for under-resourced Zambian languages.

## Method Summary
The Zambezi Voice corpus contains ~80 hours of read speech across four Zambian languages and 168 hours of segmented radio broadcast recordings. The authors trained baseline models using Fairseq S2T with a small Transformer base architecture (71M parameters) and fine-tuned the XLS-R-0.3B (300M parameters) model using HuggingFace Transformers. Training used CTC beam search decoder with 5-gram language models, with configurations of 5 epochs, learning rate 3e-4, and early stopping. Models were evaluated using word error rate on test sets.

## Key Results
- Zambezi Voice corpus contains 80+ hours of labeled speech data for four Zambian languages
- Fine-tuning XLS-R-0.3B achieved WER ranging from 22.45% to 46.64% across languages
- Training from scratch yielded poor results (~90% WER) on low-resource languages like Tonga and Lozi
- Multilingual fine-tuning outperformed monolingual fine-tuning in most cases

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach leverages transfer learning from a large multilingual pre-trained model (XLS-R) to overcome the limited labeled data available for Zambian languages. By fine-tuning on these under-resourced languages rather than training from scratch, the model benefits from knowledge learned from related languages and acoustic patterns. The inclusion of both read speech and radio broadcast data provides complementary training signals - read speech offers clean, controlled utterances while radio broadcasts capture more natural, conversational speech patterns.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Raw audio -> Feature extraction -> Wav2Vec2.0 backbone -> Transformer layers -> CTC output -> Beam search decoder -> LM rescoring

**Critical Path**: Data preprocessing → Model fine-tuning → Evaluation pipeline
The critical path follows standard speech recognition workflow: first prepare and normalize data, then fine-tune pre-trained model on each language (or multilingual), and finally evaluate using WER metric with beam search and language model.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The choice between training from scratch vs fine-tuning pre-trained models represents a fundamental tradeoff between model capacity and data efficiency. Training from scratch requires more data but may better capture language-specific patterns, while fine-tuning trades some specificity for rapid convergence with limited data. The authors chose fine-tuning given the data constraints.

**Failure Signatures**: High WER (~90%) when training from scratch indicates insufficient data for the model to learn language-specific patterns. Overfitting manifests as validation loss increasing while training loss decreases. Poor performance on certain languages suggests either data quality issues or insufficient representation in the pre-training corpus.

## Limitations
- Labeled data remains limited for truly robust speech recognition, particularly for Tonga (22 hours) and Lozi (6 hours)
- Paper lacks detailed error analysis to identify specific linguistic or acoustic challenges in these languages
- Practical utility for real-world applications in Zambia is not evaluated
- Preprocessing pipeline is minimally described, affecting reproducibility for Zambian language-specific characters

## Confidence

**High confidence**: Corpus release and basic statistics are verifiable and well-documented. Methodology follows standard practices.

**Medium confidence**: WER results are plausible given data constraints, with fine-tuning showing expected advantages over training from scratch.

**Low confidence**: Specific contributions to downstream tasks beyond speech recognition are not demonstrated, and real-world application utility is unevaluated.
