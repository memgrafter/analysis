# Direct Fact Retrieval from Knowledge Graphs without Entity Linking

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12416
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12416
- Reference count: 40
- The paper proposes DiFaR, a direct fact retrieval framework that retrieves relevant triplets from knowledge graphs without entity linking.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces DiFaR, a framework for retrieving relevant triplets from knowledge graphs without relying on entity linking. Unlike conventional pipeline methods that require entity detection, disambiguation, and relation classification, DiFaR directly embeds both facts and queries into a shared representation space and retrieves nearest neighbors based on semantic similarity. The approach leverages a language model trained with contrastive loss on query-fact pairs and introduces a reranking strategy to refine top-k results using a joint encoder. Experiments on QA and dialogue datasets show significant improvements over pipeline-based baselines, especially when reranking is applied. The method is also shown to generalize across different knowledge graphs and handle both single- and multi-hop queries effectively.

## Method Summary
DiFaR is a direct fact retrieval framework that bypasses the conventional entity linking pipeline by encoding both queries and knowledge graph facts into a shared dense embedding space using a language model trained with contrastive loss. During inference, it retrieves the top-k most similar facts via FAISS approximate nearest neighbor search. A reranker then refines these results by jointly encoding each candidate fact with the query using a cross-encoder trained on confusing samples. The framework requires only text-triplet pairs for training, making it label-efficient and generalizable to new entities and relations.

## Key Results
- DiFaR significantly outperforms pipeline-based baselines on QA and dialogue datasets.
- Reranking improves retrieval accuracy, especially for single-hop queries.
- The method generalizes well to different knowledge graphs and handles both single- and multi-hop queries.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Direct retrieval works because the triplet can be encoded into a dense vector that captures enough semantic information to match input queries without entity linking.
- Mechanism: A language model (LM) encodes both the input query and the knowledge graph fact (as a token sequence) into a shared embedding space. During training, contrastive loss pulls together embeddings of relevant query-fact pairs and pushes apart irrelevant pairs, so nearest neighbor search in this space retrieves the correct fact.
- Core assumption: The LM can effectively embed the short triplet form into a useful semantic representation, even though it has little context compared to full documents.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we first embed all facts in KGs onto a dense embedding space by using a language model trained by only pairs of input texts and facts"
  - [section 3.2] "we simply forward the triplet tokens to Eθ to obtain the triplet representation"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; only one related paper mentions "large-scale knowledge graphs" but not dense retrieval of short triplets.
- Break condition: If the LM cannot capture sufficient semantic information from the sparse triplet representation, retrieval accuracy will degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reranking improves accuracy by jointly encoding the query and candidate fact, allowing token-level matching that the independent embeddings cannot capture.
- Mechanism: After retrieving the top-k facts using the dense retriever, a second LM encodes the concatenated query and fact sequence to produce a similarity score. This score reorders the top-k list, prioritizing facts that align better at the token level.
- Core assumption: Joint encoding can detect subtle contextual relationships missed by separate encoding plus cosine similarity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose to further refine ranks of top-k retrieved facts with a reranker that contextualizes the input text and the fact jointly"
  - [section 3.3] "we use another LM, Eφ, that returns the similarity score of the pair of the input text and the retrieved triplet by encoding them simultaneously"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; assumption is based on LM design intuition.
- Break condition: If the reranker is not trained on confusing samples (close but wrong facts), it will not improve over the dense retriever.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training without entity linking labels makes the system generalizable to new entities and relations not seen during training.
- Mechanism: The model learns to map query text directly to fact embeddings based on semantic similarity, bypassing the need for entity span detection, disambiguation, and relation classification modules that require labeled entities.
- Core assumption: Semantic similarity between query and fact is sufficient to identify relevant facts without explicitly identifying the entities in the query.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This process requires only text-triplet pairs without using extra labels, unlike the conventional pipeline approach for fact retrieval"
  - [section 3.2] "our objective is simply formulated with the single sentence encoder model Eθ without introducing extra variables (e.g., m and e)"
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; claim is based on architecture design.
- Break condition: If the semantic similarity space is too coarse, the model will fail to distinguish facts involving similar but different entities.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Contrastive learning with positive and negative pairs
  - Why needed here: To shape the embedding space so that relevant query-fact pairs are close and irrelevant pairs are far apart.
  - Quick check question: In the contrastive loss, what happens to the similarity between a query and a negative fact?
- Concept: Nearest neighbor search in high-dimensional dense spaces
  - Why needed here: To efficiently retrieve the top-k facts for a given query embedding without exhaustive search.
  - Quick check question: Why is FAISS used instead of a naive linear scan over all facts?
- Concept: Reranking with cross-encoder models
  - Why needed here: To refine the top-k results by jointly considering query and fact context, improving precision.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between the retriever encoder and the reranker encoder in terms of input handling?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Retriever encoder (DistilBERT-base) → Embedding of query and fact → FAISS index → Efficient storage and search of fact embeddings → Reranker encoder (MiniLM) → Joint encoding of query and candidate fact → Training loop → Contrastive loss for retriever, binary cross-entropy for reranker
- Critical path: Query → Retriever → FAISS search → Top-k facts → Reranker → Final ranked list
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Retriever simplicity vs. reranker accuracy
  - Dense retrieval speed vs. potential recall loss vs. exact sparse matching
  - Training data efficiency (no entity labels) vs. potential lower accuracy on very ambiguous queries
- Failure signatures:
  - Low MRR/Hits@1 but high Hits@10: Retriever embeddings too coarse
  - Reranker adds little improvement: Negative samples not confusing enough
  - Training diverges: Learning rate too high or batch size too small
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train retriever only on a small subset, evaluate MRR on dev set.
  2. Add reranker, compare Hits@1 with and without reranking.
  3. Test zero-shot transfer to a new KG, measure performance drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does the performance of DiFaR compare to pipeline approaches when handling multi-hop queries that require traversing multiple relations in the knowledge graph?
- How does the choice of language model architecture (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa, T5) impact the performance of DiFaR in fact retrieval tasks?
- Can DiFaR be effectively extended to handle multilingual fact retrieval tasks, and if so, what are the challenges and limitations of such an extension?

## Limitations
- The paper's claim of improved generalizability across KGs relies on semantic similarity matching, but this assumes the language model can robustly encode the semantic differences between entities and relations, especially in cases of highly ambiguous or rare entities.
- The zero-shot transfer evaluation is encouraging but lacks direct comparison to strong baselines that also avoid entity linking.
- The reranking mechanism's effectiveness depends heavily on the quality of negative samples, but the method for constructing these "confusing" samples is not fully detailed, leaving potential reproducibility gaps.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism of using contrastive learning to embed facts and queries into a shared semantic space is well-supported by the architecture and training details.
- **Medium Confidence**: The reported performance gains over pipeline approaches are credible, but the extent of improvement depends on dataset specifics and the exact nature of the baselines.
- **Low Confidence**: The generalizability claims are plausible but not rigorously validated across diverse KGs and tasks beyond the scope of the paper.

## Next Checks
1. **Negative Sample Quality Audit**: Examine the distribution and diversity of negative samples used in reranking training; test reranking performance with varying levels of sample difficulty to ensure the model is learning to distinguish truly confusing cases.
2. **Cross-KG Generalization Test**: Apply the trained model to a KG structurally different from Wikidata and Freebase (e.g., YAGO or DBpedia) and measure retrieval performance drop; analyze if the embedding space degrades or if the issue is entity/relation vocabulary mismatch.
3. **Multi-hop Query Breakdown**: Manually inspect model outputs on multi-hop queries to identify where failures occur (e.g., missing intermediate facts, incorrect entity resolution in the semantic space) and correlate with MRR degradation to guide model refinement.
