# FOOCTTS: Generating Arabic Speech with Acoustic Environment for Football Commentator

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07936
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07936
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: High-quality Arabic football commentary speech generation with crowd noise using only 15 minutes of training data through unsupervised ASR-based labeling

## Executive Summary
The FOOCTTS system automatically generates football commentary speech in Arabic with background crowd noise using minimal domain-specific recordings. The authors collected 15 minutes of Tunisian commentator audio from YouTube and employed an unsupervised pipeline involving Arabic ASR transcription, CTC segmentation, and vowelization to create training data. By fine-tuning a VITS TTS model pretrained on broadcast news, the system produces commentary that is indistinguishable from human speech. The approach demonstrates that unsupervised data labeling combined with transfer learning enables high-quality speech synthesis even with severely limited training data.

## Method Summary
The FOOCTTS pipeline processes 15 minutes of Tunisian football commentary audio through voice activity detection, automatic Arabic speech recognition for transcription, and CTC segmentation for alignment. The transcriptions undergo vowelization using Farasa to match spoken forms. The system employs transfer learning by first pretraining VITS on 1 hour of broadcast news from the QASR corpus, then fine-tuning on the processed football commentary dataset. The resulting TTS model generates commentary speech with crowd noise based on user text input, requiring no manual transcription or extensive domain-specific recordings.

## Key Results
- Generated commentary speech achieves high quality and is indistinguishable from human speech despite only 15 minutes of training data
- Unsupervised data labeling via Arabic ASR enables system development without manual transcription
- Transfer learning from broadcast news domain to football commentary domain enables effective adaptation with minimal target domain data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Unsupervised data labeling via ASR enables high-quality speech synthesis with minimal domain recordings.
- Mechanism: The system uses a large pre-trained Arabic ASR model to automatically transcribe football commentary audio, which is then matched to text via CTC segmentation and vowelization. This eliminates the need for manual transcription, allowing the system to build a training corpus from only 15 minutes of commentary.
- Core assumption: The ASR model's transcriptions are accurate enough to serve as labels for TTS training, despite dialectal variations and noise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our pipeline included Arabic automatic speech recognition for data labeling, CTC segmentation, transcription vowelization to match speech..."
  - [section]: "We use a large pre-trained code-switching Arabic ASR model to transcribe the speech samples... Our ASR model was able to transcribe the French words..."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence on ASR accuracy for Tunisian dialect; corpus only shows related papers, not ASR performance metrics.
- Break condition: ASR transcription errors exceeding a threshold that degrades TTS pronunciation quality beyond usability.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Transfer learning from broadcast news to football commentary enables high-quality synthesis with limited target domain data.
- Mechanism: The system first pre-trains VITS on 1 hour of broadcast news (QASR corpus), then fine-tunes on the small football commentary dataset. The pre-training provides general Arabic speech patterns, while fine-tuning adapts to the specific speaking style and acoustic environment of commentary.
- Core assumption: Acoustic and prosodic features from broadcast news transfer sufficiently to football commentary, despite different domains.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our approach is fully unsupervised... We use transfer learning broadcast news domain and finetune it for the football commentating domain."
  - [section]: "For baseline model, we start with previous work in [4] and fine-tune it with 15 minutes of Tunisian commentator."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence on transfer learning effectiveness; corpus shows related papers but no performance comparison.
- Break condition: Insufficient overlap in acoustic characteristics between domains causes the fine-tuned model to produce unnatural speech patterns.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Vowelization matching spoken form to text enables accurate pronunciation in Arabic TTS.
- Mechanism: The system uses Farasa for diacritization, but acknowledges the challenge that vowelization should reflect how words are actually spoken rather than grammatical rules. This preprocessing step ensures the TTS model receives text that matches the phonetic content of the audio.
- Core assumption: Farasa's diacritization can be adjusted or guided to match spoken forms in the football commentary domain.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "In FOOCTTS, we employ the diacritizer provided by Farasa... One of the main issues is that vowelization is applied based on grammatical rules whereas if we want to perfect our output we should have the text vowelized according to how it was spoken."
  - [abstract]: "Our pipeline included... transcription vowelization to match speech..."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence on vowelization accuracy or its impact on TTS quality.
- Break condition: Grammatical vowelization that conflicts with actual pronunciation causes systematic mispronunciation errors in the generated speech.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: YouTube audio extraction -> VAD preprocessing -> Arabic ASR transcription -> CTC segmentation -> Farasa vowelization -> VITS fine-tuning -> Flask web application -> Generated audio output

- Critical path: User input → Farasa vowelization → TTS inference → Audio output
  The path from user text to generated speech is the most time-critical component.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Unsupervised labeling vs. manual transcription: Saves time but depends on ASR quality
  - Broadcast news pre-training vs. direct training on commentary: Enables better results with limited data but requires careful fine-tuning
  - Web-based Farasa integration vs. local processing: Simpler deployment but adds network dependency

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor ASR transcription → mismatched audio-text pairs → degraded TTS pronunciation
  - Insufficient fine-tuning → speech sounds like broadcast news, not commentary
  - Vowelization mismatch → incorrect pronunciation of words despite correct text

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test ASR transcription accuracy on 5-minute commentary sample vs. manual transcription
  2. Compare TTS output quality with and without broadcast news pre-training using AB testing
  3. Evaluate pronunciation accuracy with different vowelization approaches (grammatical vs. adjusted)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can the unsupervised data labeling approach generalize to other low-resource languages and dialects beyond Arabic?
- What is the impact of including crowd noise in the training data on the quality of the generated speech?
- How does the limited 15-minute training data affect the model's ability to generalize to new vocabulary and speaking styles?

## Limitations
- ASR transcription accuracy for Tunisian dialect is not empirically validated
- Vowelization uses grammatical rules rather than phonetic matching, potentially causing pronunciation errors
- Crowd noise generation capability is claimed but not separately evaluated

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The technical pipeline architecture (ASR → CTC segmentation → vowelization → VITS fine-tuning) is feasible and follows established approaches in speech processing.
- **Medium Confidence**: The unsupervised labeling approach can work given a sufficiently accurate ASR model, but ASR accuracy for Tunisian dialect is unverified.
- **Medium Confidence**: Transfer learning from broadcast news to football commentary is plausible given shared acoustic characteristics, but effectiveness depends on fine-tuning quality.
- **Low Confidence**: The claim that generated speech is "indistinguishable from human speech" without user studies or objective quality metrics.
