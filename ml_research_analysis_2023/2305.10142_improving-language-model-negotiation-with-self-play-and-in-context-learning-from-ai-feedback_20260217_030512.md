# Improving Language Model Negotiation with Self-Play and In-Context Learning from AI Feedback

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10142
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10142
- Reference count: 34
- Key outcome: Strong LLMs (GPT-4, Claude-v1.3) improve negotiation performance through self-play and AI feedback, achieving better prices at the cost of higher deal failure rates

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can autonomously improve their negotiation strategies through self-play and AI feedback. The authors set up a bargaining game where two models negotiate as buyer and seller, with a third model providing feedback to improve one player's strategy. Using in-context learning, they incorporate feedback from previous rounds to iteratively refine negotiation strategies. The key finding is that only strong and well-aligned models can continuously improve their negotiation performance over multiple rounds, achieving better prices at the cost of higher deal failure rates. The paper demonstrates that AI feedback can be comparable to human feedback in improving model negotiation skills, opening possibilities for autonomous model improvement with minimal human intervention.

## Method Summary
The method involves three LLM agents (seller, buyer, critic) negotiating a balloon price ($10-$20 range) through multiple rounds. After each negotiation, a critic model provides three suggestions to improve the seller's strategy. Previous negotiation history and AI feedback are concatenated as in-context demonstrations for the next round, enabling iterative strategy refinement. The study tests various model combinations (GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4, Claude-v1.3, Claude-instant-v1.0, Cohere-command, Jurassic-2) to evaluate improvement potential and role-specific learning dynamics.

## Key Results
- Strong models (GPT-4, Claude-v1.3) continuously improve negotiation performance through multiple rounds
- Weaker or misaligned models (Cohere-command, Jurassic-2) fail to understand game rules or improve from feedback
- Role-specific learning dynamics emerge: sellers improve more easily than buyers, with buyers being harder to improve
- AI feedback can achieve comparable results to human feedback in improving negotiation strategies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: AI feedback in natural language serves as effective in-context demonstrations for iterative improvement
- Mechanism: The critic model provides structured feedback (3 suggestions) that is concatenated with previous dialog history to form few-shot prompts for the next round
- Core assumption: LLMs can parse natural language feedback and incorporate it into subsequent responses through in-context learning mechanisms similar to gradient descent
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We let the two agents play multiple rounds, using previous negotiation history and AI feedback as in-context demonstrations to improve the model's negotiation strategy iteratively"
  - [section]: "By doing this, the critic's suggestions for improvements and the player's actual improvement in the previous rounds effectively become the few-shot prompts for the next round of negotiation"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for natural language feedback effectiveness in this specific negotiation game setting
- Break condition: If the LLM cannot parse natural language feedback or fails to connect feedback to actionable strategy changes, improvement will not occur

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Competitive self-play creates measurable performance gradients that drive improvement
- Mechanism: Two agents negotiate with opposing objectives (seller wants higher price, buyer wants lower price), creating a measurable outcome (deal price) that serves as a performance signal for iterative refinement
- Core assumption: The negotiation game has well-defined rules and clear performance metrics that enable meaningful comparison across rounds
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "They aim to reach a deal with the buyer targeting a lower price and the seller a higher one"
  - [section]: "We use the deal price as the evaluation metric"
  - [corpus]: Moderate evidence - related work on "SPIRAL: Self-Play on Zero-Sum Games" shows self-play can incentivize reasoning in competitive settings
- Break condition: If performance metrics become saturated or if models reach equilibrium where further improvement is impossible

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Role-specific learning dynamics emerge where sellers improve more easily than buyers
- Mechanism: The asymmetric nature of negotiation roles creates different learning challenges, with sellers finding it easier to improve from feedback than buyers
- Core assumption: The psychological and strategic demands of selling versus buying create different cognitive loads for the LLM
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Models' abilities to learn from the feedback differ when playing different roles. For example, it is harder for Claude-instant to improve as the buyer than as the seller"
  - [section]: "Buyers are harder to improve than sellers: AI feedback does not quite move buyers' deal price distribution"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct corpus support for role-specific learning asymmetries in LLM negotiation
- Break condition: If role-specific dynamics disappear with stronger models or if training procedures eliminate these asymmetries

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: In-context learning through few-shot prompting
  - Why needed here: The system relies on previous rounds of negotiation history and AI feedback being concatenated as demonstrations for the next round
  - Quick check question: Can you explain how concatenating previous dialog history with feedback creates an effective few-shot prompt?

- Concept: Game theory and zero-sum competitive dynamics
  - Why needed here: The negotiation game is inherently competitive with opposing objectives, requiring understanding of strategic interactions
  - Quick check question: What game-theoretic principle explains why sellers generally have an easier time improving than buyers in this setup?

- Concept: Reinforcement learning without explicit reward signals
  - Why needed here: The system achieves improvement through iterative feedback rather than traditional reward maximization
  - Quick check question: How does natural language feedback serve as an implicit reward signal compared to scalar rewards in traditional RL?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Seller → Buyer → Moderator → Critic → Seller improvement loop
- Critical path: Seller → Buyer → Moderator (state classification) → Critic (feedback generation) → Seller (strategy refinement) → Next round
- Design tradeoffs: Using in-context learning avoids expensive fine-tuning but limits adaptation scope; natural language feedback is more interpretable but potentially less precise than scalar rewards
- Failure signatures: Models failing to understand game rules, inability to incorporate feedback, improvement plateauing after 1-3 rounds, decreasing deal success rate as price optimization continues

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the iterative AI feedback process scale when applied to more complex negotiation scenarios or larger language models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the models were tested in a relatively simple bargaining game and that more complex games like board games and textual RPGs were challenging for current agents. The authors also note that tuning large language models with reinforcement learning is prohibitively expensive.
- Why unresolved: The current study focuses on a simple negotiation game, and the scalability of the approach to more complex scenarios or larger models is not explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with more complex negotiation scenarios or larger language models to determine if the iterative AI feedback process remains effective and scalable.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the long-term effects of continuous improvement through self-play and AI feedback on the negotiation strategies and performance of language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that when unrolling the game to multiple rounds, stronger agents can consistently improve their performance by meaningfully using previous experiences and iterative AI feedback, yet have a higher risk of breaking the deal.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses on short-term improvements and does not explore the long-term effects of continuous improvement on the models' strategies and performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with a larger number of rounds to observe the long-term effects of continuous improvement on the models' negotiation strategies and performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the effectiveness of AI feedback compare to human feedback in terms of the quality of negotiation strategies and the final deal prices achieved by the models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that AI feedback can be comparable to human feedback in improving model negotiation skills, opening possibilities for autonomous model improvement with minimal human intervention.
- Why unresolved: The study only compares the effectiveness of AI feedback to randomly sampled human feedback from a predefined pool, and does not explore the potential differences in the quality of negotiation strategies and final deal prices achieved by the models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments where human feedback is provided in a more structured and consistent manner, and comparing the resulting negotiation strategies and final deal prices to those achieved with AI feedback.

## Limitations

- Empirical scope limited to simple bargaining games with fixed price ranges and two fixed roles
- Reliance on models' ability to parse natural language feedback without fine-tuning may limit improvement potential
- Role-specific learning asymmetries observed but not fully explained, suggesting underlying model biases or task asymmetries
- Lack of exploration of long-term effects with more rounds or more complex negotiation scenarios

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The core claim that in-context learning from AI feedback can drive iterative improvement in negotiation games for strong, well-aligned models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude-v1.3)
- **Medium confidence**: The observation that weaker or misaligned models (e.g., Cohere-command, Jurassic-2) fail to improve or even engage with the task as intended
- **Medium confidence**: The finding that sellers tend to improve more than buyers, suggesting role-dependent learning dynamics
- **Low confidence**: The mechanism by which natural language feedback is internalized as effective few-shot demonstrations, given the lack of direct evidence or theoretical grounding in the corpus

## Next Checks

2. **Extend the number of negotiation rounds**: Conduct experiments with more than 5 rounds to determine if improvement plateaus, continues, or reverses, and whether models develop more sophisticated negotiation strategies over time

3. **Cross-role and cross-model pairing experiments**: Swap roles and models (e.g., test GPT-4 as buyer vs. GPT-3.5-Turbo as seller) to isolate whether role-specific learning asymmetries are due to model capabilities, task structure, or interaction effects
