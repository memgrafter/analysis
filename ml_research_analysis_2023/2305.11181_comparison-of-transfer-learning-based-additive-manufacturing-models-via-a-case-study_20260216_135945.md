# Comparison of Transfer Learning based Additive Manufacturing Models via A Case Study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11181
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11181
- Reference count: 40
- This paper investigates transfer learning challenges in additive manufacturing modeling through a case study comparing six TL-based models.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses key challenges in applying transfer learning to additive manufacturing modeling: selecting appropriate source domains, determining optimal target-to-source data ratios, and deciding whether to preprocess data. Through a case study using an open-source metal AM dataset, the authors compare six transfer learning-based models (integrated with decision tree regression and neural networks) against baseline models. The results demonstrate that transfer learning can improve modeling performance when source and target domains share high qualitative similarity, and that careful consideration of data preprocessing is essential for maximizing performance gains.

## Method Summary
The study constructs six transfer learning-based models by integrating five TL methods (Two-stage TrAdaBoost.R2, subspace alignment, source model transformation, fine-tuning, and multi-task learning) with decision tree regression (DTR) and artificial neural network (ANN). These are compared against baseline DTR and ANN models using a validation framework with 30 iterations of random sampling. The case study uses an open-source laser powder bed fusion dataset with Ti-6Al-4V material, where two source tasks (EOS M270, SLM 250 HL) and one target task (SLM 125 HL) are defined based on different printers and the output variable (relative density). Performance is evaluated using median MSE improvement and positive transfer ratio metrics.

## Key Results
- Transfer learning methods improve target modeling performance when source and target domains have high qualitative similarity
- Optimal target-to-source training data size ratio falls within [0.2, 0.7] range
- Data preprocessing effects vary significantly depending on the TL framework and baseline model used

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Transfer learning improves modeling performance when source and target domains have high qualitative similarity.
- Mechanism: Higher qualitative similarity (e.g., same machine series, material, process) reduces domain shift, enabling more effective knowledge transfer.
- Core assumption: Similarity between source and target can be assessed qualitatively (e.g., same printer series, material type) when quantitative metrics are unavailable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Finally, the source AM domain with larger qualitative similarity... are recommended."
  - [section 5.1] "the qualitative similarity between source task 2 and the target task is higher... a clear increase in the positive transfer ratio is monitored in I-DTR, FT-ANN, and MTL-ANN with source task 2..."
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct quantitative similarity metrics cited in related papers.
- Break condition: If source and target domains differ in critical parameters (e.g., material, process) that strongly influence the output variable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: An optimal range of target-to-source training data size ratio (e.g., [0.2, 0.7]) balances computational expense and transfer learning performance.
- Mechanism: Too little target data leads to insufficient target information for effective knowledge extraction; too much target data reduces the relative benefit of transfer learning as the target model becomes accurate on its own.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.2] "When n_train^t / n_s is smaller than a threshold (e.g., 0.2 in this case)... negative transfer occurs... When the sizes of target training data and source data are close (e.g., n_train^t / n_s > 0.7 in this case), a decreasing trend is monitored..."
  - [section 5.2] "Therefore, in real-world AM problems, the size ratio n_train^t / n_s is recommended to be in a certain range (e.g., [0.2,0.7])..."
  - [corpus] Weak: No external validation of this specific ratio range in related works.
- Break condition: If the source domain knowledge is highly generalizable or if target data is extremely expensive to collect.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Data preprocessing should be applied carefully; it can harm performance when source and target data are directly compared or reused, but may help in fine-tuning and MTL frameworks.
- Mechanism: Preprocessing can artificially reduce differences between source and target inputs, leading to misleading comparisons in instance-based or feature-based TL. However, in model-based TL (fine-tuning, MTL), preprocessing can improve baseline model performance and thus the relative improvement from TL.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.3] "the four TL-based DTR models trained with min-max scaled and standardized data have a larger median MSE than those with raw data... they also result in the worst improvements caused by TL..."
  - [section 5.3] "On the contrary, a different trend is observed in the baseline ANN, FT-ANN, and MTL-ANN models... a better modeling performances for FT-ANN and MTL-ANN in most cases..."
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct discussion of preprocessing effects in related TL-AM papers.
- Break condition: If preprocessing is essential for the baseline ML method (e.g., neural networks often benefit from normalization).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the optimal range of target-to-source training data size ratio vary across different TL methods and AM processes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper recommends a target-to-source training data size ratio range of [0.2,0.7] for balancing computational expense, modeling accuracy, and TL performance, but this was derived from a specific case study with limited TL methods.
- Why unresolved: The recommended ratio range may be specific to the particular TL methods, AM processes, and data characteristics studied. Different TL methods and AM processes may have different optimal ratio ranges.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments applying the same validation framework to diverse TL methods and AM processes with varying data characteristics to empirically determine optimal ratio ranges for each combination.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the quantitative relationship between qualitative similarity of source and target domains and TL performance across different AM modeling problems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests using qualitative similarity to select source domains but notes that quantifying similarity is difficult before data collection. It found higher qualitative similarity improved TL performance in its case study.
- Why unresolved: The case study only compared two source domains with different printers. The quantitative impact of other similarity factors (materials, processes, geometries) on TL performance across diverse AM modeling problems is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring TL performance while systematically varying similarity factors between source and target domains across diverse AM modeling problems to establish quantitative relationships.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of data preprocessing technique interact with TL method and AM process characteristics to affect modeling performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper found data preprocessing should be applied carefully, as it can either improve or degrade TL performance depending on the combination of preprocessing method, TL framework, and baseline model. Raw data generally performed better for instance-based and feature-based TL methods.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested two preprocessing techniques (min-max scaling and Z-score standardization) on a limited set of TL methods and baseline models. The interaction effects with other preprocessing techniques, TL methods, and AM processes are unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments testing diverse preprocessing techniques with various TL methods and baseline models across different AM processes to establish guidelines for optimal preprocessing selection based on method and process characteristics.

## Limitations

- Findings are based on a single case study with limited sample sizes (49, 32, and 24 samples)
- Qualitative similarity assessment relies on domain expert judgment rather than quantitative metrics
- Recommended target-to-source data size ratio [0.2, 0.7] may not generalize beyond this specific AM modeling scenario
- Only two preprocessing techniques (min-max scaling and Z-score standardization) were evaluated

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Transfer learning improves modeling performance when source and target domains have high qualitative similarity
- **Medium Confidence**: The optimal target-to-source data size ratio falls within [0.2, 0.7]
- **Medium Confidence**: Data preprocessing effects depend on the TL framework used

## Next Checks
