# Convergence of SARSA with linear function approximation: The random horizon case

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04548
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04548
- Reference count: 22
- The paper proves convergence of SARSA with linear function approximation for random horizon MDPs under ε-soft and Lipschitz continuous behavior policies.

## Executive Summary
This paper extends convergence results for SARSA with linear function approximation from infinite horizon discounted MDPs to random horizon MDPs. The key insight is that by updating the weight vector and policy only at the end of each trajectory (after reaching the absorbing state), the algorithm can avoid issues caused by policy discontinuity during online updates. Under assumptions that the behavior policy is ε-soft and Lipschitz continuous with respect to the weight vector with a sufficiently small Lipschitz constant, the algorithm converges with probability one.

## Method Summary
The paper analyzes SARSA with linear function approximation for random horizon MDPs where updates occur only at trajectory endpoints. The method uses a weight vector θ to parameterize an ε-soft behavior policy πθ, which is updated after each trajectory using the accumulated rewards and state-action visitations. The convergence proof relies on verifying Robbins-Monro conditions for stochastic approximation, showing that the expected update is continuous in θ, the second moment of updates is bounded, and the Lipschitz continuity condition ensures stability of the iteration.

## Key Results
- Proves convergence with probability one for SARSA with linear function approximation on random horizon MDPs
- Shows that weight updates only at trajectory endpoints avoid policy discontinuity issues
- Establishes conditions under which the Lipschitz continuity of the behavior policy ensures convergence

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SARSA with linear function approximation converges almost surely for random horizon MDPs when the behavior policy is ε-soft and Lipschitz continuous.
- Mechanism: The algorithm updates weights only at trajectory endpoints, avoiding the issues caused by policy discontinuity in online updates. Under the Robbins-Monro framework, this satisfies the stability condition (6) when the Lipschitz constant is small enough, ensuring convergence.
- Core assumption: The behavior policy πθ is ε-soft and Lipschitz continuous in θ with sufficiently small Lipschitz constant C.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "if the behaviour policy is ε-soft and Lipschitz continuous with respect to the weight vector... with small enough Lipschitz constant, then the algorithm will converge with probability one"
  - [section] "we show that using this algorithm to solve a random horizon MDP, when the behaviour policy is ε-soft and Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. the weight vector with sufficiently small Lipschitz constant, the weight vector will converge with probability one"
- Break condition: If the Lipschitz constant C exceeds the threshold C0, the stability condition fails and convergence is no longer guaranteed.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The expected number of visits to each state-action pair before absorption, ηπ, is a continuous function of the policy π.
- Mechanism: This continuity allows the transition matrix Pπ and related matrices (Dπ, Aπ, bπ) to be continuous in π, which in turn ensures that the fixed point θπ exists and is continuous in π.
- Core assumption: All policies are proper (reach terminal state with probability one).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "By (3) we have η⊤π = λ⊤π (I − Pπ)−1. λπ and Pπ are continuous functions of π, I − Pπ is nonsingular... hence ηπ is a continuous function of π"
  - [section] "Using Assumption 4.4 and that π is ε-soft, all transient state-action pairs have a positive probability of being visited, hence Dπ is positive definite"
- Break condition: If some policy is not proper (does not reach terminal state with probability one), the matrix (I − Pπ)−1 may not exist or the continuity properties fail.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The algorithm satisfies the Robbins-Monro assumptions, specifically the square integrability condition (5).
- Mechanism: By rewriting the update equation using Sutton's formulation and applying Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, we show that the second moment of the update is bounded by a constant times (1 + ||θ||²), satisfying the square integrability condition.
- Core assumption: Rewards are bounded (|r(s,a,s')| ≤ rmax) and the basis functions are bounded (Φmax < ∞).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "using Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, together with general expressions for the expectation and variance of the number of steps before being absorbed in an absorbing Markov chain, it is shown that the square integrability condition holds"
  - [section] "Eθ[||H(θ, Xt+1)||²] ≤ 2Φ²max(r²max + 4Φ²max||θ||²)"
- Break condition: If rewards or basis functions are unbounded, the square integrability condition may fail, breaking the convergence proof.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: MDP environment -> SARSA algorithm -> Linear function approximation -> Weight updates at trajectory endpoints -> Converged policy
- Critical path: 
  1. Initialize θ0 and π0 = πθ0
  2. Generate trajectory using πθt until absorption
  3. Update θt+1 using the trajectory data
  4. Update πθt+1 = π(θt+1)
  5. Repeat until convergence
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Weight updates only at trajectory endpoints vs. online updates: Reduces policy discontinuity issues but may slow convergence for long trajectories
  - ε-soft vs. greedy policies: ε-soft ensures exploration and satisfies proof requirements but may be suboptimal for performance
  - Lipschitz constant threshold: Small C ensures convergence but may limit the algorithm's ability to approach optimal policies
- Failure signatures:
  - Non-convergence: May indicate the Lipschitz constant C exceeds the threshold C0
  - Oscillations: Could suggest the step-size parameters don't satisfy ∑αt = ∞ and ∑αt² < ∞
  - Poor performance: May indicate ε is too large, preventing the policy from approaching optimal behavior
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify convergence on a simple random horizon MDP with known optimal policy, varying the Lipschitz constant C
  2. Test sensitivity to the ε parameter by measuring both convergence and final policy performance
  3. Compare convergence speed with the online SARSA variant on MDPs with varying trajectory lengths

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the convergence of the online version of SARSA with linear function approximation be proven for random horizon MDPs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that obtaining convergence results for the online version of the algorithm, where the weight vector and policy are updated after each iteration, could still be of interest for problems with trajectories of medium length.
- Why unresolved: The online version of the algorithm presents additional challenges due to the non-ergodic nature of the Markov chain induced by a policy in the random horizon case.
- What evidence would resolve it: A rigorous proof showing that the online version of SARSA converges for random horizon MDPs, possibly using advanced techniques such as quasi-stationary distributions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the exact value of C0 in Theorem 1, and how does it depend on the properties of the MDP?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the value of C0 in Theorem 1 is not specified and depends on the properties of the MDP, which might be unknown.
- Why unresolved: The exact value of C0 is not provided in the theorem, and it may depend on unknown transition probabilities and other MDP properties.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the MDP properties and their impact on C0, providing a concrete expression or bound for the constant.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the approximation error between the learned policy and the true optimal policy be quantified?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that there is no guarantee on how close the approximation is to the true optimal action-value function and the true optimal policy.
- Why unresolved: The convergence proof only guarantees convergence to a fixed point, but does not provide information on the quality of the approximation relative to the true optimal solution.
- What evidence would resolve it: Bounds on the approximation error in terms of the MDP parameters, exploration rate, and function approximation error, providing insights into the quality of the learned policy.

## Limitations
- The Lipschitz constant threshold C0 depends on unknown MDP properties, making it difficult to verify in practice
- The requirement to update weights only at trajectory endpoints may lead to slow convergence for long trajectories
- The analysis assumes bounded rewards and basis functions, which may not hold in all applications

## Confidence
- Convergence proof mechanism (High): The mathematical derivation follows standard stochastic approximation theory with explicit verification of Robbins-Monro conditions
- Lipschitz continuity threshold (Medium): While the existence of C0 is proven, its actual value depends on problem-specific parameters that aren't characterized
- Practical applicability (Low): The theoretical requirements (ε-soft policies, Lipschitz continuity, end-of-trajectory updates) may be too restrictive for real-world applications
