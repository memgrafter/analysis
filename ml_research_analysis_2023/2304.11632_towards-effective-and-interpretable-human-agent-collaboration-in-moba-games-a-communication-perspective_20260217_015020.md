# Towards Effective and Interpretable Human-Agent Collaboration in MOBA Games: A Communication Perspective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.11632
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11632
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: MCC framework achieves 37-54% win rates vs. 23-42% baselines in human-agent MOBA collaboration

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of effective human-agent collaboration in MOBA games by introducing the Meta-Command Communication (MCC) framework. The framework enables explicit, interpretable communication between humans and agents through structured meta-commands that encode macro-strategies. Experiments in Honor of Kings demonstrate that MCC agents achieve significantly higher win rates and are preferred by human teammates across different skill levels and team compositions.

## Method Summary
The MCC framework consists of three stages: (1) Meta-Command Conversion Stage where humans send explicit strategies converted to structured meta-commands, (2) Meta-Command Communication Stage with a selector that estimates meta-command values using target attention, and (3) Human-Agent Collaboration Stage where agents execute selected commands. The framework trains sequentially with a Command Encoding Network (CEN) learning from expert replays, a Meta-Command Conditioned Action Network (MCCAN) learning execution through self-play, and a Meta-Command Selector (CS) learning to select valuable commands.

## Key Results
- MCC agents achieve 37-54% win rates compared to 23-42% for baseline agents
- MCC agents receive significantly higher subjective preference scores from human teammates
- The framework generalizes effectively to different numbers of human teammates and skill levels
- Response rates remain high across diverse team compositions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Meta-commands bridge the communication gap between humans and agents by providing an interpretable common ground.
- Mechanism: Humans send explicit macro-strategies (like "Come And Kill The Dragon") which are converted into structured meta-commands (<Location, Event, Time Limit>). Agents generate meta-commands from observations using a Command Encoding Network, creating a unified representation that both parties can understand.
- Core assumption: Both humans and agents can interpret the same meta-command structure meaningfully.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "To bridge the communication gap between agents and humans, we designed the Meta-Command - a common ground between humans and agents for bidirectional communication."
  - [section 3.2]: "We divide a macro-strategy into three components: where to go, what to do, and how long."
- Break condition: If either humans or agents cannot map their intentions to the <Location, Event, Time Limit> format, communication breaks down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The Meta-Command Selector models agents' value systems for meta-commands, enabling intelligent selection rather than random choice.
- Mechanism: The selector estimates Q-values for each received meta-command based on observation and meta-command embeddings, using target attention to directly relate observation information to each meta-command's value.
- Core assumption: Agents can learn to value meta-commands similarly to how humans do through experience.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "To achieve effective collaboration, we constructed the Meta-Command Selector - a value estimator for agents to select valuable meta-commands to collaborate with humans."
  - [section 3.3]: "agents must select the most valuable meta-command from the received meta-command candidate set C to achieve effective human-agent collaboration."
- Break condition: If the value estimation is inaccurate or training fails to capture human-like value preferences, agents will make poor collaboration decisions.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The hierarchical architecture with CEN → MCCAN → CS enables effective learning progression from command understanding to execution to selection.
- Mechanism: The framework trains in three phases: CEN learns meta-command distribution from humans, MCCAN learns to execute meta-commands with intrinsic/extrinsic rewards, CS learns to select optimal meta-commands through self-play.
- Core assumption: Sequential training allows each component to specialize before the next layer is added.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1]: "The process of the MCC framework consists of three stages: (I) the Meta-Command Conversion Stage, (II) the Meta-Command Communication Stage, and (III) the Human-Agent Collaboration Stage."
  - [section 4.1]: "Due to the complexity of MOBA games and limited resources, we train the CEN, the MCCAN, and the CS sequentially instead of training the MCC framework jointly."
- Break condition: If any phase fails to converge or components are not properly frozen during subsequent training, the entire system's performance degrades.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: observation → CEN (generates meta-command) → CS (selects meta-command) → MCCAN (executes actions)
- Critical path: The critical execution path is: observation → CEN (generates meta-command) → CS (selects meta-command) → MCCAN (executes actions). The communication path involves humans sending meta-commands → CS evaluation → action execution.
- Design tradeoffs: The framework trades computational complexity for interpretability - meta-commands provide clear communication but require additional processing. The sequential training approach saves resources but may miss joint optimization opportunities. The fixed 20-second meta-command duration simplifies timing but may not always match optimal strategy timing.
- Failure signatures: Poor collaboration performance may indicate: CEN not properly learning human meta-command distribution (random or incorrect meta-commands), MCCAN not effectively executing meta-commands (low completion rate), or CS not accurately valuing meta-commands (selecting suboptimal commands). Each component can be evaluated independently.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test CEN alone by comparing generated meta-command distribution against human meta-commands from expert replays using KL divergence.
  2. Test MCCAN alone by evaluating completion rate of generated meta-commands against baseline action network without meta-command conditioning.
  3. Test CS alone by evaluating response rate to human vs. random meta-commands in a simplified environment where agents only receive meta-commands without generating their own.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the MCC framework's communication efficiency scale to larger team sizes (e.g., 10v10 or 20v20 MOBA games) without degrading performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions MCC agents can generalize to different numbers of human teammates but doesn't test beyond 5v5 format. The meta-command communication happens every 20 seconds which may become a bottleneck with more players.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only tested up to 3H+2A team configurations. Larger team sizes could introduce coordination challenges that weren't explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying team sizes from 5v5 to larger formats while measuring win rates, response times, and communication overhead would clarify scalability limits.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would the MCC framework perform when extended to different game genres beyond MOBA, such as FPS or MMO games?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "the meta-commands we proposed are generic to MOBA games and cannot be directly extended to other types of games, such as FPS and Massively Multiplayer Online (MMO)."
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested in Honor of Kings and acknowledges the framework isn't directly transferable to other genres.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and testing MCC variants in FPS (e.g., Counter-Strike) and MMO games would demonstrate adaptability across genres.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the minimum amount of human gameplay data needed to train the CEN to achieve comparable performance to the 26-hour training described?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "We extract meta-commands from expert game replay authorized by the game provider" and trains the CEN for 26 hours using 8 GPUs, but doesn't explore data efficiency.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't investigate how training performance scales with different amounts of expert data or training duration.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the amount of expert replay data and training time while measuring CEN convergence and downstream MCC performance would quantify data requirements.

## Limitations

- The meta-command structure (<Location, Event, Time Limit>) may not capture the full complexity of strategic decisions in MOBA games
- The sequential training approach may prevent discovery of optimal joint representations that could emerge from end-to-end training
- The framework is limited to MOBA games and cannot be directly extended to other genres like FPS or MMO

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The empirical results showing improved win rates (37-54% vs. 23-42%) and human preference across different skill levels are well-supported by the controlled experiments described in the paper.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that meta-commands provide effective interpretability relies heavily on the assumption that humans and agents share common understanding of the <Location, Event, Time Limit> format.

**Low Confidence**: The scalability of the approach to larger meta-command spaces or more complex game scenarios is not thoroughly explored.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study on meta-command components**: Systematically remove or modify each component of the <Location, Event, Time Limit> structure to quantify how much each contributes to collaboration success.

2. **Cross-game generalization test**: Implement the MCC framework in a different MOBA game or strategy game to assess whether the communication architecture transfers effectively across game environments.

3. **Human interpretability assessment**: Conduct user studies where human players rate the clarity and usefulness of meta-commands they receive, and measure whether better-understood meta-commands correlate with improved collaboration outcomes.
