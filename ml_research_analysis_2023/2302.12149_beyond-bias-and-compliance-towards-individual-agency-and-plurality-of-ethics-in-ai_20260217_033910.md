# Beyond Bias and Compliance: Towards Individual Agency and Plurality of Ethics in AI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12149
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12149
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Proposes embedding ethics into AI through user-driven data labeling rather than bias removal or compliance checklists.

## Executive Summary
The paper critiques the prevailing bias and compliance approaches to AI ethics, arguing they fail to capture the true nature of ethical decision-making. Instead, it proposes a method called daios that centers on data labeling as the primary mechanism for integrating ethical values into machine learning systems. The approach emphasizes co-creating algorithms with end users, allowing them to provide feedback and influence the ethical direction of AI through direct involvement in the training data creation process.

## Method Summary
The daios method involves collecting training data with ethical labels provided by end users, fine-tuning ML models using this labeled data to align with user values, and establishing a continuous feedback loop where users can influence AI behavior. The approach treats data as the ground truth that determines model behavior, arguing that ethical values must be embedded directly into the data rather than adjusted through model parameters or governance frameworks.

## Key Results
- Data labeling determines the "ground truth" that models learn from, making it the key mechanism for embedding ethical values
- Co-creating algorithms with end users creates a positive feedback loop where AI behavior reflects the values of those most affected
- Ethics in AI is fundamentally a philosophical problem about values, not a technical or compliance problem

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Ethical AI behavior can be embedded directly into ML systems by labeling training data with ethical values rather than adjusting model parameters or adding governance checklists. Data labeling determines the "ground truth" that models learn from, and by explicitly tagging data points with value-based labels, the model internalizes these criteria during training.

### Mechanism 2
Co-creating algorithms with end users creates a positive feedback loop where AI behavior reflects the values of those most affected, rather than the designers' assumptions. The daios builds a user interface that allows direct moral feedback on algorithm outputs, which is fed back into the training pipeline for iterative refinement.

### Mechanism 3
Ethics in AI is fundamentally a philosophical problem about values, not a technical or compliance problem. Therefore, solutions must start from ethical theory, not governance frameworks or model tuning. The daios grounds its approach in philosophical axioms and builds a platform that operationalizes these through data labeling and user co-creation.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Machine learning fundamentals (data → model → behavior)
  - Why needed here: Understanding that data determines model behavior is central to the daios claim that ethics must be embedded in data, not model parameters
  - Quick check question: If you change the training data distribution, what happens to the model's behavior?

- Concept: Ethics as value-based reasoning vs. compliance
  - Why needed here: The paper distinguishes ethics from legal compliance; engineers must recognize this to avoid building "ethics as checklist" systems
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between acting ethically and acting legally?

- Concept: Subjectivity of data and observation
  - Why needed here: The claim that "data cannot be objective" underpins the need for user feedback to surface diverse ethical perspectives
  - Quick check question: How does the perspective of the data labeler influence the resulting model?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data ingestion layer -> Ethical labeling interface -> Model training pipeline -> Feedback loop module -> Monitoring dashboard
- Critical path: 1. Collect training data with ethical labels, 2. Train model incorporating value labels, 3. Deploy model to users, 4. Capture user feedback on ethical outputs, 5. Update data with feedback, retrain model, 6. Monitor KPIs for ethical alignment
- Design tradeoffs: Granularity of ethical labels impacts model complexity and user burden; real-time feedback vs. batch updates affects latency and model stability; user anonymity vs. traceability affects data quality and trust
- Failure signatures: Model performance degrades if feedback is unrepresentative; ethical drift occurs if feedback loop is broken or biased; system becomes unusable if labeling interface is too complex
- First 3 experiments: 1. A/B test: model trained with ethical labels vs. without, measure user-reported ethical satisfaction, 2. Simulate user feedback injection at different frequencies, observe impact on ethical KPIs, 3. Test scalability: run labeling interface with 100 vs. 10,000 concurrent users, measure latency and data quality

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How can AI systems be designed to effectively incorporate diverse ethical values from end users without becoming overly complex or inconsistent? The paper emphasizes the need for AI systems to reflect the plurality of values and the freedom of individual expression, but acknowledges the complexity of integrating diverse ethical perspectives.

### Open Question 2
What specific metrics or KPIs can be used to measure the ethical performance of AI systems in a way that is both meaningful and scalable? The paper discusses the difficulty of measuring the impact of ethics methods and the need for meaningful KPIs to evaluate ethical outputs.

### Open Question 3
How can AI systems be designed to adapt to changing ethical norms and values over time without requiring constant manual intervention? The paper suggests that AI systems should be able to evolve with societal changes but does not detail how this can be achieved in practice.

## Limitations
- The philosophical claims about ethics being about "values" and "intent" are asserted but not externally validated
- Critical technical implementation details about how ethical labels are created, validated, and integrated into training pipelines are absent
- The approach assumes user feedback will create ethical alignment but doesn't address potential issues like feedback manipulation or conflicting values

## Confidence

### Confidence Labels
- High Confidence: The core insight that data determines model behavior and that ethics must be embedded in data rather than added as governance layers
- Medium Confidence: The claim that user co-creation can create more ethical AI systems, though practical implementation details are missing
- Low Confidence: The philosophical foundations and their translation into technical mechanisms
