# Adversarial Attacks on Online Learning to Rank with Stochastic Click Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19218
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19218
- Reference count: 40
- This paper proposes the first study of adversarial attacks on online learning to rank (OLTR) algorithms with stochastic click models, introducing two attack strategies that achieve sublinear attack cost.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first comprehensive study of adversarial attacks on online learning to rank algorithms operating under stochastic click models. The authors propose two attack strategies: a generalized list poisoning attack (GA) that manipulates the ranking list presentation, and an attack-then-quit (ATQ) strategy that manipulates click feedback. Both attacks aim to place a target item at the top of the ranking list for a linear number of times while maintaining sublinear attack cost. The attacks are theoretically analyzed and validated through experiments on both synthetic and real-world data.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces two attack strategies targeting online learning to rank algorithms. The Generalized List Poisoning Attack (GA) manipulates the ranking list by replacing non-target items with auxiliary items of lower attractiveness, causing the ranker to believe the target item is most attractive. The Attack-Then-Quit (ATQ) strategy manipulates click feedback for a threshold number of rounds, then stops attacking. Both attacks exploit the ranker's assumption that click feedback corresponds to the original ranking list. The methods are designed to work with general stochastic click models and achieve sublinear attack costs relative to the time horizon T.

## Key Results
- GA attack can efficiently target any no-regret ranker in general stochastic click models with sublinear attack cost
- ATQ strategy successfully attacks BatchRank and TopRank algorithms with sublinear attack cost
- Both attacks achieve their goals while maintaining stealth by ensuring click feedback follows the unknown click model
- Experimental results on synthetic and MovieLens datasets validate the effectiveness and cost-efficiency of both attack strategies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Generalized List Poisoning Attack (GA)
- Claim: GA can efficiently attack any no-regret ranker by manipulating the ranking list presented to the user, causing the ranker to believe a target item has the highest attractiveness.
- Mechanism: The attacker replaces non-target items in the original ranking list with auxiliary items of lower attractiveness, creating a modified list where the target item appears to be the most attractive. This manipulation is stealthy because the click feedback still follows the unknown click model.
- Core assumption: The ranker assumes the click feedback corresponds to the original ranking list, not the manipulated one. The click model is a black box to the attacker.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose generalized list poisoning attacks that perturb the ranking list presented to the user. This strategy can efficiently attack any no-regret ranker in general stochastic click models."
  - [section 3]: "The attacker perturbs the ranking list presented to the user and pretends the click feedback represents the user's interests in the original ranking list. This guarantees the feedback always follows the unknown click model, making the attack stealthy."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Related papers focus on different attack models or settings.
- Break condition: If the ranker can detect or is robust to list manipulation, or if the click model does not satisfy Assumption 1 (unique optimal list).

### Mechanism 2: Attack-Then-Quit (ATQ) Strategy
- Claim: ATQ can efficiently attack elimination-based and divide-and-conquer-based OLTR algorithms by manipulating click feedback for a sublinear number of rounds, then quitting.
- Mechanism: The attacker maximizes the click feedback of the target item and minimizes the click feedback of non-target items for a threshold number of rounds (T1). This manipulation causes the ranker to incorrectly estimate the target item's attractiveness as the highest, placing it at the top for the remaining rounds.
- Core assumption: The OLTR algorithms being attacked use elimination or divide-and-conquer strategies that rely on click feedback to estimate item attractiveness. The attacker knows the algorithm's structure but not the click model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Furthermore, we propose a click poisoning-based strategy named attack-then-quit that can efficiently attack two representative OLTR algorithms for stochastic click models."
  - [section 4.1]: "Once these rankers make this judgment, they would place the target item at the first position of the list Rt and the attacker achieves the attack goal."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Related papers focus on different attack models or settings.
- Break condition: If the ranker uses a different algorithmic approach (e.g., UCB-type algorithms) or if the click model is not a general stochastic click model.

### Mechanism 3: Cost Efficiency via Sublinear Attack Cost
- Claim: Both GA and ATQ achieve attack success with sublinear attack cost, defined as the number of manipulated clicks or list positions.
- Mechanism: GA achieves sublinear cost by only manipulating lists when the original list contains non-target items, and the number of such manipulations is bounded by the regret of the no-regret ranker. ATQ achieves sublinear cost by manipulating clicks for only T1 rounds, where T1 is sublinear in the total number of rounds T.
- Core assumption: The no-regret ranker has a sublinear regret, meaning it presents the optimal list for T - o(T) rounds. The OLTR algorithms being attacked have specific properties (e.g., BatchRank and TopRank) that allow for efficient attack with sublinear cost.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The goal of the adversary is to misguide the online learning to rank algorithm to place the target item on top of the ranking list linear times to time horizon T with a sublinear attack cost (o(T))."
  - [section 3]: "Due to the optimal list becomes T during the attack, if Rt\T ≠ ∅, the per step regret is at least ∆min > 0... With this in mind, we can further bound the total cost by C(T) ≤ KR(T)/∆min."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Related papers focus on different attack models or settings.
- Break condition: If the no-regret ranker has a linear regret, or if the OLTR algorithms being attacked do not have the specific properties required for efficient attack.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Ranker -> Ranking List -> User -> Click Feedback -> Ranker
- Critical path:
  1. Ranker selects a ranked list R_t based on its current estimates of item attractiveness.
  2. Attacker observes R_t and decides whether to manipulate the list (GA) or click feedback (ATQ).
  3. If attacking, attacker modifies the list or click feedback to achieve the attack goal.
  4. User observes the (potentially manipulated) ranked list and generates click feedback.
  5. Ranker receives the (potentially manipulated) click feedback and updates its estimates of item attractiveness.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Stealthiness vs. Effectiveness: More aggressive attacks may be more effective but also more likely to be detected.
  - Attack Cost vs. Success Rate: Increasing the attack cost (e.g., by manipulating more lists or clicks) may increase the success rate but also make the attack more expensive.
  - Target Algorithm vs. Generalizability: Attacks designed for specific OLTR algorithms (e.g., BatchRank, TopRank) may be more effective but less generalizable to other algorithms.

- Failure signatures:
  - Ranker detects manipulation: If the ranker can detect or is robust to list or click feedback manipulation, the attack may fail.
  - Click model violated: If the manipulation causes the click feedback to violate the assumptions of the click model, the attack may be detected or ineffective.
  - Sublinear cost not achieved: If the attack cost is linear in the number of rounds, it may not be efficient or practical.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement GA and ATQ attacks on a simple OLTR algorithm (e.g., PBM-UCB) with a synthetic dataset and position-based click model. Measure the attack success rate and cost.
  2. Implement GA and ATQ attacks on a more complex OLTR algorithm (e.g., TopRank) with a synthetic dataset and general stochastic click model. Measure the attack success rate and cost.
  3. Implement GA and ATQ attacks on an OLTR algorithm with a real-world dataset (e.g., MovieLens) and compare the results to the synthetic experiments. Analyze the impact of dataset characteristics on attack effectiveness.

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantees rely heavily on specific assumptions about click model structure and ranker algorithmic properties
- The claim of attacking "any no-regret ranker" may be overly broad, as it depends on the ranker's regret being sublinear and the optimal list being unique
- Both attack strategies require knowledge of the ranker's algorithm and the ability to manipulate either the ranking list or click feedback, which may not be feasible in all practical settings

## Confidence
