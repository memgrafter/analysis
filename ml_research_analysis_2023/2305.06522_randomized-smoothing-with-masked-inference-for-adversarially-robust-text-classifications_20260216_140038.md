# Randomized Smoothing with Masked Inference for Adversarially Robust Text Classifications

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06522
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06522
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: RSMI improves adversarial robustness by 2-3x over state-of-the-art methods on three benchmark datasets

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces RSMI, a two-stage framework that combines randomized smoothing (RS) and masked inference (MI) to improve the adversarial robustness of NLP systems. RSMI transforms a classifier into a smoothed classifier via multi-layer noise injection, while MI denoises adversarial perturbations by masking tokens based on gradient-based feature attribution. Experimental results on three benchmark datasets show that RSMI improves adversarial robustness by 2-3 times over existing state-of-the-art methods in terms of key metrics such as robust accuracy, attack success rate, and average number of queries.

## Method Summary
RSMI combines randomized smoothing with multi-layer noise injection and masked inference using gradient-based saliency. The framework injects Gaussian noise at multiple hidden layers of PLMs during training, creating a smoothed classifier with provable L2 robustness. During inference, tokens with highest gradient-based saliency scores are masked, reducing the influence of adversarially perturbed tokens while leveraging context for prediction. A two-step Monte Carlo sampling approach improves inference efficiency by using small k0 samples for initial decision and larger k1 samples only when needed.

## Key Results
- RSMI improves robust accuracy (RAcc) by 2-3 times over state-of-the-art methods
- Attack success rate (ASR) decreases significantly across TextFooler, PWWS, and BAE attacks
- Two-step sampling improves RAcc while reducing average number of queries (AvgQ)
- RSMI shows consistent improvements across BERT-base, RoBERTa-base, BERT-Large, RoBERTa-Large, and T5-Large models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Gaussian noise injection at hidden layers improves adversarial robustness by smoothing the decision boundary.
- Mechanism: Adding multi-layer noise transforms the classifier into a smoothed classifier via convolution with a Gaussian distribution, provably robust within an L2 ball around input points.
- Core assumption: Perturbed representations from PLMs retain sufficient smoothness guarantees for robustness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "RS transforms a classifier into a smoothed classifier to obtain robust representations"
  - [section 2.1]: "Theorem 1... provides provable robustness guarantee by the randomized smoothing approach"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus matches for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If noise level is too low to achieve meaningful smoothing, or too high causing significant accuracy degradation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Gradient-guided masked inference denoises adversarial perturbations by forcing the model to rely on surrounding context.
- Mechanism: Tokens with highest gradient-based saliency scores are masked, reducing the influence of adversarially perturbed tokens while leveraging context for prediction.
- Core assumption: Adversarial attacks primarily target salient tokens, and masking these tokens effectively removes the adversarial signal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "MI denoises adversarial perturbations by masking tokens based on gradient-based feature attribution"
  - [section 2.2]: "MI draws an inference on an input sequence via a noise reduction process that masks adversarially 'salient' tokens"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus matches for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If attack strategies shift to target less salient tokens or distribute perturbations across many tokens.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-step Monte Carlo sampling improves inference efficiency while maintaining robustness against attack obfuscation.
- Mechanism: First step uses small k0 samples for initial decision, second step uses larger k1 samples only when needed, making it harder for attacks to estimate optimal perturbation direction.
- Core assumption: Adversarial examples are less likely to achieve consensus across stochastic predictions than clean samples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.3]: "Our two-step sampling based inference is based on an assumption that textual adversarial examples are liable to fail to achieve consensus"
  - [section 4.5]: "two-step sampling significantly improves the RAcc of RSMI models"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus matches for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If attacks adapt to account for stochastic sampling or if the threshold-based switching becomes predictable.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input processing -> Noise layer injection (multi-layer) -> Gradient computation -> Token masking -> Prediction aggregation
  Key components: Gaussian noise layers, gradient-based saliency computation, masking mechanism, two-step sampling inference

- Critical path:
  Forward pass with noise injection -> Gradient computation for saliency -> Masked inference with context exploitation -> Majority voting or logit averaging for final prediction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Noise level vs accuracy: Higher noise improves robustness but may hurt clean accuracy
  - Number of masked tokens vs performance: More masking improves denoising but may lose important information
  - Sample count vs computation: More samples improve robustness estimates but increase inference time

- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in robustness metrics across runs (indicates instability in noise or masking)
  - Significant drop in clean accuracy (noise level too high)
  - Attack success rate remains high despite training (masking not effective or noise insufficient)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline test: Run fine-tuned model on clean and adversarial examples to establish performance without any defense
  2. Single component test: Implement RSMI without masking to isolate noise layer effects on robustness
  3. Full system test: Run complete RSMI with both noise and masking on benchmark datasets to validate combined effectiveness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of hyperparameters (σ, M, Nl) in RSMI affect the trade-off between adversarial robustness and standard accuracy across different NLP tasks and model architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the impact of these hyperparameters on ASR and SAcc in Section 4.6, showing that increasing σ, M, or Nl generally decreases ASR but also decreases SAcc.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows trends, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how these hyperparameters interact or how to optimally tune them for different tasks and models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensive experiments across various NLP tasks and model architectures, systematically varying the hyperparameters and measuring both robustness and accuracy. Analysis of the interaction effects between hyperparameters.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the theoretical guarantees of RSMI's robustness be extended to more realistic attack scenarios beyond L2 norm bounded perturbations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that RSMI provides certifiable robustness within an L2 norm ball, but acknowledges limitations in extending this to other attack scenarios in Section 6.
- Why unresolved: The current theoretical framework relies on the L2 norm, which may not capture all types of adversarial attacks, especially those that exploit the discrete nature of text.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of new theoretical frameworks that can handle different attack scenarios, such as attacks that modify the structure of sentences or use synonyms. Empirical validation of the robustness of RSMI under these scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the two-step sampling-based inference in RSMI compare to other inference strategies, such as ensemble methods or Bayesian approaches, in terms of both robustness and computational efficiency?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes the two-step sampling-based inference in Section 2.3 and compares it to a single-step approach in Section 4.5, showing improved robustness.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows that the two-step approach outperforms a single-step approach, it does not compare it to other inference strategies that could potentially offer better robustness or efficiency.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments between the two-step sampling-based inference and other inference strategies, such as ensemble methods or Bayesian approaches, on various datasets and attack scenarios. Analysis of the trade-offs between robustness and computational efficiency.

## Limitations

- Theoretical gaps exist in extending randomized smoothing guarantees to multi-layer noise injection settings
- Empirical evaluation is limited to three specific attack methods and three benchmark datasets
- Implementation details for critical hyperparameters are not precisely specified
- Computational overhead of the approach is not thoroughly analyzed for larger models

## Next Checks

1. **Adaptive Attack Evaluation**: Implement an attack specifically designed to target RSMI's vulnerabilities - for example, an attack that distributes perturbations across many tokens rather than focusing on highly salient ones, or an attack that attempts to estimate the noise distribution during the attack process.

2. **Component Ablation with Different Noise Distributions**: Systematically vary the noise distribution (Gaussian vs uniform, different variances per layer) and evaluate how each component contributes to the final robustness. This would help identify whether the claimed improvements come from the specific noise injection method or could be achieved with simpler approaches.

3. **Certified Robustness Verification**: Implement the exact certification procedure from randomized smoothing theory for the multi-layer noise setting to verify the claimed robustness radii. This would involve computing the exact relationship between noise variance and certified bounds in the presence of multiple noise layers.
