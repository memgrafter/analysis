# On the Effect of Adversarial Training Against Invariance-based Adversarial Examples

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.08257
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08257
- Reference count: 21
- One-line primary result: Simultaneous adversarial training achieves combined robustness advantage across specific perturbation-to-invariance ratio ranges compared to consecutive training approaches

## Executive Summary
This work investigates training convolutional neural networks to be robust against both perturbation-based and invariance-based adversarial examples. Through experiments on the MNIST dataset, the authors demonstrate that simultaneous adversarial training - where both types of adversarial examples are used together in each training iteration - outperforms consecutive training approaches. The study reveals a fundamental trade-off between robustness against perturbation-based and invariance-based adversarial examples, with optimal performance occurring within specific ratio ranges. The findings suggest that human-defined labels for invariance-based adversarial examples yield better performance than algorithm-defined labels.

## Method Summary
The authors implement a CNN architecture similar to Madry et al. on the MNIST dataset, training models using different adversarial training strategies. They generate 500 algorithm-defined and 500 human-defined invariance-based adversarial examples using Tramer et al.'s algorithm, along with 100 perturbation-based adversarial examples using l∞-projected gradient descent (ε=0.3). Models are trained with simultaneous adversarial training combining both example types, using RMSprop optimizer with batch size 1024 and learning rate 0.001. Performance is evaluated using clean accuracy, ptb-robustness, and inv-robustness metrics, with a combined robustness metric calculated as a weighted mix of both types of robustness.

## Key Results
- Simultaneous adversarial training outperforms consecutive training for invariance-to-perturbation ratios between 0.430 and 0.898
- Human-defined labels for invariance-based adversarial examples yield better performance than algorithm-defined labels
- A fundamental trade-off exists between robustness against perturbation-based and invariance-based adversarial examples
- Training instability confirms the trade-off between model invariance and sensitivity

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Simultaneous adversarial training improves combined robustness over sequential training
- Mechanism: The model is trained on both perturbation-based and invariance-based adversarial examples in each training iteration, preventing overfitting to one type and balancing sensitivity and invariance
- Core assumption: Training on both example types simultaneously allows the model to develop a balanced representation that is robust to both threat models
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We show that when adversarial training with invariance-based and perturbation-based adversarial examples is applied, it should be conducted simultaneously and not consecutively."
  - [section]: "we identify a tradeoff between robustness against perturbation-based and invariance-based adversarial examples, depending on the training procedure"
  - [corpus]: Weak signal; corpus contains general adversarial training papers but no direct mention of simultaneous training effectiveness
- Break condition: If the ratio of perturbation-based to invariance-based examples is outside the identified sweet spot (.430 to .898), the combined robustness advantage disappears

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Human-defined labels for invariance-based adversarial examples yield better performance than algorithm-defined labels
- Mechanism: Human judgment better captures semantic meaning changes than the algorithm's label assignment, leading to more effective training
- Core assumption: The algorithm from Tramer et al. [20] incorrectly determines labels for invariance-based adversarial examples, while human annotators capture the semantic shift more accurately
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we find that the algorithm used for generating invariance-based adversarial examples in prior work does not correctly determine the labels and therefore we use human-determined labels"
  - [section]: "we analyze two different label assignment approaches... human-defined labels and the labels defined by the algorithm"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence in corpus; this appears to be a novel finding
- Break condition: If the algorithm for generating invariance-based adversarial examples is improved to correctly determine labels, the performance gap between human and algorithm labels may narrow or disappear

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: There is a fundamental trade-off between invariance and sensitivity in ML models
- Mechanism: Improving robustness against one type of adversarial example (perturbation-based or invariance-based) inherently reduces robustness against the other type
- Core assumption: The model cannot simultaneously maximize both types of robustness due to architectural constraints and the nature of adversarial examples
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "we experience instability in our training method... confirms and sheds more light into the fundamental trade-off between a model's invariance and sensitivity"
  - [section]: "With rising ptb-robustness, the inv-robustness is decreasing"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; this is a novel theoretical contribution
- Break condition: If a new architecture or training method is developed that can achieve high robustness for both types simultaneously without trade-offs

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we achieve high robustness against both perturbation-based and invariance-based adversarial examples simultaneously?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies a fundamental trade-off between robustness against these two types of adversarial examples, where improving robustness against one type typically reduces robustness against the other.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores this trade-off in the context of a simple CNN on the MNIST dataset, leaving open the question of whether this trade-off exists and can be managed in more complex architectures and datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results demonstrating high robustness against both types of adversarial examples in more complex models and real-world datasets, potentially through novel training techniques or architectural modifications.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal ratio of perturbation-based to invariance-based adversarial examples for training robust models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies "sweet spots" where simultaneous adversarial training outperforms other approaches, but only for specific ratios between the two types of examples.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests a limited range of ratios on a single dataset and model architecture, leaving open the question of whether these optimal ratios generalize.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across multiple datasets, model architectures, and threat scenarios to identify universal or context-dependent optimal ratios for training robust models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the algorithm for generating invariance-based adversarial examples impact the resulting model's robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that the algorithm used in prior work does not correctly determine labels, necessitating human-defined labels instead.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests one algorithm for generating invariance-based adversarial examples and does not explore how different generation methods might affect training outcomes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using multiple algorithms for generating invariance-based adversarial examples, measuring their impact on model robustness and generalization to unseen examples.

## Limitations
- Narrow experimental scope limited to MNIST dataset only
- Single CNN architecture prevents analysis of architectural effects
- No comparison with other robust training methods beyond standard and perturbation-based
- Lack of computational overhead analysis between training strategies

## Confidence
- Simultaneous training effectiveness: Medium confidence (MNIST-only experiments, single architecture)
- Human-label advantage: Lower confidence (subjective judgment quality, inconsistent evaluation)
- Fundamental trade-off claim: Medium confidence (primarily demonstrated through observed instability rather than formal proof)
