# Less Likely Brainstorming: Using Language Models to Generate Alternative Hypotheses

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19339
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19339
- Reference count: 19
- Generates less likely but relevant hypotheses to reduce cognitive biases in decision-making

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces "less likely brainstorming," a novel task aimed at generating alternative hypotheses to correct for human cognitive biases. The authors propose BRAINSTORM, a contrastive learning method that uses margin-based and similarity losses to train language models to differentiate between likely and less likely outputs. Evaluated on brain MRI interpretation and commonsense reasoning datasets, BRAINSTORM outperforms baseline methods in generating less likely hypotheses while maintaining relevance and fluency, demonstrating potential for reducing decision-making errors.

## Method Summary
The BRAINSTORM method trains language models using a contrastive learning framework with three loss components: standard MLE loss, margin loss, and similarity loss. The margin loss encourages the model to assign higher probability to likely outputs and lower probability to less likely outputs based on an indicator variable. The similarity loss uses contrastive objectives to align input-indicator pairs with their corresponding outputs while contrasting with negative examples. The method can be applied at training time through these loss functions or at decoding time using methods like DEXPERTS and Contrastive Decoding to further refine output distributions.

## Key Results
- BRAINSTORM outperforms baseline methods in generating more "less likely" hypotheses on both brain MRI interpretation and commonsense reasoning datasets
- Automatic evaluation shows significant improvement in the fraction of less likely outputs compared to MLE and MLE-LL baselines
- Human evaluation confirms that BRAINSTORM maintains relevance and fluency while generating alternative perspectives

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The margin loss ensures the model assigns higher probability to human reference outputs given the correct indicator, and lower probability when the indicator is flipped.
- Mechanism: By penalizing the model when P(y | x, ¬i) is too close to P(y | x, i), the model learns to associate specific indicators with corresponding output types (likely vs less likely).
- Core assumption: The model can learn this association through margin-based discrimination without requiring paired examples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose a controlled text generation method that uses a novel contrastive learning strategy to encourage models to differentiate between generating likely and less likely outputs according to humans."
  - [section 4.1]: "Therefore, we apply a margin-based loss: Lmargin = max(0, P (y | x, ¬i)−P (y | x, i)+ m)"
- Break condition: If the margin value m is set too high, the model may struggle to generate any outputs for the less likely indicator due to overly restrictive constraints.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The similarity loss encourages the model to maximize agreement between input-indicator pairs and their corresponding outputs while contrasting with negative examples.
- Mechanism: By comparing the hidden representations of (x, i) with positive and negative outputs, the model learns to map indicator-conditioned inputs to their appropriate output distributions.
- Core assumption: The contrastive objective effectively learns the relationship between indicators and target sequences when both positive and negative examples are available.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "BRAINSTORM, a novel contrastive learning strategy to generate 'less likely' hypotheses"
  - [section 4.1]: "Lsim encourages the model to maximize the agreement between zx,i and its corresponding output zy"
- Break condition: When positive and negative examples are not available simultaneously (as in MRII NTERPRET), this mechanism cannot be applied directly.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using both training-time and decoding-time methods together can improve less likely hypothesis generation, though decoding-time methods may reduce fluency.
- Mechanism: Training-time methods like BRAINSTORM establish the fundamental capability, while decoding-time methods like DEXPERTS and CD can further refine the output distribution.
- Core assumption: The combination of methods can leverage their respective strengths while mitigating individual weaknesses.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6.1]: "Both CD and DExperts can improve the fraction of less likely hypotheses, but at a very high cost to perplexity."
  - [section 6.1]: "Although the fraction of less likely outputs can improve, both of these methods significantly increase the perplexity of generations"
- Break condition: When the fluency degradation from decoding-time methods becomes too severe, the practical utility of the generated text is compromised despite higher less likely fractions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: BART encoder-decoder architecture with three loss components: standard MLE loss, margin loss (operates on probability distributions), and similarity loss (operates on hidden representations)
- Critical path: During training, the model takes (x, i) pairs and corresponding human references y, computes all three losses, and updates parameters to minimize the combined objective. At inference, the model conditions on the indicator i to generate outputs.
- Design tradeoffs: The approach trades increased training complexity (additional loss terms and contrastive objectives) for improved ability to generate less likely hypotheses. The similarity loss requires careful batch construction to ensure effective contrastive learning.
- Failure signatures: Common failure modes include generating irrelevant outputs, producing repetitions of the input, or defaulting to likely hypotheses despite the less likely indicator. The automatic evaluation classifier may also misclassify outputs if the model's distribution shifts too far from training data.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train BRAINSTORM with only the margin loss (no similarity loss) on the E-CARE dataset to verify that this component alone provides improvement over MLE-LL.
  2. Implement BRAINSTORM ′ (without batch-wise similarity) on MRII NTERPRET to test the alternative similarity mechanism when paired examples are unavailable.
  3. Combine BRAINSTORM with DEXPERTS decoding at various α values on ART to find the optimal tradeoff between less likely fraction and perplexity.

## Limitations

- The evaluation relies heavily on an automatic classifier to measure "less likely" outputs, which may not fully capture the intended semantic properties
- Human evaluation was conducted on a subset of data and focused on relevance and fluency rather than the actual quality of alternative hypotheses
- The method shows tradeoffs between generating less likely outputs and maintaining fluency, with some decoding-time methods significantly degrading text quality

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The contrastive learning framework and loss formulations are technically sound and well-defined. The experimental results showing improved less likely fraction on automatic metrics are robust.
- **Medium confidence**: The practical utility of generating "less likely" hypotheses for reducing cognitive errors needs more validation beyond the initial human evaluation. The tradeoff between less likely fraction and perplexity requires careful consideration.
- **Low confidence**: The classifier's ability to accurately identify "less likely" hypotheses across different domains and the generalization of results to real-world decision-making contexts.
