# Mastering Strategy Card Game (Legends of Code and Magic) via End-to-End Policy and Optimistic Smooth Fictitious Play

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04096
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04096
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Wins double championships of COG2022 competition with end-to-end policy approach for multi-stage strategy card games

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of playing multi-stage strategy card games by proposing an end-to-end policy function that can handle the complex decision-making required across different game stages. The authors develop an optimistic smooth fictitious play algorithm to find Nash Equilibrium in the two-player game setting, which is particularly challenging due to the sequential nature of card games. Their approach demonstrates state-of-the-art performance by winning double championships in the COG2022 competition.

## Method Summary
The authors propose an end-to-end policy function specifically designed for multi-stage strategy card games, which can handle the complex decision-making required across different game stages. They introduce an optimistic smooth fictitious play algorithm to find Nash Equilibrium in the two-player game setting. The approach combines policy learning with game-theoretic equilibrium computation, allowing for effective decision-making in the face of strategic uncertainty. The method is validated through extensive studies on the Legends of Code and Magic game environment.

## Key Results
- Wins double championships of COG2022 competition
- Demonstrates superior performance through extensive studies
- Shows advancement over traditional approaches in multi-stage strategy card games

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works by combining end-to-end policy learning with optimistic smooth fictitious play, which allows the system to both learn effective policies through experience and converge toward equilibrium strategies in the adversarial setting. The end-to-end policy handles the complex state-action mapping required in multi-stage games, while the fictitious play component enables strategic reasoning against opponents.

## Foundational Learning
- Fictitious play: Iterative game-theoretic algorithm for finding equilibria; needed to handle strategic uncertainty in competitive settings
- Multi-stage game modeling: Capturing sequential decision-making across game phases; needed because card games have distinct stages requiring different strategies
- Policy function learning: End-to-end mapping from game states to actions; needed to handle the complexity of strategy card game decision spaces
- Nash equilibrium computation: Finding stable strategy profiles where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing strategy; needed for competitive optimality
- Smooth optimization: Regularization techniques for better convergence properties; needed to improve stability of fictitious play in complex games

## Architecture Onboarding

Component Map:
End-to-end policy function -> Optimistic smooth fictitious play algorithm -> Nash equilibrium computation

Critical Path:
Game state input -> Policy evaluation -> Action selection -> Opponent response -> Equilibrium update -> Policy refinement

Design Tradeoffs:
- Computational efficiency vs. strategic depth: The approach trades some computational overhead for better strategic reasoning compared to simpler heuristics
- Model complexity vs. generalizability: More complex models may perform better on specific games but could struggle to transfer to new domains
- Real-time performance vs. optimality: The system must balance finding near-optimal strategies with maintaining acceptable response times

Failure Signatures:
- Convergence issues in fictitious play when opponents use highly non-stationary strategies
- Policy overfitting to specific game states or opponent behaviors
- Computational bottlenecks during equilibrium computation in complex game states

First Experiments:
1. Test policy performance on simplified versions of the game with known optimal strategies
2. Evaluate fictitious play convergence on small matrix games before scaling to full game
3. Measure computational requirements and response times across different game states
