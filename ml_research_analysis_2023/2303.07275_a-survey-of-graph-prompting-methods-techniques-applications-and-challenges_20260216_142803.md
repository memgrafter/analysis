# A Survey of Graph Prompting Methods: Techniques, Applications, and Challenges

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.07275
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07275
- **Reference count**: 7
- **Primary result**: Survey categorizes graph prompt learning methods by how they incorporate graph knowledge (manual, discrete, continuous) and identifies key challenges including pre-trained model selection and knowledge injection.

## Executive Summary
This survey systematically reviews graph-based prompt learning methods that leverage graph-structured knowledge to improve prompt design and model generalization. The paper introduces a taxonomy of graph prompt methods based on how they incorporate graph knowledge (node attributes, topology, edge relations) into prompts. Applications span graph learning tasks, recommender systems, and NLP. The survey identifies key challenges including selecting appropriate pre-trained models, designing robust graph encoders, and effective knowledge injection in answer mapping functions. The authors highlight the potential of graph prompts to enhance personalization and interpretability in downstream tasks.

## Method Summary
Graph prompt learning follows the "pre-train, prompt, predict" paradigm, reformulating downstream tasks to align with pre-trained model objectives. The survey categorizes methods into three types: manual prompts use human-designed templates, discrete prompts incorporate graph knowledge through textual descriptions, and continuous prompts use learnable embeddings. Each approach differs in how it represents and injects graph-structured knowledge into prompts. The framework typically involves preprocessing graph data, designing prompt templates, generating prompted inputs for pre-trained models, obtaining predictions, and mapping results to target labels using answer mapping functions.

## Key Results
- Graph prompt learning methods can be categorized into manual, discrete, and continuous designs based on how they incorporate graph knowledge
- Graph-structured knowledge provides richer context than manual templates alone, potentially improving model generalization
- Continuous prompt embeddings can capture graph knowledge more precisely than discrete textual templates
- Key challenges include pre-trained model selection, robust graph encoders, and effective knowledge injection in answer mapping

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Graph-based prompts enable better adaptation of pre-trained models to graph-structured data by reformulating tasks in a way that matches pre-training objectives.
- Mechanism: The prompting function reformulates the downstream task as a pre-training task by embedding graph knowledge (nodes, edges, topology) into the prompt template. This allows the pre-trained model to apply its learned representations directly to the new task without extensive fine-tuning.
- Core assumption: The pre-trained model's objective (e.g., masked language modeling) can be aligned with the target task when the prompt includes sufficient task-relevant graph context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The recent 'pre-train, prompt, predict' paradigm has gained popularity as a way to learn generalizable models with limited labeled data."
  - [section 2.3]: "The 'pre-train, prompt, predict' paradigm is another way to relax the massive labeled data requirement, enabling zero-shot learning (no labeled data)."
  - [corpus]: The survey mentions that graph prompt learning "has been proven as a sufficient way to improve the vanilla prompt learning method," though specific quantitative evidence is limited.
- Break condition: If the pre-trained model's pre-training objective is too dissimilar from the target task, or if the graph knowledge injected into the prompt is not task-relevant, the prompting approach may fail to improve performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Graph-structured knowledge provides more precise and task-relevant context compared to manual templates alone, improving model generalization.
- Mechanism: Graph data explicitly models entity interactions (edges) and node attributes, allowing prompts to be tailored with structured knowledge that differentiates between input samples and provides richer context for the pre-trained model.
- Core assumption: The graph structure contains valuable, task-relevant information that can be effectively extracted and incorporated into prompts to improve model performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The limitation can be addressed by using graph data, as graphs serve as structured knowledge repositories by explicitly modeling the interaction between entities."
  - [section 2.3]: "The structured graph serves as a knowledge base by storing features of nodes and edges and modeling their interactions, upon which one is easy to infer the related knowledge to fill in the prompt template."
  - [corpus]: Limited quantitative evidence; the survey states this is "the first review of graph prompt methods," suggesting the field is still emerging.
- Break condition: If the graph structure is noisy, incomplete, or not relevant to the target task, incorporating it into prompts may introduce noise and degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Continuous prompt embeddings can capture graph knowledge more precisely than discrete textual templates, leading to better model performance.
- Mechanism: Continuous prompts represent graph knowledge as learnable embeddings that are concatenated with input representations, allowing for more flexible and precise encoding of graph information compared to fixed textual templates.
- Core assumption: The continuous representation space can encode graph knowledge more effectively than discrete tokens, and the pre-trained model can effectively process these continuous prompts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3]: "Discrete templates describe the graph knowledge with words from a finite vocabulary set, which likely excludes the optimal representations of the knowledge. In contrast, continuous representations can capture the knowledge more precisely and thoroughly."
  - [corpus]: The survey mentions this approach but provides limited quantitative evidence of its effectiveness compared to discrete prompts.
- Break condition: If the continuous prompt space is not properly regularized or if the pre-trained model is not designed to handle continuous prompts, this approach may fail to improve performance or may even degrade it.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Pre-trained model -> Prompting function -> Graph encoder -> Answer mapping function -> Task-specific head
- Critical path:
  1. Preprocess graph data and extract relevant features
  2. Design prompt template incorporating graph knowledge
  3. Generate prompted inputs for the pre-trained model
  4. Obtain predictions from the pre-trained model
  5. Map predictions to target labels using answer mapping function
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Manual vs. discrete vs. continuous prompts: Manual prompts are simple but less flexible; discrete prompts can incorporate graph knowledge but are limited by vocabulary; continuous prompts are flexible but require more parameters.
  - Task-specific vs. general prompts: Task-specific prompts may perform better but are less reusable; general prompts are more reusable but may require more adaptation.
  - Pre-trained model selection: Different pre-trained models have different strengths and weaknesses; choosing the right one is crucial for performance.
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on target task despite good performance on pre-training task
  - Prompts not effectively incorporating graph knowledge
  - Answer mapping function not correctly mapping predictions to target labels
  - Overfitting to training data due to complex prompt templates
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare performance of manual, discrete, and continuous prompts on a simple graph task (e.g., node classification)
  2. Evaluate the impact of different graph features (e.g., node attributes, edge relations, topology) on prompt performance
  3. Test the robustness of the prompting approach to noisy or incomplete graph data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How to effectively design graph-based prompt templates that balance specificity and generalization across different tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the challenge of designing prompts that can adapt to various tasks while leveraging graph-structured knowledge.
- Why unresolved: While the paper categorizes prompt designs, it does not provide a systematic method to optimize prompt templates for task-specific generalization.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study comparing task-specific vs. generalized graph prompt templates on multiple benchmarks with consistent performance improvements.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can graph knowledge be effectively integrated into answer mapping functions for tasks where pre-training objectives differ from the target task?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the need for knowledge injection in answer mapping and highlights the challenge of mapping pre-trained model outputs to target labels.
- Why unresolved: Current methods rely on manual rules or simple mappings, which may not fully utilize graph-structured knowledge for complex tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments demonstrating improved performance using graph-aware answer mapping functions compared to traditional methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can pre-trained graph neural networks (GNNs) be effectively utilized in graph-based prompt learning to leverage their structural understanding?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies the challenge of selecting appropriate pre-trained models and notes that GNNs may be more suitable for graph-related domains than language models.
- Why unresolved: There is limited research on adapting GNNs for prompt learning, and the paper does not provide a framework for integrating GNNs with graph-based prompts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing GNN-based prompt learning to traditional language model approaches on graph-specific tasks.

## Limitations
- The field is nascent with limited quantitative evidence supporting claimed benefits of different prompting approaches
- No systematic performance comparisons between manual, discrete, and continuous prompting approaches on standardized benchmarks
- Insufficient analysis of computational overhead and parameter efficiency of different prompting strategies

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The categorization framework distinguishing manual, discrete, and continuous prompts is well-grounded and provides a useful taxonomy
- **Medium confidence**: Claims about graph-structured knowledge improving prompt relevance and generalization are plausible but lack comprehensive empirical validation
- **Low confidence**: Specific claims about continuous prompts capturing graph knowledge more precisely than discrete templates remain largely theoretical
