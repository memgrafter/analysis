# Logion: Machine Learning for Greek Philology

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.01099
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01099
- Reference count: 34
- One-line primary result: BERT-based models can effectively detect scribal errors and fill gaps in premodern Greek manuscripts while revealing interpretable grammatical features.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents machine learning methods to address key challenges in Greek philology, specifically detecting scribal errors and filling gaps in premodern manuscripts. The authors train a BERT model on a large premodern Greek dataset and develop interpretable metrics combining chance, confidence, and scribal distance to flag potential errors. They also demonstrate the model's capacity to fill artificial gaps and discover that certain attention heads encode grammatical features of premodern Greek. The work bridges computational linguistics and classical studies, offering tools that domain experts can use to verify and improve upon automated analysis.

## Method Summary
The authors train a BERT model on 70 million words of premodern Greek text, then fine-tune it on specific authors like Michael Psellus. For error detection, they use a chance-confidence ratio that combines contextual probability with scribal distance metrics to flag potential errors. Gap filling is achieved by estimating missing token counts from character length and using beam search to generate probable sequences. Interpretability is assessed by analyzing attention head patterns for grammatical feature encoding. Domain expert collaboration validates results, particularly for gap filling tasks.

## Key Results
- The chance-confidence ratio effectively flags potential scribal errors when combined with scribal distance metrics
- The model successfully fills artificial gaps in manuscripts when collaborating with domain experts
- Specific attention heads in the BERT model encode select grammatical features of premodern Greek without explicit training

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The model can detect scribal errors by ranking words using the chance-confidence ratio.
- Mechanism: The ratio compares the probability of a word appearing in its context (chance) to the highest probability of any contextually similar word (conﬁdence), conditioned on scribal distance ≤ k. Words with low ratios are flagged as potential errors.
- Core assumption: Scribal errors are more likely to be words with low contextual probability relative to plausible alternatives.

- Break condition: If scribes frequently introduce errors that are contextually plausible, the ratio may not flag them effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The model can fill gaps in manuscripts by estimating the number of missing tokens and using beam search.
- Mechanism: Given a gap with known character length, the model estimates the number of missing tokens, then uses beam search over possible token sequences to fill the gap, selecting the most probable sequence.
- Core assumption: The number of missing tokens can be estimated from the number of missing characters and surrounding context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "they demonstrate the model's capacity to fill gaps caused by material deterioration of premodern manuscripts"

  - Fine-tuning on specific authors improves accuracy but reduces generalization
  - Analyzing attention heads provides interpretability but is computationally expensive
- Failure signatures:
  - High false positive rate in error detection: chance-confidence ratio thresholds may be too lenient
  - Poor gap filling performance: token estimation or beam search parameters may need adjustment
  - Uninterpretable attention patterns: the model may not have learned relevant grammatical features

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the BERT model's attention mechanism truly encode grammatical features of premodern Greek, or are the observed correlations coincidental?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors demonstrate that certain attention heads perform well at specific grammatical tasks without explicit training, suggesting potential encoding of grammatical features.
- Why unresolved: The study only shows correlation, not causation. The authors acknowledge that no part of the training objective explicitly encourages attention heads to specialize in learning individual grammatical features.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further analysis of attention heads across different languages and grammatical structures, or ablation studies to determine if the observed correlations persist when specific attention heads are removed or altered.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the model be adapted to better handle scribal errors that skew towards easier readings of the text, rather than the original?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that scribal errors may increase the probability of certain words due to the principle of "lectio difficilior potior" (the more difficult reading should be preferred), but they do not provide a solution to this issue.
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge the problem but do not propose a method to address it. The issue is complex and may require a more sophisticated model architecture or additional constraints.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of a model that incorporates the "lectio difficilior potior" principle or other methods to counteract the bias towards easier readings in scribal errors.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the model be improved to handle the variability in scribal errors across different time periods and scribes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that scribal errors are often dependent on individual scribes, the context in which they were working, and their interest in what they were copying, making it difficult to reproduce the data-generating mechanism.
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge the complexity of scribal errors but do not propose a method to account for the variability across different scribes and time periods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of a model that can adapt to different scribes and time periods, possibly through the use of domain adaptation techniques or by incorporating additional metadata about the scribes and their working context.

## Limitations

- The model may struggle to distinguish genuine scribal errors from legitimate textual variations like archaic spellings or regional variants
- The integration methodology between computational methods and domain expert knowledge is not fully specified
