# Assisting clinical practice with fuzzy probabilistic decision trees

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.07788
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07788
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: Fuzzy Probabilistic Trees achieve 96.9% accuracy on thyroid nodule classification and 77.2% on CKD progression prediction while maintaining interpretability

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Fuzzy Probabilistic Trees (FPTs), an interpretable AI method that combines probabilistic trees with fuzzy logic to assist clinical decision-making. FPTs allow for the representation of vague medical concepts (like "high fever" or "young patient") using fuzzy sets, enabling smoother transitions between states and better handling of uncertainty compared to traditional crisp thresholds. The method was tested on two clinical case studies: classifying malignant thyroid nodules (accuracy: 96.9%) and predicting progression to end-stage renal disease in chronic kidney disease patients (accuracy: 77.2%). FPTs outperformed traditional probability trees and matched or exceeded interpretable benchmark models like logistic regression and decision trees, particularly in sensitivity for detecting positive cases. A user-friendly interface was developed to support clinician interaction with the model, promoting trust and transparency in AI-assisted diagnosis.

## Method Summary
Fuzzy Probabilistic Trees integrate fuzzy logic with probabilistic decision trees to create an interpretable AI method for clinical decision support. The approach uses fuzzy membership functions to represent vague medical concepts, allowing smooth transitions between states rather than artificial thresholds. Each feature can be either crisp or fuzzy, with fuzzy variables assigned membership degrees across multiple sets. The prediction algorithm traverses the tree, applying fuzzy weights at each node based on membership degrees. A user-friendly interface was developed to display probabilities and support counterfactual reasoning, allowing clinicians to test "what if" scenarios by adjusting input conditions and observing probability changes.

## Key Results
- Thyroid nodule classification: FPT achieved 96.9% accuracy, slightly outperforming traditional probability trees with improved sensitivity (1% point higher)
- CKD progression prediction: FPT achieved 77.2% accuracy, outperforming probability trees by 5.5% in sensitivity while maintaining similar overall performance
- FPT matched or exceeded interpretable benchmark models (Logistic Regression, Decision Trees) across both case studies
- User interface successfully demonstrated counterfactual reasoning capabilities for clinical hypothesis testing

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Fuzzy membership functions allow smooth transitions between medical states, reducing artificial threshold effects.
- **Mechanism:** Fuzzy sets model partial membership (0-1) for vague concepts like "high fever" or "large nodule," enabling the FPT to consider multiple paths in the tree weighted by membership degree.
- **Core assumption:** Medical variables often exist on a continuum rather than discrete categories.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "introducing fuzzy variables into the probabilistic model brings significant nuances that are lost when using the crisp thresholds set by traditional probabilistic decision trees."
  - [section 4.1.2]: "The FPT considers the feature 'nodule dimensions' to be a fuzzy variable... a nodule with dimensions of 18mm belongs to the class of 'large nodules' to a (membership) degree of 0.80."

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The FPT provides inherently interpretable predictions in the form of probabilities and counterfactual reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** The tree structure maintains human-understandable rules, while fuzzy integration allows reasoning about "what if" scenarios by adjusting membership degrees and observing probability changes.
- **Core assumption:** Clinicians can interpret probabilistic outputs better than black-box classifications.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "one of the methodology's strength is the capability to decrease the frequency of misdiagnoses by providing an estimate of uncertainties and counterfactuals."
  - [section 6.1]: "An example question that may be answered with the help of counterfactual statements in the CKD case study is: 'Given a patient that developed kidney failure and did not take any medication. What would be the probability of this patient developing kidney failure if he/she had in fact taken RASI medication?'"

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** FPT achieves comparable or better performance than traditional interpretable models on clinical datasets.
- **Mechanism:** By integrating fuzzy logic, the FPT captures nuanced relationships that crisp thresholds miss, leading to improved sensitivity in detecting positive cases.
- **Core assumption:** The data contains genuine fuzzy relationships that are relevant for prediction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.1]: "The FPT slightly outperforms the PT across all performance metrics. The biggest difference is in the sensitivity (FPT scores 1 percent point higher)."
  - [section 5.2]: "The main difference between the two models lies in the sensitivity scores, where the FPT outperforms the PT by 5.5%."

## Foundational Learning

- **Fuzzy Set Theory**
  - Why needed here: Enables modeling of vague medical concepts like "young patient" or "high fever" where crisp thresholds would be arbitrary.
  - Quick check question: What is the membership degree of a patient aged 48 in a fuzzy set "50Plus" with linear MF from 45 to 55?
  - Answer: (48-45)/(55-45) = 0.3

- **Probability Trees**
  - Why needed here: Provides the interpretable tree structure where each path represents a combination of feature values with associated probabilities.
  - Quick check question: In a PT with variables X∈{A,B} and Y∈{1,2}, how many total realisations exist?
  - Answer: 2×2 = 4 total realisations

- **Counterfactual Reasoning**
  - Why needed here: Allows clinicians to test "what if" scenarios by modifying input conditions and observing probability changes.
  - Quick check question: What type of reasoning allows testing alternate realities in the FPT?
  - Answer: Counterfactual reasoning

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Fuzzy membership functions -> Probabilistic tree structure -> Prediction algorithm -> User interface
- **Critical path:** 1. Data preparation and fuzzy set definition 2. Tree construction with transition probabilities 3. Prediction using Algorithm 1 (tree traversal with fuzzy weighting) 4. Result display in user interface
- **Design tradeoffs:** Crisp vs fuzzy variables: Crisp is simpler but loses nuance; fuzzy is more accurate but requires careful MF design; Tree depth vs interpretability: Deeper trees capture more complex relationships but become harder to interpret; Sensitivity vs specificity: Adjusting probability threshold affects which patients are flagged
- **Failure signatures:** Poor performance despite good data quality: Likely indicates poorly designed fuzzy membership functions; Extremely long prediction times: May indicate inefficient tree traversal algorithm; Inconsistent results across similar patients: Could indicate missing important features or poorly calibrated MFs
- **First 3 experiments:** 1. Implement FPT on synthetic thyroid nodule data with known fuzzy relationships to verify Algorithm 1 produces expected probabilities 2. Compare FPT performance vs PT on a simple clinical dataset where fuzzy relationships are well-established 3. Create a simple user interface prototype to test counterfactual reasoning functionality with mock data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How can FPT models be extended to support multi-class target variables beyond binary classification? The paper only demonstrates FPT performance on binary classification tasks, leaving the methodology for multi-class scenarios unexplored.

- What heuristics or optimization methods could be used to automate the design of probabilistic trees in FPT? Current FPT construction relies on domain expertise and manual feature selection, which limits scalability and may introduce bias.

- How would FPT performance change when trained on larger, more diverse patient cohorts? Both case studies used relatively small datasets, raising questions about generalizability and performance on larger populations.

## Limitations

- The effectiveness of FPTs heavily depends on the quality of fuzzy membership function design, which requires significant domain expertise that may not always be available
- The clinical significance of marginal performance gains (1-5.5% improvements) in real-world diagnostic accuracy remains unclear
- User interface evaluation appears limited to qualitative feedback rather than systematic usability testing

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the interpretability advantage: The tree structure inherently provides human-understandable decision paths
- **Medium confidence** in performance claims: Statistical comparisons show improvements, but confidence intervals overlap substantially in some cases
- **Low confidence** in generalizability: Only two case studies from different clinical domains, raising questions about broader applicability

## Next Checks

1. Conduct a controlled experiment where clinicians use both FPT and traditional PT interfaces to diagnose synthetic cases, measuring both accuracy and time-to-diagnosis to quantify the practical benefit of fuzzy reasoning
2. Perform sensitivity analysis on membership function parameters to determine how robust the performance improvements are to variations in fuzzy set definitions
3. Implement cross-domain testing by applying the same FPT methodology to a third clinical dataset from a different specialty to assess generalizability beyond the two presented case studies
