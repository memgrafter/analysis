# Calibration of Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.10761
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10761
- Reference count: 36
- Key outcome: Temperature scaling generally improves calibration without hurting accuracy, while histogram binning and matrix scaling can overfit with many classes

## Executive Summary
This paper surveys calibration methods for neural networks, focusing on techniques to produce reliable confidence estimates alongside predictions. The survey covers post-processing methods like histogram binning, isotonic regression, and temperature scaling, as well as training-based approaches such as label smoothing and focal loss. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet, and Tiny ImageNet datasets compare methods across metrics including Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Negative Log-Likelihood, and Brier Score. The results show that temperature scaling consistently improves calibration while preserving accuracy, making it a reliable default choice.

## Method Summary
The survey evaluates calibration methods across four image classification datasets using pre-trained neural networks from various repositories. Post-processing methods (histogram binning, isotonic regression, temperature scaling, vector scaling, matrix scaling) are applied to uncalibrated model outputs, while training-based methods (label smoothing, focal loss) modify the loss function during training. Calibration quality is assessed using ECE, cwECE, MCE, NLL, and Brier Score on held-out test sets. Reliability diagrams visualize the relationship between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy.

## Key Results
- Temperature scaling consistently improves calibration across all datasets without degrading accuracy
- Label smoothing and focal loss produce better-calibrated models during training compared to cross-entropy loss
- Histogram binning and matrix scaling show overfitting tendencies when the number of classes is large
- Matrix scaling degrades classification quality significantly in large-scale problems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Temperature scaling improves calibration by adjusting the sharpness of the softmax output distribution without changing the predicted class.
- Mechanism: Temperature scaling introduces a scalar temperature parameter T that divides the logits before applying softmax. This reduces or increases the entropy of the output distribution, allowing the model to produce more calibrated probabilities.
- Core assumption: The model's predicted class remains unchanged after temperature scaling, but the confidence estimates become more aligned with true probabilities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Temperature scaling generally improves calibration without hurting accuracy"
  - [section] "Temperature scaling...doesn't affect classification predictions, while other calibration options almost always reduce accuracy"
- Break condition: If the model is already well-calibrated or if the calibration set is too small, temperature scaling may overfit and degrade calibration.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Label smoothing improves calibration by preventing overconfident predictions during training.
- Mechanism: Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target distribution with a smoothed version that assigns some probability mass to non-target classes. This encourages the model to be less confident in its predictions, leading to better-calibrated probabilities.
- Core assumption: By reducing the maximum probability mass assigned to the correct class, label smoothing prevents the model from becoming overconfident, which improves calibration.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "methods like label smoothing yield better-calibrated models during training"
  - [section] "Focal loss and label smoothing result in better calibrated models than the ones optimizing the cross-entropy"
- Break condition: If the smoothing parameter is too large, it may degrade the model's accuracy or if the dataset is very small, it may lead to underfitting.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Histogram binning improves calibration by mapping confidence estimates to empirical accuracy rates within each bin.
- Mechanism: Histogram binning divides the confidence range into bins and replaces all confidence estimates within each bin with the observed accuracy rate for that bin. This directly aligns confidence with empirical accuracy.
- Core assumption: The empirical accuracy within each bin is a good estimate of the true probability, and the bin boundaries can be chosen to minimize calibration error.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Histogram binning...A calibration map in histogram binning is piecewise constant"
  - [section] "Histogram binning changes probabilities too aggressively when the number of classes is large"
- Break condition: If the number of classes is large or the calibration set is small, histogram binning may overfit and produce poor calibration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do calibration methods perform on real-world datasets with extreme class imbalance compared to balanced academic datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet, and Tiny ImageNet, which have relatively balanced class distributions. The focal loss method is mentioned as originally designed for class imbalance, but its performance on highly imbalanced real-world data is not explored.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only evaluate calibration on balanced or moderately balanced datasets, leaving the effectiveness of calibration methods on severely imbalanced data untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical experiments comparing calibration methods on real-world imbalanced datasets (e.g., medical imaging, rare event detection) would provide evidence of their robustness and generalizability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between model architecture complexity and the effectiveness of different calibration techniques?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper compares calibration methods across various architectures (DenseNet, ResNet, VGG, etc.) but does not systematically analyze how architectural complexity (e.g., depth, width, attention mechanisms) influences calibration performance.
- Why unresolved: While different architectures are tested, the paper does not isolate the impact of architectural features on calibration, leaving questions about which designs are inherently better calibrated.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled study varying architectural parameters (e.g., depth, width, attention) while keeping other factors constant would clarify their impact on calibration.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of calibration set size affect the performance of post-processing methods, especially in high-dimensional or multi-class settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that methods like matrix scaling can overfit when the number of classes is large and the calibration set is small, but it does not systematically explore the trade-off between calibration set size and method performance.
- Why unresolved: The experiments use fixed calibration set sizes, and the paper does not investigate how varying these sizes impacts calibration quality, especially for complex methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying calibration set sizes across different datasets and methods would reveal optimal sizing strategies and the robustness of calibration techniques to limited data.

## Limitations
- The survey focuses primarily on image classification datasets, limiting generalizability to other domains
- Matrix scaling can be computationally prohibitive for large-scale problems
- The analysis assumes the original model is well-trained and that the calibration set is representative

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The effectiveness of temperature scaling for calibration (supported by multiple experiments across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet, and Tiny ImageNet)
- Medium Confidence: The claim that label smoothing improves calibration during training (based on comparisons with cross-entropy loss, but dependent on the specific smoothing parameter)
- Low Confidence: The assertion that histogram binning is problematic for many classes (based on theoretical reasoning rather than extensive empirical validation across diverse datasets)

## Next Checks
1. Cross-domain validation: Test temperature scaling and label smoothing on non-image datasets (e.g., text classification or tabular data) to verify generalizability beyond image classification tasks
2. Calibration set size sensitivity: Systematically vary the size of the calibration set to determine the minimum sample size required for each method to maintain calibration without overfitting
3. Multi-label extension: Evaluate whether the calibration methods (particularly temperature scaling and label smoothing) transfer effectively to multi-label classification problems where confidence interpretation differs from single-label scenarios
