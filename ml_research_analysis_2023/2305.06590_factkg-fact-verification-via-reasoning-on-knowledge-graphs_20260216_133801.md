# FactKG: Fact Verification via Reasoning on Knowledge Graphs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06590
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06590
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Introduces FACTKG dataset with 108k claims across 5 reasoning types, showing graph evidence improves fact verification accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces FACTKG, a novel dataset for fact verification using knowledge graphs. The dataset contains 108k claims derived from WebNLG graph-text pairs, categorized into five reasoning types: One-hop, Conjunction, Existence, Multi-hop, and Negation. Claims are transformed into both written and colloquial styles. The authors also develop GEAR, a baseline approach that uses graph evidence for fact verification, demonstrating superior performance compared to claim-only baselines, particularly for existence and negation types.

## Method Summary
The FACTKG dataset is constructed by generating claims from WebNLG graph-text pairs and transforming them into written and colloquial styles. The GEAR baseline approach consists of two modules: subgraph retrieval (using relation and hop classifiers) and claim verification (using a Transformer encoder). Claims are verified by retrieving relevant graph paths and using them as evidence to determine if the claim is SUPPORTED or REFUTED. The dataset is evaluated using accuracy metrics and cross-style fact verification to assess generalization.

## Key Results
- GEAR substantially outperforms claim-only baselines, especially for existence and negation types
- Cross-style evaluation shows good generalization between written and colloquial claims
- Multi-hop claims remain challenging due to increased complexity in graph retrieval
- GEAR's graph evidence approach improves fact verification accuracy over claim-only methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Knowledge graphs provide more reliable reasoning than text-based fact verification because they allow clear inferential links between evidence and verdicts
- Mechanism: The structured nature of KGs enables explicit representation of reasoning processes using logic rules on nodes and edges, making inferential steps transparent and verifiable
- Core assumption: The graph structure itself is sufficient to encode all necessary reasoning steps without additional context from unstructured text
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A KG consists of nodes and edges which makes it clear how concepts are linked together, allowing machines to reason over chains of topics."
  - [section 1] "Compared to text and tables, a KG can simply represent reasoning process with logic rules on nodes and edges (Liang et al., 2022)."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence supporting this mechanism's effectiveness
- Break condition: When claims require reasoning that depends heavily on contextual or qualitative information not captured in the graph structure

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Graph evidence improves fact verification accuracy, especially for existence and negation types of claims
- Mechanism: By retrieving relevant subgraphs as evidence, the model can directly verify claims against structured knowledge rather than relying solely on learned patterns from claim text
- Core assumption: The subgraph retrieval process can find evidence paths that are both relevant and sufficient for verification
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.4] "GEAR substantially outperforms Claim Only baselines. Especially, in existence and negation, GEAR substantially outperforms Claim Only baselines."
  - [section 4.3.2] "GEAR consists of the subgraph retrieval module and the claim verification module."
  - [corpus] Weak - no corpus evidence showing this is the primary driver of performance improvement
- Break condition: When the subgraph retrieval process fails to find relevant evidence paths or retrieves misleading information

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Cross-style generalization works because colloquial claims are constructed in various forms that capture the semantic essence of written claims
- Mechanism: The colloquial style transfer process preserves the core factual content while adapting the linguistic style, allowing models trained on written claims to generalize to colloquial claims
- Core assumption: The style transfer process maintains semantic equivalence while changing linguistic form
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.4] "Contradict our expectation, in C→W setting, BERT and BlueBERT show an improvement in performance over C→C."
  - [section 3.2.1] "Following the filtering process, the AFLITE method (Sakaguchi et al., 2019), which utilizes adversarial filtering, is applied to select the most colloquial style sentence among the surviving sentences."
  - [corpus] Weak - no corpus evidence supporting this generalization claim
- Break condition: When colloquial claims introduce ambiguities or cultural references not present in the original written claims

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of GEAR degrade as the number of hops in multi-hop claims increases?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that GEAR performs worse on multi-hop claims due to increased complexity in graph retrieval and potential incorrect graph paths
- Why unresolved: The paper only reports overall performance on multi-hop claims, not how performance varies with the number of hops
- What evidence would resolve it: An ablation study showing GEAR's accuracy on multi-hop claims with different hop counts (2-hop, 3-hop, 4-hop, etc.)

### Open Question 2
- Question: How much does the colloquial style transfer affect model performance compared to the written style?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper reports cross-style evaluation results but doesn't compare colloquial style performance to written style performance directly
- Why unresolved: The cross-style evaluation only compares different training/testing style combinations, not the absolute performance difference between styles
- What evidence would resolve it: Separate performance metrics for models trained and tested exclusively on colloquial style claims versus written style claims

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of using more sophisticated subgraph retrieval methods on multi-hop claim performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests that GEAR's subgraph retrieval method struggles with multi-hop claims and proposes this as a limitation
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses a basic BERT-based retrieval method and doesn't explore alternative approaches
- What evidence would resolve it: Performance comparison between GEAR's current retrieval method and more advanced graph neural network-based retrieval methods on multi-hop claims

## Limitations

- The dataset construction relies heavily on automatic claim generation from WebNLG graph-text pairs, which may introduce biases or unrealistic claim patterns not representative of real-world fact verification scenarios
- The evaluation focuses on accuracy metrics without detailed error analysis across different reasoning types or styles
- The GEAR baseline uses a relatively simple subgraph retrieval approach that may not scale well to larger, more complex knowledge graphs or claims requiring deeper reasoning chains

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The dataset construction methodology and the basic claim categorization into five reasoning types are well-defined and reproducible. The experimental setup comparing claim-only vs. graph-evidence baselines is clearly specified.

**Medium Confidence**: The cross-style generalization results showing improved performance in C→W setting require further validation, as the explanation provided seems counterintuitive and may indicate dataset artifacts rather than genuine model capability.

**Low Confidence**: The effectiveness of the subgraph retrieval mechanism in GEAR for complex multi-hop claims is not thoroughly validated, and the paper lacks detailed ablation studies to isolate the contribution of different components.

## Next Checks

1. **Subgraph Retrieval Accuracy**: Manually evaluate the subgraph retrieval module's performance on a sample of development set claims to verify that retrieved paths are both relevant and sufficient for claim verification, particularly for multi-hop and negation types.

2. **Cross-Style Robustness**: Conduct additional cross-style experiments with more granular analysis, including confusion matrix analysis and error categorization, to understand why C→W setting shows unexpected improvements and whether this reflects genuine capability or dataset artifacts.

3. **Generalization to External Claims**: Test the trained models on a small set of human-annotated claims or claims from different domains to assess whether the patterns learned from generated claims transfer to more realistic fact verification scenarios.
