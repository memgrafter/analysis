# On adversarial robustness and the use of Wasserstein ascent-descent dynamics to enforce it

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.03662
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03662
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Achieves 62.21% accuracy against PGD attacks on MNIST using Wasserstein adversarial training

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a novel approach to adversarial robustness by framing supervised learning as a min-max game between a learner and an adversary over probability measures. The authors propose a system of interacting particles that evolve according to Wasserstein gradient ascent-descent dynamics, which converge to mean-field limit PDEs under appropriate assumptions. They show that time averages of these dynamics yield approximate Nash equilibria of the adversarial learning problem, and validate their approach on MNIST with improved robustness compared to standard training.

## Method Summary
The method trains a robust classifier by interpreting adversarial learning as a game between a learner (minimizing loss) and an adversary (maximizing loss within an L2-ball). This game is formulated over probability measures using Wasserstein geometry. The algorithm uses interacting particle systems that approximate Wasserstein gradient flows, with particles representing both data points and model parameters. Through time-averaging of the particle dynamics, the method converges to approximate Nash equilibria, providing adversarial robustness while maintaining accuracy on clean data.

## Key Results
- Achieves 62.21% accuracy against PGD attacks on MNIST versus 0.68% for baseline
- Maintains competitive clean accuracy at 96.34% compared to standard training
- Demonstrates relative loss improvements with ra=0.21% and rm=1.82%

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The particle system converges to a mean-field limit PDE that approximates a Nash equilibrium of the adversarial learning game.
- Mechanism: As the number of particles N → ∞, the empirical measures πN and νN converge to solutions of the mean-field PDEs in (1.10), which are gradient ascent-descent dynamics in the Wasserstein space of probability measures. The time-averages of these dynamics converge to approximate Nash equilibria under convexity-concavity assumptions.
- Core assumption: The payoff function U is convex in ν and concave in π in the linear interpolation sense (Assumption 1.13), and the first variations are Lipschitz continuous (Assumption 1.4).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "These interacting particle dynamics are shown to converge toward appropriate mean-field limit equations in certain large number of particles regimes... these mean-field equations converge, in the large time limit, toward approximate Nash equilibria of the original adversarial learning problems."
  - [section] "Theorem 1.16 (Long time behavior mean-field PDE)... under Assumptions 1.4, 1.6, and 1.13... there exists T* such that for all t>T* supπ∈P(Z2) s.t πz=µ U(π*,ν(t))−infν∈P(Θ) U(π(t),ν*)≤ε."
- Break condition: If the initializations π0 and ν0 do not satisfy the mass assumptions (dν0/dθ > k, dπ0(˜z|z)/d˜z(˜z) > k), the convergence guarantees may not hold.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: For nonconvex-concave problems, a Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition on U(·,ν) ensures convergence to approximate Nash equilibria even with arbitrary π0 initialization.
- Mechanism: When the adversarial cost C has a strong convexity parameter ca large enough, the PL condition (Assumption 1.18) holds, which allows replacing the need for careful initialization with a time-dependent learning rate schedule. This leads to convergence guarantees via Theorem 1.21.
- Core assumption: The PL condition holds (e.g., when Z is convex, activation and loss functions are twice differentiable, and ca is large enough).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Example 1.20... if the parameter ca is large enough, then Assumption (1.18) is satisfied."
  - [section] "Theorem 1.21... Suppose Assumptions 1.4, 1.6, 1.13, and 1.18 hold... there exists K0,r0,r1,t0 > 0 such that, if K≥K0 and η/K≤r1, then for all t≥ max{t0,K/r0}, we have sup˜π∈P(Z2) s.t. ˜πz=µ U(˜π,¯νt)−inf˜ν∈P(Θ) U(¯πt,˜ν)≤ε."
- Break condition: If the PL condition fails (e.g., ca too small or Z non-convex), the convergence guarantee may not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The KL divergence between the learner's measure ν and its perturbed version ˆν evolves according to the gradient ascent dynamics, providing a Lyapunov function for convergence.
- Mechanism: By analyzing the time evolution of H(ˆνt||νt) and H(ˆπt||πt), we can bound the change in payoff U(π*,ν(t))−U(π(t),ν*) in terms of these divergences and the dual norms of the first variations, leading to the ε-Nash equilibrium guarantee in Lemma 4.5.
- Core assumption: The measures ˆπ0 and ˆν0 have densities with respect to π0 and ν0 that are in L∞.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Proposition 4.3... H(ˆνt||νt)−H(ˆν0||ν0)≤κ∫₀ᵗ(∫ΘUν(πs,νs;θ)d(ˆνs−νs)(θ))ds, ∀t≥0."
  - [section] "Lemma 4.5... U(π*,¯ν(t))−U(¯π(t),ν*)≤B(Qπ(π0,π*;κBt)+Qν(ν0,ν*;κBt))+2B²t∫₀ᵗ∫₀ˢητdτds."
- Break condition: If the densities dˆν0/dν0 or dˆπ0/dπ0 are not in L∞, the time evolution of KL divergence may not be controlled.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Wasserstein gradient flows and optimal transport geometry
  - Why needed here: The algorithm is interpreted as a gradient ascent-descent in the Wasserstein space of probability measures, which requires understanding the Riemannian structure and continuity equation formulation.
  - Quick check question: What is the role of the projection maps PZ and PΘ in Algorithm 1, and how do they relate to the Wasserstein geometry?

- Concept: Interacting particle systems and mean-field limits
  - Why needed here: The convergence analysis relies on showing that the particle system approximates the mean-field PDE as N → ∞, which requires knowledge of propagation of chaos and chaos propagation results.
  - Quick check question: How does the initialization condition (3.12) ensure that the particle system's conditional distributions are consistent with the mean-field limit?

- Concept: Convexity and concavity in the OT sense vs. linear interpolation
  - Why needed here: The convergence results require convexity-concavity assumptions on U, but the OT geometry may not induce geodesic convexity, so the results only guarantee approximate Nash equilibria.
  - Quick check question: Why is it important to distinguish between convexity in the linear interpolation sense (Assumption 1.13) and geodesic convexity with respect to the Wasserstein geometry?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input: Data distribution µ, parameter space Θ, activation function f, loss function ℓ, adversarial budget parameter ca
  - Algorithm: Wasserstein ascent-descent dynamics with particle system (Algorithm 1)
  - Output: Time-averaged measures ¯ν(t) and ¯π(t) that are approximate Nash equilibria
  - Supporting modules: Mean-field limit analysis, KL divergence evolution analysis, numerical experiments

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize particle system with Zi,0 in support of µ and appropriate weights
  2. Iterate ascent-descent updates for Zi,t, ˜Zi,t, ωi,t, ϑi,t, αi,t
  3. Compute time averages ¯zij and ¯ϑk
  4. Use ¯ν(t) and ¯π(t) as approximate Nash equilibria

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Memory vs. accuracy: Time-averaging vs. keeping all intermediate networks
  - Convexity vs. generality: Linear interpolation convexity vs. geodesic convexity
  - Initialization vs. convergence: Careful initialization vs. PL condition for arbitrary initialization

- Failure signatures:
  - Divergence of particle system: Weights ωi,t or αi,t becoming too large or too small
  - Poor convergence: KL divergence H(ˆνt||νt) or H(ˆπt||πt) not decreasing
  - Numerical instability: Gradients ∇˜zUπ or ∇θUν becoming too large

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test convergence of particle system for simple convex-concave problem (e.g., quadratic payoff) with known Nash equilibrium
  2. Verify KL divergence evolution for simple initialization (e.g., Dirac measures)
  3. Check numerical stability of Algorithm 1 for small N and simple neural network architecture

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the use of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with mini-batches affect the convergence properties of the proposed Wasserstein gradient ascent-descent dynamics compared to full-batch updates?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note that their theoretical analysis assumes full-batch updates, but acknowledge that in practice mini-batch SGD is commonly used. They explicitly state that studying the effect of SGD on the resulting PDE system is an interesting direction for future research.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical framework presented in the paper is built on deterministic, full-batch updates. The authors have not provided analysis or experiments demonstrating how stochasticity from mini-batch sampling affects convergence rates or solution quality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Numerical experiments comparing full-batch and mini-batch implementations on various datasets, showing convergence trajectories and final solution quality. Theoretical analysis establishing convergence bounds under stochastic gradient noise.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the proposed framework be extended to handle multiclass classification problems in a way that is both theoretically sound and practically effective?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss this as a limitation in their conclusions, noting that while their framework works naturally for regression problems, extending it to multiclass classification is less obvious. They specifically mention the challenge of defining a reasonable cost structure for the adversary when the response variable has a discrete structure.
- Why unresolved: The current formulation relies on the Wasserstein distance over a combined feature and label space, which is straightforward for real-valued labels but not for discrete class labels. The authors have not provided a concrete method for handling multiclass problems.
- What evidence would resolve it: A concrete formulation of the multiclass adversarial learning problem within the Wasserstein framework, accompanied by theoretical convergence guarantees and numerical experiments on standard multiclass datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of the time-averaged networks compare to other state-of-the-art adversarial training methods when evaluated on robustness to stronger attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors report results on MNIST using PGD attacks with 20 steps, showing their method achieves 62.21% accuracy compared to 0.68% for a baseline. However, they acknowledge this is a preliminary investigation and note that calculating time averages on weights only (as opposed to full resampling) may be more effective in some settings.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation is limited to a single dataset and attack strength. The authors have not compared their approach to other modern adversarial training techniques, nor have they tested against stronger attacks or real-world adversarial examples.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive benchmark comparisons on multiple datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, ImageNet) against state-of-the-art adversarial training methods, testing against various attack strengths and types (e.g., PGD with more steps, AutoAttack).

## Limitations
- Theoretical convergence guarantees rely on convexity-concavity assumptions that may not hold for realistic neural networks
- Particle system convergence requires careful initialization conditions that may be difficult to verify in practice
- Numerical experiments are limited to a single dataset (MNIST) with a relatively simple architecture

## Confidence
- Convergence theory for convex-concave problems: High confidence, supported by rigorous proofs
- PL condition analysis for nonconvex problems: Medium confidence, depends on specific problem structure
- Numerical validation: Low confidence, limited experimental scope and lack of ablation studies
