# How Ready are Pre-trained Abstractive Models and LLMs for Legal Case Judgement Summarization?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01248
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01248
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 19
- Key outcome: Abstractive models achieve higher ROUGE/METEOR/BLEU scores than extractive models but generate inconsistent and hallucinated summaries, requiring human-in-the-loop approach

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the readiness of pre-trained abstractive summarization models and large language models for legal case judgement summarization using Indian Supreme Court documents. The authors test multiple state-of-the-art models including domain-specific variants and find that while abstractive models outperform extractive baselines on traditional metrics, they consistently produce hallucinations and inconsistencies. These include incorrect dates, person names, and irrelevant court references that would be problematic in legal contexts. The research concludes that current models are not ready for fully automatic deployment and recommends a human-in-the-loop approach for practical applications.

## Method Summary
The study applies 8 models (2 LLMs with 2 variations each, 2 legal-domain abstractive models with 2 variations each) to 100 test documents from the IN-Abs dataset containing 7,130 Indian Supreme Court judgement-summary pairs. Long documents exceeding 1,024 words are chunked into segments, each summarized individually, then concatenated. Models are evaluated using traditional metrics (ROUGE, BLEU, METEOR) and consistency metrics (SummaC for logical consistency, NumPrec for number accuracy, NEPrec for named entity precision). The evaluation compares general vs. domain-specific fine-tuned variants to assess the benefits of legal domain adaptation.

## Key Results
- Abstractive models (Legal-Pegasus, Legal-LED) achieve higher ROUGE, METEOR, and BLEU scores than extractive models but produce inconsistent summaries
- Domain-specific fine-tuning (LegPegasus-IN, LegLED-IN) improves performance over general models, showing benefits of in-domain adaptation
- All models exhibit significant hallucinations including incorrect dates, person names, and irrelevant court references
- Current models are not ready for fully automatic deployment in legal case summarization

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific fine-tuning techniques could be employed to reduce hallucinations and inconsistencies in abstractive summarization models for legal documents?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how domain-specific fine-tuning (LegPegasus-IN and LegLED-IN) improves performance over general models (LegPegasus and LegLED), but still notes the presence of inconsistencies.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the benefits of fine-tuning but does not explore specific techniques beyond basic fine-tuning on legal datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different fine-tuning strategies (e.g., curriculum learning, adversarial training, multi-task learning) on legal summarization datasets with quantitative metrics showing reduction in hallucinations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we develop automated methods to detect subtle inconsistencies in abstractive legal summaries, such as confusing appellant names with lawyer names?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the difficulty of detecting certain types of errors (e.g., confusing appellant names with lawyer names) and states that these errors are "very difficult to detect by automatic methods."
- Why unresolved: Current consistency metrics (NumPrec, NEPrec, SummaC) are insufficient for catching complex semantic inconsistencies in legal documents.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and evaluation of new detection algorithms specifically designed for legal entity disambiguation and relationship extraction, validated on datasets with annotated inconsistencies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal chunk size and summary length allocation strategy for summarizing long legal documents using LLMs with token limitations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a uniform chunking strategy where each chunk is allocated the same summary length based on document compression ratio, acknowledging this is "not a simple way of knowing the relative importance of different chunks."
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the limitation of uniform allocation but does not propose or evaluate alternative strategies for importance-based chunking.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing different chunking strategies (e.g., content-based importance scoring, hierarchical summarization) with metrics measuring summary coherence and information retention.

## Limitations
- Single legal domain (Indian Supreme Court) restricts cross-jurisdiction applicability
- Absence of human evaluation limits understanding of summary utility and practical accuracy
- Reliance on automated consistency metrics may miss nuanced legal inaccuracies

## Confidence
- High confidence: Models exhibit significant consistency issues (incorrect dates, person names, irrelevant court references)
- Medium confidence: Domain adaptation benefits shown through comparison of fine-tuned vs. general models
- Low confidence: Chunking strategy effectiveness due to equal importance assumption without validation

## Next Checks
