# Safety Assessment of Chinese Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.10436
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10436
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 4
- Key outcome: Comprehensive safety benchmark covering 8 typical scenarios and 6 instruction attacks for 15 LLMs, finding instruction attacks most effective at exposing safety issues

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive safety assessment benchmark for Chinese large language models (LLMs), addressing the growing concerns about LLMs generating unsafe content. The benchmark covers 8 typical safety scenarios (e.g., insult, discrimination, crimes) and 6 types of instruction attacks. The authors develop a safety evaluation method using LLM's own evaluation capability, automatically judging the safety of generated responses. They assess 15 LLMs including OpenAI's GPT series and Chinese models, finding that instruction attacks are more effective at exposing safety issues. Their public leaderboard ranks models across 14 safety dimensions. Additionally, they release a dataset of 100k augmented safety prompts (SAFETY PROMPTS) to promote research in LLM safety. The work provides both a practical assessment framework and valuable resources for developing safer LLMs.

## Method Summary
The paper develops a comprehensive safety assessment framework using LLM-based evaluation. The method involves collecting safety prompts (both manual and augmented), using GPT-3.5-turbo as an evaluator with dynamic prompt templates for each safety scenario, and calculating safety scores as the proportion of safe responses. The evaluation process converts natural language judgments into binary safe/unsafe labels, with scores averaged across all scenarios. The framework covers 8 typical safety scenarios and 6 instruction attack types, using a leaderboard interface to rank 15 evaluated LLMs including both OpenAI GPT series and Chinese models.

## Key Results
- Instruction attacks are more effective at exposing safety issues than typical scenarios across all evaluated LLMs
- ChatGPT performs significantly better than other models in resisting instruction attacks, particularly goal hijacking
- The LLM-based evaluation method provides a scalable approach for safety assessment, though correlation with human evaluation needs validation
- Chinese models generally show lower safety performance compared to ChatGPT, especially in instruction attack scenarios

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Using LLM-based evaluation provides a scalable and consistent safety assessment method
- Mechanism: The paper leverages the strong evaluation ability of LLMs by prompting them to judge whether generated responses are safe or not, converting natural language judgments into binary labels (safe/unsafe)
- Core assumption: LLMs can accurately assess safety content when given appropriate prompts and context
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we utilize the LLM's strong evaluation ability and develop it as a safety evaluator by prompting"
  - [section]: "Recently, LLMs have been proven to have a strong evaluation ability (Wang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Gilardi et al., 2023). Following these related works, we design prompt templates and conduct safety evaluations by 'asking' LLM with the aid of the strong evaluation ability of LLM itself"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - the related papers focus on general LLM evaluation rather than safety-specific evaluation

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Instruction attacks are more effective at exposing safety issues than typical scenarios
- Mechanism: The paper identifies six types of instruction attacks (goal hijacking, prompt leaking, etc.) that bypass normal safety guardrails by manipulating the model's context or role
- Core assumption: Models are less resistant to sophisticated adversarial inputs than to direct safety violations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we find that instruction attacks are more likely to expose safety issues of all LLMs"
  - [section]: "The performance gap of Instruction Attack safety is the biggest reason for the overall safety gap between ChatGPT and the other models"
  - [corpus]: Moderate evidence - related work (Perez and Ribeiro, 2022) also identified goal hijacking and prompt leaking as effective attack vectors

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Data augmentation using prompted examples improves safety prompt diversity and coverage
- Mechanism: The paper uses GPT-3.5-turbo to generate additional safety prompts based on manually written examples, creating a 100k prompt library
- Core assumption: LLMs can generate realistic safety-relevant prompts that follow the patterns of human-written examples
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we augment the collected safety prompts by LLMs and make them publicly available as SAFETY PROMPTS"
  - [section]: "We give three examples randomly sampled in manually-written prompts into the prompt template, which can promote the diversity and groundedness of LLM generations"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - the paper doesn't provide quantitative analysis of augmentation quality or diversity

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: The paper's entire framework depends on correctly categorizing safety issues into 8 typical scenarios and 6 instruction attack types
  - Quick check question: Can you explain the difference between "Physical Harm" and "Mental Health" scenarios, and give an example of each?

- Concept: LLM-based evaluation methodology
  - Why needed here: The safety assessment relies on using one LLM to evaluate the outputs of other LLMs
  - Quick check question: How does the paper convert natural language evaluation responses into binary safe/unsafe labels?

- Concept: Instruction attack vectors
  - Why needed here: Understanding how different attack types work is crucial for both implementing the benchmark and defending against these attacks
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between "Goal Hijacking" and "Role Play Instruction" attacks, and how might a model defend against each?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Prompt collection (manual + augmented) -> LLM evaluation engine -> Score calculation module -> Leaderboard interface
- Critical path: Test prompts → Model generation → LLM evaluation → Score calculation → Leaderboard update
- Design tradeoffs: Using LLM evaluation provides scalability but introduces potential bias; using multiple evaluation prompts per scenario could improve accuracy but increases cost
- Failure signatures: Inconsistent evaluation scores across similar prompts, high variance in instruction attack performance, or systematic blind spots in certain safety categories
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test the consistency of the LLM evaluator by running the same prompts multiple times and measuring variance
  2. Compare human evaluation with LLM evaluation on a subset of prompts to validate accuracy
  3. Test the effectiveness of different prompt templates for the LLM evaluator to optimize for accuracy

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How effective is the LLM-based evaluation method compared to human evaluation across all safety dimensions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the automatic evaluation method correlates highly with human evaluation and plans to add human evaluation for top-ranked models
- Why unresolved: The authors only mention high correlation with human evaluation but don't provide quantitative comparisons or validation across all safety dimensions
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed comparison showing precision, recall, and F1 scores of LLM evaluator versus human annotators across all 14 safety dimensions

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal prompt template structure for maximizing evaluation accuracy across different safety scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that prompt templates are manually tuned for each safety issue and that in-context learning improves evaluation accuracy in some scenarios
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't systematically compare different template structures or provide guidelines for optimal template design
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies comparing different template structures and in-context learning approaches across all safety scenarios

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of decoding parameters impact safety performance across different LLM architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that decoding algorithms impact generation and uses recommended parameters for each model, but acknowledges this could affect results
- Why unresolved: The authors only use recommended parameters without exploring the parameter space or quantifying the impact on safety scores
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of safety scores across different decoding parameters (temperature, top-k, top-p) for each evaluated model

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does instruction attack effectiveness vary across different safety scenarios and model architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds instruction attacks are more effective at exposing safety issues and that ChatGPT performs significantly better against goal hijacking attacks
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide detailed analysis of which instruction attack types are most effective for specific safety scenarios or model architectures
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed breakdown showing effectiveness of each instruction attack type across all safety scenarios and model comparisons

### Open Question 5
- Question: What is the long-term impact of safety training on model performance in non-safety related tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on safety improvements but doesn't address potential trade-offs with general task performance
- Why unresolved: No evaluation of how safety training affects general language model capabilities
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of general task performance (e.g., MMLU, HELM) between safety-trained and non-safety-trained models of similar architectures

## Limitations

- The reliance on LLM-based evaluation for safety assessment may introduce systematic biases that are not captured by the limited human validation mentioned
- The quality and diversity of augmented prompts generated by GPT-3.5-turbo is not quantitatively validated against human-written prompts
- The comparative analysis between ChatGPT and other models is based on a single evaluation run without addressing potential variance

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The observation that instruction attacks are more effective at exposing safety issues than typical scenarios is well-supported by the experimental results and aligns with established literature on adversarial prompting.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that LLM-based evaluation provides a scalable and consistent safety assessment method is plausible but requires validation through human evaluation correlation studies, which are not provided.

**Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of data augmentation for improving safety prompt diversity is reasonable given the methodology described, but lacks quantitative validation of the augmented prompt quality or coverage improvements.

## Next Checks

1. **Human validation study**: Conduct a human evaluation study comparing LLM evaluator judgments with human safety assessments on a stratified sample of 500 prompts across all 14 safety scenarios. Calculate inter-rater reliability and Cohen's kappa between human and LLM evaluations.

2. **Prompt quality analysis**: Perform a systematic analysis of augmented versus manually written prompts by having human evaluators rate the realism, diversity, and safety relevance of 100 randomly selected augmented prompts versus 100 manual prompts, using a standardized rubric.

3. **Reproducibility test**: Re-run the safety assessment on a subset of 3-5 models (including ChatGPT and 2-3 Chinese models) using different random seeds for decoding and multiple LLM evaluation runs. Calculate coefficient of variation in safety scores to establish measurement stability.
