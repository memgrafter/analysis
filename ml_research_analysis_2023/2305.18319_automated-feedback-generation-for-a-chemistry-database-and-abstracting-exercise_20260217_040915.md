# Automated Feedback Generation for a Chemistry Database and Abstracting Exercise

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18319
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18319
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: BERT-based models achieved 92% accuracy in classifying abstract sentences into background, technique, and observation categories

## Executive Summary
This study presents an automated feedback system for chemistry education that provides both scoring and structural feedback on student database and abstracting exercises. The system uses BERT-based neural models to classify sentences in student abstracts into three categories: background, technique, and observation. By analyzing the structural composition of abstracts, the system generates targeted feedback to help students improve their abstract writing. While the automated scoring showed moderate correlation with human markers (r² = 0.51), ethical concerns prevent its use for actual grading, limiting the system to providing feedback only.

## Method Summary
The system employs a two-stage training approach using BERT-based neural models. First, the model is pre-trained on large datasets (approximately 15,000 samples from ASAP datasets) to develop general language understanding. Then, it is fine-tuned on 80% of 207 student submissions from chemistry courses. The model classifies sentences into three categories (background, technique, observation) to analyze abstract structure and generate feedback. An additional AES (Automated Essay Scoring) component using BiLSTM with attention mechanism provides scoring, though this is restricted to feedback use only due to ethical concerns.

## Key Results
- 92% accuracy in classifying abstract sentences into background, technique, and observation categories
- Automated scoring showed correlation of r² = 0.51 with human markers
- Students tend to focus more on background information than techniques and observations compared to professional abstracts
- Structural feedback successfully identified imbalances in student abstract composition

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> BERT model with classification head -> Training pipeline (pre-training on ASAP, fine-tuning on student submissions) -> Evaluation (classification accuracy, human score comparison) -> Feedback generation (structural analysis to templates)

- Critical path: 1. Load and preprocess student submissions 2. Classify sentences using fine-tuned BERT model 3. Analyze classification distribution 4. Generate feedback based on structural analysis 5. (Optional) Score abstract using AES system

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using pre-trained BERT vs. training from scratch: Pre-trained BERT offers better performance with less data but is computationally expensive.
  - Three-class vs. five-class classification: Simpler classification may be less accurate but more interpretable for feedback.
  - Automated scoring vs. feedback only: Scoring provides quantitative assessment but raises ethical concerns.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low classification accuracy: Indicates issues with model architecture, training data, or domain mismatch.
  - Poor correlation between automated and human scores: Suggests the model isn't capturing important features of good abstracts.
  - Unhelpful feedback: May indicate incorrect assumptions about what structural information students need.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate BERT model on held-out student submissions to establish baseline classification accuracy.
  2. Compare classification results for student abstracts vs. professional abstracts to quantify structural differences.
  3. A/B test different feedback templates to determine which types of structural guidance lead to the most improvement in student submissions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of automated feedback generation systems vary across different scientific disciplines, and what domain-specific adjustments are needed for optimal results?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates success with chemistry abstracts but notes the assumption that abstract structure is invariant across fields, while using medical science data for training.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested the system on chemistry assignments and trained on medical abstracts, without comparing performance across different scientific domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies applying the same feedback system to multiple scientific disciplines (biology, physics, engineering) with domain-specific training data and evaluating performance differences.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the specific ethical concerns that prevent automated scoring from being used in high-stakes assessments, and how can these be systematically addressed?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states "ethical issues surrounding the implementation of AES" prevent its use for actual grading, especially in high-stakes assignments.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the ethical concerns but doesn't specify what they are or propose solutions for addressing them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic analysis of specific ethical concerns (transparency, bias, accountability), case studies of institutions successfully implementing AES, and development of frameworks to address these concerns.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the transparency of neural network-based scoring systems be improved to increase stakeholder confidence in automated assessment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "lack of transparency" is a major disadvantage of neural models, making it impossible to tell how conclusions are reached.
- Why unresolved: While identified as a problem, the paper doesn't explore specific methods to improve model interpretability or explainability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation and evaluation of explainable AI techniques (SHAP values, attention visualization) applied to AES models, with user studies measuring stakeholder trust and understanding.

## Limitations
- Small dataset size (207 student submissions) limits generalizability of results
- Domain mismatch between medical abstracts (PubMed) used for pre-training and chemistry abstracts
- Ethical restrictions prevent use of automated scoring for actual grading, limiting practical application
