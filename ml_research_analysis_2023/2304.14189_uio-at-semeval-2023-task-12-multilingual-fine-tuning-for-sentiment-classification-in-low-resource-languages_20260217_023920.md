# UIO at SemEval-2023 Task 12: Multilingual fine-tuning for sentiment classification in low-resource languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14189
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14189
- Reference count: 7
- Primary result: Monolingual fine-tuning outperforms multilingual fine-tuning for African languages when training data contains thousands of samples

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates fine-tuning strategies for sentiment classification in low-resource African languages using the multilingual XLM-Twitter-sentiment model. The authors compare monolingual fine-tuning with multilingual approaches that group languages by family. They find that with training sets in the thousands of samples, monolingual fine-tuning yields the best results, likely because the large in-language data provides sufficient signal without adding noise from other languages. The study provides insights into leveraging multilingual models for low-resource languages and highlights the importance of data quantity and language proximity to pretraining languages.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune the XLM-Twitter-sentiment model on datasets for 12 African languages from the MasakhaNER corpora. They experiment with three approaches: monolingual fine-tuning on each language separately, multilingual fine-tuning across all languages, and multilingual fine-tuning grouped by language families. The model uses a classification head added to the pretrained architecture and is trained using the standard cross-entropy loss. The authors evaluate performance using accuracy and F1-score metrics on development and test sets.

## Key Results
- Monolingual fine-tuning outperforms multilingual fine-tuning when training sets contain thousands of samples
- Languages with larger training sets or closer to higher-resourced languages seen during pretraining perform best
- Multilingual fine-tuning may be beneficial when training samples drop below approximately 500 per language

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Languages closer to higher-resourced languages seen during pretraining perform better.
- Mechanism: Pretrained models have stronger representations for languages closer to those seen during pretraining, leading to better transfer learning.
- Core assumption: The multilingual model's pretraining included languages closer to some of the target African languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Languages with larger training sets or closer to higher-resourced languages seen during pretraining perform best."
  - [section]: "we find that models fine-tuned on larger training sets, and models fine-tuned on languages close to those seen both during model pretraining and initial fine-tuning, perform the best."

  - Poor performance on languages far from pretraining languages

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal amount of training data needed for monolingual fine-tuning to outperform multilingual fine-tuning for low-resource African languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors found that for datasets with thousands of samples, monolingual fine-tuning performed best, but they suggest testing with even fewer samples.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not specify the exact threshold where monolingual fine-tuning becomes superior to multilingual fine-tuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing monolingual vs multilingual fine-tuning with varying amounts of training data (e.g., 10, 100, 500, 1000 samples) to identify the tipping point.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does code-switching within tweets impact the performance of multilingual sentiment classification models for African languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the datasets contain various degrees of code-switching but does not analyze its impact on model performance.
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge code-switching but do not quantify or study its effects on classification accuracy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis comparing model performance on monolingual vs code-switched tweets, and studying how different levels of code-switching affect results.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What linguistic features or language similarities most strongly predict which low-resource languages will benefit from multilingual fine-tuning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors found that languages with more training data or closer to higher-resourced languages seen during pretraining performed best, suggesting language similarity plays a role.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies factors that influence performance, it does not develop a predictive model for when multilingual fine-tuning will be beneficial.
- What evidence would resolve it: Quantitative analysis of linguistic features (e.g., lexical overlap, typological similarity) and their correlation with performance gains from multilingual fine-tuning across different language pairs.

## Limitations

- Data Size Dependency: Key findings depend on training sets being in the "thousands of samples" with unclear thresholds for when this relationship breaks down
- Language Family Grouping: Grouping approach lacks systematic analysis of which families benefit from multilingual fine-tuning
- Pretraining Language Proximity: Claims about language proximity to pretraining languages lack specificity and empirical validation

## Confidence

- High Confidence: Monolingual fine-tuning performs better than multilingual fine-tuning for the specific datasets used (thousands of samples per language)
- Medium Confidence: Larger training sets provide sufficient signal to outweigh multilingual benefits, but requires more rigorous testing
- Low Confidence: Language proximity to pretraining languages being a determining factor, as the paper provides minimal empirical evidence

## Next Checks

1. **Data Size Threshold Analysis**: Systematically test the performance crossover point by conducting experiments with varying training set sizes (100, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000 samples) for a representative language to identify the exact threshold where monolingual fine-tuning stops being optimal.

2. **Linguistic Distance Quantification**: Calculate measurable linguistic distances (lexical, syntactic, phonological) between target African languages and the languages seen during XLMT pretraining, then correlate these distances with performance differences to empirically validate the proximity hypothesis.

3. **Cross-Lingual Transfer Evaluation**: Design experiments that test zero-shot or few-shot transfer from high-resource to low-resource African languages within the same language family to determine whether multilingual pretraining provides benefits beyond what in-language data alone can achieve.
