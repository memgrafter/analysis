# Aligning Language Models with Preferences through f-divergence Minimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.08215
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08215
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: f-DPG framework unifies RLHF and GDC, with JS divergence often outperforming other f-divergences across 13 tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces f-DPG, a general framework for aligning language models with preferences through f-divergence minimization. The framework unifies existing approaches like RLHF and GDC by treating them as special cases of f-divergence minimization, enabling experimentation with alternative objectives. Experiments on 13 diverse tasks show that carefully chosen f-divergences can significantly outperform existing methods, with Jensen-Shannon divergence frequently striking the best balance between alignment and diversity.

## Method Summary
The f-DPG framework treats language model alignment as minimizing an f-divergence between the model distribution πθ and a target distribution p that encodes preferences. By choosing different generator functions f, f-DPG recovers gradients of existing methods (reverse KL for RLHF, forward KL for GDC) and enables using other divergences like Total Variation and Jensen-Shannon. The method uses policy gradient with a baseline technique to reduce variance, and extends naturally to conditional distributions for tasks like summarization and code generation.

## Key Results
- Jensen-Shannon divergence frequently outperforms forward KL divergence by wide margins across 13 diverse tasks
- f-DPG successfully unifies RLHF and GDC frameworks as special cases of f-divergence minimization
- The framework achieves better alignment-diversity trade-offs than existing methods, with reverse KL showing mode collapse and forward KL being sensitive to sampling noise

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Minimizing f-divergences with a parametric policy family can yield better alignment than traditional KL-based methods, even when the policy is mis-specified. Different f-divergences have distinct pseudo-reward structures - forward KL emphasizes coverage of the target distribution's support, while reverse KL concentrates mass on high-probability regions, and JS divergence provides a smoother, more balanced trade-off.

### Mechanism 2
The f-DPG framework unifies and generalizes existing alignment methods like RLHF and GDC by treating them as special cases of f-divergence minimization. By choosing different generator functions f, f-DPG can recover the gradients of RLHF (reverse KL) or GDC (forward KL), enabling experimentation with alternative objectives on the same target.

### Mechanism 3
Adding a baseline to the pseudo-reward in f-DPG reduces variance and improves convergence, especially for divergences with extreme pseudo-reward values. Subtracting a baseline (e.g., average pseudo-reward) from the pseudo-reward does not introduce bias, analogous to policy gradient baselines in RL.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: f-divergences and their generators**
  - Why needed here: The paper's core innovation is using different f-divergences as alignment objectives; understanding the generator functions and their derivatives is essential to implement f-DPG.
  - Quick check question: What is the generator function for Jensen-Shannon divergence, and how does its derivative behave as πθ(x)/p(x) → 0 or ∞?

- **Concept: Policy gradient and its variance reduction techniques**
  - Why needed here: f-DPG is a policy gradient method; knowing how baselines and batch size affect variance is critical for stable training.
  - Quick check question: How does the variance of the pseudo-reward rθ(x) = -f'(πθ(x)/p(x)) scale with the choice of f and the distance between πθ and p?

- **Concept: Conditional target distributions and their gradients**
  - Why needed here: Many alignment tasks are conditional (e.g., summarization, code generation); the paper extends f-DPG to this setting via an expectation over contexts.
  - Quick check question: How does the gradient of Ec∼τ(c)[Df(πθ(·|c)||pc(·))] differ from the unconditional case, and what are the computational implications?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Target distribution module -> Policy network -> f-divergence module -> Baseline estimator -> Sampler
- **Critical path**: 
  1. Sample x ~ πθ (or x ~ πθ(·|c) for conditional tasks)
  2. Compute p(x) (or pc(x)) and pseudo-reward rθ(x) = -f'(πθ/p)
  3. Update baseline estimate
  4. Compute gradient: g = rθ(x) - baseline * ∇θ log πθ(x)
  5. Update θ via optimizer
- **Design tradeoffs**: Choice of f affects alignment-diversity trade-off and convergence stability; batch size reduces variance but increases memory/compute cost; baseline reduces variance but adds complexity
- **Failure signatures**: High variance in pseudo-rewards → noisy gradients, poor convergence; entropy collapse (especially for RKL) → mode collapse, low diversity; slow convergence in forward KL → poor coverage of target support
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Implement and test f-DPG on a simple binary constraint task (e.g., enforce presence of a word) and compare all four f-divergences
  2. Apply f-DPG to a scalar preference task (e.g., sentiment maximization) and analyze the alignment-diversity trade-off for each f
  3. Extend f-DPG to a conditional task (e.g., factual consistency in summarization) and evaluate the impact of different f-divergences on named entity precision/recall

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the choice of f-divergence impact the diversity of generated text in language models? While the paper provides insights into how different f-divergences affect alignment and diversity, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the impact on text diversity. A detailed study comparing the diversity of generated text using different f-divergences, with metrics such as Distinct-n, Self-BLEU-n, and Perplexity, could provide concrete evidence of the impact on text diversity.

### Open Question 2
Can the f-DPG framework be extended to handle more complex control objectives beyond binary and scalar preferences? The paper demonstrates the application of f-DPG to binary preferences, scalar preferences, and distributional preferences, but does not explore more complex control objectives such as multi-objective optimization or constraints involving multiple features.

### Open Question 3
How does the choice of f-divergence affect the performance of language models on downstream tasks? The paper shows that different f-divergences can lead to different levels of approximation quality when evaluated under the lens of the forward KL, but does not investigate the impact on downstream tasks such as summarization, translation, or dialogue generation.

## Limitations

- The evaluation framework primarily focuses on synthetic and single-objective tasks, leaving unclear how well findings generalize to complex, multi-objective alignment scenarios
- Computational cost analysis is limited - no systematic comparison of training efficiency across different f-divergence choices
- Analysis of why certain f-divergences perform better on specific tasks remains largely empirical rather than providing deeper theoretical guarantees

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The core theoretical framework connecting f-divergences to existing alignment methods (RLHF and GDC) is mathematically sound and well-established.

**Medium confidence**: Experimental results showing JS divergence's superior performance are compelling but may be sensitive to implementation details and hyperparameter choices.

**Low confidence**: Claims about the general applicability of f-DPG to arbitrary alignment objectives are supported by the theoretical framework but lack extensive empirical validation across diverse real-world scenarios.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation on hyperparameter sensitivity**: Systematically vary learning rates, batch sizes, and baseline parameters across all four f-divergences on a representative subset of tasks to determine if observed performance differences persist under different training configurations.

2. **Multi-objective alignment test**: Design and implement a multi-objective task (e.g., simultaneously optimize for sentiment, topic coverage, and lexical constraints) to evaluate how well different f-divergences handle competing objectives compared to single-objective scenarios.

3. **Theoretical analysis of mode coverage**: Develop and validate theoretical bounds on how different f-divergences trade off between mode coverage and concentration, then empirically verify these predictions on synthetic target distributions with known mode structure.
