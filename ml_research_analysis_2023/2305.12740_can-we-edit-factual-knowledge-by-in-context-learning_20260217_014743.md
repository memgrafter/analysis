# Can We Edit Factual Knowledge by In-Context Learning?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12740
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12740
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: IKE achieves competitive success rates compared to gradient-based methods without parameter updates.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores whether in-context learning can be used to edit factual knowledge in large language models. The authors propose in-context knowledge editing (IKE), a method that constructs demonstrations to teach models to copy, update, and retain predictions for different prompts. Experiments on GPT-J and OPT models show that IKE achieves competitive success rates compared to gradient-based methods while requiring no parameter updates, reducing computation costs and side effects like over-editing and forgetting. The method works by designing demonstration formatting and organization strategies, including copy, update, and retain demonstrations, and retrieving relevant knowledge facts from the training corpus as demonstrations.

## Method Summary
IKE is a method for editing factual knowledge in large language models using in-context learning without modifying model parameters. It constructs demonstrations using three formatting templates: copy (injects new facts), update (improves generalization), and retain (preserves unrelated facts). The method retrieves relevant knowledge facts from the training corpus and organizes them as demonstrations. IKE is evaluated on GPT-J (6B) and OPT (175B) using the COUNTERFACT dataset, comparing performance against gradient-based methods (FT, MEND, ROME) and a baseline (PROMPT). The key metrics are Efficacy Score (ES), Paraphrase Editing Score (PS), and Neighborhood Score (NS).

## Key Results
- IKE achieves competitive success rates compared to gradient-based methods without any parameter updates.
- IKE reduces computation costs and side effects like over-editing and forgetting.
- IKE scales well to larger models and shows resilience to over-editing issues.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: In-context demonstrations teach the model to copy, update, and retain knowledge without parameter updates.
- Mechanism: Demonstrations provide explicit examples that show the model how to behave on related prompts (copy), how to generalize to similar prompts (update), and how to preserve unrelated facts (retain).
- Core assumption: The model's attention mechanism can learn from demonstrations without explicit gradient-based optimization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Experiments show that in-context knowledge editing (IKE), without any gradient and parameter updating, achieves a competitive success rate compared to gradient-based methods"
  - [section 4.2.1] "We define three types of demonstration formatting templates including (i) copy, which aims to inject new facts into LMs; (ii) update, which improves the generalization of injected knowledge fact; and (iii) retain, which guides LMs to preserve unrelated knowledge facts."
- Break condition: If the demonstrations are not well-formatted or not representative, the model may fail to learn the desired behavior or may over-generalize.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Demonstration selection and organization impact the model's ability to edit knowledge effectively.
- Mechanism: Relevant demonstrations are retrieved from the training corpus based on similarity to the target fact, and their order in the context influences the model's understanding.
- Core assumption: The model can use contextual information from demonstrations to infer the appropriate response for a given prompt.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2.2] "we retrieve relevant knowledge facts from the training corpus as demonstration inputs"
  - [section 5.3.1] "Removing the selection procedure (i.e., Random Selection) leads to a clear drop in the NS score from 77.0 to 45.0"
- Break condition: If demonstrations are not relevant or are presented in a confusing order, the model may struggle to learn the intended editing behavior.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Larger language models benefit more from in-context knowledge editing.
- Mechanism: As the model scales up, its capacity to retain and generalize knowledge improves, leading to better performance in in-context knowledge editing.
- Core assumption: Larger models have more parameters and can better handle the complexity of in-context learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.3.2] "As demonstrated in Table 4, we find that the performance of IKE is positively correlated with the scale of the LM"
  - [section 1] "Traditional knowledge editing methods refine LLMs via fine-tuning on texts containing specific knowledge. However, with the increasing scales of LLMs, these gradient-based approaches bring large computation costs."
- Break condition: If the model is not sufficiently large or complex, it may not be able to effectively utilize the in-context demonstrations.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Demonstration formatting templates (copy, update, retain) -> Demonstration organization (retrieval and ordering) -> Language model
- Critical path: Retrieve relevant demonstrations -> Format demonstrations using templates -> Organize demonstrations in context -> Present to language model for in-context learning
- Design tradeoffs: IKE trades off computational efficiency and interpretability for potential limitations in handling a large number of editing facts or diverse prompt formats.
- Failure signatures: Failure in IKE may manifest as over-generalization, forgetting of original knowledge, or inability to generalize to different prompt formats.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can IKE be extended to accommodate a larger number of editing facts beyond the context length limit?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Considering the limited input length of language models, it may not be feasible to include tremendous editing facts within the context."
- Why unresolved: The paper only briefly mentions this as a potential issue and suggests using an external memory and retrieval-augmented method, but does not explore this approach experimentally.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing IKE with and without an external memory and retrieval-augmented method for handling multiple factual edits.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can IKE be adapted to handle different formats and domains of facts and prompts beyond the consistent format used in the experiments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "In IKE, the domain and format of facts and prompts are kept consistent. However, in real-world settings, facts and prompts come in diverse forms."
- Why unresolved: The paper only briefly mentions this as a potential issue and suggests using different forms of prompts and longer, more complex neutral data as facts, but does not explore this experimentally.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing IKE's performance on consistent vs. diverse formats and domains of facts and prompts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the order of demonstration types (copy, update, retain) affect IKE's performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "The order of demonstration types is an under-explored influencing factor of IKE."
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a predefined type order but does not explore how different orderings affect performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing IKE's performance using different orderings of demonstration types.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can IKE be combined with gradient-based methods to improve performance while maintaining computational efficiency and interpretability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Comparing PM(y|x,f,C) with PM'(y|x), it can be found that: (i) ICL requires no gradient estimation for the target fact and keeps the original LM untouched after knowledge editing."
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on comparing IKE to gradient-based methods separately, but does not explore combining them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing a combined approach to IKE and gradient-based methods alone.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How does IKE's performance scale with model size beyond the 175B parameter OPT model tested in the paper?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "As there are no parameter modifications, IKE is applicable to LLMs such as OPT-175B and exhibits better memorization ability."
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests IKE on models up to 175B parameters, leaving questions about performance on even larger models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results testing IKE on models larger than 175B parameters.

## Limitations

- Scalability: IKE may face challenges in handling a large number of editing facts due to the limited input length of language models.
- Generalization: The method's performance on diverse formats and domains of facts and prompts beyond the consistent format used in experiments is unclear.
- Computational Overhead: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with demonstration retrieval and formatting.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The experimental results demonstrating IKE's competitive performance compared to gradient-based methods on the COUNTERFACT dataset.
- Medium Confidence: The claim that IKE scales well with model size, as the evaluation is limited to a few model architectures and sizes.
- Medium Confidence: The assertion that IKE reduces side effects like over-editing and forgetting, as the evaluation metrics (ES, PS, NS) may not fully capture these phenomena.
