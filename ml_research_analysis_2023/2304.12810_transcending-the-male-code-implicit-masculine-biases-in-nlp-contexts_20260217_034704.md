# Transcending the "Male Code": Implicit Masculine Biases in NLP Contexts

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.12810
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12810
- Reference count: 0
- This paper examines implicit masculine biases in two natural language processing (NLP) data sets for virtual assistant (VA) training.

## Executive Summary
This paper examines implicit masculine biases in two natural language processing (NLP) data sets for virtual assistant (VA) training. The authors developed a new dictionary called AVA to identify ambiguous gendered language in VA contexts. They found that both data sets contain implicit masculine biases, especially in terms of masculine-as-norm language (e.g., pronoun use, gender-marked words, names). Even after accounting for ambiguous terms, the biases persisted, with MASSIVE showing a greater degree of masculine language bias than ReDial. The results highlight the need for more rigorous analysis of gender biases in NLP data sets and suggest that AVA can be used as a tool to identify and analyze such biases.

## Method Summary
The study analyzed two NLP data sets (MASSIVE and ReDial) for implicit masculine biases using automated content analysis with R packages (quanteda, Tidyverse). The authors applied existing gendered language dictionaries (Roberts & Utych, Gaucher et al.) and created a new AVA dictionary of 44 VA-specific ambiguous words. They conducted contextual validation to filter out false positives and calculated gender bias ratios across different linguistic features (pronouns, gender-marked words, names).

## Key Results
- Both MASSIVE and ReDial data sets contain implicit masculine biases, particularly in masculine-as-norm language
- MASSIVE showed a greater degree of implicit masculine language bias compared to ReDial
- The AVA dictionary successfully identified and filtered ambiguous terms that were VA-specific rather than truly gendered

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we distinguish between singular and plural uses of the pronoun "they" in automated content analysis of NLP data sets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors noted the difficulty in determining when "they/them" pronouns are used in the singular versus plural, which is a key challenge in their analysis.
- Why unresolved: Automated content analysis tools may not be sophisticated enough to accurately distinguish between these uses, leading to potential misclassification of gender neutrality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of more advanced natural language processing algorithms capable of reliably distinguishing singular from plural pronoun usage.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent do the implicit masculine biases found in MASSIVE and ReDial data sets impact the behavior and responses of virtual assistants trained on these data sets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss the potential for these biases to be encoded within virtual assistants and their possible effects on user interactions.
- Why unresolved: The study focused on detecting biases in the data sets but did not evaluate the actual impact on virtual assistant behavior or user experiences.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the behavior and responses of virtual assistants trained on biased versus debiased data sets, including user feedback and interaction analysis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we create a truly gender-diverse and inclusive dictionary of ambiguous terms for virtual assistant contexts that includes perspectives from genderqueer, non-binary, and trans* individuals?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors developed the AVA dictionary but acknowledge its limitations and the need for broader community engagement.
- Why unresolved: The AVA dictionary was created by a small team and may not fully capture the nuances and perspectives of diverse gender identities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Collaborative development of an expanded dictionary through focus groups and co-design activities with gender-diverse participants, followed by validation studies.

## Limitations

- The study relies on two specific data sets (MASSIVE and ReDial) that may not be representative of all VA training corpora
- The AVA dictionary covers only 44 terms and may miss other context-dependent ambiguities
- The analysis treats gender as binary and does not account for non-binary or gender-neutral language patterns

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The existence of gendered language patterns in both data sets is well-established through multiple analytical approaches
- **Medium confidence**: The relative comparison between MASSIVE and ReDial showing different bias patterns, given the contextual differences between data sets
- **Medium confidence**: The effectiveness of AVA in reducing false positives, though broader validation would strengthen this claim
