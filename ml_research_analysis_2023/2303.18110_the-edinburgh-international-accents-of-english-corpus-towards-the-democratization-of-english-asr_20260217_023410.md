# The Edinburgh International Accents of English Corpus: Towards the Democratization of English ASR

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.18110
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18110
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: EdAcc shows 19.7% average WER for state-of-the-art ASR, much higher than 2.7% on US English clean read speech, revealing persistent accent bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Edinburgh International Accents of English Corpus (EdAcc), a new dataset of nearly 40 hours of English dyadic conversations aimed at better representing the diversity of global English accents. The dataset includes 40+ self-reported English accents from speakers with 51 different first languages, along with detailed linguistic background profiles. Evaluation on state-of-the-art models, including Whisper and Wav2vec2.0, shows that EdAcc is challenging for current ASR systems, with the best model achieving an average 19.7% word error rate—much higher than the 2.7% obtained on US English clean read speech. Performance drops notably for Indian, Jamaican, and Nigerian English speakers, highlighting persistent bias issues in ASR. The authors hope EdAcc will encourage further research on a wider range of English accents.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates three classes of ASR models—Wav2vec2.0 (fine-tuned on various datasets), Whisper (pre-trained on 680k hours of multilingual data), and a commercial ASR system—on the EdAcc dataset. The dataset comprises nearly 40 hours of dyadic video call conversations, with linguistic background profiles for each speaker. The evaluation pipeline includes normalizing transcriptions, splitting data into development and test sets, and computing word error rate (WER) across different accent groups. Performance is compared against Librispeech benchmarks and analyzed by accent group to identify systematic biases.

## Key Results
- EdAcc achieves an average 19.7% WER across tested models, much higher than 2.7% on US English clean read speech.
- The best model (Wav2vec2.0 with LM) still shows significant errors, especially for Indian, Jamaican, and Nigerian English speakers.
- Conversational speech in EdAcc introduces naturalistic challenges—overlaps, hesitations, laughter—that are absent in read speech corpora.
- Self-reported accent labels and linguistic profiles enable fine-grained error analysis but also introduce subjectivity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The dataset's dyadic conversation format exposes ASR models to more naturalistic speech patterns than scripted or read speech corpora.
- Mechanism: Conversational speech includes natural pauses, overlaps, hesitations, and varied speaking styles that reflect real-world usage, which challenges ASR models to handle disfluencies and speaker dynamics.
- Core assumption: Models trained on read speech fail to generalize because they do not encounter the acoustic and linguistic variability present in spontaneous conversation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] states the dataset contains "almost 40 hours of dyadic video call conversations between friends" emphasizing naturalness.
  - [section 3.1] notes that conversations range in duration from 20 to 60 minutes and include "overlaps between speakers, noise, laughter and hesitations."
  - [corpus] indicates the data includes spontaneous speech but lacks explicit acoustic marker annotations, so some disfluency cues may be underrepresented.
- Break condition: If conversational speech does not include sufficient speaker overlap or spontaneous interruptions, the naturalness advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Inclusion of diverse linguistic backgrounds and self-reported accents allows fine-grained error analysis across English varieties.
- Mechanism: By documenting each speaker's first language, learning history, and self-described accent, the dataset enables researchers to map ASR performance to specific accent groups and identify systematic biases.
- Core assumption: ASR errors correlate with measurable linguistic factors such as L1 influence and exposure to English-speaking environments.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] highlights that EdAcc "includes a wide range of first and second-language varieties of English and a linguistic background profile of each speaker."
  - [section 3.1] details the questionnaire covering first languages, second languages, English learning history, and places lived.
  - [corpus] confirms availability of linguistic profiles but notes that accent mapping is subjective and contested.
- Break condition: If self-reported accents are too imprecise or overlap significantly, performance differences between groups may become statistically insignificant.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The dataset's size and diversity reveal model weaknesses that smaller or less varied corpora cannot expose.
- Mechanism: With nearly 40 hours of data spanning 40+ self-reported accents from speakers of 51 first languages, EdAcc provides a broad test bed that uncovers accent-specific and model-generalization issues.
- Core assumption: Larger, more diverse datasets will show performance gaps that narrow datasets mask.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] states the dataset contains "almost 40 hours of dyadic video call conversations" and "more than 40 self-reported English accents from speakers from 51 different first languages."
  - [section 4.2] reports that the best model achieves 19.7% WER on EdAcc versus 2.7% on US English clean read speech, showing a stark performance drop.
  - [corpus] confirms the scale and diversity but lacks comparative baseline results from other accent corpora.
- Break condition: If the dataset's accent distribution is skewed toward certain varieties, underrepresented accents may still be under-tested.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Word Error Rate (WER) calculation and interpretation
  - Why needed here: WER is the primary metric for evaluating ASR performance and comparing across models and datasets.
  - Quick check question: If a model outputs "cat sat on mat" for the reference "the cat sat on the mat," what is the WER?

- Concept: Self-supervised learning in speech models
  - Why needed here: Many evaluated models (e.g., Wav2vec2.0, Whisper) rely on self-supervised pre-training, so understanding this concept is key to interpreting performance gaps.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between supervised fine-tuning and self-supervised pre-training in speech models?

- Concept: Linguistic accommodation and its impact on speech data
  - Why needed here: The dataset design acknowledges accommodation effects, so understanding them helps interpret speaker interactions and accent variability.
  - Quick check question: How might two speakers with different L1 backgrounds adjust their English when conversing with each other?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data collection → transcription → preprocessing → evaluation; models: Wav2vec2.0, Whisper, commercial ASR.
- Critical path: Record → transcribe → normalize orthography → force-align → compute WER; key checkpoints are transcription quality and alignment accuracy.
- Design tradeoffs: Natural conversation vs. acoustic consistency; broad accent coverage vs. balanced representation; manual transcription vs. scalability.
- Failure signatures: High WER spikes on specific accents; misalignment in force alignment causing evaluation noise; inconsistent accent labels reducing group comparisons.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run WER evaluation on dev set using the selected Wav2vec2.0 encoder and LM configuration.
  2. Compare WER per accent group on dev set to identify worst-performing accents.
  3. Visualize speaker embeddings using the language ID model to confirm accent clustering structure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific strategies could be employed to accurately classify or cluster accents in EdAcc, ensuring a more balanced and representative dataset?
- Basis in paper: explicit
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge the difficulty in assigning accurate accent labels and suggest this as a promising direction for future work. The complexity arises from the subjective nature of accent perception and the influence of multiple linguistic backgrounds on individual speakers.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of robust accent classification or clustering algorithms that can handle the complexity and diversity of accents in EdAcc, with results demonstrating improved balance and representation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of ASR models on EdAcc compare to their performance on other conversational datasets with different domains or recording conditions?
- Basis in paper: explicit
- Why unresolved: While the paper compares EdAcc performance to Librispeech, it does not explore comparisons with other conversational datasets. This limits the understanding of how EdAcc's difficulty relates to factors beyond accent diversity, such as domain or recording quality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluation of ASR models on multiple conversational datasets with varying domains and recording conditions, including EdAcc, to identify the specific factors contributing to performance differences.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the specific linguistic features or characteristics of Jamaican, Nigerian, and Indian English that contribute to the observed performance drop in ASR models?
- Basis in paper: explicit
- Why unresolved: The paper notes a performance drop for these accents but does not provide a detailed linguistic analysis of the reasons behind this. Understanding these features could guide targeted model improvements.
- What evidence would resolve it: Linguistic analysis of Jamaican, Nigerian, and Indian English, focusing on phonetic, phonological, and lexical features that may differ significantly from the accents used in training data, and their correlation with ASR performance on EdAcc.

## Limitations
- Subjective nature of self-reported accent labels may introduce imprecision in group-level analysis.
- Linguistic profiles may not capture all relevant variables influencing ASR performance, such as exposure to specific English varieties.
- Exact transcription normalization and post-processing rules are not fully detailed, potentially affecting evaluation consistency.

## Confidence
- **High**: Dataset size, diversity, and evaluation methodology are clearly specified and reproducible.
- **Medium**: Self-reported accents and linguistic profiles are subjective, potentially limiting reliability of group comparisons.
- **Low**: Commercial ASR system details and exact normalization procedures are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
