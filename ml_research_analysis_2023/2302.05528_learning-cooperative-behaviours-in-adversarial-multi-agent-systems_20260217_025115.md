# Learning cooperative behaviours in adversarial multi-agent systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05528
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05528
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 14
- One-line primary result: Bug agent learns to cooperate with Ant to push Spider out of arena, increasing team competency and efficiency.

## Executive Summary
This work extends the RoboSumo platform to create TripleSumo, enabling investigation of cooperative behaviors in continuous action spaces with physical contact in an adversarial environment. The scenario involves two agents ('Bug' and 'Ant') teaming up to push a third agent ('Spider') out of an arena. The newly added agent 'Bug' is trained during an ongoing match using Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) with a hybrid reward structure combining dense and sparse rewards. The cooperative behavior is quantitatively evaluated by mean winning rate and mean number of steps needed to win. Results show that 'Bug' successfully learns to cooperate with 'Ant', increasing the team's competency and efficiency.

## Method Summary
The study extends RoboSumo to create TripleSumo with three agents (Bug, Ant, Spider) in a sumo game environment using MuJoCo physics. Ant is pre-trained for 3,000 epochs, then Bug is trained for 20,000 epochs during ongoing matches using DDPG with a hybrid reward structure (dense velocity-based terms + sparse win/loss signals). The environment provides continuous observations including agent positions and velocities, and continuous action spaces for movement and pushing. Performance is measured by mean winning rate and mean number of steps needed to win.

## Key Results
- Bug successfully learns cooperative behavior with Ant, increasing team competency
- Mean winning rate rises from negative to positive values during training, stabilizing at 0.15-0.47 in testing
- Mean number of steps needed to win decreases over training epochs, indicating improved teamwork efficiency

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Bug learns cooperative behavior by inferring Ant's and Spider's strategies during ongoing matches.
- **Mechanism**: Bug receives dense rewards based on relative velocities (its own, partner Ant's, and opponent Spider's) plus sparse win/loss signals, enabling policy adaptation in a non-stationary environment.
- **Core assumption**: Continuous observation of agent velocities provides sufficient signal to infer strategic intentions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] states Bug must "develop awareness of the other agents' actions, infer the strategy of both sides"
  - [section] describes dense reward decomposition into opponent velocity reward, partner velocity reward, and self velocity reward
  - [corpus] shows related work on MARL algorithms that use velocity-based reward shaping
- **Break condition**: If velocity signals become decoupled from strategic intent (e.g., in highly stochastic environments), Bug cannot infer strategies effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Hybrid reward structure (dense + sparse) accelerates learning of effective cooperation.
- **Mechanism**: Dense rewards provide immediate feedback for each action step, while sparse rewards signal match outcomes, creating a shaped reward landscape that guides policy toward winning strategies.
- **Core assumption**: Dense reward shaping based on velocity alignment correlates with eventual match success.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section] defines reward = Dense Reward + Sparse Reward with detailed velocity-based terms
  - [section] shows dense and sparse rewards increase over training epochs
  - [corpus] references prior work combining dense and sparse rewards in MARL
- **Break condition**: If dense reward shaping misaligns with true cooperative objectives, the agent may optimize for local velocity gains rather than team victory.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: DDPG's off-policy learning enables Bug to learn from Ant's pre-trained policy while adapting to Spider's behavior.
- **Mechanism**: DDPG maintains separate actor and critic networks that learn Q-values and policies concurrently, allowing Bug to learn cooperative behaviors even when paired with a non-stationary opponent.
- **Core assumption**: Off-policy learning with experience replay can handle the non-stationarity introduced by a learning partner.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] states "DDPG is implemented with a hybrid reward structure"
  - [section] details DDPG implementation with actor-critic architecture and hyperparameters
  - [corpus] cites Lillicrap et al.'s original DDPG paper and its application to continuous control
- **Break condition**: If experience replay buffer becomes dominated by outdated opponent strategies, learning efficiency degrades.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: RoboSumo physics engine (MuJoCo) -> agent dynamics and contact forces -> OpenAI Gym environment wrapper -> observation/action spaces and episode termination -> DDPG agent -> actor network (policy), critic network (Q-function), replay buffer -> reward function -> dense velocity-based terms + sparse win/loss signals -> training loop -> alternating policy updates with target network synchronization

- **Critical path**: Observation -> DDPG policy -> action -> environment step -> reward -> experience replay -> network update -> improved policy

- **Design tradeoffs**: Continuous action spaces provide fine-grained control but require function approximation; dense rewards accelerate learning but may create suboptimal local minima; pre-training Ant simplifies initial conditions but limits exploration of alternative team strategies

- **Failure signatures**: Plateauing mean rewards with oscillating winning rates suggests poor reward shaping; consistently negative winning rates indicate the agent learns to avoid loss rather than pursue cooperation; high variance in steps-to-win metric suggests unstable cooperative behavior

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train Bug with only dense rewards (no sparse win/loss signals) to isolate the effect of immediate feedback
  2. Swap Ant and Spider roles to test whether learned cooperation transfers across team compositions
  3. Vary the relative strengths (contact forces) between agents to explore how physical asymmetry affects cooperative learning

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How would the system perform with more complex reward structures that include multiple agents with varying roles and responsibilities?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper mentions future work will investigate a more complex scenario of non-predeﬁned pairing for cooperation in TripleSumo, with three or more agents playing against one another, and all agents learning to freely choose to team up with a non-predeﬁned partner.
- **Why unresolved**: The current study only investigated a simple scenario with three agents where two are pre-defined to team up, and the third is trained to cooperate with one of them.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing the more complex scenario and evaluating the performance of the agents in terms of their ability to cooperate with non-predeﬁned partners, and comparing the results with the current study.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How would the system perform with other reinforcement learning algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper mentions that while this work has focussed exclusively on DDPG, future research will investigate the performance of other RL algorithms with respect to learning cooperative strategies in multi-agent systems.
- **Why unresolved**: The current study only implemented DDPG and did not compare its performance with other RL algorithms.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing other RL algorithms and comparing their performance with DDPG in terms of the mean winning rate and mean number of steps needed to win.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How would the system perform with more complex morphologies and physical features of the agents?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper mentions that morphological and physical features of the agents are subject to free choices according to future research demands, and the current study used four-legged agents of the same size.
- **Why unresolved**: The current study only used a simple morphology and physical features for the agents.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing more complex morphologies and physical features for the agents and evaluating their performance in terms of their ability to cooperate and win the game.

## Limitations
- Agent-specific contact force ranges create physical asymmetries that may artificially facilitate cooperation
- Hand-crafted dense reward structure may not transfer to other cooperative tasks without significant redesign
- Stability of learned cooperation (MWR 0.15-0.47) suggests partial success but indicates room for improvement

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: Bug learns to cooperate with Ant
- **Medium confidence**: Hybrid reward structure accelerates learning
- **Low confidence**: Velocity-based dense rewards universally enable strategy inference across different multi-agent environments
