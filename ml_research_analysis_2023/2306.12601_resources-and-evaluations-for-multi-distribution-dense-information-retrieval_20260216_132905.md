# Resources and Evaluations for Multi-Distribution Dense Information Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.12601
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12601
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: Allocation strategies improve Recall@100 by 3.8-8.0 points in multi-distribution retrieval where systems must retrieve from unseen domains

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of multi-distribution dense information retrieval, where systems must retrieve relevant passages from multiple distinct corpora, some of which are unseen during training. The authors propose simple allocation strategies that divide a fixed retrieval budget across domains to prevent over-retrieving from known distributions. Using modified QA and entity matching datasets, they demonstrate that their methods consistently improve retrieval performance by an average of 3.8 points and up to 8.0 points in Recall@100 across different base retrieval models.

## Method Summary
The authors propose simple methods for multi-distribution dense information retrieval that allocate a fixed retrieval budget (top-k passages) strategically across domains. They fine-tune dense retrievers on one known distribution (D1) and evaluate retrieval performance when passages from both D1 and unseen distributions (D2) are required. The methods include naive merging of all passages, fixed per-task allocation (splitting k into k1 for D1 and k2 for D2), and per-query allocation that adjusts the split based on query characteristics. Experiments use three benchmark datasets: Walmart-Amazon, Amazon-Google, and ConcurrentQA, with RoBERTa-base or all-MiniLM-L6-v2 as base models.

## Key Results
- Fixed allocation strategies improve Recall@100 by 3.8 points on average across datasets
- Maximum improvement of 8.0 points in Recall@100 observed on Amazon-Google dataset
- Improvements are consistent when using different base retrieval models (RoBERTa and MiniLM)
- Per-query allocation provides additional benefits over fixed allocation when sufficient seed pairs are available

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fixed retrieval budget allocation prevents over-retrieving from the known distribution
- Mechanism: Dense retrievers trained on D1 exhibit bias toward retrieving from D1. By explicitly allocating a fixed number of retrieval slots (k1 from D1 and k2 from D2), the method ensures known distribution doesn't monopolize the retrieval budget, forcing retrieval from D2.
- Core assumption: Similarity scores from trained retriever are systematically biased toward training distribution, and this bias can be mitigated by controlling allocation of retrieval slots.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Abstract: "We propose simple methods for this task which allocate the fixed retrieval budget (top-k passages) strategically across domains to prevent the known domains from consuming most of the budget."
  - Section: "Suppose C consists of many distributions of passages {D1, D2, D3}... We ask whether M is biased towards retrieving from {D1} such that naively selecting the top-k documents will lead to over-retrieving from {D1} and under-retrieving from {D2, D3}?"
  - Corpus: "We plot the t-SNE of BERT embeddings of the passages in the Walmart-Amazon corpus... descriptions form two separate distributions while the titles do not."
- Break condition: If similarity score distributions for D1 and D2 become similar (e.g., after fine-tuning on both), allocation strategy may no longer provide benefits.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Per-query allocation can better match specific retrieval needs compared to fixed per-task allocation
- Mechanism: Instead of uniform allocation fraction across all queries, per-query allocation adjusts budget split based on query characteristics, allowing more slots to the distribution more likely to contain relevant passage for that specific query.
- Core assumption: Different queries have different retrieval needs, and one-size-fits-all allocation fraction is suboptimal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Abstract: "We show that our methods lead to an average of 3.8+ and up to 8.0 points improvements in Recall@100 across the datasets and that improvements are consistent when fine-tuning different base retrieval models."
  - Section: "We sample seed pairs from the full benchmark and report results over pairs that are not included in the seed set... as few as 10-100 seed pairs are sufficient to select an allocation fraction that outperforms naive merging."
  - Corpus: "The benchmarks are constructed by modifying existing entity-matching and question answering datasets... each question requires retrieving one passage from Di and one from Dj in order to answer the question."
- Break condition: If cost of determining per-query allocation outweighs retrieval performance gain, or if seed set is insufficient to predict allocation accurately.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training on small amount of data from known distribution is sufficient to calibrate retriever for multi-distribution retrieval
- Mechanism: Retriever is fine-tuned on queries and passages from D1 only. This training is enough to learn similarity scoring function that can distinguish relevance within D1, and with allocation strategy, can also retrieve from D2.
- Core assumption: Retriever's ability to distinguish relevance within D1 transfers to some extent to task of retrieving from D2 when combined with controlled allocation strategy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Abstract: "We propose simple methods for this task which allocate the fixed retrieval budget... We show that our methods lead to an average of 3.8+ and up to 8.0 points improvements in Recall@100 across the datasets..."
  - Section: "We fine-tune RoBERTa encoders with parameters shared between EQ and EP... Training takes between 4-10 hours depending on the dataset."
  - Corpus: "The Amazon and Walmart corpora have 21891 and 2520 products respectively... The unmatched products are used for training the retrievers."
- Break condition: If similarity score distributions for D1 and D2 are too dissimilar, or if retrieval task requires deep understanding of D2's characteristics.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Query Encoder (EQ) -> Passage Encoder (EP) -> Similarity Computation -> Allocation Strategy -> Top-k Retrieval

- Critical path:
  1. Encode the query using EQ
  2. Encode passages from D1 and D2 using EP
  3. Compute similarity scores between query embedding and passage embeddings
  4. Apply allocation strategy to determine number of passages to retrieve from each distribution
  5. Retrieve the top-k passages based on similarity scores and allocation strategy

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fixed vs. Per-query allocation: Fixed allocation is simpler but may not match specific needs of each query. Per-query allocation can be more accurate but requires additional computation and method to predict allocation.
  - Training data size: More training data can improve retriever's performance but also increases training time and computational cost.
  - Choice of base model: Different base models (e.g., RoBERTa vs. all-MiniLM-L6-v2) can have different performance characteristics and training requirements.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low Recall@k: Retriever is not effectively retrieving relevant passages from either distribution
  - High variance in Recall@k across queries: Allocation strategy may not be well-calibrated for all queries
  - Performance degradation when increasing retrieval budget (k): Retriever may be overfitting to training distribution (D1) and not generalizing well to D2

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement naive merging baseline: Combine D1 and D2 into single corpus and retrieve top-k passages without any allocation strategy
  2. Implement per-task allocation strategy: Split retrieval budget (k) into fixed fractions (k1 for D1 and k2 for D2) and retrieve top-k1 passages from D1 and top-k2 passages from D2
  3. Implement per-query allocation strategy: Develop method to predict allocation fraction for each query based on small seed set of query-passage pairs, and retrieve passages accordingly

- Basis in paper: [inferred] Authors mention that future work could extend study to larger number of distributions, hypothesizing this would compound challenges of task.
- Why unresolved: Paper only evaluates on datasets with two distributions, leaving performance and challenges of scaling to more distributions unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing retrieval performance on datasets with three or more distributions, including analysis of how retrieval budget allocation strategies scale and how performance degrades with increasing numbers of distributions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the most effective strategies for predicting per-query allocation fractions in multi-distribution retrieval, and how do these compare to simple seed-set approach proposed in paper?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors propose simple seed-set method for predicting allocation fractions and note future work should explore more sample-efficient and unsupervised strategies.
- Why unresolved: Paper only presents basic seed-set approach and acknowledges need for more sophisticated methods, without exploring alternative strategies or their effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of various per-query allocation prediction methods, including unsupervised approaches, sample-efficient techniques, and their impact on retrieval performance across different datasets and scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does performance of multi-distribution retrieval methods vary when incorporating limited training data from unseen distribution, and what is minimal amount of data needed to significantly improve retrieval accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Authors note that fine-tuning encoders does not require lot of data, suggesting even small amounts of training data from unseen distribution could be beneficial, but this is not experimentally explored.
- Why unresolved: Paper focuses on scenarios where no training data is available for unseen distribution, leaving potential benefits and optimal amount of limited data from this distribution unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying amount of training data from unseen distribution and measuring impact on retrieval performance, including analysis of diminishing returns and minimal effective data size.

## Limitations

- The study evaluates only three synthetic benchmarks derived from existing datasets, limiting generalizability to real-world multi-distribution retrieval scenarios
- Allocation strategies rely on fixed retrieval budget (top-k), which may not be optimal when relevance distributions across domains vary significantly
- Performance gains are measured against naive baseline that merges all distributions, but comparison against more sophisticated multi-domain retrieval approaches is absent

## Confidence

- High confidence: Core claim that fixed allocation strategies prevent over-retrieval from known distributions is well-supported by experimental results showing consistent Recall@100 improvements (3.8-8.0 points) across all three benchmarks
- Medium confidence: Claim that per-query allocation provides additional benefits is supported by oracle experiments but lacks evaluation of practical implementation challenges
- Low confidence: Scalability and effectiveness of these methods for scenarios with more than two distributions or extremely large corpora remain untested

## Next Checks

1. **Distributional validation**: Analyze similarity score distributions for passages from D1 and D2 to quantify bias that allocation strategies address, and test whether this bias persists across different base retrieval models

2. **Cross-dataset generalization**: Apply allocation strategies to additional multi-domain retrieval datasets (e.g., from BEIR benchmark) to assess performance beyond three constructed benchmarks

3. **Allocation strategy comparison**: Implement and evaluate against alternative multi-domain retrieval approaches (e.g., domain-specific re-ranking or mixture-of-experts models) to establish relative effectiveness of simple allocation strategies
