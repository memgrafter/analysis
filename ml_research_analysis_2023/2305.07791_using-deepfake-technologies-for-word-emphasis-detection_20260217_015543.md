# Using Deepfake Technologies for Word Emphasis Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07791
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07791
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: 92% accuracy, 89.23% F1 score for word emphasis detection

## Executive Summary
This work addresses the challenging task of detecting word emphasis in spoken language by leveraging deepfake technologies to generate an emphasis-devoid version of a speaker's utterance. The approach extracts text from the original speech, synthesizes a neutral version using the same speaker's voice, and compares the two to identify emphasized words through FFT cross-correlation of spectrograms. Experiments on 100 voice samples from 5 speakers achieved strong performance metrics (92% accuracy, 89.14% precision, 89.33% recall, 89.23% F1 score).

## Method Summary
The method uses SV2TTS architecture to synthesize emphasis-devoid speech from a speaker's voice sample, which serves as a neutral baseline for comparison. The system extracts text from the original utterance using speech-to-text, generates neutral speech using the TTS pipeline conditioned on speaker embeddings, and segments both versions into words using RMS sliding windows. FFT spectrograms of corresponding words are compared via cross-correlation, where pitch-based emphasis appears as frequency peak shifts and skew-based emphasis shows low correlation across frequencies.

## Key Results
- Achieved 92% accuracy in detecting emphasized words across 100 voice samples
- Precision of 89.14%, recall of 89.33%, and F1 score of 89.23%
- Successfully distinguished between pitch-based and skew-based emphasis patterns
- Demonstrated effectiveness across 5 speakers each emphasizing one of four words in 5 sentences

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Deepfake-generated emphasis-devoid speech can serve as a reliable reference for detecting speaker emphasis.
- Mechanism: The SV2TTS model generates speech that lacks emotional emphasis by design, providing a neutral baseline. By comparing the pitch and spectral characteristics of this neutral speech with the original utterance using FFT cross-correlation, emphasis patterns emerge as deviations in frequency peaks or spectral skew.
- Core assumption: The TTS synthesizer does not introduce its own emphasis, and differences between synthesized and original speech are due solely to speaker emphasis.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Cross-correlation of FFT spectrograms effectively isolates emphasis-induced pitch shifts and spectral skew.
- Mechanism: For each word, the FFT of the synthesized (neutral) and original speech are compared. Pitch-based emphasis appears as a shift in the peak of the cross-correlation, while skew-based emphasis shows low correlation across all frequency shifts.
- Core assumption: Emphasis manifests as either a consistent frequency shift (pitch) or a broader spectral distribution (skew), both detectable through FFT-based correlation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Speaker-specific embedding vectors allow the synthesizer to produce highly personalized neutral speech for comparison.
- Mechanism: The speaker encoder extracts a fixed-size embedding from a voice sample, which conditions the TTS synthesizer to generate speech that mimics the speaker's voice without emphasis. This personalization reduces confounding factors like accent or dialect.
- Core assumption: The embedding vector fully captures speaker-specific acoustic traits, enabling the synthesizer to produce a true neutral version of the speaker's voice.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Speaker Encoder -> TTS (with embedding) -> STT -> Word segmentation -> FFT comparison -> Emphasis detection
- Critical path: Speaker Encoder → TTS (with embedding) → STT → Word segmentation → FFT comparison → Emphasis detection
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using FFT cross-correlation is computationally efficient but may miss non-stationary emphasis patterns
  - Personalization via embeddings improves accuracy but requires a good-quality voice sample
  - Word segmentation via RMS sliding window is simple but may misalign words in noisy speech
- Failure signatures:
  - Low accuracy when speaker has heavy accent or background noise
  - False positives if TTS model inadvertently adds prosody
  - Misalignment of word boundaries leading to incorrect emphasis detection
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate that the TTS output is truly emphasis-devoid by comparing it to the original with known neutral speech
  2. Test FFT cross-correlation sensitivity by applying synthetic pitch shifts and skew to the synthesized speech
  3. Evaluate word segmentation accuracy by manually annotating word boundaries in test samples

## Limitations
- The method assumes TTS synthesis produces truly emphasis-devoid speech, but this may not hold if the synthesizer introduces prosodic patterns
- FFT cross-correlation may struggle with non-stationary emphasis patterns or rapid pitch modulations
- Word segmentation using RMS sliding windows may misalign words in noisy speech or with unusual prosody

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The overall framework of using synthesized neutral speech as a comparison baseline is sound, given the strong results (92% accuracy, 89.23% F1 score) on the test dataset.

**Medium Confidence**: The assumption that TTS synthesis produces truly emphasis-devoid speech is reasonable but not fully validated.

**Low Confidence**: The exact thresholds and parameters for FFT cross-correlation detection are not specified, requiring empirical tuning.

## Next Checks

Calculate the false positive rate for emphasis detection in known neutral speech to establish a baseline.

2. **Cross-Correlation Threshold Calibration**: Systematically vary the FFT cross-correlation thresholds for pitch shift and skew detection on a held-out validation set. Plot precision-recall curves to identify optimal operating points and assess robustness across different speakers and emphasis types.
