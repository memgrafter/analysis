# Referral Augmentation for Zero-Shot Information Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.15098
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15098
- Reference count: 5
- The paper introduces Referral-Augmented Retrieval (RAR), which improves zero-shot information retrieval by concatenating document indices with text from other documents that cite or link to the given document, achieving 37% and 21% absolute improvements on ACL paper retrieval Recall@10.

## Executive Summary
Referral-Augmented Retrieval (RAR) is a method that improves zero-shot information retrieval by leveraging document referrals (citations or hyperlinks) as auxiliary information. The key insight is that referrals provide a more complete, multi-view representation of a document, similar to how incoming page links in PageRank provide a comprehensive idea of a webpage's importance. RAR works with both sparse and dense retrievers and outperforms generative text expansion techniques such as DocT5Query and Query2Doc.

## Method Summary
RAR works by augmenting document representations with text from other documents that cite or link to them. For sparse models like BM25, this is done through text concatenation, while for dense models like SimCSE, embeddings from referral text are aggregated using mean or shortest path methods. The method requires access to citation or hyperlink metadata and can be applied to any retrieval model. The approach enables up-to-date information retrieval without re-training and scales well as the number of referrals increases.

## Key Results
- 37% and 21% absolute improvement on ACL paper retrieval Recall@10 compared to DocT5Query and Query2Doc
- Works with both sparse (BM25) and dense (SimCSE, DPR) retrievers
- Enables up-to-date information retrieval without re-training

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Referral augmentation provides hard positive pairs that bridge lexical gaps between queries and documents. Citations and hyperlinks naturally express how documents are referred to in context, providing semantically equivalent but lexically different representations of the same content. Core assumption: referring sentences capture meaningful semantic equivalence between documents that can improve retrieval performance.

### Mechanism 2
Concatenation of referral text with document text improves sparse model performance by enriching term frequency distributions. For BM25 and similar sparse models, adding referral text increases the likelihood of matching query terms through expanded term coverage. Core assumption: Additional text from referrals will contain query-relevant terms not present in the original document.

### Mechanism 3
Mean aggregation of dense embeddings creates robust document representations by averaging multiple semantic views. For dense models like SimCSE, averaging embeddings from multiple referring sentences creates a more stable representation that captures diverse aspects of document relevance. Core assumption: Averaging multiple referral embeddings will create a more robust representation than any single view.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Hard positive pairs in contrastive learning
  - Why needed here: Understanding how RAR uses referrals as hard positives to improve embedding alignment
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes a hard positive from a regular positive example in contrastive learning?

- Concept: Term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) weighting
  - Why needed here: BM25 relies on TF-IDF principles, and concatenation changes the term distribution that BM25 uses
  - Quick check question: How does adding referral text affect the IDF weighting of terms in a document?

- Concept: Vector averaging and its effects on embedding space
  - Why needed here: Mean aggregation assumes averaging multiple views creates better representations, which depends on understanding vector space properties
  - Quick check question: What happens to the semantic properties of an embedding when you average it with multiple other embeddings?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Document index → Referral collector → Augmentation strategy selector → Retriever model → Query processor
- Critical path: Query → Retriever → Document ranking → Results
- Design tradeoffs: Memory vs. performance (more referrals = better recall but higher memory), simplicity vs. sophistication (concatenation vs. complex aggregation)
- Failure signatures: Decreased precision despite increased recall, slow retrieval times, inconsistent performance across document types
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare BM25 performance with 0, 10, 20, 30 referrals per document to find optimal referral count
  2. Test different aggregation methods (concatenation, mean, shortest path) on a dense model like SimCSE
  3. Evaluate referral quality by comparing hyperlinks vs. citations on the same retrieval task

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the quality and diversity of referrals impact retrieval performance across different domains and tasks? The paper discusses the potential for referrals to provide multiple views of a document but doesn't deeply explore how the quality and diversity of these referrals affects performance across various domains (e.g., scientific papers vs. Wikipedia vs. social media).

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal strategy for aggregating multiple referrals to maximize retrieval performance? The paper explores different methods for multi-referral aggregation (concatenation, mean, shortest path) and finds that the optimal strategy depends on the model type, but doesn't provide a comprehensive analysis of the trade-offs and limitations of each approach.

### Open Question 3
How can referrals be effectively used to personalize and update retrieval systems in real-time? The paper discusses the potential for referrals to enable dynamic updates to retrieval systems by incorporating trending keywords, user-specific preferences, and temporary labels, but doesn't provide concrete methods or evaluate the effectiveness of these approaches.

## Limitations
- Effectiveness depends heavily on the quality and relevance of referral links
- May not generalize well beyond academic paper and Wikipedia domains
- Computational overhead of storing and processing referral text for large corpora

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The core claim that referral augmentation improves zero-shot retrieval performance compared to DocT5Query and Query2Doc is well-supported by experimental results showing 37% and 21% absolute improvements on ACL paper retrieval Recall@10.
- **Medium confidence**: The mechanism explanation that referrals provide hard positive pairs bridging lexical gaps is theoretically sound but relies on assumptions about referral quality that weren't thoroughly validated across diverse domains.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim about scalability as referral count increases is supported by experiments but doesn't address the practical memory and processing constraints of real-world deployment.

## Next Checks

1. **Domain transferability test**: Apply RAR to a non-academic domain (e.g., news articles or product reviews) to verify if the performance gains generalize beyond paper retrieval tasks.

2. **Referral quality analysis**: Systematically evaluate how referral relevance scores correlate with retrieval performance improvements, identifying thresholds where noisy referrals begin degrading results.

3. **Memory overhead measurement**: Measure actual storage and retrieval latency costs when scaling from the ACL dataset (16K papers) to a corpus of 1M+ documents with 30 referrals each.
