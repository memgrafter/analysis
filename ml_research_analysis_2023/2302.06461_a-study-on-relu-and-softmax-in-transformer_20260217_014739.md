# A Study on ReLU and Softmax in Transformer

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.06461
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06461
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: ReLU activation outperforms Softmax in Transformer when dealing with large numbers of value slots, and FFN and key-value memory become equivalent when layer normalization is applied to Softmax.

## Executive Summary
This paper conducts extensive studies on ReLU and Softmax activation functions in Transformer architectures. The authors find that ReLU is superior to Softmax when the number of value slots is large due to less centralized distributions. They also demonstrate that FFN and key-value memory are equivalent when an additional layer normalization module is introduced on Softmax. Based on these insights, they propose ReLUFormer, a full ReLU Transformer architecture that achieves superior performance on long sequence tasks.

## Method Summary
The paper investigates the differences between ReLU and Softmax activation functions in Transformer models through controlled ablation studies. The authors implement a ReLUFormer architecture that replaces Softmax with ReLU in both self-attention networks (SAN) and feed-forward networks (FFN), adding variance reduction factors and regularization loss functions to stabilize training. They evaluate their approach on IWSLT14 German-English, WMT14 English-German, and Europarl7 English-German datasets using BLEU score and latency metrics.

## Key Results
- ReLU outperforms Softmax in FFN layers when the number of value slots is large
- Layer normalization can restore FFN performance when replacing ReLU with Softmax
- ReLUFormer achieves superior performance on long sequence tasks compared to vanilla Transformer and sparse attention baselines

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Layer normalization can restore FFN's effectiveness when replacing ReLU with Softmax. Softmax normalization creates small variance outputs that get dominated by residuals; layer normalization scales these outputs to proper variance levels. The variance of FFN outputs directly impacts parameter utilization and model capacity.

### Mechanism 2
ReLU outperforms Softmax when dealing with large numbers of value slots due to less centralized distributions. Softmax's exponential normalization creates highly centralized distributions (top elements dominate), while ReLU maintains more distributed activations, better utilizing large memory spaces. Memory utilization efficiency improves when more slots receive meaningful activation weights.

### Mechanism 3
ReLU variance grows with sequence length in self-attention, requiring variance reduction techniques. Each ReLU output follows N(0, n/2) distribution where n is sequence length, causing exploding variance; scaling by √(n/2) stabilizes training. Self-attention output variance must remain bounded regardless of sequence length.

- **Variance propagation in neural networks**: Understanding why ReLU outputs have different variance characteristics than Softmax outputs. Quick check: What is the variance of ReLU(x) where x ~ N(0,1)?

- **Entropy and distribution concentration**: Quantifying how centralized vs distributed activation patterns affect memory utilization. Quick check: How does entropy change when moving from uniform to highly peaked distributions?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Input → Self-attention (ReLU/Softmax → Variance reduction → Regularization → Residual) → FFN (ReLU/Softmax → LayerNorm → Residual) → Output
- **Critical path**: Activation function choice → Normalization strategy → Training stability → Final performance
- **Design tradeoffs**: ReLU offers better scalability and distribution properties but requires variance management; Softmax is stable but centralized
- **Failure signatures**: Training instability with ReLU self-attention, performance degradation with Softmax FFN, poor memory utilization with centralized distributions
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Replace ReLU with Softmax in FFN, observe performance drop and variance ratio change
  2. Add layer normalization to Softmax FFN, verify restored performance
  3. Replace Softmax with ReLU in self-attention with variance reduction factor, observe training stability

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does ReLU outperform Softmax in self-attention for long sequences due to reduced centralization of attention weights? While the paper demonstrates empirical superiority of ReLU for long sequences and provides qualitative analysis of attention weight distributions, a more rigorous theoretical explanation of why reduced centralization leads to better performance is needed.

### Open Question 2
Is the proposed variance reduction factor in ReLU-based self-attention optimal, or can it be further improved? The paper introduces a variance reduction factor to stabilize training of ReLU-based self-attention, but acknowledges that the factor is a hyperparameter that may not be optimal for all sequence lengths.

### Open Question 3
Can the insights from ReLU and Softmax activation functions in Transformers be applied to other neural network architectures? The paper focuses on the Transformer architecture, but the findings about activation function differences and their impact on variance and normalization could be relevant to other architectures.

## Limitations

- Claims about ReLU's superiority in large-value-slot scenarios lack broader architectural validation beyond controlled ablation studies
- The variance-reduction factor for ReLU in self-attention is presented as a hyperparameter fix rather than an emergent property
- The mechanism's generality across diverse NLP tasks beyond translation benchmarks remains unproven

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Mathematical derivation of ReLU variance scaling with sequence length (N(0, n/2)) and necessity of variance reduction factors
- **Medium confidence**: FFN-Softmax equivalence with layer normalization supported by ablation studies but requires further validation
- **Low confidence**: Superiority of ReLU over Softmax for large value slots demonstrated empirically but lacks rigorous distributional analysis

## Next Checks
