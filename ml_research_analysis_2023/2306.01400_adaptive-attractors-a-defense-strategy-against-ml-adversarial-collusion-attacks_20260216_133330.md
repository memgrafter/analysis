# Adaptive Attractors: A Defense Strategy against ML Adversarial Collusion Attacks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01400
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01400
- Reference count: 22
- Key outcome: Adaptive Attractors reduces collusion attack success rates to ~15% even with 40 copies, while maintaining 95.1% CIFAR-10 accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses adversarial collusion attacks in multi-copy ML model distribution, where attackers collude across multiple copies to generate adversarial samples that compromise all models. The authors propose Adaptive Attractors, a defense strategy that dynamically adjusts attractor weights based on input proximity to decision boundaries using a U-shaped curve. The method injects more attractors near and far from decision boundaries while reducing influence in intermediate regions, creating a moving target for attackers. Experimental results show significant improvements over existing methods, with attack success rates converging to around 15% under custom collusion attacks with 40 copies, while maintaining better model accuracy (95.1% on CIFAR-10 vs 94.5% for fixed-weight approaches).

## Method Summary
The Adaptive Attractors defense strategy builds on parameter rewriting with attractor-based rewriters but introduces dynamic weight assignment guided by a U-shaped curve based on distance to decision boundaries. For each input sample, the method calculates its proximity to the decision boundary and assigns a weight α using the U-shape function—higher weights near and far from boundaries, lower weights in intermediate regions. This creates diverse gradient landscapes across model copies, making it harder for attackers to find consistent attack directions that work across multiple copies. The approach requires no retraining and can be applied during model distribution, with the flexible weight assignment improving both robustness against collusion attacks and clean accuracy compared to fixed-weight attractor methods.

## Key Results
- Under custom collusion attacks, attack success rates converge to ~15% even with 40 model copies, compared to linear increases with fixed-weight approaches
- Model accuracy improves to 95.1% on CIFAR-10 compared to 94.5% for fixed-weight attractor methods
- Demonstrates resilience against both replication and collusion attacks while maintaining clean accuracy
- Shows consistent performance across both CIFAR-10 and GTSRB datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Adaptive attractors reduce collusion attack success by dynamically adjusting weights based on distance to decision boundary. The method uses a U-shaped curve to assign higher attractor weights near and far from the decision boundary, with lower weights in intermediate regions. This creates a moving target for attackers who must navigate varying attractor influence. The core assumption is that attackers primarily succeed by finding directions that minimize both model and attractor influence simultaneously.

### Mechanism 2
Reducing intermediate attractor influence prevents attackers from finding stable attack directions. By lowering attractor weights in the middle region (where the difference between highest and second-highest class scores is moderate), the method prevents attackers from finding consistent gradients that point toward adversarial samples. The core assumption is that adversarial attacks rely on consistent gradients across iterations to converge to successful samples.

### Mechanism 3
Enhanced attractor influence near decision boundaries disrupts attacks that start close to the boundary. By increasing attractor weights for samples with small score differences between top classes, the method makes it harder for attackers to cross the decision boundary without being strongly influenced by attractors. The core assumption is that many successful attacks start from samples already near the decision boundary.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Input sample -> Original model + Attractor rewriter -> Distance to boundary calculator -> U-shape weight function F -> Combined output (L1-normalized sum)
- **Critical path**: Input sample x is fed to both the original model and the attractor-based rewriter → The distance to decision boundary is calculated from the original model's output → The adaptive weight calculator F uses this distance and the U-shape curve to determine α → The final output is the L1-normalized sum of the original model and weighted attractor outputs
- **Design tradeoffs**: More aggressive U-shape curves provide better attack resistance but may impact clean accuracy; the choice of where to place the curve's minimum affects the balance between robustness and accuracy; using more complex functions for F instead of the U-shape could provide better adaptability but at the cost of interpretability and simplicity
- **Failure signatures**: Attack success rate increasing linearly with the number of colluding copies; significant drop in clean accuracy (beyond the reported 0.6% improvement over fixed-weight approach); attackers finding ways to consistently generate samples in regions with low attractor influence
- **First 3 experiments**: 
  1. Implement the basic adaptive attractor mechanism with a simple U-shape curve and test on CIFAR-10 with a single copy under standard adversarial attacks (FGSM, PGD)
  2. Evaluate the clean accuracy trade-off by testing on the full test set and comparing with the fixed-weight baseline
  3. Test the collusion resistance by simulating attacks with increasing numbers of colluding copies and measuring the convergence of attack success rate

## Limitations

- U-shaped curve parameters are not fully specified, making exact reproduction challenging
- Custom collusion attack implementation details are underspecified, particularly the aggregation method across multiple copies
- Experimental evaluation focuses primarily on image classification tasks, limiting generalizability to other domains

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The theoretical framework of adaptive attractors and its basic mechanism of dynamic weight assignment based on decision boundary proximity
- **Medium Confidence**: The experimental results showing reduced attack success rates and maintained accuracy, given the unspecified curve parameters
- **Low Confidence**: The claim about convergence to 15% attack success rate under custom collusion attacks, as the exact attack methodology is not fully detailed
