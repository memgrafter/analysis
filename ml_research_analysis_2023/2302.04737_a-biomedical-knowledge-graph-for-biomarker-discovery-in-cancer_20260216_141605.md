# A Biomedical Knowledge Graph for Biomarker Discovery in Cancer

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.04737
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04737
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: BERT-based models achieved F1 scores of 91.36% (BioBERT) and 90.75% (SciBERT) for NER in cancer-related biomedical text

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive approach to constructing a biomedical knowledge graph (KG) for cancer biomarker discovery by integrating heterogeneous data from scientific literature. The method employs BERT-based information extraction models (BioBERT and SciBERT) to extract entities and relations from PubMed articles, which are then harmonized with existing biomedical ontologies and knowledge bases. The resulting KG enables semantic reasoning and interactive explanations for biomarker-disease relationships, outperforming traditional NER approaches and providing a foundation for decision support in cancer research.

## Method Summary
The method constructs a domain-specific ontology (OncoNet Ontology) and enriches it with cancer-related knowledge extracted from PubMed articles using BERT-based information extraction models. BioBERT and SciBERT are fine-tuned for named entity recognition of genes/proteins and diseases, achieving high F1 scores. The extracted entities and relations are integrated into a knowledge graph using RDF triples, enabling semantic reasoning and question answering. The KG is validated through quality assessment metrics and can provide interactive explanations for biomarker discovery predictions.

## Key Results
- BERT-based models achieved F1 scores of 91.36% (BioBERT) and 90.75% (SciBERT) for NER in biomedical text
- The KG enables semantic reasoning for verifying predictions of relations between diseases and genes
- The system supports SPARQL queries for retrieving domain-specific knowledge about biomarkers and associated evidence

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: BERT-based models outperform traditional CRF and BiLSTM-CRF approaches for named entity recognition in biomedical text.
- **Mechanism**: The transformer architecture with bidirectional attention captures richer context than sequential models, allowing better identification of entity boundaries and types in scientific articles.
- **Core assumption**: Pre-training on large biomedical corpora (PubMed abstracts and full-text articles) provides domain-specific linguistic knowledge that generalizes to NER tasks.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "The BERT-based models outperformed traditional approaches (CRF, BiLSTM-CRF) in named entity recognition, achieving F1 scores of 91.36% and 90.75% for BioBERT and SciBERT, respectively."
  - [section]: "Although Bi-LSTM approaches outperform CRF-based approaches, their shortcoming lies in the incapability of semantic annotation and validation of the extracted entities and relations, i.e., not all relations can be represented by triggered words."
  - [corpus]: Weak - neighbor papers focus on LLM-to-KG pipelines but don't provide comparative F1 scores.
- **Break condition**: If domain-specific pre-training data is insufficient or if entity types are highly novel and not represented in pre-training corpus.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Ontology-guided information extraction enables semantic reasoning over the constructed knowledge graph.
- **Mechanism**: By reusing and extending existing biomedical ontologies (OGG, DOID) and mapping extracted entities to ontology classes, the KG gains semantic richness that allows automated reasoning and question answering.
- **Core assumption**: The domain ontology correctly captures the relevant biological relationships and can be extended with extracted facts without creating logical inconsistencies.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "The KG is then developed and enriched by harmonizing the ONO, additional metadata schemas, ontologies, controlled vocabularies, and additional concepts from external sources using a BERT-based information extraction method."
  - [section]: "Ontology-based named entity relation extraction (NER) and disambiguation help with unambiguous identification of entities in heterogeneous data and assertion of applicable named relationships that connect these entities together."
  - [corpus]: Weak - neighbor papers mention ontologies but don't detail the integration process or reasoning capabilities.
- **Break condition**: If the ontology becomes too complex or inconsistent, or if extracted facts contradict established biological knowledge.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The constructed knowledge graph enables interactive explanations for biomarker discovery predictions.
- **Mechanism**: By representing biomarkers, diseases, and their relationships as triples in RDF format, users can query the KG to understand the evidence and reasoning behind predicted biomarker-disease associations.
- **Core assumption**: The integration of evidence from multiple sources (PubMed, MeSH, CancerIndex) provides sufficient justification for the relationships encoded in the KG.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "The KG enables semantic reasoning for verifying predictions of relations between diseases and genes, facilitating question answering and interactive explanations about learned biomarkers."
  - [section]: "Since cross-disciplinary explanations are important for accurate diagnosis, it is important to query the KG to provide interactive explanations about learned biomarkers."
  - [corpus]: Weak - neighbor papers focus on LLM-to-KG pipelines but don't detail the explanation mechanisms or user interaction capabilities.
- **Break condition**: If the KG lacks sufficient detail or if the reasoning over it becomes too complex for practical explanation purposes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Article collection and preprocessing pipeline -> BERT-based NER models (BioBERT, SciBERT, BERT-cased) -> Entity linking and relation extraction module -> RDF triple generation and KG construction -> SPARQL query interface and reasoning engine -> Quality assessment metrics and explanation generation

- **Critical path**: Article collection → NER → Entity linking → RDF generation → KG construction → Query interface
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Precision vs. recall in NER: Higher precision ensures fewer false positives but may miss some relevant entities
  - Ontology complexity vs. reasoning efficiency: More detailed ontologies enable richer reasoning but may slow down query processing
  - Evidence integration: Including multiple evidence sources (PubMed, MeSH, CancerIndex) increases reliability but also complexity
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low F1 scores in NER indicating model overfitting or insufficient domain-specific training data
  - SPARQL query timeouts suggesting the KG is too large or poorly indexed
  - Inconsistent reasoning results indicating ontology errors or conflicting facts in the KG
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run NER on a small set of PubMed abstracts and manually verify entity recognition accuracy
  2. Construct a small KG from a single article and test basic SPARQL queries
  3. Integrate evidence from two sources (e.g., PubMed and MeSH) and test reasoning on a simple biomarker-disease relationship

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the quality of the knowledge graph be comprehensively assessed to ensure correctness and completeness of embedded knowledge?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that quality assessment is crucial but only uses lightweight measures like OOPS and SANSA metrics, acknowledging that a more comprehensive assessment is needed.
- Why unresolved: Comprehensive quality assessment for KGs requires measuring syntactic accuracy, semantic accuracy, timeliness, and other dimensions not fully explored in the study.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and applying a comprehensive quality assessment framework with multiple metrics and validation methods to evaluate the KG's correctness, completeness, and relevance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can symbolic reasoning methods be made robust to noise and applicable to non-symbolic contexts in biomedical data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that symbolic methods are not robust to noise and cannot be applied to non-symbolic contexts, highlighting a limitation for biomedical data.
- Why unresolved: Symbolic reasoning relies on structured knowledge and is vulnerable to errors and ambiguity in real-world data, which is common in biomedical domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing hybrid AI systems that combine symbolic reasoning with machine learning techniques to handle noise and ambiguity in biomedical data.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the expressiveness of description logic (DL) be tested against incompleteness and inconsistency in knowledge graphs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the expressiveness of DLx is not tested against incompleteness and inconsistency, which is a potential limitation.
- Why unresolved: DL expressiveness needs to be validated in real-world scenarios where knowledge graphs are inherently incomplete and may contain inconsistencies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments to evaluate DL expressiveness in handling incomplete and inconsistent knowledge graphs, and developing techniques to improve DL reasoning in such scenarios.
