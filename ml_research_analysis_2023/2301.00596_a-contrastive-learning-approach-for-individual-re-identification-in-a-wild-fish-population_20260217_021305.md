# A contrastive learning approach for individual re-identification in a wild fish population

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.00596
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00596
- Reference count: 32
- Achieves 35% one-shot accuracy, 56% five-shot accuracy, and 88% accuracy at 100 shots for individual fish re-identification

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of identifying individual fish in wild populations using image-based re-identification, which can replace physical tagging in ecological studies. The authors propose a contrastive learning approach using a modified Inception v3 network with a projection head to generate embeddings for individual identification. The model is trained on a dataset of corkwing wrasse images collected over multiple years, capturing natural changes in fish morphology. Results show the model achieves 35% one-shot accuracy, 56% five-shot accuracy, and 88% accuracy at 100 shots, with better performance on head crops compared to full-body images. The study demonstrates the feasibility of automated individual re-identification in marine ecology, though further data collection is needed to improve precision for practical use.

## Method Summary
The approach employs a contrastive learning framework built on a modified Inception v3 architecture. A projection head is added to generate embeddings that capture distinctive features of individual fish. The model is trained on a dataset of corkwing wrasse images collected over multiple years to account for natural morphological changes. Training uses standard contrastive loss functions to learn representations that maximize similarity between images of the same individual while minimizing similarity between different individuals. The system processes both full-body and head-only images, with separate evaluation for each input type. Model performance is assessed using one-shot, five-shot, and 100-shot learning scenarios to evaluate identification accuracy across different reference image availability conditions.

## Key Results
- 35% one-shot accuracy for identifying individual fish from single reference images
- 56% five-shot accuracy when five reference images are available per individual
- 88% accuracy at 100 shots, demonstrating improved performance with more training examples
- Head crop images achieve better identification performance than full-body images

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The contrastive learning approach works by learning to map images of the same individual fish to nearby points in embedding space while pushing embeddings of different individuals apart. The modified Inception v3 architecture provides a strong feature extraction backbone, while the projection head transforms these features into a space optimized for the contrastive objective. Training on multi-year data helps the model learn representations that are robust to natural morphological changes that occur over time, such as changes in coloration, size, and minor injuries. The head-focused approach succeeds because fish heads contain distinctive patterns and features that remain relatively stable compared to body patterns that may change more dramatically with growth and environmental factors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contrastive learning**: A self-supervised learning technique that trains models to distinguish between similar and dissimilar pairs of examples. Needed to learn discriminative features without requiring explicit labels beyond identity matching. - **Transfer learning with pre-trained networks**: Using Inception v3 as a feature extractor, pre-trained on ImageNet, provides a strong starting point for the fish identification task. Needed because training deep networks from scratch requires massive datasets that are impractical for individual animal identification. ## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Image input -> Inception v3 backbone -> Projection head -> Embedding space -> Similarity comparison

**Critical path**: Image acquisition → Pre-processing (cropping, normalization) → Feature extraction (Inception v3) → Embedding generation (projection head) → Nearest neighbor search in embedding space → Identity prediction

**Design tradeoffs**: The choice of head crops versus full-body images represents a key tradeoff between information richness and stability of identifying features. Head crops provide more consistent identifying features but may miss important body markings, while full-body images contain more information but suffer from greater variability due to pose and environmental factors. The multi-year training data requirement balances the need for robustness against temporal changes with the practical constraints of long-term ecological monitoring programs.

**Failure signatures**: Poor performance on one-shot identification suggests the embedding space may not be sufficiently discriminative for fine-grained individual differences. The performance gap between head and body images indicates that certain feature types are more reliable than others, potentially pointing to overfitting on body-specific patterns. Low accuracy on individuals with minimal training examples reveals sensitivity to class imbalance and limited data scenarios.

## Limitations
- The model's accuracy, while promising, shows significant room for improvement before practical deployment in field studies.
- The 35% one-shot accuracy indicates that the current system would struggle with reliable individual identification when only a single reference image is available, which is often the case in ecological monitoring.
- The dataset, while collected over multiple years to capture natural changes, may still be limited in its representation of the full variability in fish appearance due to factors like lighting conditions, camera angles, and seasonal changes.

## Confidence

High confidence: The experimental methodology is sound, using established contrastive learning techniques with appropriate evaluation metrics. The results are presented transparently with clear performance numbers across different shot scenarios.

Medium confidence: The claim that this approach can replace physical tagging is supported by the results but requires field validation. The ecological significance of reducing handling stress is well-established, but the practical workflow for implementing this system in real-world monitoring programs needs further development.

Low confidence: Generalizability to other fish species and environments is suggested but not demonstrated. The long-term stability of the identification system across extended timeframes beyond the study period is not addressed.
