# Data leakage in cross-modal retrieval training: A case study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.12258
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12258
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Identified and addressed data leakage in SoundDesc benchmark dataset for text-based audio retrieval, showing retrieval scores drop from ~27% to ~20% when duplicates and metadata-based groups are properly separated

## Executive Summary
This study identified significant data leakage in the SoundDesc benchmark dataset for text-based audio retrieval caused by duplicate audio recordings appearing in both training and evaluation splits. Using audio fingerprinting, the authors found exact duplicates and overlapping recordings that artificially inflated retrieval performance. They proposed two improved dataset splits - a "clean" split preventing exact duplicates from crossing splits, and a "group-filtered" split that additionally groups recordings sharing metadata to avoid weak contamination. Experimental results showed retrieval performance dropped notably (R@1 from ~27% to ~20%) in the group-filtered split, demonstrating it serves as a more challenging and valid benchmark for cross-modal audio retrieval research.

## Method Summary
The authors used audio fingerprinting (Panako) to detect duplicate audio files in the SoundDesc dataset, identifying exact duplicates and recordings with overlapping metadata (date, recordist, topic). They created two new dataset splits: a "clean" split that prevents exact duplicates from appearing in both training and evaluation sets, and a "group-filtered" split that additionally groups recordings sharing metadata to avoid weak contamination. They evaluated retrieval performance using two models - Collaborative-Experts architecture and a bi-encoder with PANNs audio encoder and distilroberta-base text encoder - measuring Recall at different levels (R@1, R@5, R@10).

## Key Results
- Models trained with leaked data achieved R@1 up to 52.2%, compared to R@1 down to 21.6% when trained without duplicates
- Retrieval performance dropped significantly (R@1 from ~27% to ~20%) in the group-filtered split
- The proposed dataset splits are publicly available for fair evaluation in cross-modal audio retrieval research

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Data leakage occurs when duplicate audio recordings appear in both training and evaluation splits, causing overly optimistic retrieval scores.
- Mechanism: Models learn exact or near-exact audio embeddings from training duplicates, so when similar audio appears in test, retrieval performance is artificially inflated.
- Core assumption: Audio duplicates are perceptually similar enough that models can transfer learned representations directly.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we find that SoundDesc contains several duplicates that cause leakage of training data to the evaluation data"
  - [section 3.3] "models trained with leaked data achieved significantly higher retrieval scores (R@1 up to 52.2%) compared to models trained without duplicates (R@1 down to 21.6%)"
- Break condition: If duplicate detection fails or if duplicates are sufficiently dissimilar (e.g., heavy noise processing), the leakage effect diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Grouping recordings by metadata (date, recordist, topic) reduces weak contamination in test splits.
- Mechanism: By ensuring all recordings sharing metadata remain in the same split, models cannot exploit subtle recording-setup similarities to artificially boost performance.
- Core assumption: Metadata groupings reflect perceptual similarity in audio content.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "To avoid weak contamination of the test data in SoundDesc we propose to not split recordings with these kinds of potential overlaps when partitioning the dataset"
  - [section 4.3] "Interestingly, the retrieval scores are significantly lower when the group-filtered split is used"
- Break condition: If metadata does not correlate with perceptual similarity, grouping will not prevent contamination.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Stratified sampling preserves category distribution across splits, maintaining benchmark validity.
- Mechanism: Ensures each split has proportional representation of categories, preventing category imbalance from confounding retrieval performance.
- Core assumption: Categories are meaningful and balanced in the original dataset.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1] "We use stratified sampling to maintain the same relative category distribution in all splits"
- Break condition: If category definitions are noisy or imbalanced, stratification may not ensure valid splits.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Audio Encoder (PANNs) -> Audio Embedding -> MLP layers -> Unified embedding space -> Retrieval layer; Text Encoder (DistilRoBERTa) -> Text Embedding -> MLP layers -> Unified embedding space -> Retrieval layer
- Critical path: Data -> Preprocess -> Encode (audio/text) -> Embed -> Rank -> Evaluate
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fixed audio encoder vs. fine-tuning: Faster training but less adaptation to task.
  - Single vector vs. sequence embeddings: Simpler but may lose temporal information.
- Failure signatures:
  - Overly high scores on duplicate-containing splits -> Data leakage.
  - Low scores on group-filtered split -> Weak contamination removal working.
  - Large variance across runs -> Training instability.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train with full dataset (including duplicates) and measure retrieval on test.
  2. Train with deduplicated dataset and compare retrieval scores.
  3. Train with group-filtered split and evaluate difficulty increase.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific characteristics of the BBC Sound Effects Archive contribute to data leakage in cross-modal retrieval datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies duplicates and overlapping recordings in the SoundDesc dataset, which was sourced from the BBC Sound Effects Archive, leading to data leakage between training and evaluation splits.
- Why unresolved: The study identifies the problem but does not analyze the specific characteristics of the archive (e.g., recording practices, metadata quality) that contribute to these duplicates and overlaps.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of the BBC Sound Effects Archive's recording and metadata practices, comparing them with other audio archives to identify unique factors contributing to data leakage.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of text-based audio retrieval models vary across different types of duplicates (exact, reprocessed, partial overlaps) in cross-modal datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions finding exact duplicates, reprocessed recordings, and recordings with partial overlaps, and shows that these lead to overly optimistic retrieval scores.
- Why unresolved: The study treats all duplicates similarly in terms of their impact on performance, without differentiating between types of duplicates.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments evaluating model performance on datasets containing only exact duplicates, only reprocessed recordings, and only partial overlaps, to quantify their individual impacts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal balance between preventing data leakage and maintaining dataset size in cross-modal retrieval benchmarks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors propose new dataset splits to prevent data leakage but acknowledge that this leads to a reduction in dataset size and potentially more challenging benchmarks.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the trade-off between dataset size and data leakage prevention, nor does it provide guidelines for determining when data leakage prevention is most critical.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the strictness of data leakage prevention measures and measuring their impact on model performance and dataset size, to identify an optimal balance.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How do different audio fingerprinting systems compare in their ability to detect various types of duplicates in cross-modal retrieval datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses the Panako audio fingerprinting system to detect duplicates in the SoundDesc dataset.
- Why unresolved: The study does not compare the effectiveness of different audio fingerprinting systems or explore how their parameters affect duplicate detection accuracy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of multiple audio fingerprinting systems applied to the same dataset, evaluating their precision, recall, and computational efficiency in detecting different types of duplicates.

## Limitations
- The study relies on BBC NHU metadata for detecting weak contamination, which may not capture all sources of similarity between recordings.
- The audio fingerprinting method used (Panako) may not detect all duplicates, particularly those with heavy post-processing or compression artifacts.
- The group-filtered split may introduce its own biases by enforcing metadata-based groupings that could correlate with specific audio characteristics.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The existence of exact duplicates causing data leakage in SoundDesc is well-documented with concrete fingerprinting evidence and clear performance differences.
- Medium confidence: The effectiveness of metadata grouping in reducing weak contamination, as this depends on metadata quality and completeness.
- Medium confidence: The proposed clean and group-filtered splits as valid benchmarks, pending broader community validation and comparison with other deduplication approaches.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-validate fingerprinting results using an independent audio fingerprinting system (e.g., Dejavu or AcoustID) to verify duplicate detection accuracy and ensure no systematic biases in Panako's matching algorithm.

2. Test metadata grouping robustness by evaluating whether removing BBC NHU metadata from the group-filtered split still maintains separation between training and evaluation data, or if contamination re-emerges.

3. Benchmark against alternative deduplication methods by applying simple audio similarity metrics (e.g., cross-correlation, spectral distance) to compare retrieval performance degradation against the group-filtered split, establishing whether the proposed approach represents an upper or lower bound on achievable robustness.
