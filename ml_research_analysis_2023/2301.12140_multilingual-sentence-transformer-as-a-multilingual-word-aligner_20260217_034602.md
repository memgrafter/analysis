# Multilingual Sentence Transformer as A Multilingual Word Aligner

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2301.12140
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12140
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: AccAlign achieves new state-of-the-art performance on seven language pairs using LaBSE's implicit word-level embeddings with adapter-based fine-tuning.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AccAlign, a parameter-efficient method for multilingual word alignment using LaBSE, a multilingual sentence transformer. The key insight is that LaBSE, trained with contrastive loss for language-agnostic sentence representations, implicitly learns language-agnostic word-level embeddings in its intermediate layers. By extracting contextual embeddings from the 6th layer and computing cosine similarity matrices, AccAlign can induce word alignments without additional training. Further improvement is achieved through a simple adapter-based fine-tuning approach on parallel corpus, achieving state-of-the-art performance across seven language pairs while using only 2.4M parameters (0.5% of LaBSE's total parameters).

## Method Summary
AccAlign uses LaBSE (471M parameters) to extract contextual word embeddings from the 6th layer for both source and target sentences. A similarity matrix is computed using cosine similarity between all word pairs, normalized with softmax, and alignments are extracted using intersection of forward and backward probability matrices with threshold 0.1. For improved performance, adapter modules (2.4M parameters, 128 hidden dimension) are added to LaBSE and fine-tuned on 3,362 parallel sentence pairs using a contrastive loss that encourages gold aligned words to have closer representations. The method is evaluated on seven language pairs from the ALIGN6 dataset using Alignment Error Rate (AER) as the primary metric.

## Key Results
- AccAlign achieves new state-of-the-art performance on seven language pairs (de-en, sv-en, fr-en, ro-en, ja-en, zh-en, fa-en)
- Zero-shot performance on unseen language pairs (ro-en, ja-en, zh-en, fa-en) outperforms previous methods trained specifically on those pairs
- Adapter-based fine-tuning improves performance while using only 0.5% of LaBSE's parameters
- Layer 6 of LaBSE provides optimal balance for word alignment, outperforming other layers by 0.1 to 26.0 averaged AER

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LaBSE implicitly learns language-agnostic word-level embeddings in intermediate layers
- Mechanism: Contrastive training on large-scale parallel corpus forces sentence embeddings to be language-agnostic, which transfers to word-level representations through shared contextualization
- Core assumption: Language-agnostic sentence embeddings imply language-agnostic word embeddings in same model
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This idea is non-trivial as LaBSE is trained to learn language-agnostic sentence-level embeddings, while the alignment extraction task requires the more fine-grained word-level embeddings to be language-agnostic"
  - [section 2.1] "However, as LaBSE does not include any word-level training loss when finetuning with contrastive loss, it is unclear whether the model has learned high-quality language-agnostic word-level embeddings"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence for word-level embeddings
- Break condition: If intermediate layers show poor word-level alignment performance

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adapter-based finetuning is more effective than full finetuning for this task
- Mechanism: Adapters add minimal parameters but target the specific alignment objective, preserving most of LaBSE's learned representations
- Core assumption: Task-specific fine-tuning benefits from preserving pretrained representations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.3] "Compared with full finetuning, adapter-based finetuning updates much less parameters and obtains better performance under both supervised and self-supervised settings"
  - [section 2.3] "Table 3: AER comparison of full finetuning and adapter-based finetuning"
  - [corpus] Moderate - shows parameter efficiency but limited ablation studies
- Break condition: If task requires significant architectural changes

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Alignment quality is layer-dependent in LaBSE
- Mechanism: Different layers capture different levels of abstraction; intermediate layers (like layer 6) best balance word-level precision with contextual information
- Core assumption: Layer position correlates with semantic abstraction level
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "Layer 6, which is the layer we use for all experiments, outperforms other layers by 0.1 to 26.0 averaged AER"
  - [section 3.3] "Table 10 in appendix shows more details"
  - [corpus] Weak - only empirical layer comparison without theoretical justification
- Break condition: If different tasks require different optimal layers

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Contrastive learning
  - Why needed here: Enables LaBSE to learn language-agnostic representations by pulling aligned sentences together and pushing non-aligned ones apart
  - Quick check question: How does the contrastive loss function encourage language-agnostic representations?

- Concept: Adapter modules
  - Why needed here: Allow task-specific fine-tuning while preserving most of the pretrained model parameters
  - Quick check question: What is the dimensionality relationship between input, adapter, and output in the adapter module?

- Concept: Word alignment evaluation (AER)
  - Why needed here: Provides quantitative measure of alignment quality to compare different approaches
  - Quick check question: How is AER calculated and what does a lower score indicate?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: LaBSE (471M parameters) -> Intermediate layer (layer 6) -> Similarity matrix computation -> Alignment extraction -> Adapter modules (2.4M parameters) for fine-tuning
- Critical path: Sentence pair -> LaBSE embeddings -> Cosine similarity matrix -> Thresholding -> Alignment extraction
- Design tradeoffs: Language-agnostic representations vs. language-specific precision; parameter efficiency vs. full fine-tuning flexibility
- Failure signatures: High AER scores; poor cross-lingual transfer; overfitting to training languages

## Limitations

- The theoretical justification for why contrastive training at sentence level induces useful word-level representations is speculative
- Limited ablation studies on adapter dimensions and alternative fine-tuning approaches
- Performance on truly low-resource language pairs and languages outside LaBSE's pretraining is not evaluated

## Confidence

**High Confidence (3-4)**: The claim that LaBSE can be used for word alignment without additional training is well-supported by empirical results showing strong zero-shot performance across seven language pairs.

**Medium Confidence (2)**: The claim about adapter-based finetuning being superior to full finetuning is supported by Table 3, but the comparison is limited to one dataset and could benefit from more extensive ablation studies.

**Low Confidence (1)**: The mechanism explaining why LaBSE implicitly learns language-agnostic word embeddings is speculative, lacking sufficient theoretical grounding despite strong empirical results.
