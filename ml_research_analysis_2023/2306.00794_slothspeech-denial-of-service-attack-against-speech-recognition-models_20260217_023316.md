# SlothSpeech: Denial-of-service Attack Against Speech Recognition Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00794
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00794
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: SlothSpeech is the first DoS attack targeting ASR systems by exploiting dynamic decoding behavior, increasing latency by up to 40x with similar perturbation magnitude to Gaussian noise

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SlothSpeech, a novel denial-of-service attack that exploits the dynamic decoding behavior of automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. The attack works by iteratively modifying audio inputs to delay the occurrence of end-of-sentence (EOS) tokens, forcing the decoder to generate more tokens and significantly increase computational latency. Evaluated on three popular ASR models (Speech2Text, Whisper, Speech2Text2) across three datasets (LibriSpeech, OpenSLR, VCTK), SlothSpeech achieves latency increases of up to 40x compared to benign inputs while maintaining similar perturbation magnitudes to Gaussian noise. The findings highlight a critical efficiency robustness vulnerability in dynamic decoder-based ASR systems.

## Method Summary
SlothSpeech is a white-box, iterative attack that targets the dynamic decoding behavior of ASR models. The attack uses the probability distribution of output tokens to formulate an optimization problem that delays EOS token occurrence. A differentiable proxy for latency is employed, combining the minimization of EOS token likelihood with a distance constraint on the perturbation (L2 or L∞ norm). The attack iteratively updates the adversarial input using gradient information from this loss function, effectively forcing the decoder to generate more tokens before reaching EOS, thereby increasing computational cost and latency.

## Key Results
- SlothSpeech increases ASR model latency by up to 40x compared to benign inputs
- The attack achieves similar perturbation magnitudes to Gaussian noise but with significantly greater effectiveness
- Tested across three ASR models (Speech2Text, Whisper, Speech2Text2) and three datasets (LibriSpeech, OpenSLR, VCTK)
- Demonstrates a critical efficiency robustness vulnerability in dynamic decoder-based ASR systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The attack delays the end-of-sentence (EOS) token to increase ASR model latency.
- Mechanism: The attack iteratively modifies the input audio to decrease the likelihood of the EOS token appearing early in the output sequence. This forces the decoder to generate more tokens before reaching EOS, increasing computational cost.
- Core assumption: ASR models with dynamic decoders allocate more computational resources to longer output sequences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The core idea is to iteratively modify audio inputs so that the ASR model's decoder generates significantly more tokens before reaching an end-of-sentence marker, thus increasing latency."
  - [section 2.1]: "The decoder begins with a special token SOS and generates subsequent output tokens iteratively... The decoding process will continue until it reaches another token EOS."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - corpus papers focus on general adversarial attacks but don't specifically discuss EOS token manipulation for DoS.
- Break condition: If the ASR model implements EOS token probability constraints or has a maximum token limit that's too restrictive for the attack to be effective.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The attack uses a differentiable proxy for latency to enable gradient-based optimization.
- Mechanism: Instead of directly optimizing for latency (non-differentiable), the attack minimizes the likelihood of EOS tokens and maximizes the likelihood of the second most probable token in the final position, creating a differentiable loss function.
- Core assumption: The relationship between EOS token probability and decoder iterations is monotonic and can be approximated with a differentiable function.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2]: "To delay the occurrence of the EOS tokens... we convert the probability distribution pi for the multi-classification task to a binary classification task... Our first objective is to decrease the likelihood of all EOS tokens in the output"
  - [section 3.2]: "the proxy of Efficiencyf will be LEOS = (1/n ∑i=1 li(x + δ)EOS + qn(x + δ))"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - corpus papers mention adversarial attacks but don't specifically discuss differentiable latency proxies.
- Break condition: If the relationship between EOS probability and actual latency becomes non-monotonic or if the differentiable approximation becomes inaccurate for certain input types.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The attack exploits the dynamic computation nature of certain ASR architectures.
- Mechanism: Dynamic decoder-based ASR models like Speech2Text and Whisper allocate computational resources based on input, generating variable numbers of tokens. By forcing more tokens to be generated, the attack increases resource consumption.
- Core assumption: Dynamic decoder architectures inherently have variable computational costs based on output length.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We show that popular ASR models like Speech2Text model and Whisper model have dynamic computation based on different inputs, causing dynamic efficiency."
  - [section 1]: "Popular ASR models like Speech2Text [6, 7] and Whisper [8] use dynamic decoder-based mechanisms. As the dynamic decoder shows dynamic efficiency, the efficiency robustness of these models is needed to be evaluated."
  - [corpus]: Moderate evidence - corpus includes papers on adversarial attacks against ASR systems, suggesting awareness of this vulnerability class.
- Break condition: If the ASR model switches to a static decoding approach or implements fixed computational budgets regardless of output length.

- Critical path: Audio input → Encoder → Dynamic decoder (multiple iterations) → Text output → Latency measurement
- Design tradeoffs:
  - L2 vs L∞ norm for perturbation constraint: L2 provides smoother optimization but L∞ might create more perceptible artifacts

  - Weight parameter c: Balances attack effectiveness against imperceptibility

  - Attack fails to increase token count despite optimization
  - Generated perturbations are easily perceptible to humans
  - Attack works on some models but not others (indicating architectural differences)
  - Performance degrades significantly with certain datasets

## Limitations

- Attack effectiveness may be heavily dependent on specific dynamic decoder implementation and may not generalize to all ASR architectures
- The paper doesn't fully characterize the attack's success rate and effectiveness across different input types, audio qualities, and language variations
- The assumption that EOS token delay directly correlates with computational cost may not hold for all dynamic decoder implementations
- The paper doesn't address potential defenses or mitigation strategies that ASR system designers might employ

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The core mechanism of exploiting dynamic decoder behavior through EOS token manipulation is well-supported by the paper's experimental results and aligns with known ASR architecture characteristics. The formulation of the differentiable proxy loss function and its implementation details are clearly specified.

**Medium confidence**: The generalizability of the attack across different ASR architectures and real-world deployment scenarios. While the paper tests three popular models, the results may not extend to all dynamic decoder-based systems or those with different architectural optimizations.

**Low confidence**: The practical impact of this attack in real-world scenarios, particularly considering potential detection mechanisms, human perception thresholds, and the trade-off between attack effectiveness and perturbation imperceptibility at scale.
