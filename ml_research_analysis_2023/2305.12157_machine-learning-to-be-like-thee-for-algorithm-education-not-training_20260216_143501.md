# (Machine) Learning to Be Like Thee? For Algorithm Education, Not Training

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that Machine Learning algorithms must be educated, not merely trained, to make ethical decisions. The authors draw an analogy between human child education and algorithmic learning, proposing that AI systems require structured, ethically-curated input across their lifecycle—ethical data, ethical programming, and ethical revision—rather than just pattern extraction from uncurated datasets. They argue that the is-ought fallacy and lack of moral context in training data lead to biased, harmful outcomes (e.g., discriminatory hiring algorithms, racist chatbots). To prevent such harms, they propose integrating moral philosophy into AI development, making ethical reasoning a core component of algorithm design and oversight. The paper calls for transdisciplinary collaboration between AI developers, ethicists, and policymakers to ensure algorithms are educated to contribute to societal good.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a framework for "algorithm education" as an alternative to traditional ML training, arguing that ethical decision-making requires more than pattern recognition. The proposed approach consists of three pillars: ethical data curation, ethical programming constraints, and ethical revision/feedback mechanisms. The method draws analogies from human child education, suggesting that AI systems need supervised learning with curated ethical content rather than unsupervised pattern extraction. The authors propose integrating moral philosophy into the AI development lifecycle to address the is-ought fallacy and prevent biased outcomes.

## Key Results
- Machine Learning algorithms perpetuate societal biases because they cannot distinguish between descriptive patterns and normative judgments
- The is-ought fallacy explains why training ML on existing data leads to replicating rather than correcting societal injustices
- Children's education provides a valid analogy for AI algorithm education, where structured learning with ethical guidance produces better outcomes than unsupervised learning

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can algorithms ever truly develop moral agency, or will they remain dependent on human-defined ethical frameworks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the debate around whether current AI systems can be considered moral agents and notes that "ML algorithms cannot understand what it means to be in the stage of social action, i.e. to be a social actor."
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges that AI lacks human-like understanding of social contexts and moral reasoning, yet it also argues for educating algorithms to make ethical decisions. The tension between AI's current limitations and the proposed educational approach creates an unresolved question about the nature and possibility of algorithmic moral agency.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies demonstrating whether AI systems can develop genuine moral reasoning capabilities independent of human input, or conversely, clear evidence that all algorithmic "ethical" decisions remain fundamentally derivative of human programming and data.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Who should have the authority to define what constitutes "ethical" data, programming, and revision in AI systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly asks "A prominent, open question concerns who is capacitated to define what is ethical. In other words, how to define fairness? Can fairness even be identified objectively, or is it profoundly contextual?"
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this as a "prominent, open question" and notes that "recent approaches suggest that for AI we might need not one but many accounts of fairness, each of them adapted to the area the algorithm is to be applied to." This highlights the complexity of establishing universal ethical standards.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of different approaches to defining AI ethics across various cultural and regulatory contexts, or development of consensus frameworks that can accommodate diverse perspectives while maintaining coherent standards.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What specific mechanisms can be implemented to ensure that algorithm education (ethical data, programming, and revision) effectively prevents harmful outcomes while preserving beneficial AI capabilities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] While the paper proposes the three pillars of algorithm education, it acknowledges the need for practical implementation and notes that "the opposite (e.g. not eliminating the side effects because it is financially inconvenient) would be immoral" when discussing how to handle AI's potential harms.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the need for algorithm education but doesn't provide specific, tested mechanisms for implementing these educational pillars in practice, nor does it address how to balance ethical constraints with AI's beneficial capabilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical evaluation of different approaches to implementing ethical data selection, programming constraints, and revision processes in real-world AI systems, demonstrating their effectiveness in preventing harmful outcomes while maintaining or improving beneficial capabilities.

## Limitations
- The framework remains largely theoretical without empirical validation or performance data
- Limited concrete implementation details for the three-pillar educational approach
- No specific methodologies provided for measuring improvements in algorithmic ethical decision-making
- The paper doesn't address how to handle conflicting moral frameworks across different cultures

## Confidence

- Medium confidence: The three-pillar framework structure and its theoretical validity
- Low confidence: Practical implementation details and effectiveness metrics
