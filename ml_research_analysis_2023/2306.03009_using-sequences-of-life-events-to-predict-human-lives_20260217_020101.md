# Using Sequences of Life-events to Predict Human Lives

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03009
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03009
- Reference count: 40
- Researchers applied transformer-based architectures to life-event sequences derived from Danish national registry data spanning 6 million individuals. By encoding labor, health, and demographic events as synthetic sentences with age and time positional encodings, they trained a life2vec model that predicts early mortality with a corrected Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.41, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines by ~11%. The model learns a robust concept embedding space where related life events cluster meaningfully, and generates person-level embeddings that can be interpreted using TCAV and saliency methods. Results demonstrate that highly detailed, multimodal life-trajectory data enables accurate individual-level predictions and opens new possibilities for studying social determinants of health.

## Executive Summary
Researchers developed life2vec, a transformer-based model that treats life event sequences as synthetic language, enabling accurate prediction of early mortality and personality traits from Danish registry data. By encoding events with age and time positional information, the model learns meaningful relationships between life events and generates interpretable person-level embeddings. The approach outperforms existing baselines and demonstrates that highly detailed, multimodal life trajectory data can serve as a powerful predictor of individual outcomes.

## Method Summary
The method transforms Danish registry data into synthetic language sequences where each life event is encoded as a concept token with age and absolute time positional encodings. A BERT-like transformer with Performer-style attention is pre-trained using masked language modeling and sequence order prediction tasks. The model learns a unified concept embedding space where related events cluster meaningfully. For prediction tasks, the model is fine-tuned to generate person-level embeddings that are interpreted using TCAV and saliency methods. The approach handles censored data through asymmetric cross-entropy loss and incorporates domain-specific loss functions for different prediction tasks.

## Key Results
- Life2vec achieves a corrected Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.41 for 4-year mortality prediction, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines by ~11%
- The model learns a robust concept embedding space where related life events cluster meaningfully, with semantic relationships preserved across event types
- Person-level embeddings generated by the model can be interpreted using TCAV and saliency methods, revealing interpretable behavioral phenotypes linked to prediction outcomes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Life-event sequences can be encoded as synthetic sentences with positional encodings, enabling transformer architectures to model life trajectories effectively.
- Mechanism: By representing each life-event as a "sentence" composed of concept tokens (e.g., job type, diagnosis, income level) and attaching both age and absolute time positional encodings, the model captures temporal structure analogous to natural language. This allows the transformer to learn contextual relationships between events across an individual's timeline.
- Core assumption: The sequential and contextual nature of life events mirrors that of language, making transformer architectures applicable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We can also represent human lives in a way that shares this structural similarity to language"
  - [section] "From one perspective, lives are simply sequences of events: People are born, visit the pediatrician, start school, move to a new location, get married, and so on."
  - [corpus] Weak—no direct paper in corpus explicitly states this, but related work on sequence modeling in other domains (e.g., protein structures, music) supports the analogy.
- Break condition: If life events lack sequential dependencies or if temporal ordering is irrelevant to outcomes, the transformer's attention mechanism loses predictive power.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: A unified concept embedding space allows the model to capture meaningful relationships between diverse life events.
- Mechanism: The model learns a single high-dimensional vector space where all event types (health, labor, demographic) coexist. Similar events (e.g., diagnoses within the same ICD-10 chapter, income levels) cluster together, revealing semantic and ordinal relationships. This structured embedding enables transfer learning across event types for prediction.
- Core assumption: Events that are semantically or functionally related in real life will have similar vector representations in the embedding space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We create embeddings of life-events in a single vector space showing that this embedding space is robust and highly structured."
  - [section] "When the model does mix up ICD-10 codes, the 'mistakes' are meaningful... the model's ability to group similar concepts that are not necessarily close in the standard classification systems."
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus lacks direct evidence of embedding space analysis for life events; closest is work on sequence classification in other domains.
- Break condition: If event relationships are too domain-specific or if the embedding space collapses into hubs (low-frequency tokens dominate), the learned associations become uninformative.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Person-level embeddings, conditioned on specific prediction tasks, compress life trajectories into interpretable behavioral phenotypes.
- Mechanism: After pre-training on structure learning (MLM + SOP), the model fine-tunes to predict outcomes (e.g., mortality, personality). During fine-tuning, it produces a single vector summarizing each individual's sequence, emphasizing features relevant to the task. TCAV and saliency methods reveal which life-event concepts drive predictions.
- Core assumption: Task-specific fine-tuning produces embeddings that highlight relevant life-event patterns for the target outcome.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The person-summaries the model learns in this last step is conditional on the classification task; it identifies and compresses patterns that maximize the certainty around a given downstream task."
  - [section] "Using explainability tools such as saliency maps [34, 35] and concept activation vectors (TCAV) [36], we show that the person-summaries are also meaningful and hold the potential to serve as a behavioural phenotype."
  - [corpus] Missing—no direct corpus evidence of person-level embeddings or TCAV usage in similar socio-economic datasets.
- Break condition: If fine-tuning overfits to spurious correlations or if embeddings are not stable across random seeds, the behavioral phenotypes become unreliable.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Transformer architecture and attention mechanisms
  - Why needed here: The model relies on self-attention to capture long-range dependencies and contextual relationships in life sequences.
  - Quick check question: Can you explain how multi-head attention with Performer-style approximation enables modeling of sequences longer than 512 tokens?

- Concept: Masked Language Modeling (MLM) and Sequence Order Prediction (SOP) pre-training tasks
  - Why needed here: MLM forces the model to learn contextual relationships between event tokens; SOP ensures it captures temporal coherence.
  - Quick check question: Why is it necessary to combine MLM with SOP, rather than using MLM alone, in this life-event context?

- Concept: Positive-unlabeled (PU) learning and loss function adaptation
  - Why needed here: The mortality prediction task includes censored data (emigrants, missing outcomes), requiring specialized loss functions to avoid bias.
  - Quick check question: How does asymmetric cross-entropy loss handle unlabeled samples differently from standard cross-entropy in this PU setting?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Raw registry data -> Synthetic language sequences with age/time encodings -> Embedding layer -> Transformer encoder stack -> Decoder(s) for pre-training/fine-tuning -> Person-level embeddings

- Critical path:
  1. Preprocess raw Danish registry data into synthetic sentences with age/absolute time encodings
  2. Train base model on MLM + SOP to learn concept space
  3. Fine-tune on target task (mortality or personality) using appropriate loss
  4. Extract person embeddings and interpret via TCAV/saliency

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using Performer-style attention allows scaling to long sequences but sacrifices some precision compared to exact softmax attention
  - Freezing most concept embeddings during fine-tuning preserves pre-learned semantic structure but limits adaptation to task-specific nuances
  - Augmenting sequences (subsampling, noise injection) improves generalization but adds complexity to training pipeline

- Failure signatures:
  - Collapse of concept embedding space into hubs (low-frequency tokens dominate)
  - Overfitting to spurious correlations in fine-tuning (e.g., predicting mortality from non-causal demographic proxies)
  - Poor performance on censored data if PU loss is not properly tuned

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train base model with only MLM (no SOP) and compare perplexity/sequence order recovery to confirm SOP's contribution
  2. Replace Performer-style attention with exact softmax attention on a small subset and measure impact on long-sequence performance
  3. Fine-tune on mortality prediction with standard cross-entropy (ignoring PU structure) and compare C-MCC to confirm necessity of PU loss

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How do life2vec predictions change when applied to populations with different social welfare systems or healthcare structures?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "we urge caution with extrapolation to other populations since we have not explored how our findings translate beyond the current study population."
  - Why unresolved: The model was trained and tested exclusively on Danish registry data, which benefits from comprehensive social services and universal healthcare. Different welfare systems may alter the predictive power of certain life events.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Training and testing life2vec on datasets from countries with different social policies (e.g., US, Germany, or Japan) and comparing prediction accuracy across these populations.

- Can life2vec effectively incorporate new or rare life events not present in the original training data?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The model's concept embedding space is shown to be robust, but the paper does not address its ability to generalize to unseen event types.
  - Why unresolved: The vocabulary and embeddings were learned from a fixed set of events; rare or emerging events (e.g., global pandemics) may not have clear analogs in the learned space.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Testing the model's performance after adding new event types to the vocabulary and retraining, or using transfer learning to adapt to new event categories.

- What is the minimum amount of longitudinal data required for life2vec to make accurate predictions?
  - Basis in paper: [inferred] The model is trained on decade-long sequences; however, the paper does not explore performance with shorter sequences or missing data.
  - Why unresolved: The dataset provides extensive, continuous records, but real-world applications may involve incomplete or sparse life event data.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Systematically reducing sequence length or introducing gaps in the data and measuring changes in predictive accuracy and embedding stability.

## Limitations

- The model's performance relies heavily on the quality and completeness of Danish registry data, which may not generalize to other populations or healthcare systems with different data collection practices.
- The use of synthetic language representation for life events assumes that the sequential and contextual structure of life events mirrors that of natural language, but this analogy may break down for certain types of life trajectories or cultural contexts.
- The concept embedding space's robustness and interpretability are demonstrated through qualitative analysis, but quantitative validation of the semantic relationships between event embeddings remains limited.

## Confidence

- High confidence in the technical implementation of the transformer architecture and pre-training/fine-tuning pipeline.
- Medium confidence in the validity of using life-event sequences as synthetic language for transformer modeling, pending further validation across diverse populations.
- Medium confidence in the interpretability of person-level embeddings and their utility as behavioral phenotypes, as TCAV and saliency methods provide insights but may not capture all relevant patterns.
