# Latent Autoregressive Source Separation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.08562
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08562
- Reference count: 10
- Key outcome: LASS achieves PSNR scores of 24.2 on MNIST and 8.96 FID on CelebA, outperforming several generative separation baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LASS (Latent Autoregressive Source Separation), a method for separating mixed signals into their constituent sources using pre-trained autoregressive models in a quantized latent space. The approach uses a Bayesian formulation where autoregressive models act as priors, and a non-parametric likelihood function is learned via frequency counts of latent sums of addend tokens. Experiments on images (MNIST, CelebA, CelebA-HQ, ImageNet) and audio (Slakh2100 dataset) show competitive separation quality with significant speedups in inference time and scalability to higher-dimensional data.

## Method Summary
LASS operates in the quantized latent space of a VQ-VAE, using pre-trained autoregressive models as priors and a learned non-parametric likelihood function. The method constructs a discrete likelihood tensor P where P[z1, z2, m] counts how often latent codes z1 and z2 combine to produce m. During inference, the posterior p(zs|z<s, m≤s) is computed by combining the autoregressive prior with the likelihood, enabling sampling-based separation without gradient-based optimization. The approach is demonstrated on image datasets (MNIST, CelebA, CelebA-HQ, ImageNet) and audio data (Slakh2100), achieving competitive results compared to baseline methods.

## Key Results
- Achieves PSNR scores of 24.2 on MNIST source separation
- Obtains FID score of 8.96 on CelebA image separation
- Demonstrates 6-7× speedup in inference time compared to gradient-based methods
- Shows sparsity levels below 7% across all tested datasets, enabling memory-efficient inference

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LASS separates sources by using the sum of latent codes as a constraint, learning a non-parametric likelihood from frequency counts of these sums.
- Mechanism: The method treats autoregressive models as priors and constructs a discrete likelihood tensor P where P[z1, z2, m] counts how often latent codes z1 and z2 combine to produce m. At inference, the posterior p(zs|z<s, m≤s) ∝ pφ(zs|z<s) p(m≤s|z≤s) is computed by combining the autoregressive prior with the likelihood, enabling sampling-based separation without gradient-based optimization.
- Core assumption: The VQ-VAE quantization is local and additive in the data domain, so the mixing operation in the data domain corresponds to a sum-like operation in the latent space, making frequency counts of latent sums a valid likelihood model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "a discrete (non-parametric) likelihood function is constructed by performing frequency counts over latent sums of addend tokens."
  - [section]: "we can drop the dependency on the previous context inside the likelihood function in Eq. (3), approximating it as p(ms|zs)."
  - [corpus]: Missing. No corpus paper directly addresses this specific likelihood construction mechanism.
- Break condition: If the VQ-VAE encoder does not preserve locality or if the data domain mixing is not additive in the latent space, the frequency count model becomes invalid and separation fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LASS can use pre-trained autoregressive models directly, avoiding retraining or fine-tuning for source separation.
- Mechanism: By separating the prior (autoregressive model) from the likelihood (frequency count tensor), LASS does not modify the autoregressive models. The learned P tensor is constructed independently from source data, allowing any pre-trained model in the latent space to be used as a prior without further optimization.
- Core assumption: The autoregressive model's distribution over latent tokens is sufficient as a prior for source separation, and the likelihood tensor can be learned independently without changing the model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "without requiring additional gradient-based optimization or modifications of existing models."
  - [section]: "This module is not invasive, neither for the VQ-VAE nor for the autoregressive priors, given that the representation space of the VQ-VAE does not change while learning the likelihood function."
  - [corpus]: Missing. No corpus paper directly addresses using pre-trained models without retraining for source separation.
- Break condition: If the autoregressive model was trained on a distribution too different from the source data, the prior will be a poor fit and separation quality will degrade.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The discrete likelihood tensor P is highly sparse, enabling memory-efficient inference even with large K.
- Mechanism: The paper reports that density (percentage of non-zero elements) in P is below 7% for all tested datasets, so storing and indexing only non-zero entries makes inference tractable. This sparsity arises because only certain combinations of latent codes can plausibly sum to observed mixtures.
- Core assumption: The mixing process in the latent domain produces a sparse set of possible (z1, z2, m) combinations, so most entries in P are zero and can be omitted.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "In practice, the tensor P is highly sparse. We showcase this in Table 1 for all our experiments, where the density of P is defined as the percentage of nonzero elements in P."
  - [section]: Table 1 lists densities ranging from 3.9×10^-3 (ImageNet) to 1.49×10^-2 (MNIST).
  - [corpus]: Missing. No corpus paper directly addresses sparsity of latent sum likelihood tensors.
- Break condition: If the latent space or mixing process changes such that more combinations become possible, density increases and memory requirements grow beyond practical limits.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - VQ-VAE encoder Eθ -> Discrete latent z -> VQ-VAE decoder Dψ
  - Autoregressive priors pφ1, pφ2 -> Model p(zs|z<s) for each source
  - Likelihood tensor P -> p(m|z1, z2) learned from frequency counts
  - Sampler -> Ancestral or beam search to draw tokens from posterior
  - Post-inference refinement -> Optimize dense embeddings to better satisfy sum constraint

- Critical path:
  1. Encode mixture y → latent m
  2. For each step s, compute prior (outer product of conditionals) and likelihood (slice of P)
  3. Combine in log space, sample tokens for z1, z2
  4. Decode z1, z2 → separated sources x1, x2
  5. (Optional) Refine x1, x2 to better match mixture

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Pre-trained vs. trained-from-scratch models: pre-trained saves compute but may not fit data distribution as well
  - Sampling strategy: ancestral is faster but beam search can find higher probability solutions at higher compute cost
  - Likelihood scaling λ: must balance prior and likelihood; too high causes overfitting to counts, too low ignores sum constraint
  - Tensor sparsity: sparsity enables memory efficiency, but if density increases (e.g., more sources) memory may become a bottleneck

- Failure signatures:
  - Separation artifacts or blurriness: likely VQ-VAE reconstruction quality issue
  - Low diversity or repetitive outputs: autoregressive prior too peaked or sampling too greedy
  - Missing components or ghosting: likelihood tensor insufficiently captures sum statistics
  - Slow inference or memory errors: likelihood tensor too dense or sampling inefficient

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a small VQ-VAE on MNIST, train an autoregressive Transformer on latent codes, implement LASS with top-k sampling, evaluate PSNR on a small test set.
  2. Compare ancestral vs. beam search sampling on a fixed dataset, measure both separation quality and runtime.
  3. Vary the likelihood scaling λ on a validation set, plot separation quality vs. λ to find optimal balance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of LASS scale when separating more than two sources simultaneously?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper explicitly mentions this as a limitation, stating "In this paper we limit our analysis to the separation of two sources" and suggests recursive separation techniques as a potential solution.
- Why unresolved: The current method requires increasing the dimensions of both the priors and likelihood function tensor when dealing with multiple sources, which could become computationally prohibitive.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments showing LASS performance with 3+ sources, including memory usage analysis and comparison with recursive separation approaches.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can LASS maintain performance when applied to source separation tasks with more complex locality assumptions, such as colorization or super-resolution?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper identifies this as a limitation, noting that tasks like colorization and super-resolution "would require a larger conditioning context" and suggesting newer quantization schemes as potential solutions.
- Why unresolved: The current method relies on a locality assumption in Eq. (4) that assumes mixing operations are local in both data and latent domains, which may not hold for all tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments applying LASS to colorization/super-resolution tasks versus methods designed specifically for those tasks, with quantitative evaluation of separation quality.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the sparsity of the likelihood tensor P affect LASS performance across different datasets and quantization levels?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper provides density statistics for likelihood functions across different datasets (Table 1), showing varying levels of sparsity from 1.49×10⁻² to 3.90×10⁻³.
- Why unresolved: While the paper notes that P is highly sparse in practice, it doesn't systematically investigate how different levels of sparsity impact separation quality or whether sparsity is beneficial or detrimental.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying the quantization level K while measuring both sparsity and separation performance, potentially revealing an optimal sparsity level for different tasks.

## Limitations
- Strong assumption that VQ-VAE quantization is additive in the data domain, which may not hold for all mixing operations
- Limited exploration of how the method scales to more than two sources simultaneously
- Reliance on pre-trained models that may not match the source data distribution

## Confidence
**High Confidence:**
- LASS can perform source separation using pre-trained autoregressive models without gradient-based optimization
- The method achieves competitive separation quality on benchmark datasets
- The likelihood tensor P exhibits significant sparsity across tested datasets

**Medium Confidence:**
- The frequency count model of latent sums is a valid approximation for source separation
- Pre-trained models can be directly applied without fine-tuning for source separation tasks
- The additive assumption in latent space holds sufficiently for practical separation

**Low Confidence:**
- The method will scale effectively to more than two sources
- The approach generalizes to arbitrary mixing operations beyond simple averaging
- The sparsity pattern will remain beneficial for larger codebook sizes or higher-dimensional data

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness to Mixing Operations**: Test LASS on non-additive mixing scenarios (e.g., multiplicative mixing, weighted sums) to validate whether the latent sum assumption holds beyond simple averaging.

2. **Scalability Analysis**: Systematically increase the number of sources from 2 to 4-5 and measure how likelihood tensor density, memory requirements, and separation quality scale, identifying the practical limits of the approach.

3. **Model Transferability**: Evaluate separation performance when using autoregressive models trained on datasets that differ from the source data distribution, quantifying how much model mismatch degrades quality and whether fine-tuning is necessary.
