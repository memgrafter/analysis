# Training Data Extraction From Pre-trained Language Models: A Survey

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16157
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16157
- Reference count: 40
- First comprehensive survey on training data extraction from pre-trained language models

## Executive Summary
This survey systematically reviews over 100 papers on training data extraction from pre-trained language models (PLMs), covering definitions of memorization, attack and defense approaches, and empirical findings. The study focuses primarily on autoregressive language models and provides a taxonomy of memorization definitions while systematizing approaches for both attacks and defenses. It highlights empirical findings about memorization trends and suggests future research directions based on the comprehensive review of literature from natural language processing and security fields.

## Method Summary
The survey synthesizes existing literature on training data extraction from PLMs, focusing on autoregressive language models like GPT-Neo. The methodology involves candidate generation through decoding methods (greedy, top-k, top-p sampling), membership inference using perplexity metrics, and evaluation of various defense mechanisms including data sanitization, differential privacy, and post-processing filtering. The study examines the relationship between model size, training data characteristics, and memorization patterns across different experimental setups and datasets.

## Key Results
- Larger PLM parameter counts exhibit near-perfect log-linear relationships with increased memorization rates
- Duplicate training data strings significantly increase memorization probability through repeated exposure
- Longer prompts successfully extract more training data by providing extended context for generation
- Various defense mechanisms show effectiveness but involve trade-offs between privacy protection and model utility

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Larger PLM parameter count increases memorization linearly
- Mechanism: As model size grows, the capacity to store more training data patterns increases, leading to higher verbatim memorization rates
- Core assumption: Training data remains constant while model capacity scales
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Carlini et al. (2023b) revealed that a near-perfect log-linear relationship exists such that the larger the model size is, the more strings are memorized"
  - [section 6.1] "Carlini et al. (2023b) revealed that a near-perfect log-linear relationship exists such that the larger the model size is, the more strings are memorized"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus focuses on survey papers, not the original Carlini 2023b findings
- Break condition: If training data size scales proportionally with model size, or if regularization techniques effectively constrain memorization

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Duplicate training data strings increase memorization probability
- Mechanism: Repeated exposure during training strengthens token sequence memorization, making duplicate strings more likely to be reproduced verbatim
- Core assumption: PLM training treats duplicate strings as independent examples, reinforcing their patterns
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6.2] "Carlini et al. (2023b) reported that a clear log-linear trend exists between the number of duplicates and the amount of memorization"
  - [section 6.2] "Kandpal et al. (2022) and Lee et al. (2022) also revealed that duplication in the training set of PLMs relates to the likelihood of memorizing strings"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains related survey papers but not the primary empirical studies
- Break condition: If data deduplication is applied during preprocessing, or if training epochs per duplicate are limited

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Longer prompts extract more training data
- Mechanism: Extended prompts provide more context, allowing PLMs to generate longer sequences that match training data substrings
- Core assumption: PLMs can leverage longer contexts to reconstruct training data sequences
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6.3] "Carlini et al. (2023b) revealed that the amount of memorization increases with the length of the prompt"
  - [section 6.3] "They measured the amount of memorization for each bucket with duplicate counts ranging from 2 to 900"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus contains survey papers but not the original Carlini 2023b prompt-length experiments
- Break condition: If prompt length is artificially capped in deployment, or if context window limits prevent long-sequence generation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we develop more effective methods for distinguishing between harmful and acceptable memorization in large language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the need to separate undesirable memorization of personal information from acceptable memorization, and mentions that current definitions of memorization are not sufficient to make this distinction.
- Why unresolved: Current definitions of memorization, including verbatim and approximate memorization, do not adequately differentiate between harmful and acceptable forms of memorization. The boundary between memorization and knowledge is also unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing the effects of different types of memorization on model utility and privacy, as well as the development of new definitions or metrics that can quantify the degree of harmfulness of memorized information.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we evaluate the effectiveness of different defense mechanisms against training data extraction attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper reviews various defense mechanisms including data sanitization, differential privacy, regularization, and post-processing techniques, but notes that their effectiveness varies and that more research is needed to determine optimal approaches.
- Why unresolved: The effectiveness of different defense mechanisms can vary depending on the specific attack scenario and the characteristics of the training data. There is a lack of comprehensive evaluations comparing different approaches under realistic conditions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Large-scale empirical studies evaluating the trade-offs between privacy protection and model utility for different defense mechanisms across various datasets and attack scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we develop more robust methods for membership inference attacks that can work with limited adversarial knowledge?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses different levels of adversarial knowledge (white-box vs black-box) and notes that attacks in black-box settings are more challenging but also more realistic, yet current methods are not fully developed for these scenarios.
- Why unresolved: Current membership inference attacks often assume white-box access or partial knowledge of the training set, which is not always realistic. Developing attacks that work with minimal information remains challenging.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of new attack algorithms that can effectively perform membership inference using only prediction labels or limited output information, along with evaluations showing their effectiveness compared to existing methods.

## Limitations
- Primarily synthesizes results from existing literature rather than conducting original experiments
- Focus on autoregressive language models may not generalize to encoder-only or encoder-decoder architectures
- Empirical claims about memorization trends rely heavily on specific datasets and model families

## Confidence
- High confidence: The taxonomy of memorization definitions and systematic categorization of attack/defense approaches
- Medium confidence: The log-linear relationships between model size, duplicate counts, and memorization
- Low confidence: Claims about deployment-time defenses and real-world effectiveness of countermeasures

## Next Checks
1. Replication of size scaling experiments: Independently verify the log-linear relationship between model size and memorization using diverse model families beyond GPT-Neo, including encoder-decoder models like T5 and hybrid architectures.

2. Cross-dataset generalization: Test whether the reported memorization trends hold when training on datasets with different characteristics (e.g., code, scientific literature, multilingual data) to assess robustness across domains.

3. Defense mechanism evaluation: Conduct head-to-head comparisons of proposed defense mechanisms under realistic threat models, measuring both privacy protection and utility degradation across multiple model sizes and tasks.
