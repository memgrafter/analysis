# Who's the Best Detective? LLMs vs. MLs in Detecting Incoherent Fourth Grade Math Answers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2304.11257
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11257
- **Reference count:** 2
- **Primary result:** LLMs (F1 ~63%) perform worse than ML classifiers (F1 ~79%) at detecting incoherent fourth-grade math answers

## Executive Summary
This study compares the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Machine Learning (ML) classifiers in detecting incoherent answers from fourth-grade students to open-ended math questions. The researchers tested three LLMs (GPT-3, BLOOM, and YOU) with various prompting strategies and compared their results with ML classifiers trained on thousands of examples. The LLMs performed worse than the ML classifiers, with F1-scores around 63% compared to 79% for the best ML model. The main challenges for LLMs were recursive questions containing both questions and answers, and responses with typical fourth-grader misspellings.

## Method Summary
The study compares GPT models (YouChat, GPT-3, BLOOM) with ML classifiers (XGBoost, BETO-mt) on a dataset of fourth-grade math answers. The GPT models were tested with zero-, one-, two-, three-, and four-shot prompting strategies on the 2017 test dataset. ML models were trained on 2019 data following Urrutia et al. (2023) methodology. The primary metric was F1-score for the incoherent class, with precision, recall, and support also measured. The comparison revealed that ML classifiers significantly outperformed LLMs despite the LLMs' advantage of having few-shot examples from the test set.

## Key Results
- ML classifiers achieved 79% F1-score compared to LLMs' ~63% F1-score
- Recursive questions (Q3 type) were a major challenge for LLMs, causing confusion between outer and inner questions
- Fourth-grade misspellings significantly reduced LLM precision, with the models struggling to recognize domain-specific spelling errors

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Recursive questions cause LLMs to confuse the outer question with the inner question, leading to incoherent predictions.
- **Mechanism:** The nested structure of recursive questions forces the model to hold two distinct reasoning contexts simultaneously. LLMs, especially those not fine-tuned for this structure, conflate the two contexts and produce predictions based on the wrong one.
- **Core assumption:** The model treats the embedded character's answer as part of the main prompt and attempts to evaluate coherence on the wrong target.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "The difficulty seems to reside in recursive questions that contain both questions and answers"; [section] "Recursivity poses a significant challenge for the GPT-3 model... the problem with Recursivity may stem from an internal feature rather than solely from prompt design."
- **Break condition:** If the prompt explicitly separates the outer and inner questions and instructs the model to evaluate only the outer, the effect is reduced but not eliminated.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Fourth-grade misspellings reduce LLM precision because the training corpus lacks this domain-specific noise pattern.
- **Mechanism:** Spelling errors in student answers differ from standard misspellings. LLMs trained on general text do not generalize well to these domain-specific noise types, leading to false incoherence detections.
- **Core assumption:** The model's internal representation of "word boundaries" and "phonetic mapping" is based on adult or standard language use, not child writing.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "responses from students with typical fourth-grader misspellings"; [section] "Our findings demonstrate that child misspelling has a significant negative effect on the precision of the GPT-3 model for incoherence prediction."
- **Break condition:** If a spelling-normalization preprocessing step is applied before LLM inference, precision improves.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Few-shot examples from the same test set inflate LLM performance due to overfitting to local idiosyncrasies.
- **Mechanism:** When shots are drawn from the same distribution as the test data, the model memorizes patterns rather than learning generalizable rules, leading to overoptimistic F1 scores.
- **Core assumption:** In-sample shots provide contextual cues specific to that test set's question style, question type, and typical misspellings.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section] "the comparison of the three GPT models reveal that using generative models with zero-, one-, and few-shot strategies is not enough to compete against supervised ML models trained with thousands of examples."; [section] "the GPT-3 model with the few-shots strategy outperform the YouChat and BLOOM models, in all their variants, but this result may be due to the fact that the shots used belong to the test set."
- **Break condition:** If shots are drawn from a disjoint dataset, the performance gap between LLM and ML models widens.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Natural Language Inference (NLI) as a subtask of incoherence detection.
  - **Why needed here:** Incoherence detection can be framed as checking whether the answer entails, contradicts, or is neutral to the question. LLMs struggle with NLI-like reasoning, which explains their poorer performance.
  - **Quick check question:** If given premise "John has 5 apples" and hypothesis "John has 3 apples", what would NLI label this? (Answer: contradiction)

- **Concept:** Prompt engineering and few-shot learning limitations.
  - **Why needed here:** LLM performance depends heavily on prompt design and the choice of in-context examples. Poorly chosen shots can mislead the model or inflate performance.
  - **Quick check question:** If you provide a single example of a coherent answer in the prompt, will the model generalize to detect incoherence? (Answer: No, it may bias toward coherence)

- **Concept:** Domain adaptation and corpus mismatch.
  - **Why needed here:** LLMs trained on general corpora lack exposure to fourth-grade language patterns, including misspellings and simple math reasoning, leading to degraded performance.
  - **Quick check question:** If an LLM is trained only on scientific papers, will it accurately classify kid-written math answers? (Answer: No, domain shift causes errors)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Data loader → Question/answer preprocessing → LLM inference (YouChat, GPT-3, BLOOM variants) → Label mapping (coherent/incoherent) → Evaluation (precision, recall, F1)
- **Critical path:** Preprocess questions/answers → Apply prompt template → LLM inference → Threshold-based label → Compute metrics
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Few-shot vs. zero-shot: Few-shot gives better raw performance but risks overfitting and leaks from test set.
  - Model size vs. speed: BLOOM-large high quality but slow; BLOOMz faster but less accurate.
  - Prompt verbosity vs. clarity: More instructions reduce recursion confusion but increase token cost.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High recall but low precision → Model labels too many coherent answers as incoherent (likely recursion or misspelling issue).
  - Consistently wrong on Q3 questions → Recursion handling failure.
  - Performance improves with spelling normalization → Misspelling sensitivity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Compare zero-shot vs. few-shot (with disjoint shots) on a held-out set to measure overfitting.
  2. Apply a child-specific spell-checker before LLM inference and measure change in precision.
  3. Modify Q3 prompts to explicitly separate outer and inner questions; test if F1 improves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can Large Language Models be specifically trained or fine-tuned to better handle recursive questions in elementary math contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper explicitly identifies recursive questions as a major challenge for LLMs, noting that "recursion is a significant challenge in various domains of AI" and that "addressing recursion in language remains unsolved."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper acknowledges that recursive questions pose a unique challenge for LLMs due to their complex nested structure, and current models struggle to distinguish between the main question and the embedded sub-question.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies testing various fine-tuning approaches on recursive question datasets, measuring improvements in F1-scores specifically for recursive question types.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific techniques or training approaches could improve LLM performance in detecting child-specific misspellings in written math responses?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper identifies child misspellings as a significant challenge, noting that "child misspelling has a significant negative effect on the precision of the GPT-3 model for incoherence prediction" and that LLMs "capture text written by fourth graders to a lesser extent."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper suggests that current LLM training data may not adequately represent the types of spelling errors made by children, but does not propose specific solutions for addressing this gap.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies training LLMs on child-specific spelling error datasets versus standard datasets, measuring improvements in incoherence detection accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the optimal prompt engineering strategy for improving LLM performance in detecting incoherence in fourth-grade math responses?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper tests various prompt engineering approaches but concludes that "inferior performance may not be due to prompting, but rather an intrinsic limitation of the models," suggesting the need for further investigation.
- **Why unresolved:** While the paper tests multiple prompt strategies, it does not find a definitive approach that significantly improves LLM performance, indicating the need for more sophisticated prompt engineering techniques.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies testing advanced prompt engineering techniques (such as chain-of-thought prompting, multi-shot learning with carefully selected examples) against the baseline approaches used in the paper.

## Limitations
- The study compares LLMs with ML classifiers trained on a different dataset (2019) than the test set (2017), raising questions about the fairness of the comparison and potential data leakage effects.
- Limited evaluation of prompt engineering variations and fine-tuning approaches that could potentially improve LLM performance.
- The performance gap may be partly attributable to domain adaptation issues rather than fundamental LLM limitations for this task.

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The observation that ML classifiers outperform LLMs on this specific task, with measurable F1-score differences (79% vs 63%).

**Medium Confidence:** The attribution of LLM underperformance to recursive questions and misspellings as primary failure modes, though other factors may contribute.

**Medium Confidence:** The finding that few-shot examples from the test set may inflate LLM performance, suggesting potential overfitting.

## Next Checks

1. Test the same LLM prompts on a held-out validation set distinct from both training and test data to verify if performance degrades without test-set leakage.
2. Implement child-specific spelling normalization preprocessing and measure the impact on LLM precision and F1-score.
3. Experiment with prompt modifications that explicitly separate recursive question components to determine if the recursion confusion can be mitigated through better prompt design.
