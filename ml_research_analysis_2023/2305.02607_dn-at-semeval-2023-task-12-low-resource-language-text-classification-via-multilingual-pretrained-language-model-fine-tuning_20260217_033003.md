# DN at SemEval-2023 Task 12: Low-Resource Language Text Classification via Multilingual Pretrained Language Model Fine-tuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.02607
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02607
- Reference count: 7
- Primary result: Achieved 3rd place in SemEval-2023 AfriSenti competition multilingual track with F1 score of 72.55%

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a system for sentiment analysis in low-resource African languages using multilingual pretrained language models. The approach leverages Afro-XLM-R-large, a model pre-trained on 17 African languages, fine-tuned on multilingual data across 14 target languages. The system employs an ensemble of five models trained on different data subsamples with voting for final predictions. While achieving competitive overall performance, the approach shows variable results across individual languages, highlighting both the potential and challenges of cross-lingual transfer for African language sentiment analysis.

## Method Summary
The system uses Afro-XLM-R-large, a multilingual transformer model pre-trained on 17 African languages, with a classification head added for sentiment analysis. Training employs 5-fold StratifiedKFold cross-validation, creating five models trained on different data subsamples. These models are then combined through voting to produce final predictions. The approach avoids extensive text preprocessing, finding it did not improve performance. Hyperparameters include Adam optimizer with learning rate 2e-5, weight decay 0.1, epochs 5, and max sequence length 128.

## Key Results
- Achieved 3rd place in SemEval-2023 AfriSenti multilingual track with F1 score of 72.55%
- Performance varied significantly across individual languages, with some showing poor results
- Text preprocessing did not significantly improve model performance despite extensive experimentation
- Voting ensemble of five models proved effective for improving multilingual task performance

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pre-training on African languages enables better cross-lingual transfer for sentiment classification.
- Mechanism: Afro-XLM-R-large was fine-tuned on multilingual data after being pre-trained on 17 African languages, allowing it to leverage shared linguistic features across these languages.
- Core assumption: Low-resource African languages share enough structural and semantic features that pre-training on multiple African languages improves downstream performance on individual languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our team achieved the third-best results in Subtask B, Track 16: Multilingual, demonstrating the effectiveness of our approach."
  - [section 2]: "Afro-XLM-R-large (Alabi et al., 2022) model... trained on 17 African languages... The embeddings produced by this model were fed into a classification layer..."
  - [corpus]: Weak support; related papers use similar models but don't confirm shared feature transfer.
- Break condition: If individual language performance drops significantly when more unrelated languages are added to the training set.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Voting ensemble of multiple fine-tuned models improves multilingual task performance.
- Mechanism: Five different fine-tuned models were trained on different subsamples of the data, and their predictions were combined via voting to produce the final output.
- Core assumption: Different training splits expose the model to varied linguistic patterns, reducing overfitting to specific data subsets and improving generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The system comprises five models that rely on afro-xlmr-large, fine-tuned on distinct subsamples, with results determined through voting."
  - [section 3]: "For each task, the training sample was split into 5 distinct validation samples. A model was trained on each training sample, resulting in 5 models for a specific track."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence for voting ensembles in this task; related work uses single models.
- Break condition: If individual model performance variance is high, voting may amplify errors rather than reduce them.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fine-tuning without preprocessing can achieve competitive results when using large pre-trained multilingual models.
- Mechanism: The authors experimented with various preprocessing steps (URL removal, user mention handling, emoticon spacing) but found no performance gain, so they used raw text.
- Core assumption: The tokenizer and embedding layers of pre-trained models can handle noisy social media text (URLs, mentions, emoticons) without explicit preprocessing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4]: "Interestingly, text preprocessing did not significantly contribute to enhancing our model's performance in this particular case."
  - [section 3]: "In the final version, we did not incorporate data preprocessing as it failed to demonstrate improved performance."
  - [corpus]: No corpus evidence; preprocessing decisions appear empirical and task-specific.
- Break condition: If models are fine-tuned on cleaner, non-social-media text or if the tokenizer was not trained on such noise.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Cross-lingual transfer learning
  - Why needed here: Low-resource African languages lack large labeled datasets, so models must leverage knowledge from related high-resource or other African languages.
  - Quick check question: What enables a model trained on Hausa to perform well on Yoruba without additional labeled data?

- Concept: Ensemble methods
  - Why needed here: Combining multiple models reduces variance and improves robustness, especially when individual models may overfit to specific data splits.
  - Quick check question: How does voting among models trained on different folds affect overall F1 score compared to a single fine-tuned model?

- Concept: Tokenization and subword modeling
  - Why needed here: Social media text includes informal elements (URLs, mentions, emoticons) that require robust tokenization strategies.
  - Quick check question: Why might Afro-XLM-R's tokenizer handle emoticons better than a standard BERT tokenizer?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Raw Twitter text in 14 African languages → Afro-XLM-R-large tokenizer → Pre-trained multilingual transformer → Linear classification layer → Voting ensemble of 5 models → Sentiment label (positive, neutral, negative)

- Critical path:
  Data → Tokenizer → Transformer → Classifier → Voting → Prediction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using Afro-XLM-R-large vs. smaller variants: Larger models have better cross-lingual capacity but higher computational cost.
  - No preprocessing vs. cleaning: Saves time and may preserve original sentiment cues; risks noisy inputs.
  - Voting ensemble vs. single best model: Higher robustness but more training time and complexity.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low performance on specific languages: Indicates insufficient cross-lingual transfer or domain mismatch.
  - High variance across folds: Suggests data quality issues or model instability.
  - Voting degrades performance: May indicate correlated errors among models.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a single Afro-XLM-R-large model on all multilingual data; evaluate per-language and overall F1.
  2. Apply different preprocessing pipelines (URL removal, mention masking, emoticon spacing) and compare performance.
  3. Train 5-fold ensemble without preprocessing; compare ensemble performance to single model baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific text preprocessing techniques could potentially improve model performance for low-resource African languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "text preprocessing did not significantly contribute to enhancing our model's performance in this particular case," but it also mentions experimenting with various preprocessing techniques like removing links, user tags, collapsing quotation marks, and handling ellipses.
- Why unresolved: The authors tried multiple preprocessing techniques but did not find significant improvement, suggesting there might be other techniques worth exploring or the need for more sophisticated preprocessing methods tailored to these languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experimentation with additional preprocessing techniques specifically designed for African languages, including handling of unique linguistic features and script variations, could provide insights into whether preprocessing can improve performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the performance of sentiment analysis models be improved for languages that showed poor results in the competition?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "our model performed poorly in some languages" and "it performed relatively poorly in others," highlighting the need for better approaches for these specific languages.
- Why unresolved: The paper achieved good overall results but acknowledges that performance varies significantly across languages, indicating that some languages may require specialized approaches or additional resources.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing language-specific models or incorporating additional linguistic resources (such as larger annotated datasets or language-specific embeddings) for underperforming languages could demonstrate whether targeted approaches improve results.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the most effective strategies for improving zero-shot learning performance for African languages with limited or no training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that for the final two zero-shot tracks, "we utilized models trained in different languages from the previous tracks" and selected the highest quality model, but acknowledges this approach has limitations.
- Why unresolved: The zero-shot learning results were not as strong as multilingual performance, suggesting that current transfer learning approaches may not be optimal for these languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of different transfer learning strategies, including cross-lingual embeddings, data augmentation techniques, or few-shot learning approaches, could identify more effective methods for zero-shot sentiment analysis in low-resource languages.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can the class imbalance in training data for some African languages be addressed to improve sentiment analysis performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "the training sample is highly unbalanced for some languages" and shows this in Figure 1, which could be affecting model performance.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the class imbalance issue, it does not explore solutions or evaluate how this affects model performance across different languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and testing various techniques for handling class imbalance (such as oversampling, undersampling, or class weighting) and evaluating their impact on model performance for different languages could provide insights into effective solutions.

## Limitations
- Performance variability across individual languages with some showing poor results despite competitive overall performance
- Limited systematic evaluation of different preprocessing strategies for African languages
- Lack of detailed per-language performance metrics to understand cross-lingual transfer effectiveness

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The use of Afro-XLM-R-large for multilingual sentiment analysis in African languages is well-supported, as the model was specifically pre-trained on 17 African languages and achieved competitive results in the SemEval competition.

**Medium Confidence:** The claim that text preprocessing does not improve performance is based on empirical testing but lacks systematic comparison across different preprocessing strategies and languages.

**Low Confidence:** The assertion that cross-lingual transfer works effectively across all 14 target languages is not fully substantiated, given the lack of per-language performance breakdowns and the known challenges of transferring between linguistically diverse African languages.

## Next Checks

1. **Per-language performance analysis:** Conduct detailed evaluation of the model's performance on each of the 14 African languages individually, including F1 scores for each sentiment class, to identify which languages benefit most from the multilingual approach and which require additional support.

2. **Preprocessing ablation study:** Systematically test different preprocessing strategies (URL removal, mention masking, emoticon handling, language-specific normalization) on a subset of languages to determine if targeted preprocessing improves performance for specific language-script combinations.

3. **Cross-lingual transfer validation:** Design experiments to test zero-shot transfer capabilities by training on subsets of languages and evaluating on unseen African languages, measuring how effectively knowledge transfers between linguistically related and unrelated language pairs.
