# The Role of Output Vocabulary in T2T LMs for SPARQL Semantic Parsing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.15108
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15108
- Reference count: 15
- Primary result: Replacing SPARQL vocabulary with English words improved performance by up to 17% on GrailQA

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how output vocabulary complexity affects text-to-text language model performance on SPARQL semantic parsing tasks. The authors systematically replaced the original SPARQL vocabulary with three alternative vocabularies of varying complexity - English words and character-level tokens - and trained T5-based models on modified datasets. They found that vocabulary design significantly impacts model performance, with vocabularies closer to the model tokenizer's native vocabulary consistently outperforming the original SPARQL vocabulary. The study also reveals that prefix tuning is more sensitive to vocabulary changes than full fine-tuning, showing steeper performance degradation when vocabularies deviate from the tokenizer's native format.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted controlled experiments by modifying the output vocabulary of text-to-text language models for SPARQL semantic parsing. They trained models on two datasets (GrailQA and Spider) using three vocabulary strategies: the original SPARQL vocabulary, English word-based vocabularies, and character-level vocabularies. Both full fine-tuning and prefix tuning approaches were evaluated to compare parameter-efficient adaptation strategies. The experiments systematically measured how different vocabulary complexities affect semantic parsing accuracy, providing insights into the relationship between vocabulary design and model performance on structured output generation tasks.

## Key Results
- Vocabulary closer to tokenizer vocabulary consistently improves performance, with up to 17% absolute gain on GrailQA
- Prefix tuning shows steeper accuracy drop than fine-tuning when vocabulary complexity changes
- English word vocabulary outperforms both original SPARQL and character-level vocabularies across datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The performance improvement occurs because language models are optimized for their native tokenizer vocabularies during pretraining. When output vocabularies align with these learned representations, the models can leverage their existing knowledge more effectively, reducing token-level mismatch and improving generation quality. This alignment allows the models to utilize their pre-trained understanding of word boundaries, semantic relationships, and syntactic patterns encoded in the tokenizer's vocabulary, rather than struggling with unfamiliar or overly granular token representations.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Natural Language Input -> T5 Encoder -> Decoder with Modified Output Vocabulary -> SPARQL Query Output

**Critical Path**: The decoder's output layer mapping to the modified vocabulary is the critical component, as it directly determines how well the model can generate valid SPARQL queries using the substituted vocabulary.

**Design Tradeoffs**: Using English vocabulary simplifies token representation but may lose SPARQL-specific semantic precision. Character-level vocabularies provide fine granularity but increase sequence length and computational cost. The choice involves balancing vocabulary familiarity against task-specific expressiveness.

**Failure Signatures**: Vocabulary mismatch causes generation errors, particularly in prefix tuning where limited parameter updates cannot compensate for unfamiliar token representations. Models struggle with SPARQL-specific constructs when using simplified vocabularies.

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to English datasets and T5-based models, limiting generalizability
- Only three vocabulary strategies tested without exploring intermediate granularity options
- Performance gains from vocabulary simplification may not translate to real-world semantic parsing where SPARQL-specific constructs are necessary

## Confidence

**High confidence**: Vocabulary design impacts model performance; closer alignment to tokenizer vocabulary improves results

**Medium confidence**: Prefix tuning is more sensitive to vocabulary changes than fine-tuning based on controlled comparison

**Medium confidence**: Practical recommendation to align output vocabulary with tokenizer vocabulary, though real-world applicability remains uncertain
