# Trustworthy, responsible, ethical AI in manufacturing and supply chains: synthesis and emerging research questions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11581
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11581
- Reference count: 0
- The paper maps risks in developing and deploying AI systems in manufacturing, using a machine learning lifecycle framework to identify 38 research questions across data collection, algorithmic development, and organizational practices

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically maps trustworthiness concerns in AI development across the manufacturing lifecycle, identifying 38 research questions that address data bias, privacy violations, model verification, and scalability challenges. By adapting a machine learning lifecycle framework and providing concrete manufacturing examples, the authors bridge the gap between high-level ethical principles and practical implementation. The work highlights critical technical and organizational gaps, emphasizing the need for explainable AI methods, fairness toolkits, and robust data governance in manufacturing contexts.

## Method Summary
The paper synthesizes literature on trustworthy, responsible, and ethical AI principles, then adapts a machine learning lifecycle framework to the manufacturing context. Through illustrative examples drawn from current manufacturing practice, the authors map each lifecycle stage to specific trustworthiness concerns. This mapping process generates 38 targeted research questions across data collection, algorithmic development, and organizational practices. The methodology combines literature review with practical example generation to create a research agenda for addressing AI trustworthiness in manufacturing and supply chains.

## Key Results
- Provides a lifecycle-based framework mapping 22 trustworthiness concerns to specific AI development stages in manufacturing
- Identifies 38 research questions targeting technical gaps (fairness toolkits, explainable AI, model verification) and organizational challenges (ethics boards, data governance)
- Demonstrates how manufacturing-specific contexts reveal unique trustworthiness challenges through 22 concrete examples
- Highlights cross-cutting considerations including affordability, outsourcing, and data monetization that impact AI trustworthiness

- **Mechanism**: By adapting Ashmore et al.'s ML lifecycle into a broadened adaptation covering data management, model creation, verification, and deployment, the paper provides a concrete scaffold for identifying where trustworthiness risks emerge.
- **Core assumption**: Trustworthy AI concerns manifest systematically across the AI development process rather than being isolated to a single stage.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "use a broadened adaptation of a machine learning lifecycle to discuss, through the use of illustrative examples, how each step may result in a given AI trustworthiness concern"
  - [section 3] Detailed mapping of lifecycle stages to trustworthiness concerns with 22 illustrative examples
  - [corpus] Strong relevance (0.59 FMR) to papers discussing AI ethics in practice and supply chain applications
- **Break condition**: If the lifecycle stages don't capture all trustworthiness concerns or if new stages emerge that the framework doesn't address, the mapping becomes incomplete.

- **Claim**: The paper identifies specific technical and organizational gaps through concrete manufacturing use cases, creating a research agenda with 38 targeted questions.
- **Mechanism**: By presenting illustrative examples (e.g., biased voice recognition systems, data provenance issues in food allergen detection), the paper translates abstract trustworthiness principles into concrete manufacturing scenarios, then derives research questions from each identified problem.
- **Core assumption**: Manufacturing-specific contexts reveal unique trustworthiness challenges not captured by general AI frameworks.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "38 research questions across data collection, algorithmic development, and organizational practices"
  - [section 3] Examples like "An engineering company would like to predict the root cause of delays during production" showing data imbalance issues

- **Break condition**: If the illustrative examples don't represent the full diversity of manufacturing contexts, the research agenda may miss critical gaps.

- **Mechanism**: By synthesizing literature on trustworthy, responsible, and ethical AI, then contextualizing these principles within manufacturing supply chains (Figure 1), the paper makes abstract concepts concrete for practitioners.

- **Critical path**: The core value flows from literature synthesis → lifecycle mapping → example generation → research question derivation. Each component builds on the previous one.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can data producers and owners be supported in implementing common data schemas and knowledge models to improve interoperability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] RQ 1 in paper text
- Why unresolved: While standardization through common terminologies and taxonomies is suggested, there's no research on practical implementation mechanisms or incentives for adoption
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of successful interoperability implementations, cost-benefit analyses of standardization efforts, and evaluation frameworks for measuring interoperability success

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can provenance mechanisms be leveraged in association with data and knowledge acquisition processes in a manufacturing context?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] RQ 2 in paper text
- Why unresolved: Although PROV-O and related research provide infrastructure, there's limited understanding of how to integrate provenance mechanisms into manufacturing workflows without significant overhead
- What evidence would resolve it: Case studies demonstrating effective provenance integration, metrics for provenance quality assessment, and evaluation of trust improvements from provenance use

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the ways in which adversarial ML attacks can take place in manufacturing and how can they be prevented?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] RQ 16 in paper text
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies manufacturing as vulnerable to adversarial attacks but doesn't provide specific attack vectors or comprehensive prevention strategies
- What evidence would resolve it: Catalog of manufacturing-specific attack scenarios, effectiveness studies of various prevention techniques, and cost-benefit analyses of security measures

## Limitations

- The paper relies on illustrative examples rather than empirical case studies from actual manufacturing implementations, potentially missing real-world complexity
- The framework assumes a linear progression through lifecycle stages, which may not reflect the iterative nature of many AI development processes in manufacturing settings
- Specific datasets or case studies used to generate the illustrative examples are not provided, limiting reproducibility and verification of the framework's applicability

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The systematic mapping of trustworthiness concerns to lifecycle stages is well-supported by the literature synthesis and provides a clear framework for understanding where risks emerge (supported by corpus evidence with FMR=0.59)

- **Medium Confidence**: The cross-cutting considerations section adequately addresses broader organizational challenges, though the practical applicability of these considerations to diverse manufacturing contexts requires further validation
