# Exploring the MIT Mathematics and EECS Curriculum Using Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08997
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08997
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: GPT-4 achieves perfect solve rate on MIT curriculum test set (excluding images) using expert prompting

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MITC OURSES, a dataset of 4,550 questions and solutions from MIT Mathematics and EECS courses, and evaluates large language models on solving them. GPT-4, with prompt engineering, achieves a perfect solve rate on the test set excluding image-based questions. The authors propose a new expert prompting technique and use embeddings to analyze course dependencies. They also introduce meta-questions to assess students' understanding of LLM-generated answers. The study provides insights into curriculum design and the potential of LLMs in education.

## Method Summary
The study creates MITC OURSES by extracting questions from MIT course materials and manually curating them into a dataset. GPT-4 is evaluated using various prompt engineering techniques including few-shot learning, chain-of-thought reasoning, self-critique, and a novel expert prompting approach. An open-source LLaMA model is fine-tuned on the dataset. Question embeddings are used to discover prerequisite relationships between courses. Meta-questions are designed to assess student understanding of LLM-generated answers through correctness evaluation and explanation tasks.

## Key Results
- GPT-4 with prompt engineering achieves perfect solve rate on test set (excluding image questions)
- Expert prompting technique improves LLM performance by aggregating simulated expert responses
- Curriculum dependency analysis reveals prerequisite relationships between MIT courses using question embeddings
- Meta-questions effectively assess student understanding of LLM-generated answers

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Expert prompting improves LLM performance by eliciting domain-specific reasoning strategies from named experts. The LLM generates expert names, simulates their responses, and aggregates them through collaborative decision-making, combining multiple specialized perspectives.

### Mechanism 2
Curriculum dependencies are discovered by analyzing question embeddings and using few-shot learning to measure prerequisite relationships. Questions are embedded into vectors, and nearest neighbor questions serve as few-shot examples to test knowledge transfer between courses, creating a dependency graph.

### Mechanism 3
Meta-questions focusing on correctness and completeness of LLM-generated answers assess student understanding in AI-augmented education. Students evaluate LLM responses for correctness and explain their reasoning, testing critical evaluation skills rather than content generation.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: PDF extraction -> Mathpix transcription -> manual correction -> dataset curation -> model evaluation -> prompting optimization -> curriculum analysis -> meta-question development
- Critical path: Dataset creation -> model benchmarking -> prompting optimization -> curriculum analysis -> meta-question development
- Design tradeoffs: Manual dataset curation ensures quality but limits scalability; expert prompting adds complexity but improves performance; embedding-based analysis is efficient but may miss nuanced relationships
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on image-based questions; inconsistent expert responses; weak embedding similarity for certain course pairs
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Benchmark all prompting techniques on a small subset of questions to establish baseline performance
  2. Test embedding similarity on questions within the same course to validate the approach
  3. Generate and evaluate a small set of meta-questions with student participants

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does expert prompting compare to other prompt engineering techniques in terms of solving more complex or creative problems? The paper demonstrates that expert prompting improves performance on MIT curriculum problems, but does not explore its effectiveness on more complex or creative tasks outside of the curriculum.

### Open Question 2
How can the proposed meta-questions be further developed to assess higher-order thinking skills and critical analysis? The paper introduces meta-questions to assess students' understanding of LLM-generated answers, but does not explore how these questions can be expanded to evaluate higher-order thinking skills and critical analysis.

### Open Question 3
What are the potential limitations of using LLMs in education, and how can they be addressed? The paper discusses the potential of LLMs in education but does not explore the potential limitations and challenges of their use in educational settings.

## Limitations
- Results rely heavily on GPT-4's performance, which may not generalize to other LLMs
- Expert prompting depends on LLM's ability to meaningfully simulate domain experts
- Embedding-based curriculum analysis may miss nuanced semantic relationships

## Confidence
- High confidence: Dataset curation methodology and basic prompting techniques (few-shot, chain-of-thought)
- Medium confidence: Expert prompting technique shows promise but needs validation across domains
- Low confidence: Curriculum dependency analysis conclusions are preliminary and need extensive validation
