# RAMP: Retrieval and Attribute-Marking Enhanced Prompting for Attribute-Controlled Translation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17131
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17131
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: RAMP improves attribute accuracy in few-shot and zero-shot multilingual translation while maintaining or improving translation quality

## Executive Summary
RAMP introduces a novel approach to attribute-controlled translation that combines similarity-based retrieval with explicit attribute marking in prompts. The method addresses the challenge of controlling stylistic attributes like formality and gender in machine translation by selecting semantically relevant in-context examples and clearly marking which parts of those examples convey the target attribute. This dual approach of retrieval and marking enables large multilingual language models to better understand and replicate desired attributes in their translations.

## Method Summary
RAMP enhances attribute-controlled translation by first retrieving semantically similar in-context examples using sentence similarity embeddings, then explicitly marking attribute-relevant spans within those examples. The retrieval process uses a sentence transformer model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) to find top-k examples that are semantically closest to the input. Attribute markers (special tokens like `<formal>` and `</formal>`) are added around the portions of retrieved examples that express the target attribute. This marked prompt is then fed to a large multilingual language model (XGLM, BLOOM, or GPT-NeoX) to generate the controlled translation. The approach is evaluated across two datasets (COCOA-MT for formality and MT-GENEVAL for gender) and includes cross-lingual prompting experiments.

## Key Results
- RAMP achieves significant improvements in attribute control accuracy (both lexical and sentential) compared to baseline prompting methods
- Translation quality (measured by BLEU and COMET) is maintained or improved compared to standard prompting approaches
- Cross-lingual prompting works effectively, with BLOOM 175B showing strong performance even for languages not in its pre-training data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
RAMP works by addressing two key challenges in few-shot learning for attribute control: finding relevant examples and clearly communicating which parts of those examples demonstrate the target attribute. Standard prompting often struggles because randomly selected examples may not be semantically relevant to the input, and language models may not clearly understand which aspects of an example demonstrate the target attribute. By retrieving semantically similar examples, RAMP ensures that the model sees relevant patterns. By explicitly marking attribute spans, it removes ambiguity about which parts of the example to follow, making the demonstration more effective.

## Foundational Learning

**Sentence Similarity Embeddings**: Vector representations of sentences that capture semantic meaning, used to find relevant in-context examples. Why needed: Enables retrieval of examples semantically similar to the input rather than random selection. Quick check: Compute similarity between sentences like "How are you?" and "What's up?" vs. "The weather is nice."

**In-Context Learning**: The ability of large language models to learn from demonstrations within prompts without parameter updates. Why needed: RAMP relies on providing examples within the prompt to guide attribute-controlled generation. Quick check: Test if a model translates correctly with and without relevant in-context examples.

**Attribute Span Marking**: Explicitly annotating which parts of text express a target attribute using special tokens. Why needed: Removes ambiguity about which aspects of an example demonstrate the attribute. Quick check: Compare translation quality when attribute spans are marked vs. unmarked in prompts.

**Cross-Lingual Transfer**: The ability to apply knowledge from one language to generate outputs in another language. Why needed: RAMP explores using examples from one language to control attributes in translations to a different language. Quick check: Generate translations with examples in a different language and measure performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Input -> Sentence Similarity Model -> Top-k Retrieval -> Attribute Marking -> Prompt Construction -> Large Multilingual LM -> Controlled Translation Output

**Critical Path**: The most important components are the sentence similarity model (for retrieving relevant examples) and the attribute marking step (for clear communication). The quality of retrieved examples and the accuracy of attribute span identification directly determine performance.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The method trades computational overhead of similarity retrieval and attribute marking for improved attribute control accuracy. While more expensive than random prompting, it provides significant gains in attribute adherence.

**Failure Signatures**: Poor attribute control may result from retrieving semantically similar but contextually mismatched examples, or from inaccurate attribute span extraction. Translation quality issues may arise if attribute marking disrupts natural language patterns.

**First Experiments**: 1) Test retrieval quality by examining top-k examples for semantic relevance to inputs. 2) Evaluate attribute marking accuracy by comparing model's interpretation of marked vs. unmarked examples. 3) Measure impact of different k values in retrieval on attribute control performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**: How effective is RAMP in zero-shot settings for attributes or languages not seen during LLM pre-training? The paper mentions BLOOM 175B's surprising effectiveness for Italian translations despite Italian not being in its training data, but doesn't systematically evaluate this zero-shot capability.

**Open Question 2**: How sensitive is RAMP to the choice of in-context example ordering and prompt template design? The authors intentionally avoided exploring these factors to limit variables, leaving their impact on performance unknown.

**Open Question 3**: How can RAMP be extended to unsupervised settings where gold attribute annotations are not available? While the paper proposes using LLM feature attribution to extract salient tokens driving attribute prediction, it doesn't empirically evaluate this approach.

## Limitations

- Evaluation limited to only two attributes (formality and gender), which may not generalize to other stylistic or linguistic attributes
- Potential for noise if retrieved examples are semantically similar but contextually mismatched
- Cross-lingual prompting may face challenges with languages having significantly different grammatical structures or cultural contexts

## Confidence

High confidence: The core retrieval and attribute-marking approach is technically sound and well-motivated by prior work on in-context learning.

Medium confidence: Performance improvements are consistent but may be partially attributed to specific datasets and model configurations used.

Low confidence: Long-term stability and robustness in production settings with varying input distributions and domain shifts remains unclear.

## Next Checks

1. Conduct an ablation study on retrieval quality by systematically evaluating different similarity thresholds and top-k values on attribute control accuracy

2. Test cross-attribute generalization by applying RAMP to control different attributes beyond formality and gender (e.g., politeness levels, regional variations)

3. Perform robustness analysis with adversarial examples that may confuse similarity retrieval or attribute marking to identify potential failure modes
