# Why is the State of Neural Network Pruning so Confusing? On the Fairness, Comparison Setup, and Trainability in Network Pruning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05219
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05219
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Pruning damages network trainability, requiring larger learning rates and more epochs for effective finetuning

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates two key mysteries in neural network pruning: why larger finetuning learning rates improve performance, and whether inheriting pretrained weights is valuable. The authors identify that damaged network trainability from the sparsifying action in pruning has been largely unrecognized in prior work, leading to sub-optimal hyperparameters and confusing experimental results. They demonstrate that larger learning rates don't fundamentally improve performance but accelerate optimization to make higher performance observable earlier. The paper establishes fairness principles for pruning comparisons and recommends more rigorous experimental setups.

## Method Summary
The study examines pruning pipelines consisting of pretraining, pruning, and finetuning phases. Using L1-norm pruning on ResNet34 and ResNet50 architectures with ImageNet and ImageNet100 datasets, the authors compare different learning rate schedules and finetuning epochs. They introduce "trainability accuracy" as a metric to measure optimization speed during finetuning. The experiments systematically vary learning rates (0.001, 0.01, 0.1) and finetuning epochs (30, 60, 90, 120) to analyze their impact on performance and trainability. The comparison between pruned models with finetuning and training from scratch under controlled conditions forms the core experimental design.

## Key Results
- Pruning damages network trainability, slowing optimization and requiring larger learning rates and more epochs
- Larger finetuning learning rates accelerate optimization rather than fundamentally improving performance
- Prior pruning works used sub-optimal hyperparameters due to unrecognized trainability damage
- Strict comparison setups with same base model and finetuning process are essential for fair evaluation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pruning damages network trainability, which slows down optimization and makes the model converge more slowly.
- Mechanism: The sparsifying action removes connections or neurons from the network, making it harder to train. This damaged trainability means the model needs more effective updates to reach convergence.
- Core assumption: The sparsifying action in pruning (removing weights) directly impacts the network's ability to learn efficiently.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The damaged network trainability was not well recognized by prior pruning works, resulting in severely sub-optimal hyper-parameters."
  - [section 5.1] "First, pruning damages trainability. This is an intuitively straightforward fact since pruning removes connections or neurons, which virtually makes the network harder to train."
- Break condition: If the pruning method preserves network connectivity (e.g., unstructured pruning with weight values set to zero but connections maintained), the trainability damage might be less severe.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: A larger finetuning learning rate does not fundamentally improve performance, but accelerates the optimization process, making higher performance observed earlier.
- Mechanism: When trainability is damaged, a larger learning rate compensates by providing more effective updates per epoch, allowing the model to converge faster. This makes it appear as if the larger LR "improves" performance, when in reality it's just reaching the same solution faster.
- Core assumption: The fundamental solution the model can reach is not dependent on the learning rate, only the speed of reaching it.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "a larger learning rate does not really improve performance, but rather accelerates the optimization process, making higher performance observed earlier."
  - [section 5.2] "our results suggest a larger LR does not really 'improve' the performance. What really happens is, a larger LR accelerates the optimization process, making the higher performance observed earlier."
- Break condition: If the model has already converged or if the learning rate is too large causing instability, further increasing the LR would not help.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Sub-optimal hyperparameters in prior pruning works (especially small finetuning LR and few epochs) stem from not recognizing damaged trainability.
- Mechanism: Prior works assumed pruning a converged model means finetuning needs only small LR and few epochs. However, damaged trainability means the model actually needs larger LR and more epochs to compensate for slower optimization.
- Core assumption: The misconception about finetuning requirements is widespread in the pruning community.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The damaged network trainability was not well recognized by prior pruning works, resulting in severely sub-optimal hyper-parameters."
  - [section 5.2] "Such thought only holds for the cases of low pruning ratios. For a moderate or large pruning ratio, this thought hardly holds. What was neglected is that the sparsifying action damages network trainability, slowing down the optimization."
- Break condition: If a pruning method preserves trainability (e.g., through specific regularization techniques), the standard finetuning assumptions might hold.

  - Why needed here: The paper argues that many confusing results stem from unfair comparisons. Understanding what constitutes a fair comparison is essential to interpreting pruning results.

  - Why needed here: The sparsifying action is identified as the root cause of damaged trainability. Understanding what this action entails is crucial for understanding the mechanisms at play.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Base model training -> Pruning method selection -> Sparsifying action -> Finetuning/Retraining -> Evaluation and comparison setup

- Critical path: Base model → Pruning method → Sparsifying action → Retraining → Evaluation
  The sparsifying action is the critical point where trainability is damaged, which then affects the entire retraining phase.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Comparison setup strictness vs. resource requirements
  - Learning rate size vs. stability during retraining
  - Pruning ratio vs. trainability damage
  - Number of finetuning epochs vs. computational cost

- Failure signatures:
  - Using small finetuning LR with few epochs on heavily pruned models → Underperformance
  - Comparing methods with different base models or finetuning schedules → Unfair comparisons
  - Not reporting complete finetuning details → Reproducibility issues

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce the L1-norm pruning baseline with different finetuning LR schedules (0.001 vs 0.01) while keeping all other factors constant to observe the LR effect.
  2. Apply the same pruning method to models with different pruning ratios (e.g., 30%, 70%, 95%) and measure trainability accuracy to confirm the relationship between pruning ratio and trainability damage.
  3. Compare scratch training vs. pruning under the strictest comparison setup (S4.2) with carefully tuned hyperparameters for both approaches to test the value of pruning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the damaged network trainability vary across different pruning methods beyond L1-norm pruning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how the fundamental reason for damaged trainability is the sparsifying action in magnitude pruning, which is employed by regularization-based methods as well.
- Why unresolved: The paper primarily focuses on L1-norm pruning and does not extensively analyze the trainability damage across various pruning methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies measuring trainability metrics across different pruning methods under controlled conditions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of additional epochs needed to fully compensate for the damaged trainability when using smaller learning rates in finetuning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that adding more epochs can close the performance gap between larger and smaller learning rates, but the exact number varies and is not clearly defined.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides examples but does not establish a general rule or formula for determining the optimal number of additional epochs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments systematically varying the number of additional epochs and measuring the point of convergence for different learning rates.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different learning rate schedules (e.g., cosine annealing) compare to step decay in terms of mitigating the effects of damaged trainability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explores the effect of LR schedules on performance and suggests that the finetuning LR effect is generic, not limited to one particular schedule.
- Why unresolved: The paper only briefly mentions cosine annealing and does not provide a comprehensive comparison of various LR schedules.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing multiple LR schedules under the same pruning and training conditions.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the damaged trainability affect the generalization ability of pruned models compared to their dense counterparts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on the optimization speed and trainability accuracy but does not explicitly discuss the impact on generalization.
- Why unresolved: The relationship between trainability damage and generalization is not directly addressed, and the paper does not provide data on generalization metrics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments measuring generalization performance (e.g., test accuracy on unseen data) of pruned models with varying degrees of trainability damage.

### Open Question 5
- Question: What are the long-term implications of using larger learning rates during finetuning on the stability and robustness of pruned models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that larger learning rates accelerate optimization but does not discuss potential trade-offs in model stability or robustness.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the effects of larger learning rates on model robustness or stability over time.
- What evidence would resolve it: Long-term studies evaluating the robustness and stability of models trained with different learning rates under various conditions.

## Limitations

- The study focuses primarily on filter pruning and L1-norm pruning methods, potentially limiting generalizability to other pruning approaches.
- The ImageNet experiments use relatively small networks (ResNet34, ResNet50) that may not reflect the behavior of larger, more complex architectures.
- The core argument about network trainability lacks a rigorous mathematical definition and relies heavily on empirical evidence.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The claim that pruning damages trainability and requires larger learning rates and more epochs for effective finetuning is well-supported by empirical evidence and aligns with established optimization theory.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that prior works used sub-optimal hyperparameters due to not recognizing trainability damage is plausible but relies on indirect evidence and reasonable inference rather than direct measurement of prior researchers' thought processes.

**Low Confidence**: The proposed solution of using "retraining" instead of "finetuning" terminology may help conceptually but doesn't address the fundamental technical challenges and could create confusion if adopted without clear definitions.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-method validation**: Test the trainability hypothesis across diverse pruning methods (structured vs unstructured, magnitude-based vs optimization-based) to verify if the damaged trainability phenomenon is universal or method-specific.

2. **Architecture scaling**: Evaluate whether the learning rate and epoch recommendations scale appropriately with network depth and complexity by testing on ResNet101, ResNet152, and vision transformer architectures.

3. **Alternative trainability metrics**: Develop and validate quantitative metrics for trainability beyond optimization speed, such as effective learning rate adaptation, gradient flow, or information bottleneck measures, to provide more rigorous support for the core hypothesis.
