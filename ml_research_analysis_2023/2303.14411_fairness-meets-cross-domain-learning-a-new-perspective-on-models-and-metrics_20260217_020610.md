# Fairness meets Cross-Domain Learning: a new perspective on Models and Metrics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.14411
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14411
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Cross-domain learning methods consistently outperform fairness-specific algorithms in mitigating bias across classification and landmark detection tasks on face and medical images.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a novel benchmark for studying the intersection of cross-domain learning and model fairness in computer vision. By framing sensitive attributes (gender, age) as domains, the authors demonstrate that cross-domain learning algorithms can effectively reduce fairness gaps across multiple visual tasks. The work introduces a new Harmonic Fairness (HF) metric that jointly evaluates accuracy and fairness improvements relative to a biased baseline. Through comprehensive experiments on 14 cross-domain methods and 3 state-of-the-art fairness algorithms, the study reveals that domain-invariant representation learning offers a powerful approach to fairness mitigation, particularly for landmark detection tasks.

## Method Summary
The authors frame fairness as a domain generalization problem by treating sensitive attributes as domains, enabling the application of cross-domain learning techniques to fairness mitigation. They evaluate 14 cross-domain methods (including regularization, adversarial training, feature alignment, and self-supervised approaches) alongside 3 fairness-specific algorithms on classification and landmark detection tasks across four datasets: CelebA, COVID-19 Chest X-Ray, Fitzpatrick17k, and UTK Face. The core contribution is the Harmonic Fairness (HF) metric, which combines relative accuracy improvement (∆DA) and maximum group accuracy improvement (∆MGA) via harmonic mean to jointly assess fairness and accuracy. Experiments use ResNet-50 for classification and ResNet-18 for landmark detection, with training procedures incorporating domain alignment objectives alongside task-specific losses.

## Key Results
- Cross-domain learning methods systematically outperform fairness-specific algorithms across both classification and landmark detection tasks
- Cross-domain models trained for one sensitive attribute (e.g., gender) effectively transfer to mitigate bias for different attributes (e.g., age)
- The Harmonic Fairness metric successfully captures both accuracy and fairness improvements, with HF consistently higher for cross-domain methods
- Landmark detection tasks show the largest fairness gaps and the most dramatic improvements from cross-domain approaches

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Cross-domain learning methods reduce fairness gaps by learning domain-invariant representations that generalize across sensitive attribute subgroups.
- Mechanism: By framing sensitive attributes (e.g., gender, age) as domains, cross-domain learning algorithms (e.g., DANN, AFN) learn to align feature distributions across these groups, thereby reducing differences in model performance between subgroups.
- Core assumption: The visual domain shift between sensitive attribute subgroups is analogous to the domain shift between source and target domains in traditional cross-domain learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The wealth of existing algorithms for promoting domain invariance could potentially benefit fairness"
  - [section] "By simply switching d with a in the SDA setting we get to the framework described for the fairness problem"
  - [corpus] Weak correlation: related papers focus on CDR and text classification, not visual fairness
- Break condition: If the visual domain shift across sensitive groups is not analogous to cross-domain shifts, or if the subgroups share no meaningful visual similarities.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Harmonic Fairness (HF) metric jointly evaluates accuracy and fairness by measuring relative improvement over a biased baseline.
- Mechanism: HF combines relative differences in accuracy (∆DA) and maximum group accuracy (∆MGA) using a harmonic mean, penalizing methods that trade off accuracy for fairness.
- Core assumption: A fair model should improve both overall accuracy and reduce the performance gap between subgroups simultaneously.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We formalize relative distances for each method with respect to its biased reference and introduce our new Harmonic Fairness metric"
  - [section] "HF = ∆DA′× ∆MGA′ / ∆DA′+ ∆MGA′"
  - [corpus] Weak correlation: related papers focus on fairness metrics in NLP, not visual domain fairness
- Break condition: If the baseline model is not representative or if improving both metrics simultaneously is not achievable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Cross-domain models trained for one sensitive attribute transfer effectively to others, suggesting broader applicability for fairness mitigation.
- Mechanism: Knowledge learned to reduce bias over one sensitive attribute (e.g., gender) can be transferred to improve fairness for a different attribute (e.g., age).
- Core assumption: There is shared knowledge between different sensitive attributes that can be leveraged for fairness mitigation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "It shows that CD models trained to overcome the bias due to one sensitive attribute can be beneficial also to prevent unfairness with respect to a different one"
  - [section] "the possibility to reuse fair models on different sensitive attributes connects with the ability of the models to capture knowledge shared across them"
  - [corpus] Weak correlation: related papers focus on CDR transferability, not cross-attribute fairness transfer
- Break condition: If the visual features relevant to different sensitive attributes are disjoint or if the transfer degrades performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why do cross-domain learning approaches outperform fairness-specific methods in mitigating bias across different tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that 14 cross-domain learning methods systematically outperformed three state-of-the-art fairness algorithms in both classification and landmark detection tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates this superiority but doesn't deeply investigate the underlying reasons why CD methods are more effective than fairness-specific approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative ablation studies analyzing which components of CD methods (e.g., adversarial training, feature alignment) contribute most to fairness improvements, and why fairness-specific methods underperform.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How transferable is bias mitigation across different sensitive attributes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors found that models trained to overcome bias for one sensitive attribute (e.g., gender) showed effectiveness for other attributes (e.g., age), suggesting cross-attribute transfer.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates this transfer effect, it doesn't investigate the limits of this transferability or identify which attributes benefit most from cross-transfer.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments testing cross-attribute transfer across multiple attribute combinations, measuring effectiveness decay rates and identifying transferability patterns.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal evaluation metric for fairness in computer vision tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors introduce the Harmonic Fairness (HF) metric as superior to existing metrics, but acknowledge that "although HF takes into proper account the model starting baseline and encourages a decrease in DA and an increase in MGA with different strengths depending on the baseline position, it might not be sufficiently informative to benchmark different unfairness mitigation approaches."
- Why unresolved: The paper proposes HF as an improvement but notes its limitations, suggesting the need for further metric development.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of HF against other fairness metrics across diverse computer vision tasks and datasets, identifying scenarios where different metrics perform better or worse.

## Limitations
- Limited generalizability beyond facial and medical images to other visual domains
- Potential oversimplification of complex fairness dynamics by the HF metric
- Assumption that visual features relevant to different sensitive attributes are transferable may not hold universally

## Confidence
- Low confidence in generalizability of cross-domain learning approaches to fairness: Limited to facial and medical images
- Medium confidence in HF metric's comprehensive fairness capture: May oversimplify complex fairness dynamics
- Low confidence in cross-attribute fairness transferability: Assumption may not hold when attributes are visually disjoint

## Next Checks
1. Test cross-domain fairness approaches on diverse visual datasets (e.g., satellite imagery, autonomous driving) to assess generalizability.
2. Evaluate the HF metric on datasets with extreme group imbalance to verify its robustness in capturing fairness.
3. Conduct ablation studies on the transferability assumption by systematically varying the visual similarity between sensitive attributes.
