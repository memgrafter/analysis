# Unsupervised Data Selection for TTS: Using Arabic Broadcast News as a Case Study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09099
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09099
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Achieved 3.9% CER and MOS scores of 4.4 (intelligibility) and 4.2 (naturalness) for unsupervised Arabic TTS

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of building text-to-speech (TTS) systems for low-resource languages like Arabic, where high-quality paired text and audio data are scarce. The authors propose a fully unsupervised framework that includes automatic data selection and pre-training/fine-tuning strategies. They use broadcast news recordings as a case study, applying techniques like DNSMOS for quality assessment, automatic vowelization for improved pronunciation, and ASR for fixing transcription errors. The approach achieves promising results with 3.9% character error rate and MOS scores demonstrating good intelligibility and naturalness.

## Method Summary
The method involves transfer learning from a high-resource language (English) to pre-train TTS models, followed by fine-tuning on Arabic data. The pipeline includes automatic data selection using DNSMOS quality assessment, speaker selection from broadcast news, manual verification of selected segments, automatic vowelization of Arabic text, and ASR-based transcription correction. The core TTS architecture uses FastSpeech2-based Conformer models trained on the processed data, with Parallel WaveGAN for waveform generation.

## Key Results
- Achieved 3.9% character error rate (CER) on Arabic broadcast news
- MOS scores of 4.4 for intelligibility and 4.2 for naturalness in subjective evaluations
- Successfully demonstrated unsupervised data selection using DNSMOS quality assessment
- Showed effectiveness of transfer learning from English to Arabic with limited data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Transfer learning from high-resource language (English) significantly reduces data requirements for good quality synthesis. Pre-training on LJSpeech initializes the TTS model with general speech synthesis capabilities that can be adapted to Arabic with minimal data.

### Mechanism 2
Automatic data selection using quality metrics like DNSMOS identifies high-quality segments from broadcast news, achieving near-human performance in selecting TTS-suitable audio without requiring transcription.

### Mechanism 3
Automatic vowelization of Arabic text significantly improves TTS output quality by providing explicit pronunciation information that is otherwise ambiguous in unvoweled text.

## Foundational Learning

- **Transfer learning in speech synthesis**: Enables building TTS systems for low-resource languages using models pre-trained on high-resource languages, reducing data requirements. Quick check: What are the key components of a TTS model that benefit most from transfer learning?

- **Automatic speech quality assessment (DNSMOS)**: Allows unsupervised selection of high-quality audio segments from large datasets without manual annotation. Quick check: How does DNSMOS differ from traditional MOS prediction methods in terms of input requirements and output?

- **Arabic vowelization (diacritization)**: Arabic text typically lacks short vowels, making pronunciation ambiguous for TTS systems; vowelization provides this crucial information. Quick check: What are the main challenges in automatically vowelizing Arabic text from broadcast news sources?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Broadcast news corpus → Quality filtering (DNSMOS) → Speaker selection → Manual verification → Text processing (ASR correction → Vowelization → Tokenization) → TTS model (Pre-training → Fine-tuning) → Vocoder (Parallel WaveGAN) → Evaluation

- **Critical path**: Data selection → Text processing → Model training → Evaluation

- **Design tradeoffs**: AR vs. non-AR models (quality vs. speed), pre-training vs. from-scratch (initialization vs. compute), manual vs. automatic data selection (quality vs. scalability)

- **Failure signatures**: High CER/WER indicates text-to-speech alignment problems; low MOS scores indicate quality issues; training instability suggests insufficient data or poor alignment

- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Train FastSpeech2-Conformer from scratch on Arabic data to establish baseline; 2) Implement transfer learning from LJSpeech and fine-tune on Arabic; 3) Compare manual data selection vs. DNSMOS-based automatic selection

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How effective are alternative data selection methods, such as unsupervised speaker clustering or active learning, compared to DNSMOS and manual selection approaches used in this study? The paper only evaluates DNSMOS and manual selection, leaving other methods unexplored.

### Open Question 2
What is the impact of using different types of broadcast news data (e.g., political debates, sports commentary) on the quality of the generated TTS? The paper uses Aljazeera Broadcast news but doesn't analyze the impact of different program types.

### Open Question 3
How does the proposed unsupervised TTS method compare to supervised TTS approaches when both have access to the same amount of data? The paper focuses on unsupervised TTS but doesn't provide direct comparison with supervised methods under similar data constraints.

## Limitations

- The evaluation relies heavily on subjective MOS scores from a limited number of listeners, introducing potential bias
- The claim of "unsupervised" data selection is questionable given the substantial manual verification component required
- Manual verification of 60-70% of selected segments may not scale to truly low-resource scenarios

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Technical implementation details for transfer learning from LJSpeech to Arabic are well-specified and reproducible
- **Medium Confidence**: Effectiveness of DNSMOS for quality assessment is supported by correlation with human ratings, but threshold selection lacks statistical justification
- **Low Confidence**: The "unsupervised" nature of the approach is undermined by the significant manual verification component

## Next Checks

3. Evaluate model performance when using different source languages (e.g., French or Mandarin) for pre-training to determine cross-lingual transfer robustness
