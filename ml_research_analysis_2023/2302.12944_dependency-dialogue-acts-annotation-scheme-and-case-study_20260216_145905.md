# Dependency Dialogue Acts -- Annotation Scheme and Case Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.12944
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12944
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces DDA, a novel framework for capturing speaker-intentions in multi-party dialogues

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the Dependency Dialogue Act (DDA) annotation scheme, a novel framework for capturing the structure of speaker-intentions in multi-party dialogues. DDA combines features from existing dialogue annotation frameworks and emphasizes the multi-relational response structure of dialogues, in addition to dialogue acts and rhetorical relations. The framework aims to represent functional, discourse, and response structure in multi-party, multi-threaded conversations. The paper demonstrates DDA's high expressiveness and recall-orientation regarding conversation dynamics between multiple speakers through case studies annotating DDA structures in multi-party, multi-threaded conversations.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces DDA as an annotation scheme that builds upon existing dialogue annotation frameworks while emphasizing multi-relational response structures. The framework captures speaker intentions through a combination of dialogue acts, rhetorical relations, and response structures in multi-party conversations. The methodology involves systematic annotation of dialogue segments to represent both the functional and discourse-level relationships between speakers' utterances. The case study applies DDA to multi-party, multi-threaded conversations to demonstrate the scheme's practical applicability and expressiveness in capturing complex dialogue dynamics.

## Key Results
- DDA successfully captures multi-relational response structures in multi-party dialogues
- The scheme demonstrates high expressiveness in representing conversation dynamics between multiple speakers
- Case studies validate DDA's effectiveness in annotating multi-threaded conversations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DDA works by integrating multiple annotation dimensions into a unified framework. It captures not just individual dialogue acts but also the relational structures between utterances across multiple speakers. The scheme's effectiveness stems from its ability to represent both the functional purpose of each utterance and its rhetorical relationships within the broader conversational context. By incorporating response structure alongside dialogue acts and rhetorical relations, DDA provides a more complete representation of how speakers interact and build upon each other's contributions in complex dialogue scenarios.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Dialogue Act Theory** - Why needed: Provides theoretical foundation for categorizing speaker intentions
   Quick check: Can identify main dialogue act types (question, assertion, command, etc.)

2. **Rhetorical Structure Theory** - Why needed: Enables understanding of discourse-level relationships between utterances
   Quick check: Can identify coherence relations like elaboration, contrast, and condition

3. **Multi-party Conversation Dynamics** - Why needed: Captures the unique challenges of conversations with multiple active participants
   Quick check: Can track speaker turns and identify overlapping speech

4. **Annotation Scheme Design** - Why needed: Ensures the framework is both expressive and practical for annotators
   Quick check: Can balance granularity with usability in annotation guidelines

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Utterance Analysis -> Dialogue Act Assignment -> Rhetorical Relation Identification -> Response Structure Mapping -> DDA Annotation

**Critical Path**: The core workflow involves first identifying individual dialogue acts, then establishing rhetorical relationships between utterances, and finally mapping the response structures that connect multiple speakers' contributions.

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- Granularity vs. Usability: Higher detail levels provide more expressiveness but increase annotation complexity
- Expressiveness vs. Generalizability: More specific categories improve precision but may limit cross-domain applicability
- Automation vs. Human Judgment: Automated tools can speed annotation but may miss nuanced conversational dynamics

**Failure Signatures**: 
- Inconsistent annotation across similar conversational contexts
- Difficulty distinguishing between closely related dialogue act categories
- Missing implicit relationships in multi-threaded conversations

**First Experiments**:
1. Annotate a simple two-party dialogue with clear turn-taking
2. Apply DDA to a multi-party conversation with explicit topic shifts
3. Test the framework's ability to capture overlapping speech in group discussions

## Limitations
- Lacks quantitative inter-annotator agreement metrics beyond qualitative examples
- Limited to English-only dialogues without cross-linguistic validation
- No systematic comparison with existing dialogue act annotation schemes

## Confidence
- **High confidence** in descriptive claims about DDA's structural features and theoretical foundations
- **Medium confidence** in claims about DDA being "highly expressive and recall-oriented" due to lack of empirical validation
- **Low confidence** in practical applicability claims without systematic evaluation across diverse dialogue domains

## Next Checks
1. Conduct systematic inter-annotator agreement measurements (e.g., Cohen's Kappa, Krippendorff's alpha) across multiple annotators for the same dialogue segments

2. Apply DDA annotation to dialogues in at least two additional languages to test generalizability and identify language-specific challenges

3. Systematically compare DDA annotations with established schemes (e.g., DAMSL, ISO 24617-2) on the same dialogue corpus to quantify improvements in expressiveness and coverage
