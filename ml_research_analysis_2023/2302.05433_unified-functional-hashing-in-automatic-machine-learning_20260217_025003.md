# Unified Functional Hashing in Automatic Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05433
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05433
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: UFH detects functionally equivalent candidates to avoid costly re-evaluation, providing significant efficiency gains across four AutoML setups while maintaining or improving results.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Unified Functional Hashing (UFH), a method for accelerating Automatic Machine Learning by detecting functionally equivalent candidates through hashing their outputs on canonical inputs. The approach generates a hash fingerprint independent of the candidate's structural representation, enabling detection of equivalent models even when coded differently. Applied through functional equivalence caching (FEC), functional change mutation (FCM), and tabulist techniques, UFH significantly reduces computational overhead in evolutionary AutoML search. Experiments across diverse AutoML contexts demonstrate consistent efficiency improvements while maintaining or enhancing final model performance.

## Method Summary
UFH computes a hash from candidate outputs on a small, fixed set of canonical inputs that serves as a functional fingerprint. The method involves generating canonical training and validation subsets, feeding these through the candidate model, collecting hashable outputs (typically logits or errors), and mixing these into an integer hash using mantissa bits, exponent, and sign information. The resulting hash is used to cache fitness values, allowing the search algorithm to skip re-evaluation when functionally equivalent candidates are detected. The unified aspect allows the same hashing algorithm to work across different candidate representations including compute graphs, imperative code, and lambda functions.

## Key Results
- FEC consistently provides significant efficiency gains across MNAS, AutoML-Zero, AutoRL, and NAS-Bench-101 setups
- Method is robust to search algorithm choice, hyperparameter settings, and evaluation noise
- Hash collisions are infrequent and can be mitigated with cache forgetfulness
- UFH enables faster and/or better AutoML discoveries by leveraging functional equivalence detection

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Functional hashing detects functionally equivalent candidates even when their representations differ structurally.
- Mechanism: UFH generates a hash from the candidate's outputs on a small set of canonical inputs, which serves as a fingerprint independent of the candidate's structure or representation.
- Core assumption: A small set of canonical inputs is sufficient to capture the functional behavior of the candidate.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our hash is 'functional' in that it identifies equivalent candidates even if they were represented or coded differently, and it is 'unified' in that the same algorithm can hash arbitrary representations— e.g. compute graphs, imperative code, or lambda functions."
  - [section]: "To compute the hash, we first construct a sequence of canonical inputs... Feeding those inputs into the model results in a corresponding sequence of outputs that can be interpreted as a fingerprint of the model."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - corpus contains related hashing papers but none specifically about functional hashing in AutoML.
- Break condition: If the canonical inputs fail to activate all relevant parts of the candidate's function, hash collisions become likely.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Caching functional equivalence evaluations dramatically reduces computational cost in evolutionary AutoML.
- Mechanism: When FEC detects a candidate hash matches a previously evaluated candidate, it skips the costly full evaluation and retrieves the cached fitness.
- Core assumption: Evolutionary search frequently proposes functionally equivalent candidates that would otherwise require redundant expensive evaluations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The central idea is to detect by hashing when the search method produces equivalent candidates, which occurs very frequently, and this way avoid their costly re-evaluation."
  - [section]: "As it turns out, the number of inputs required to effectively hash the candidate is very small compared to the number of inputs required to train it, resulting in important compute reduction from the point of view of AutoML."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - corpus contains hashing papers but none specifically about caching in evolutionary algorithms.
- Break condition: If the search space has low redundancy or the evaluation noise is high enough to cause cache poisoning.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: UFH can be applied to multiple evolutionary techniques beyond simple caching.
- Mechanism: The same unified functional hash can be used with functional change mutation (FCM) and tabulist techniques to avoid redundant work.
- Core assumption: Different evolutionary techniques can benefit from functional equivalence detection in complementary ways.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our hash is 'unified' in that the same algorithm can hash arbitrary representations; e.g. compute graphs, imperative code, or lambda functions."
  - [section]: "To demonstrate the usefulness of UFH, we use it to implement three evolutionary techniques and demonstrate the resulting gains in various AutoML setups."

- Break condition: If the evolutionary technique relies heavily on structural rather than functional differences between candidates.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Canonical inputs and outputs
  - Why needed here: UFH relies on a fixed set of inputs and their corresponding outputs to create a functional fingerprint. Understanding what makes inputs "canonical" (fixed, reproducible) versus training/validation data (randomized) is crucial.
  - Quick check question: Why must the random number generator be reset with a fixed seed before hashing each candidate?

- Concept: Hash mixing and precision
  - Why needed here: The AddToHash function combines floating-point outputs into an integer hash. Understanding mantissa truncation, sign, exponent, and mixing is essential for tuning collision rates.
  - Quick check question: How does the choice of mantissa bits (mbits) affect the probability of hash collisions?

- Concept: Cache hit vs. cache miss cost analysis
  - Why needed here: FEC saves computation when cache hits occur. Understanding when NE × NS ≪ h × NT (where h is cache hit rate) helps justify the approach.
  - Quick check question: Given NE=10, NS=3, NT=10000, and h=0.8, is hashing likely to provide significant savings?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Hashing engine -> Cache system -> Evolutionary controller -> Evaluation inner loop

- Critical path:
  1. Candidate generation by mutation
  2. UFH computation (seed RNG, initialize, forward/backward passes on canonical data)
  3. Cache lookup by hash
  4. If hit: retrieve fitness; if miss: full evaluation -> cache result
  5. Selection based on fitness

- Design tradeoffs:
  - More canonical examples (NE) -> fewer collisions but slower hashing
  - More mantissa bits (mbits) -> fewer collisions but higher storage requirements
  - Larger cache -> fewer re-evaluations but more memory usage
  - Cache forgetfulness probability -> better collision recovery but occasional recomputation

- Failure signatures:
  - Low cache hit rates -> search space has low redundancy or hashing parameters need tuning
  - High collision rates -> too few mantissa bits or canonical examples don't probe function adequately
  - Degraded search quality -> cache poisoning from evaluation noise or poor hyperparameter choices

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run a short FEC counterfactual experiment to measure collision rate by comparing cache-retrieved fitness with full re-evaluation on the same candidate
  2. Vary mantissa bits (e.g., 8, 16, 24) while keeping NE and NS fixed to find the minimal setting that maintains low collision rates
  3. Compare single-threaded vs. distributed search with FEC to observe how parallelization affects evolutionary dynamics

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the functional equivalence caching (FEC) method affect the search dynamics when hash collisions occur frequently?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the impact of hash collisions on FEC performance and suggests using a forgetful cache to mitigate this issue.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that high hash collisions can degrade search quality, but the effectiveness of the forgetful cache approach is not fully validated across diverse scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing FEC with and without forgetful cache across various search spaces and collision rates would provide clarity on its effectiveness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the unified functional hashing (UFH) method be effectively applied to reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms beyond the AutoRL setup discussed in the paper?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that UFH can be applied to RL but only provides preliminary results in the AutoRL setup.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the application of UFH to other RL algorithms or environments, leaving its broader applicability uncertain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing UFH on different RL algorithms and environments would demonstrate its versatility and effectiveness in diverse RL contexts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the long-term impacts of using fake data for hashing on the search performance and efficiency?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests using fake data for hashing in the AutoRL setup due to computational costs but notes potential limitations in probing the candidate.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the trade-offs between using real and fake data for hashing in terms of search performance and efficiency.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of search performance and efficiency using real versus fake data across multiple setups would highlight the trade-offs and optimal use cases.

## Limitations

- Effectiveness depends critically on selecting appropriate canonical inputs that adequately probe candidate behavior
- Hash collisions, while rare, can lead to cache poisoning and degraded search quality
- Strongest results in evolutionary AutoML contexts with high candidate redundancy; less clear benefit for gradient-based methods

## Confidence

- High confidence in the core mechanism of functional hashing for candidate fingerprinting
- Medium confidence in the generalization across diverse AutoML setups due to limited cross-domain testing
- Medium confidence in the efficiency claims, as the paper demonstrates improvements but absolute speedup factors vary significantly by setup
