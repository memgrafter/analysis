# A optimization framework for herbal prescription planning based on deep reinforcement learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.12828
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12828
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: PrescDRL achieves 117-153% improvement in single-step reward, 269-292% improvement in single-step cumulative return, and 387-402% improvement in multi-step cumulative return compared to clinical doctors

## Executive Summary
This study presents PrescDRL, a deep reinforcement learning framework that optimizes sequential herbal prescription treatment planning for chronic diseases in traditional Chinese medicine. The framework addresses the challenge of generating optimized treatment strategies by focusing on long-term effectiveness rather than maximizing immediate rewards at each step. PrescDRL constructs a high-quality benchmark dataset for sequential diabetes diagnosis and treatment, then employs deep reinforcement learning models (DQN and DRQN) to predict optimal herbal prescription clusters. The experimental results demonstrate that PrescDRL significantly outperforms both clinical doctors and baseline methods across multiple performance metrics.

## Method Summary
PrescDRL constructs a virtual environment using historical clinical data, where patient symptom observations serve as states and herbal prescriptions are clustered into discrete action spaces using K-means clustering. The framework implements deep reinforcement learning models (DQN and DRQN) to learn sequential treatment policies that maximize long-term cumulative rewards. A reward function combines symptom improvement and prescription similarity, while a prescription recommendation network translates cluster-level decisions into specific herbal prescriptions. The model is trained on 80% of the dataset and evaluated on the remaining 20%, with performance measured across single-step returns, cumulative returns, and prescription prediction accuracy metrics.

## Key Results
- PrescDRL achieves 117-153% improvement in single-step reward compared to clinical doctors
- The framework shows 269-292% improvement in single-step cumulative return and 387-402% improvement in multi-step cumulative return
- Prescription prediction component demonstrates 40.5% improvement in precision and 63% improvement in recall over baseline methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
PrescDRL achieves superior treatment outcomes by optimizing for long-term effectiveness rather than immediate reward maximization. The framework uses deep reinforcement learning (DQN and DRQN) to model sequential herbal prescription planning as a Markov Decision Process, focusing on cumulative return over multiple treatment steps rather than single-step rewards. The true patient state is not directly observable, so symptom observations serve as proxy states for the RL agent.

### Mechanism 2
Clustering herbal prescriptions into discrete action spaces improves RL model convergence and performance. K-means clustering algorithm groups 9,695 distinct herbal prescriptions into 30 clusters based on herb composition, reducing the action space complexity while maintaining meaningful differentiation between treatment categories. Herbs within each cluster share similar therapeutic properties, making cluster-based actions clinically meaningful.

### Mechanism 3
The virtual environment construction using historical clinical data enables effective RL training without real-world experimentation. Offline virtual environment built from patient symptom-observation and prescription pairs creates transition tuples (s1, a, r, s2) that simulate patient state changes and reward outcomes for training the RL model. Historical treatment outcomes accurately reflect the causal relationship between symptoms, treatments, and patient recovery.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Patient symptoms → State representation → RL agent (DQN/DRQN) → Action (herbal prescription cluster) → Reward function → Virtual environment → State transition prediction → Prescription recommendation network
- Critical path: Symptom observation → RL action selection → Virtual environment simulation → Reward calculation → State transition → Prescription prediction
- Design tradeoffs: Larger action spaces provide more treatment options but increase training complexity; simpler state representations are easier to learn but may miss important patient information
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on prescription prediction indicates issues with the RL model or the integration between RL outputs and the recommendation network
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline comparison: Compare single-step returns between PrescDRL and clinical doctors using the same dataset
  2. Hyperparameter tuning: Test different numbers of herbal prescription clusters (HPC) to find optimal action space granularity
  3. Ablation study: Evaluate prescription prediction performance with and without the RL-derived treatment plans as input features

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal number of herbal prescription clusters (HPC) that maximizes both statistical difference and clinical effectiveness?
- Basis in paper: The paper tests different HPC values from 30 to 150 and finds that 30 clusters yield the highest statistical difference (Chi-square test, -log10P = 23.27±8.27), but acknowledges that "the number of HPCs has little influence on the prediction performance of PrescDRL" and that DRQN shows better stability with increasing HPC numbers.
- Why unresolved: The study identifies 30 clusters as optimal based on statistical criteria, but doesn't fully explore the trade-off between statistical optimality and clinical performance across different HPC values, particularly for the DRQN model which shows better stability with more clusters.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive comparative analysis of clinical outcomes across different HPC values (e.g., 30, 50, 100, 150) using real-world clinical trial data to determine if higher cluster counts provide clinically meaningful improvements that justify their increased complexity.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does PrescDRL's performance compare to clinical outcomes in real-world settings versus simulation environments?
- Basis in paper: The paper states "all the experimental results presented in this study are based on simulation experiments" and acknowledges that "To validate the effectiveness of our proposed PrescDRL model, it is crucial to apply it in real-world clinical diagnosis and treatment systems to evaluate the specific effects."
- Why unresolved: While PrescDRL shows significant improvements in simulation (117-153% improvement in single-step reward), the model has not been validated in actual clinical practice where factors like patient variability, real-time decision-making, and complex comorbidities exist.
- What evidence would resolve it: A prospective clinical trial comparing PrescDRL's recommendations against standard clinical practice across multiple treatment centers, measuring both short-term symptom improvement and long-term patient outcomes.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What strategies can be implemented to reduce the length of diagnosis and treatment sequences generated by PrescDRL while maintaining or improving treatment effectiveness?
- Basis in paper: The paper notes that "the diagnosis and treatment sequences generated by PrescDRL are longer compared to those provided by doctors" and suggests this as an area for future research, stating "it would be necessary to investigate a diagnosis and treatment optimization model that can provide shorter sequences while maintaining high efficacy."
- Why unresolved: Although PrescDRL achieves better cumulative returns, the longer treatment sequences could increase patient burden, healthcare costs, and treatment complexity. The current model prioritizes long-term effectiveness without optimizing for treatment duration.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and testing of modified reward functions or optimization constraints that explicitly penalize longer treatment sequences, followed by comparative analysis of sequence length and treatment outcomes against the original PrescDRL model.

## Limitations

- The study relies on historical clinical data that may contain inherent biases in treatment patterns and patient demographics
- The virtual environment construction assumes historical treatment outcomes accurately reflect causal relationships, which may not hold true for all patient subgroups
- The framework has not been validated in real-world clinical settings beyond the diabetes dataset used, limiting generalizability to other chronic diseases

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The framework architecture and general methodology are well-specified and reproducible. The performance improvements over clinical doctors are clearly demonstrated with quantitative metrics.
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of the reward function design and the validity of the virtual environment assumptions. While the general approach is sound, the specific implementation details and hyperparameter choices significantly impact results.
- **Low Confidence**: The clinical generalizability of the findings beyond the diabetes dataset used. The study does not provide evidence for how well the framework would perform on other chronic diseases or different patient populations.

## Next Checks

1. Conduct ablation studies testing different numbers of herbal prescription clusters (HPC) to determine the optimal action space granularity and validate that the chosen 30 clusters are not arbitrarily selected.
