# An Information Extraction Study: Take In Mind the Tokenization!

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15100
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15100
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Tokenization patterns in language models introduce inductive bias that can improve biomedical information extraction performance

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how tokenization patterns in language models affect information extraction performance, particularly in the biomedical domain. The authors analyze the impact of subword tokenization on entity recognition and relation extraction, finding that aggregation of subword representations can improve results when certain tokenization patterns are present. They conduct a comparative study of subword-based and character-based models, showing that character-level models like CharacterBERT can achieve competitive performance without tokenization artifacts. The research reveals domain-specific linguistic patterns in biomedical text and concludes that while tokenization can introduce useful inductive bias, transitioning to token-free models is feasible and promising.

## Method Summary
The study uses the Partition Filter Network (PFN) architecture to jointly model Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Relation Extraction (RE) tasks on the ADE dataset containing drugs and adverse effects. The research compares different language models including cased BERT, b-BERT, ALBERT, and CharacterBERT, with experiments involving aggregation steps (summation and averaging) for subword models. The methodology includes similarity analysis of learned entity representations, cross-validation evaluation, and analysis of tokenization patterns across different models. The study also examines hyphenation patterns and morphological features specific to biomedical text.

## Key Results
- Aggregation of subword representations improves IE performance when tokenization patterns correlate with entity boundaries
- CharacterBERT achieves competitive performance (0.7% better in NER, 1.3% better in RE) compared to subword-based models
- Biomedical text exhibits consistent morphological patterns (like "itis", "osis", "emia") that character models can learn directly
- Tokenization introduces domain-specific inductive bias that can be beneficial for information extraction tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Tokenization patterns introduce inductive bias that improves information extraction performance
- Mechanism: When entity-related words are split into multiple subword pieces, aggregation of these subword representations creates more robust entity embeddings by combining multiple informative pieces
- Core assumption: The subword split patterns correlate with entity boundaries and semantic units in biomedical text
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "tokenization patterns can introduce inductive bias that results in state-of-the-art performance"
  - [section 3.2]: "coupling this finding with the tokenization analysis, the pattern of word-piece splitting for words of interest acts as inductive bias when aggregation is used"
  - [corpus]: Weak - the corpus shows related work on token-free models but lacks direct evidence about this specific inductive bias mechanism

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Character-based models can achieve competitive performance without tokenization artifacts
- Mechanism: CharacterBERT processes text at the character level, learning representations directly from character sequences without the vocabulary constraints and split patterns of subword tokenization
- Core assumption: Character-level representations can capture the necessary linguistic information without relying on learned tokenization patterns
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "character-based models produce promising results; thus, transitioning to token-free IE models is feasible"
  - [section 4.2]: "The model that leverages CharacterBERT is very competitive in the biomedical text" and outperforms b-BERT by 0.7% in NER and 1.3% in RE
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus shows related work on token-free models but lacks direct comparison evidence for this specific claim

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Special linguistic morphology in biomedical text (hyphenation patterns) explains why character-based models perform well
- Mechanism: Biomedical entities have consistent subword patterns (like "itis", "osis", "emia") that character models can learn directly, while subword models may split these patterns in suboptimal ways
- Core assumption: The consistent morphological patterns in biomedical text provide an advantage to character-based models that can learn these patterns without tokenization interference
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3]: "A special morphology is noticeable for both entity types" and identifies 11 subwords with frequency >20 for Drug entities and 19 for Adverse-Effect entities
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus shows related work on token-free models but doesn't provide evidence about biomedical-specific morphological patterns

- Failure signatures: Poor entity boundary detection when aggregation is missing despite consistent tokenization patterns; character models failing to capture long-range dependencies

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different tokenization algorithms (WordPiece, BPE, SentencePiece) compare in their impact on inductive bias for biomedical IE tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares cased BERT (WordPiece), b-BERT (WordPiece), and ALBERT (SentencePiece) tokenization algorithms, finding different patterns of inductive bias.
- Why unresolved: The study only examines three tokenization algorithms in one domain. A broader comparison across more algorithms and domains is needed.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study comparing 5+ tokenization algorithms across 3+ domains (biomedical, general, legal) using the same IE architecture.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the inductive bias from tokenization persist when models are trained on larger datasets or does it diminish with more training data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses 10-fold cross-validation on a relatively small biomedical dataset and observes significant inductive bias, suggesting dataset size might be a factor.
- Why unresolved: The study doesn't explore how inductive bias scales with dataset size or whether it disappears with sufficient training data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training the same model on progressively larger subsets of the same domain (100, 1K, 10K, 100K examples) and measuring changes in inductive bias.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can token-free models be designed to deliberately incorporate beneficial inductive biases that tokenization currently provides?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows character-based models perform competitively but doesn't explore whether they can be enhanced to capture tokenization-like patterns.
- Why unresolved: CharacterBERT and similar models remove tokenization but haven't been optimized to capture domain-specific morphological patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing character-based models with architectural modifications (e.g., position-aware character convolutions) that deliberately capture morphological patterns and testing performance gains.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies heavily on qualitative observations of subword splits without quantitative measures of pattern consistency
- Character-based model comparison lacks ablation studies to isolate the effect of token-free processing from other architectural differences
- The study focuses on a single biomedical domain, limiting generalizability to other domains or languages
- Evidence for the feasibility of token-free models is based on one character-based model comparison

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The core finding that aggregation of subword representations can improve IE performance when tokenization patterns are present. This is directly supported by the experimental results showing performance differences between models with and without aggregation steps.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that tokenization patterns introduce meaningful inductive bias for biomedical IE tasks. While the evidence shows correlation between tokenization patterns and performance, the causal mechanism could also be explained by other factors such as vocabulary coverage or positional information.

**Low confidence**: The assertion that transitioning to token-free models is "feasible and promising" based solely on one character-based model comparison. The evidence would need broader validation across multiple token-free architectures and domains.

## Next Checks

1. **Quantitative Pattern Analysis**: Conduct a systematic statistical analysis of subword tokenization patterns across the entire ADE dataset to measure the correlation between specific split patterns and entity boundary accuracy. This would involve computing precision/recall metrics for different tokenization patterns and their relationship to entity detection performance.

2. **Ablation Study for CharacterBERT**: Perform controlled experiments comparing CharacterBERT against a subword-based model with identical architecture but different tokenization, isolating the effect of token-free processing from other architectural differences. This should include varying the character embedding dimension and CNN kernel sizes to understand their impact on performance.

3. **Cross-Domain Validation**: Test the tokenization bias hypothesis on non-biomedical text corpora to determine whether the observed effects are domain-specific or represent a more general phenomenon. This would help validate whether the morphological patterns in biomedical text are uniquely advantageous for character-based models.
