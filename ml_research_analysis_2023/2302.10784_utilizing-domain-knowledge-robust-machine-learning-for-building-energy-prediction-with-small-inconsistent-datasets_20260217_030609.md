# Utilizing Domain Knowledge: Robust Machine Learning for Building Energy Prediction with Small, Inconsistent Datasets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.10784
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10784
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Component-based machine learning significantly improves robustness against data sparseness and accepts incomplete data while maintaining high interpretability

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces component-based machine learning (CBML) as a method to integrate domain knowledge into machine learning models, specifically addressing the challenge of small, inconsistent datasets in building energy prediction. The approach decomposes building energy models into components (walls, floors, zones) and trains separate component-level models, converting extrapolation problems into interpolation problems. A case experiment demonstrates that CBML achieves significant improvements in model robustness, data utilization efficiency, and training time reduction compared to traditional monolithic approaches.

## Method Summary
The authors propose encoding building structural knowledge as semantic information in model organization through component-based decomposition. They implement CBML using LightGBM with 3-fold cross-validation and 300 early stopping epochs, training separate models for each building component (walls, windows, roof, ground floor, infiltration) on synthetic building energy datasets. The approach is compared against monolithic ML models across various sampling rates (1% to 0.0125%) using R² accuracy metrics. The method involves extracting domain knowledge, defining building components, collecting and preprocessing energy data, training component-level models, assembling predictions, and evaluating performance against sparse data conditions.

## Key Results
- CBML achieves significantly higher robustness against data sparseness compared to monolithic ML approaches
- The method efficiently utilizes data from different building entities and accepts incomplete data with high interpretability
- Training time is reduced through the decoupled component structure while maintaining prediction accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Embedding domain knowledge as semantic information in the model organization reduces ML data dependency by decomposing building energy models into components and training separate component-level models. This converts extrapolation problems into interpolation problems, requiring less data for accurate predictions.

### Mechanism 2
Knowledge-encoded ML models maintain robustness against data sparseness while preserving interpretability by training component-level models on separate data subsets and reassembling them for building-level predictions, reducing the impact of sparse data on individual components.

### Mechanism 3
Knowledge-encoded ML reduces training time and accepts incomplete data with high interpretability through decoupled component structure that allows each component to be trained separately on smaller datasets, reducing overall training time and enabling use of incomplete data.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Component-level ML models (walls, windows, roof, ground floor, infiltration) -> Building-level prediction assembly module -> Data preprocessing and sampling module -> Experiment design and evaluation module

- **Critical path**: 1. Extract domain knowledge and define building components 2. Collect and preprocess building energy data 3. Train component-level ML models 4. Assemble component predictions for building-level output 5. Evaluate model performance against sparse data conditions

- **Design tradeoffs**: Component-based vs. monolithic ML (component-based offers better robustness and interpretability but may accumulate uncertainties), algorithm choice (LightGBM provides good generalization but may not capture all complex patterns), data granularity (time-series data provides detailed information but increases data size and complexity)

- **Failure signatures**: Component-level models fail to learn patterns (negative R² values), accumulated uncertainties lead to significant accuracy loss in building-level predictions, training time does not decrease as expected

- **First 3 experiments**: 1. Train component-level models on full dataset and evaluate building-level accuracy 2. Train models on reduced datasets (1%, 0.1%, 0.05% sampling rates) and compare accuracy degradation 3. Test model robustness with noisy/incomplete input data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What specific engineering domains beyond building energy prediction would benefit most from knowledge-encoded ML approaches like CBML? The paper states CBML's features "certainly contribute to adopting this framework to fit broader domains and scenarios" but doesn't explore other domains where prerequisites are met.

### Open Question 2
What is the theoretical limit of data sparseness beyond which knowledge-encoded ML models like CBML begin to fail? The experiments stop at 0.0125% sampling rate, leaving open questions about the model's absolute limits with extremely sparse data.

### Open Question 3
How does the performance of knowledge-encoded ML models compare to traditional physics-based simulation methods when data is extremely sparse? The paper doesn't directly compare CBML's performance against physics-based simulations in sparse data scenarios.

## Limitations
- Simulation-based dataset generation may not fully capture real-world building performance complexity and variability
- Transferability of extracted domain knowledge across different building types and geographical locations remains unproven
- Performance on genuinely incomplete data (missing entire components) versus artificially sampled data is not demonstrated

## Confidence
- **High confidence** in domain knowledge encoding reducing data dependency for building energy models
- **Medium confidence** in claimed robustness improvements, as results show strong performance on simulated data but lack validation on real-world datasets
- **Medium confidence** in training time reduction claims, as detailed timing comparisons are not provided

## Next Checks
1. Test CBML on a dataset from actual buildings with known energy consumption patterns, including measurement errors and missing data points
2. Apply the same domain knowledge extraction approach to a different building type (e.g., commercial vs residential) to assess knowledge transferability
3. Systematically remove or corrupt individual component data to quantify how CBML handles genuinely incomplete information versus controlled sampling scenarios
