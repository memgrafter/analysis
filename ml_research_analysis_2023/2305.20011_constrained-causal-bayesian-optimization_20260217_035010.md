# Constrained Causal Bayesian Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.20011
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20011
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: cCBO reduces search space using causal structure, models effects with GPs, and selects interventions via constrained EI, achieving successful trade-offs between convergence speed and feasibility in synthetic and real-world causal graphs.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces constrained causal Bayesian optimization (cCBO), a method for finding optimal interventions in known causal graphs while satisfying constraints. The approach reduces the search space by exploiting causal structure and observational data, then models target and constraint effects with Gaussian processes. Interventions are selected via a constrained expected improvement acquisition function that balances optimization and feasibility. The method is evaluated on synthetic and real-world causal graphs, demonstrating effective trade-offs between fast convergence and high percentages of feasible interventions.

## Method Summary
cCBO operates by first reducing the search space through causal structure exploitation and observational data analysis. The method then models target and constraint effects using Gaussian process surrogates, with three increasingly sophisticated modeling approaches: single-task GP (STGP), multi-task GP (MTGP), and graph-based MTGP (G-MTGP). Interventions are selected using a constrained expected improvement (cEI) acquisition function that balances target optimization against constraint satisfaction. The algorithm iterates between selecting interventions, collecting samples, and updating GP models until convergence or budget exhaustion.

## Key Results
- Search space reduction using causal structure significantly decreases the number of intervention sets requiring evaluation.
- Multi-task GP models capture correlations between target and constraint effects, improving both convergence speed and feasibility rates.
- The constrained EI acquisition effectively balances exploration and constraint satisfaction, outperforming baseline methods in many cases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The method reduces the search space by eliminating intervention sets whose effects are invariant under subsets, thereby avoiding redundant evaluations.
- Mechanism: The `cMISReduce` procedure removes intervention sets that are not minimal with respect to their constraint effects, exploiting causal graph structure to prune the power set of interventions.
- Core assumption: The target and constraint effects remain unchanged under certain nested interventions (i.e., µW do(X=x) = µW do(X′=x′) for all W in C∪Y and any SCM).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "cCBO first reduces the search space by exploiting the graph structure and, if available, an observational dataset;"
  - [section 3.1]: Definition 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 formalize minimal intervention sets and prove sufficiency of the reduced search space.
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus mention of MIS reduction; this is novel to the paper.
- Break condition: If the causal graph is misspecified, the ancestor relationships and d-separation used to justify invariance may be incorrect, invalidating the pruning.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Multi-task Gaussian processes (MTGP, G-MTGP) capture correlations among target and constraint effects, improving both convergence speed and feasibility.
- Mechanism: Instead of modeling each effect independently, these models assume a shared latent structure or exploit SCM structure to compute covariances across effects, allowing joint posterior updates.
- Core assumption: Effects on correlated variables are jointly distributed and share statistical dependencies that can be learned from observational or interventional data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose different surrogate models that enable to integrate observational and interventional data while capturing correlation among effects with increasing levels of sophistication."
  - [section 3.2]: MTGP uses linear coregionalization; G-MTGP explicitly computes covariances via the SCM under no unobserved confounders.
  - [corpus]: No corpus paper explicitly discusses multi-task GP modeling of causal intervention effects; closest is general multi-task BO but not causal.
- Break condition: If unobserved confounders exist, the G-MTGP covariance assumptions break; if correlations are weak, the multi-task advantage may be negligible.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The constrained expected improvement (cEI) acquisition balances target optimization and constraint satisfaction, guiding exploration toward feasible optimal interventions.
- Mechanism: cEI is defined as the expected improvement of the target effect divided by the intervention cardinality, but only when all constraints are met; otherwise the improvement is zero.
- Core assumption: The acquisition function can trade off exploration of the feasible space against exploitation of promising interventions without excessive constraint violations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "by sequentially selecting interventions via a constrained expected improvement acquisition function."
  - [section 3.3]: Formal definition of cEI and its Monte Carlo approximation for multi-task GPs.
  - [corpus]: cEI is used in constrained BO literature (Gardner et al., 2014) but not in causal BO; the paper extends it to causal settings.
- Break condition: If constraints are too tight or noisy, cEI may rarely be positive, stalling exploration; if too loose, feasibility is ignored.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Structural causal models (SCMs) and do-calculus.
  - Why needed here: The method relies on known causal graph structure to reduce search space and model effects under interventions; understanding do-calculus is essential to formalize µdo(X=x).
  - Quick check question: Given a causal graph with edges X→Z and Z→Y, what is the effect of do(X=x) on Y in terms of the SCM?

- Concept: Gaussian process surrogate modeling and multi-task extensions.
  - Why needed here: Target and constraint effects are unknown and modeled as GPs; multi-task GPs capture dependencies between effects for better uncertainty quantification.
  - Quick check question: How does the covariance between two GP outputs change if they share a latent process versus being independent?

- Concept: Bayesian optimization acquisition functions with constraints.
  - Why needed here: The method must select interventions that improve the target while satisfying constraints; constrained EI is the key mechanism.
  - Quick check question: What happens to the EI acquisition if the probability of feasibility is zero at a candidate point?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Causal graph parser -> search space reduction (cMISReduce) -> intervention set filter (nMC∪Y,G)
  -> GP surrogate models (STGP, MTGP, G-MTGP) per intervention set
  -> Acquisition engine (cEI) -> intervention selector
  -> Simulator/experiment runner -> data collection
  -> Posterior update module -> GP updates

- Critical path:
  1. Parse graph -> compute nMC∪Y,G
  2. Initialize GPs with observational data (if available)
  3. Loop: cEI -> intervention -> collect samples -> update GPs
  4. Return best feasible found

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single-task GP: Simpler, scales with |nMC∪Y,G|, ignores correlations
  - Multi-task GP: Captures dependencies, higher per-set cost O(M³N³I), but can reduce total trials
  - G-MTGP: Best when SCM is accurate and no unobserved confounders, but fragile to misspecification

- Failure signatures:
  - High percentage of infeasible interventions -> cEI too aggressive or constraints too tight
  - Slow convergence -> GP hyperparameters poorly tuned or observational data insufficient
  - Empty nMC∪Y,G -> all constraint sets violate thresholds in observational data

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Synthetic graph with no observational data, known constraints -> validate search space reduction and cEI behavior.
  2. Same graph with synthetic observational data -> test STGP+ prior mean integration.
  3. Graph with high correlation between target and constraints -> compare MTGP vs STGP convergence and feasibility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can cCBO be extended to handle unknown or misspecified causal graphs?
- Basis in paper: Explicit mention in Discussion section.
- Why unresolved: The paper assumes a known causal graph, but in practice this may not be satisfied. Using an incorrect graph could lead to inaccurate GP prior parameters and invalid search space reduction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing cCBO performance with true vs. incorrect causal graphs, or theoretical analysis of sensitivity to causal graph misspecification.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does cCBO scale with increasing number of constrained variables or interventional data samples?
- Basis in paper: Explicit mention in Discussion section.
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions computational cost scaling issues but doesn't provide experimental results or solutions for high-dimensional settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Scalability experiments with varying numbers of constraints and data samples, and comparisons with alternative methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can cCBO be extended to handle soft interventions rather than hard interventions?
- Basis in paper: Explicit mention in Discussion section.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on hard interventions where variables are set to specific values, but mentions soft interventions as a potential extension.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing cCBO performance with hard vs. soft interventions, or theoretical analysis of soft intervention formulation.

## Limitations

- Graph Misspecification Risk: The search space reduction and G-MTGP covariance assumptions both critically depend on an accurate causal graph. If the true graph differs from the assumed structure, the pruned intervention sets may exclude optimal solutions, and G-MTGP's covariance computations will be invalid.
- Observational Data Requirements: While observational data can improve prior means and covariance estimates, the quality of the observational dataset directly impacts the reliability of the reduced search space and GP priors.
- Computational Scaling: The method's complexity grows with the number of reduced intervention sets and the dimensionality of effects, potentially limiting real-world applicability for large graphs or high-dimensional effects.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The core algorithmic framework (search space reduction, GP modeling, constrained EI acquisition) is mathematically sound and well-specified. The synthetic experiments clearly demonstrate the benefits of search space reduction and multi-task modeling when assumptions hold.
- Medium Confidence: Performance claims on the real-world interventional dose-response example are less certain. The paper reports success but doesn't provide extensive comparisons or ablation studies showing how each component contributes to the results.
- Low Confidence: The paper doesn't address how to handle cases where nMC∪Y,G becomes empty or how to automatically tune the trade-off between exploration and constraint satisfaction.

## Next Checks

1. **Graph Perturbation Analysis**: Systematically vary the assumed causal graph structure (add/remove edges) and measure how search space reduction quality and final optimization performance degrade. This would quantify the method's sensitivity to graph misspecification.

2. **Observational Data Quality Study**: Generate synthetic observational datasets with varying sample sizes and noise levels, then measure how GP prior accuracy, search space reduction effectiveness, and overall optimization performance change. This would reveal data requirements for practical deployment.

3. **Scaling Experiment**: Test the method on progressively larger causal graphs (increasing |V|, |I|, |C|) and measure computational time, memory usage, and optimization performance. Include comparison with baseline methods that don't use search space reduction to quantify the practical benefits of the pruning strategy.
