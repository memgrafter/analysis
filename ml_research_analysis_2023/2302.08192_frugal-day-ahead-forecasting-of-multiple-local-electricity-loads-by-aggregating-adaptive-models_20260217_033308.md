# Frugal day-ahead forecasting of multiple local electricity loads by aggregating adaptive models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.08192
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08192
- Reference count: 11
- This paper develops a frugal method for forecasting electricity consumption for over a thousand substations in France using aggregated adaptive models

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of forecasting electricity consumption for over a thousand substations in France by developing a frugal method based on aggregating adaptive generalized additive models (GAMs) and state-space representations. The authors demonstrate that instead of estimating individual models for each substation, training GAMs and Kalman filters on a small subset of time series and transferring this knowledge to all substations through aggregation of experts can significantly reduce computational costs while maintaining competitive accuracy. The method achieves median NMAEs of 4.29%, 5.26%, and 4.44% across three time periods, outperforming individual benchmark models.

## Method Summary
The method trains generalized additive models with Kalman filtering on a small subset of representative substations, then uses online aggregation algorithms to combine predictions for all 1,344 substations. This transfer learning approach reduces computational costs by avoiding individual model estimation for each substation while maintaining accuracy through adaptive state-space modeling that captures evolving consumption patterns. The aggregation methods (ML-Poly) combine predictions from multiple base models in real-time, with the number of experts optimized through grid search.

## Key Results
- AGG GAM-Kalman TL achieved median NMAEs of 4.29%, 5.26%, and 4.44% across three time periods
- The method outperformed individual benchmark models while reducing computational costs
- During COVID-19 lockdown, the aggregation methods showed median NMAE increases of only 1.26-1.45%, demonstrating adaptation capability

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Aggregation of experts reduces computational cost while maintaining accuracy for multi-time-series forecasting
- Mechanism: Instead of training individual models for each of 1,344 substations, the method trains a small number of base models (GAMs with Kalman filters) on representative subsets, then uses online aggregation to combine predictions for all substations
- Core assumption: Different substations have similar underlying patterns that can be captured by shared base models, with aggregation handling substation-specific variations
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] states the aggregation approach transfers knowledge from small subsets to all substations; [section] discusses building variants for different parameter transfer levels

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Transfer learning through aggregation enables adaptation to new consumption patterns (e.g., COVID-19 lockdowns)
- Mechanism: Kalman filter adaptation allows base models to track changes over time, while aggregation provides flexibility to combine experts that may specialize in different regimes
- Core assumption: The consumption patterns before and during the COVID-19 lockdown are sufficiently similar that base models can be adapted to handle the shift
- Evidence anchors: [section] shows aggregation methods achieved equivalent performances to individual models; [section] demonstrates lower downgrades during first lockdown

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Interpretability of GAMs combined with aggregation provides operational insights
- Mechanism: GAMs decompose consumption into additive effects (calendar, weather, past consumption), while aggregation weights indicate which base models are most relevant for specific substations and times
- Core assumption: The additive structure of GAMs captures the main drivers of consumption, and the aggregation weights can identify when different base models are most useful
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] highlights model interpretability importance; [section] analyzes aggregation methods at effect scale with state coefficient evolution

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> Base model training -> Aggregation engine -> Adaptation module -> Evaluation pipeline
- Critical path: Data → Base models → Aggregation → Prediction → Evaluation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of base models vs. computational cost: More models may improve accuracy but increase training time
  - Granularity of aggregation: Per-substation vs. per-region aggregation
  - Adaptation frequency: How often to update Kalman filter parameters
- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in aggregation weights may indicate poor base model selection
  - Degradation in performance during regime changes (e.g., lockdowns) suggests insufficient adaptation
  - Computational bottlenecks in base model training or Kalman filter optimization
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline: Train individual GAM+Kalman models on all substations, measure NMAE
  2. Aggregation test: Train on 9 representative substations, aggregate for all, compare NMAE to baseline
  3. Adaptation test: Introduce synthetic consumption shift, measure aggregation's ability to maintain accuracy

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the random selection of time series used to train GAMs and Kalman variances be improved beyond the current method?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests characterizing clusters of substations according to geographic, weather, or type of consumers characteristics and selecting one representative per cluster as a potential improvement.
- Why unresolved: The current method uses random selection which may not capture the most informative time series for transfer learning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing the performance of aggregation methods using clustered selection versus random selection across different numbers of substations and geographical regions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of experts (sources) to include in the aggregation methods for different forecasting scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper performs a grid search to find optimal values (n1=9, n2=9, n3=6) but acknowledges this is context-dependent and the elbow phenomenon suggests diminishing returns.
- Why unresolved: The optimal number likely varies with the number of target series, their heterogeneity, and computational constraints which weren't fully explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the number of experts across different numbers of target series, different levels of series similarity, and measuring both accuracy and computational cost trade-offs.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of aggregation methods scale when applied to much larger datasets, such as consumption at a finer granularity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions this as a potential extension, noting that computational complexity depends on estimating a fixed number of models.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested on 1,344 substations and theoretical scaling properties weren't examined.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the aggregation methods on datasets with 10x or 100x more series while measuring accuracy, computational time, and memory usage to identify scaling bottlenecks.

## Limitations
- Performance degradation during COVID-19 lockdown (1.26-1.45% median NMAE increase) indicates limitations in handling regime shifts
- Reliance on transfer learning from small subset may not capture all consumption patterns across full dataset
- Interpretability claims lack quantitative validation against operational decision-making criteria

## Confidence

- Mechanism 1 (Aggregation reducing computational cost): **High** - Well-supported by experimental results and computational analysis
- Mechanism 2 (Adaptation to COVID-19 patterns): **Medium** - Performance degradation observed, though still competitive
- Mechanism 3 (Interpretability for operations): **Medium** - Qualitative evidence present but lacks operational validation
