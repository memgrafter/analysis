# Identification, explanation and clinical evaluation of hospital patient subtypes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.08019
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08019
- Reference count: 32
- Primary result: Unsupervised clustering of hospital patient vital signs identified five clinically meaningful subtypes with varying risk profiles, validated by clinician review

## Executive Summary
This study presents a pipeline for identifying patient subtypes in hospital settings using unsupervised machine learning on six vital signs from UK hospital admissions (2017-2021, n=95,825 cases). The approach combines HDBScan clustering with UMAP dimensionality reduction, followed by surrogate explanation methods (TabularBlimey) to interpret cluster assignments and statistical analysis of cluster characteristics. Clinicians independently validated the clusters by examining sample patients and their clinical records. The analysis revealed five distinct clusters with varying levels of consciousness, oxygen saturation, and NEWS scores. Cluster 0 showed the highest risk profile with limited consciousness and elevated NEWS scores. The integration of automatic and clinician-based explanations enabled better understanding of patient groupings, demonstrating the potential for more personalized risk scoring systems beyond traditional metrics like NEWS.

## Method Summary
The study used six vital signs (temperature, systolic BP, heart rate, SATS, respiratory rate, consciousness) from UK hospital admissions, applying UMAP for dimensionality reduction followed by HDBScan clustering. Surrogate explanations were generated using TabularBlimey with 25,000 synthetic samples per cluster to interpret decision boundaries. Clinical validation involved two clinicians independently reviewing 10 random patients from each cluster. Cluster characteristics were analyzed using NEWS scores and ICD10 code frequencies, with patients color-coded by cluster assignment in the embedding space.

## Key Results
- Five distinct patient clusters identified with varying vital sign profiles and NEWS scores
- Cluster 0 exhibited highest risk with limited consciousness and elevated NEWS scores
- Clinicians validated clusters through independent review of sample patients
- Surrogate explanations quantified each vital sign's contribution to cluster membership
- Approach demonstrates potential for personalized risk scoring beyond traditional metrics

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Combining HDBScan clustering with UMAP dimensionality reduction enables identification of clinically meaningful patient subgroups using only six vital signs.
- Mechanism: UMAP reduces high-dimensional vital sign data to a lower-dimensional embedding that preserves local and global structure, while HDBScan identifies clusters based on density variations in this embedding space, allowing detection of clusters with varying shapes and sizes that traditional clustering might miss.
- Core assumption: The six vital signs contain sufficient information to distinguish meaningful patient subtypes when properly embedded and clustered.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The approach combines HDBScan clustering with UMAP dimensionality reduction"
  - [section] "dimensionality reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)... based on the six vitals"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct comparison studies found in corpus
- Break condition: If the six vital signs lack discriminatory power for certain patient populations, or if the embedding fails to preserve clinically relevant distinctions.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Surrogate explanation methods (TabularBlimeyTreedecisiontreeexplainer) provide interpretable decision boundaries for cluster assignments, enabling clinical validation.
- Mechanism: The explainer generates synthetic samples and builds a decision tree to approximate the clustering decision boundaries, quantifying each vital sign's contribution to cluster membership and revealing the key discriminators between clusters.
- Core assumption: A surrogate decision tree can adequately approximate the non-linear decision boundaries created by HDBScan in the UMAP embedding space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "With the use of state-of-the-art explainability techniques, the identified subtypes are interpreted and assigned clinical meaning"
  - [section] "Each vital sign's contribution for the assignment of patients into each cluster was determined by generating and mutating 25,000 surrogate samples per cluster using the TabularBlimeyTreedecisiontreeexplainer"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct studies found on surrogate explanation of HDBScan clusters in medical contexts
- Break condition: If the clustering boundaries are too complex for the surrogate model to approximate accurately, or if the synthetic samples don't adequately represent the true data distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Parallel clinical evaluation by domain experts validates and provides additional context to automatically generated clusters, creating a human-in-the-loop system that increases trust and clinical utility.
- Mechanism: Clinicians independently review sample patients from each cluster, examine their full clinical records, and assess intra-cluster similarities and inter-cluster differences based on their domain knowledge, providing feedback that complements the automatic explanations.
- Core assumption: Clinicians can reliably identify clinically meaningful patterns in patient data that align with or extend the machine learning-generated clusters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "In parallel, clinicians assessed intra-cluster similarities and inter-cluster differences of the identified patient subtypes within the context of their clinical knowledge"
  - [section] "Clinical validation was conducted, independently of the previously described analysis, by providing two clinicians with ten sample patients for each cluster"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - limited examples of parallel clinician validation in similar medical ML studies found
- Break condition: If clinicians' assessments significantly diverge from the automated results without clear justification, or if the sample size is insufficient to capture cluster characteristics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different clustering algorithms and parameter settings affect the identification of clinically meaningful patient subtypes?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses HDBScan clustering with specific parameters and UMAP for dimensionality reduction. It mentions that "Future studies will increase the number of clinicians for additional feedback, increasing the acceptance of and trust in the identified patient subtypes."
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores one clustering algorithm (HDBScan) and one dimensionality reduction technique (UMAP). Different algorithms and parameters might reveal different patient subtypes or groupings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic comparison of multiple clustering algorithms (k-means, hierarchical clustering, etc.) with various parameter settings, evaluated by clinical experts for meaningfulness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the inclusion of additional clinical features beyond the six vitals affect the identified patient subtypes and their clinical utility?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Future studies and the deployment in hospital settings should consider this approach to increase fairness, accountability, and transparency. This also aids in building trust between the clinicians and machine learning systems." The paper acknowledges that "the whole analysis is based on a dataset from one hospital. Adding data from other hospitals as well as adding other features may reveal other or alter the identified patient subtypes."
- Why unresolved: The current study only uses six vitals, which may not capture the full complexity of patient conditions. Additional features could provide more nuanced subtypes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Integration of additional clinical features (medical history, lab results, medications, etc.) and evaluation of how this affects cluster formation and clinical interpretation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal balance between automated machine learning approaches and clinical expert input in the development of patient subtyping systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper emphasizes the "mutual benefit of combining machine learning techniques with clinical expertise" and discusses the integration of surrogate explanations with clinician validation. It states "twoclinicianswerepartoftheteaminordertoco-designtheprocess.Futurestudieswillincreasethenumberofcliniciansforadditionalfeedback."
- Why unresolved: While the paper integrates both approaches, it doesn't establish clear guidelines for how much weight to give each or how to optimize their interaction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies testing different ratios of automated vs. expert input, measuring outcomes like clinical acceptance, predictive accuracy, and practical utility.

## Limitations

- Unsupervised approach lacks ground truth labels for validation, making clinical validity assessment difficult beyond expert opinion
- Small sample size (10 patients per cluster) for clinical validation may not capture full cluster characteristics
- Reliance on only six vital signs within 24 hours may miss important temporal patterns or additional clinical features

## Confidence

- **High Confidence:** The clustering methodology (UMAP + HDBScan) is technically sound and appropriate for the task; the surrogate explanation approach is well-established in the literature
- **Medium Confidence:** Clinical validation results are promising but limited by small sample size; the clinical meaningfulness of clusters requires further validation with larger expert review
- **Low Confidence:** The generalizability of the five-cluster solution to other hospital settings or time periods is uncertain without external validation
