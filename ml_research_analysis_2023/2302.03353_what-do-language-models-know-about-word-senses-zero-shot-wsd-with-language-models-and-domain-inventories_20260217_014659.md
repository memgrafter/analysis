# What do Language Models know about word senses? Zero-Shot WSD with Language Models and Domain Inventories

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03353
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03353
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Language models can perform zero-shot WSD by casting it as textual entailment, significantly outperforming random baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether pre-trained language models can perform zero-shot Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD) without any task-specific training. The authors propose a novel approach that casts WSD as a textual entailment problem, where different hypotheses refer to the domains of word senses from various domain inventories. By using prompts with BERT and RoBERTa models, they show that language models have some notion of word senses, achieving significantly better performance than random baselines and sometimes approaching supervised state-of-the-art systems. The study evaluates three different domain inventories (BabelDomains, CSI, and WordNet Domains) and provides insights into the correlation between domain labeling and WSD tasks.

## Method Summary
The authors leverage the relationship between word senses and domains to convert the WSD task into a textual entailment problem. They use pre-trained BERT and RoBERTa models with prompts to predict whether a context entails a hypothesis about the domain of a word sense. The approach involves three domain inventories (BabelDomains, CSI, and WordNet Domains) to provide coarse-grained labels for word senses. The models are evaluated on WSD datasets from SemEval and Senseval competitions without any fine-tuning, using domain labeling as an intermediate step to assess the models' ability to discriminate between domains.

## Key Results
- Language models perform zero-shot WSD significantly better than random baselines across all evaluated datasets
- NLI-based models consistently outperform NSP models, with word prompts showing better performance than gloss-only prompts
- Performance varies substantially across word categories, with adjectives showing notably lower performance than nouns and verbs
- The correlation between domain labeling and WSD tasks is surprisingly poor, suggesting these tasks measure different capabilities

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: BERT/RoBERTa model -> Prompt templates -> Textual entailment model (NLI/NSP) -> Domain inventory labels
- Critical path: Convert WSD to textual entailment using prompts -> Generate contextualized representations with language model -> Predict most probable domain hypothesis using entailment model
- Design tradeoffs: The approach trades off the granularity of word senses for a more coarse-grained label space using domain inventories, reducing task complexity but potentially losing semantic nuances
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on domain labeling tasks, low correlation between domain labeling and WSD, significant performance drops on specific word categories (especially adjectives)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate the language model on the domain labeling task using different domain inventories to understand its ability to discriminate between domains
  2. Evaluate the language model on the WSD task using different prompts and domain inventories to understand its ability to perform zero-shot WSD
  3. Analyze the correlation between the domain labeling and WSD tasks to understand the relationship between the two tasks and identify potential sources of error

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of language models on WSD tasks scale with model size?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that they plan to evaluate larger language models in the future to understand the impact of scaling on sense recognition.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates BERT and RoBERTa models, and does not explore the effect of model size on WSD performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating a range of language model sizes (e.g., GPT-3, T5) on the WSD task and comparing their performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the correlation between Domain Labelling and WSD tasks change when using more diverse and representative datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note that the poor correlation between the two tasks might be due to the specific structure of WordNet glosses and the need to predict among possible labels in WSD, rather than the entire label space in Domain Labelling.
- Why unresolved: The authors only use WordNet glosses and SemEval/Senseval datasets, which may not be representative of the broader domain labeling and WSD tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the correlation between Domain Labelling and WSD tasks using more diverse and representative datasets, such as domain-labeled text from various sources and WSD datasets with different word categories.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do language models perform on WSD tasks in languages other than English?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on English language models and datasets, but WSD is a cross-lingual task.
- Why unresolved: The authors do not evaluate the performance of language models on WSD tasks in languages other than English.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the performance of language models on WSD tasks in multiple languages using multilingual datasets and models.

## Limitations

- The approach relies on the assumption that language models encode sufficient semantic knowledge about word senses and their associated domains
- The correlation between domain labeling and WSD tasks is surprisingly poor, suggesting these tasks measure different capabilities
- Performance varies substantially across word categories, with adjectives showing notably lower performance
- The paper lacks explicit comparison to recent token-level WSD models like DoLaMo or HyperLex

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The claim that language models can perform zero-shot WSD better than random baselines is well-supported by the experimental results across multiple datasets and domain inventories.

**Medium confidence**: The assertion that NLI models outperform NSP models and that word prompts work better than gloss-only prompts is supported by the results, but the analysis could be deeper.

**Low confidence**: The interpretation of the poor correlation between domain labeling and WSD tasks is speculative, with alternative explanations not thoroughly explored.

## Next Checks

1. **Prompt sensitivity analysis**: Systematically test different prompt templates and variations for each domain inventory to determine if performance improvements can be achieved through prompt engineering, particularly for challenging categories like adjectives.

2. **Cross-task correlation validation**: Conduct a more detailed analysis of where domain labeling succeeds but WSD fails (and vice versa) to identify specific error patterns and understand whether the tasks are truly measuring different capabilities or if there are systematic issues with the approach.

3. **Fine-grained performance breakdown**: Analyze performance per individual domain label across all inventories to identify which specific domains are most challenging and whether certain domain categories (e.g., technical vs. general) consistently pose difficulties for the model.
