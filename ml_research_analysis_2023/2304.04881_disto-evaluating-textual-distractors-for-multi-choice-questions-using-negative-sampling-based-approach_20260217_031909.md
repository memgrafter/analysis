# DISTO: Evaluating Textual Distractors for Multi-Choice Questions using Negative Sampling based Approach

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.04881
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04881
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: DISTO achieves 0.92 correlation with human ratings, outperforming traditional MT metrics

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces DISTO, the first learned evaluation metric for distractor generation (DG) in reading comprehension. DISTO uses a negative sampling strategy with distractor augmentation techniques to model the consistency of distractors within a given context. The proposed model takes [Q, An, D, Ar] as input and outputs a consistency score [0-1]. DISTO uses a transformer-based architecture to evaluate distractors, outperforming traditional machine translation metrics like BLEU and ROUGE.

## Method Summary
DISTO employs a transformer-based architecture (SepT and SIAM-COS-SIM) trained on both good distractors from human-created datasets and bad distractors generated through augmentation techniques. The augmentation methods include answer replication, random distractors, farthest cluster points, and BERT mask filling. The model learns to distinguish distractor quality based on semantic relevance to context rather than surface-level similarity. DISTO uses DistillRoBERTa as an encoder, processes inputs as [QUES]Q[ANS]An[DIS]D[ART]Ar, and outputs a 0-1 consistency score through a linear layer with sigmoid activation.

## Key Results
- DISTO achieves 0.92 correlation with human ratings on 500 distractors evaluated by 15 annotators on AMT
- DISTO outperforms traditional MT metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) by significant margins in correlation with human judgments
- DISTO provides different rankings for state-of-the-art DG models compared to MT metrics, revealing that models performing well on BLEU/ROUGE may generate poor distractors

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: DISTO learns semantic consistency rather than text overlap by using negative sampling with distractor augmentation
- Mechanism: The model is trained on both good distractors (from human-created datasets) and bad distractors (generated through augmentation techniques like answer replication, random distractors, farthest cluster points, and BERT mask filling). This teaches the model to distinguish distractor quality based on semantic relevance to context rather than surface-level similarity
- Core assumption: Negative sampling with carefully crafted bad distractors effectively teaches the model what makes a distractor inconsistent with context
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DISTO uses a negative sampling strategy with distractors augmentation techniques to model the consistency of distractors within a given context"
  - [section] "Our proposed model takes [Q, An, D, Ar] as an input and outputs a consistency score [0-1]. We design our model to take a single distractor in each instance... For the instances where we have good distractors, we set the score to one, and for the ones that have augmented distractors (bad distractors), we set it to zero"
  - [corpus] "We found that these techniques are very effective to modify the original distractors in a way that the new distractors share some characteristics with the original ones, but at the same time, they are sometimes less contextually relevant"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: DISTO achieves high correlation with human ratings because it captures semantic relationships rather than relying on text matching
- Mechanism: The model uses transformer-based architectures (SepT and SIAM-COS-SIM) that leverage contextual embeddings to understand semantic relationships between distractors and their context, unlike BLEU/ROUGE which only measure n-gram overlap
- Core assumption: Transformer-based semantic understanding better captures what makes a distractor good or bad than surface-level text matching
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DISTO uses a transformer-based architecture to evaluate distractors, outperforming traditional machine translation metrics like BLEU and ROUGE"
  - [section] "The results show that there is a huge gap between both of the proposed architectures compared to the baseline... we attribute this due to the ability of both architectures to model the semantics of the inputs, where the Tf-Idf vectors in the BOW are not able to get the semantic meaning"
  - [corpus] "The results demonstrated the effectiveness of DISTO since it was trained on a large set of data created by professional educators"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: DISTO provides different rankings than MT metrics because it evaluates distractor contextual relevance rather than similarity to gold answers
- Mechanism: By training on semantic consistency, DISTO identifies which distractors are truly contextually appropriate, revealing that models performing well on BLEU/ROUGE may actually generate poor distractors
- Core assumption: Models optimized for MT metrics produce distractors that are textually similar to gold answers but may lack contextual appropriateness
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DISTO ranks the performance of state-of-the-art DG models very differently from MT-based metrics, showing that MT metrics should not be used for distractor evaluation"
  - [section] "Notice that DISTO gives a completely different ranking compared to the MT results. The model that performs the best in terms of BLEU and BLEURT metrics (BDG) has the lowest DISTO result"
  - [corpus] "We can clearly see that MT metrics are providing misleading conclusions regarding DG models. Indeed, the MT metrics assign the highest score to a model that generates distractors that are not the most contextually consistent"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: [QUES]Q[ANS]An[DIS]D[ART]Ar -> DistillRoBERTa encoder -> [CLS] token representation -> Linear layer with sigmoid -> 0-1 consistency score

- Critical path:
  1. Format input as [QUES]Q[ANS]An[DIS]D[ART]Ar
  2. Pass through DistillRoBERTa encoder
  3. Extract [CLS] token representation
  4. Apply sigmoid to produce 0-1 consistency score
  5. Calculate MSE loss against ground truth (1 for good, 0 for bad)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single distractor per instance vs. multiple distractors together: The design chooses to evaluate one distractor at a time for simplicity, averaging scores for multi-distractor questions
  - Transformer choice: Distilled RoBERTa balances performance with efficiency compared to larger models
  - Negative sampling vs. other approaches: This method creates synthetic negative examples rather than relying solely on existing data

- Failure signatures:
  - Low correlation with human ratings despite high training accuracy: Indicates the model learned the wrong features or the augmentation created misleading negative examples
  - Inconsistent scores across similar distractors: Suggests instability in the scoring mechanism or insufficient training data
  - Scores that correlate strongly with distractor length or complexity rather than semantic relevance: Indicates the model is learning spurious correlations

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train DISTO on a small subset of RACE data with basic augmentation (answer replication only) and evaluate correlation with human ratings on a held-out set
  2. Compare SepT architecture against SIAM-COS-SIM on the full training corpus to validate architectural choice
  3. Evaluate DISTO on distractors generated by a simple T5 model to establish baseline performance and identify any scoring inconsistencies

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How would DISTO perform on evaluating distractors for languages other than English, and what modifications might be necessary to make it multilingual?
  - Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper mentions this as a future direction: "Second, we will work to make DISTO multilingual to support the evaluation of distractors in other languages."
  - Why unresolved: The current implementation of DISTO is only tested on English datasets. Its performance on other languages and the necessary modifications for multilingual support are not explored.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying DISTO to non-English datasets, and analysis of any required architectural or training modifications for different languages.

- Question: How would the performance of DISTO change if additional augmentation techniques were integrated, particularly those that generate grammatically incorrect distractors?
  - Basis in paper: Inferred - The paper mentions this as a future direction: "First, we plan to integrate more augmentation techniques to cover more negative cases by including, e.g., grammatical modifications."
  - Why unresolved: The current augmentation techniques assume grammatically correct distractors. The paper acknowledges uncertainty about how DISTO would handle grammatically incorrect distractors.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments incorporating grammatically incorrect distractors in the training data and evaluating DISTO's performance on these cases.

- Question: How does the computational cost of DISTO, particularly the "Farthest Point in a Cluster" augmentation technique, impact its practical application in large-scale evaluation scenarios?
  - Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper states: "Finally, we want to highlight that those two techniques are computationally expensive, especially the 'Farthest Point in a Cluster' technique."
  - Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the computational expense, it doesn't provide detailed analysis of the impact on large-scale applications or potential optimizations.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Performance benchmarks of DISTO on large datasets, including time and resource requirements, and comparisons with other evaluation methods.

## Limitations

- The augmentation techniques, while effective, may not fully capture the diverse ways distractors can be contextually inappropriate
- The human evaluation introduces potential subjectivity and variability in distractor quality assessment
- The evaluation focuses primarily on semantic consistency but may not capture other important distractor qualities like plausibility or difficulty calibration

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the core mechanism: DISTO's approach of using negative sampling with transformer architectures is technically sound and well-supported by the correlation results with human ratings
- **Medium confidence** in generalization claims: While DISTO outperforms traditional MT metrics on the tested datasets, the evaluation is limited to English reading comprehension datasets
- **Low confidence** in the augmentation technique robustness: The paper demonstrates that augmentation works for creating negative examples, but doesn't explore whether these techniques might inadvertently create false negatives or miss important edge cases in distractor quality

## Next Checks

1. Cross-dataset validation: Test DISTO's performance on distractors from different domains (scientific, historical, technical) to assess whether the model generalizes beyond the RACE and similar reading comprehension datasets used in training

2. Ablation study of augmentation techniques: Systematically evaluate each negative sampling technique's contribution by training DISTO with different combinations of augmentation methods, and test whether removing any single technique significantly impacts performance

3. Human expert validation: Conduct a more rigorous evaluation with subject matter experts rather than crowdworkers to establish a gold standard correlation, and test whether DISTO's rankings align with expert consensus across diverse distractor types
