# MParrotTTS: Multilingual Multi-speaker Text to Speech Synthesis in Low Resource Setting

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.11926
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11926
- Reference count: 0
- MParrotTTS achieves MOS scores between 3.05-4.17 on seen speakers and 3.05-4.07 on unseen speakers with only 5 hours of paired data per language.

## Executive Summary
MParrotTTS introduces a modular, multilingual text-to-speech system that operates effectively with minimal supervised training data. The framework leverages a pre-trained mHuBERT encoder to extract language-agnostic discrete speech units, which are then mapped from text using a non-autoregressive encoder and reconstructed into high-quality audio by a HiFiGAN decoder. Trained on just 5 hours of paired data per language across six languages (Hindi, Marathi, German, French, English, Spanish), the system outperforms fully supervised baselines in naturalness and speaker similarity while supporting cross-lingual synthesis with preserved native accents.

## Method Summary
MParrotTTS employs a modular pipeline where mHuBERT extracts discrete sound units from audio, a non-autoregressive text encoder maps text to these units, and a HiFiGAN decoder reconstructs high-quality speech. The system trains in two phases: first learning discrete units from unlabeled speech data, then mapping text to units using paired data. The speech decoder trains on unpaired audio to learn speaker characteristics. The framework uses character tokens for Indic languages and phonemes for European languages, achieving strong performance across seen and unseen speakers with minimal paired data.

## Key Results
- Achieved naturalness MOS scores of 3.05-4.17 on seen speakers and 3.05-4.07 on unseen speakers
- Outperformed fully supervised FastSpeech2 and Meta-TTS models
- Preserved speaker identity across languages with >90% native accent identification in cross-lingual synthesis

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The modularized training pipeline enables language-agnostic speech content extraction using mHuBERT, which facilitates effective cross-lingual synthesis.
- Mechanism: By first extracting language-independent discrete speech units with a pre-trained mHuBERT encoder, the model learns to disentangle speaker identity and acoustic variations from linguistic content. This allows the text encoder to map input text into the same discrete unit space, regardless of the language.
- Core assumption: The discrete sound units produced by mHuBERT are language-agnostic and contain only the essential speech content without language-specific information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Beneﬁting from a modularized training paradigm exploiting self-supervised speech representations, MParrotTTS adapts to a new language with minimal supervised data"
  - [section 2.2]: "A signiﬁcant advantage of using HuBERT-based embeddings is that they ignore speaker and acoustic information and encode only the speech content [13]"
- Break condition: If the discrete units are not truly language-agnostic, the cross-lingual synthesis would fail because the text encoder cannot map text from one language into the same unit space as audio from another.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The use of minimal paired data (5 hours per language) is sufficient because the speech decoder learns from unpaired audio data.
- Mechanism: The speech decoder is trained on all available speech-only data from multiple speakers, learning to reconstruct high-quality audio from discrete sound units. This allows the model to generalize speaker characteristics without requiring paired text-speech data for each speaker.
- Core assumption: The speech decoder can effectively learn speaker identity and acoustic characteristics from unpaired audio data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "using only a small fraction of supervised training data"
  - [section 3.4]: "We train a multi-speaker speech-decoder using speech-only data from all 12 speakers since training this model doesn't require any paired data"
- Break condition: If the speech decoder cannot effectively learn speaker characteristics from unpaired data, the model would fail to preserve speaker identity across languages.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The choice of text representation (character vs. phoneme) significantly impacts performance across different language families.
- Mechanism: Different languages have different optimal text representations - Indic languages perform better with character tokens while European languages benefit from phoneme representations. This is likely because phonemes provide better pronunciation guidance for languages with complex orthography.
- Core assumption: The effectiveness of text representation varies systematically across language families based on their orthographic complexity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4]: "For Indic languages (Hindi and Marathi), representing the text in characters (CTE) performs better than phonemes (PTE). Whereas for English and the European Languages (German, French, and Spanish), phoneme representation PTE serves better"
- Break condition: If the relationship between language family and optimal text representation doesn't hold, performance would degrade when using the wrong representation for a given language.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Self-supervised learning in speech representation
  - Why needed here: The mHuBERT encoder is pre-trained on large amounts of unlabeled speech data, which allows it to extract meaningful speech units without requiring language-specific labeled data.
  - Quick check question: How does the mHuBERT encoder handle languages it hasn't seen during pre-training?

- Concept: Disentanglement of content and speaker information
  - Why needed here: The model needs to separate linguistic content from speaker identity to enable cross-lingual synthesis where the same speaker can speak different languages.
  - Quick check question: What would happen if the model didn't effectively disentangle speaker information from content?

- Concept: Non-autoregressive sequence modeling
  - Why needed here: The text encoder uses a non-autoregressive architecture to predict all sound units simultaneously, which improves inference speed and reduces error propagation.
  - Quick check question: How does the non-autoregressive approach differ from autoregressive models in terms of training and inference?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Speech encoder (mHuBERT) -> Text encoder (NAR transformer) -> Speech decoder (HiFiGAN)
- Critical path: Text input -> Text encoder -> Discrete sound units -> Speech decoder -> Audio output
- Design tradeoffs: Using minimal paired data reduces training cost but requires effective use of unpaired data for the speech decoder. The choice of text representation affects performance across languages.
- Failure signatures: Poor naturalness scores indicate issues with the text encoder or speech decoder. Poor speaker similarity indicates failure to preserve speaker identity. Cross-lingual synthesis failure suggests issues with the speech encoder's language-agnostic representations.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train with minimal paired data (5 hours per language) and evaluate naturalness MOS on seen speakers.
  2. Test cross-lingual synthesis by synthesizing speech in one language using a speaker from another language.
  3. Compare performance using character vs. phoneme text representations for different language families.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the duration predictor in MParrotTTS be made speaker-adaptive to overcome the limitation of leaning towards the style of a single seen speaker?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly mentions this as a limitation and states "Future research will investigate methods to obtain speaker adaptive duration prediction or allow levers to control the duration prediction."
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this as a limitation but does not provide any specific solutions or experimental results for addressing it.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results demonstrating improved cross-speaker duration prediction performance, along with ablation studies showing the impact of different speaker adaptation techniques on duration prediction accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would fine-tuning the HuBERT-based embeddings using raw audio from a diverse set of languages (South Asian, Latin, English, etc.) affect the quality of quantized sound units for multilingual TTS?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "Another direction of future exploration would be to fine-tune self-supervised HuBert-based embeddings using the raw audio from a diverse set of languages (South Asian, Latin, English, etc.) to have a more comprehensive quantized code set of sound units."
- Why unresolved: The current self-supervised backbone is trained on a limited set of languages, and the paper suggests this as a future direction without providing experimental results.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative MOS scores and speaker similarity metrics between the current approach and a version with fine-tuned embeddings, along with analysis of the diversity and coverage of the resulting sound unit inventory.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How effective would MParrotTTS be when applied to other resource-scarce, less studied, and less privileged languages beyond the six languages evaluated in this paper?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions "Another area of future work will be releasing an open-source, multilingual TTS model, allowing the community to apply the investigated ideas to other resource-scarce, less studied, and less privileged languages."
- Why unresolved: The current evaluation is limited to six languages, and the paper suggests releasing the model for broader community evaluation but does not provide results for other languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: MOS scores and speaker similarity metrics for additional languages, particularly those from different language families and with varying amounts of available training data, along with analysis of the model's generalization capabilities across diverse linguistic contexts.

## Limitations

- Limited language diversity in evaluation, focusing primarily on Indo-European languages without testing typologically diverse languages like tone languages or those with complex morphology
- Small sample size (10 participants) for cross-lingual accent preservation evaluation reduces statistical robustness of native accent identification claims
- Assumes 5 hours of paired data is sufficient without systematically exploring minimum data requirements or degradation patterns with less data

## Confidence

**High confidence** in core naturalness claims (MOS 3.05-4.17) due to consistent experimental results across multiple languages and speakers using established modular architecture.

**Medium confidence** in cross-lingual synthesis accent preservation claims due to small participant sample size (n=10) and limited language pair testing, though >90% native accent identification is promising.

**Low confidence** in scalability to truly low-resource languages with non-Latin scripts, complex morphology, or tones since evaluation focuses on languages with relatively straightforward orthographic-to-phonetic mappings.

## Next Checks

1. Test MParrotTTS on typologically diverse languages including tone languages (Mandarin Chinese, Vietnamese) and languages with complex morphology (Turkish, Finnish) to verify language-agnostic unit extraction across different language families.

2. Conduct systematic experiments varying paired training data amounts (1 hour, 5 hours, 10 hours per language) to identify minimum effective training requirements and degradation patterns with less data.

3. Expand cross-lingual evaluation to include more language pairs and speakers with larger participant pool (n > 50) and formal statistical analysis to validate native accent identification claims.
