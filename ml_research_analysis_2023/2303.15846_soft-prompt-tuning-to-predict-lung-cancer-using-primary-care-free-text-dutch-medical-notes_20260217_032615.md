# Soft-prompt tuning to predict lung cancer using primary care free-text Dutch medical notes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15846
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15846
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Soft-prompt tuning outperforms standard fine-tuning for lung cancer prediction from Dutch primary care notes

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates soft-prompt tuning for early lung cancer prediction using Dutch primary care free-text medical notes. The study compares soft-prompt tuning with standard fine-tuning using two Dutch PLMs (RobBERT and MedRoBERTa.nl) and static FastText embeddings across varying class imbalance ratios (balanced, 1:10, 1:100, 1:250) and few-shot learning scenarios (2-128 patients). Results show that soft-prompt tuning consistently outperforms standard fine-tuning in AUROC and AUPRC metrics, though calibration (Brier score) shows no clear trend. PLMs demonstrate better discrimination but worse calibration than FastText embeddings as class imbalance increases. In few-shot scenarios, FastText performs better with 128 patients while PLMs excel with fewer training examples. Per-patient predictions using minimum probability aggregation improve discrimination but sacrifice calibration compared to per-note predictions.

## Method Summary
The study employs soft-prompt tuning as an alternative to standard fine-tuning for Dutch language models RobBERT and MedRoBERTa.nl, alongside static FastText embeddings. Soft-prompt tuning involves inserting trainable embeddings into the input sequence before the PLM layers, allowing task-specific adaptation without modifying the underlying model weights. The methodology trains models on Dutch primary care medical notes to predict lung cancer, testing across multiple class imbalance scenarios (from balanced to 1:250) and few-shot learning settings (2-128 patients). Per-note predictions are aggregated to per-patient predictions using a minimum probability approach. Models are evaluated using AUROC, AUPRC, and Brier score metrics to assess both discrimination and calibration performance.

## Key Results
- Soft-prompt tuning consistently outperforms standard fine-tuning in AUROC and AUPRC across all class imbalance levels
- PLMs achieve better discrimination (higher AUROC/AUPRC) but worse calibration (Brier score) than FastText embeddings as class imbalance increases
- FastText embeddings outperform PLMs when trained on 128 patients in few-shot scenarios, but PLMs perform better with fewer training examples
- Per-patient predictions using minimum probability aggregation improve AUROC and AUPRC compared to per-note predictions, but at the cost of model calibration

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Soft-prompt tuning enables task-specific adaptation without modifying PLM weights, making it more parameter-efficient than full fine-tuning. By inserting trainable embeddings into the input sequence, the model can learn task-relevant representations while preserving the PLM's general language understanding capabilities. This approach is particularly effective for medical text classification where labeled data may be limited and class imbalance is common. The minimum probability aggregation for per-patient predictions captures the most conservative estimate of cancer risk across multiple notes, improving discrimination by focusing on the highest-risk evidence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How can soft-prompt tuning be combined with static word embedding models to achieve both high discrimination and calibration in imbalanced medical text classification tasks? The authors explicitly state this as future work, noting current results show PLMs achieve better discrimination but worse calibration than static embeddings with no clear solution for combining their strengths.

**Open Question 2**: What aggregation methods beyond the minimum probability approach can improve both discrimination and calibration in per-patient predictions from per-note classifications? The paper found minimum probability aggregation consistently improves AUROC and AUPRC at the expense of model calibration, suggesting better strategies may exist.

**Open Question 3**: What training strategies can stabilize predictions and reduce randomness in few-shot learning scenarios when training on small numbers of patients? The study observed oscillations in performance metrics across different training set sizes, indicating instability that needs addressing.
