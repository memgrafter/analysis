# Bkd-FedGNN: A Benchmark for Classification Backdoor Attacks on Federated Graph Neural Network

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10351
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10351
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Comprehensive benchmark for classification backdoor attacks on Federated Graph Neural Networks across 13 datasets and 13 critical factors, revealing that node-level tasks are more vulnerable than graph-level tasks with most attacks achieving >90% success rates

## Executive Summary
This paper presents Bkd-FedGNN, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating classification backdoor attacks on Federated Graph Neural Networks (FedGNN). The research systematically decomposes graph backdoor attacks into trigger generation and injection steps, extending them to node-level federated settings to cover both node-level and graph-level classification tasks. Through extensive experiments across 13 benchmark datasets and 13 critical factors including data distribution, attacker numbers, attack timing, trigger sizes/types/positions, and poisoning rates, the study evaluates over 8,000 individual tests to provide comprehensive insights into FedGNN vulnerabilities.

## Method Summary
The benchmark framework implements federated graph backdoor attacks by first generating triggers using various methods (Renyi, WS, BA, GTA, UGBA) and then injecting them into training data through different techniques (Random, Degree, Cluster). The framework supports both node-level and graph-level classification tasks using GCN, GAT, and GraphSage models within a MPNN framework. Experiments are conducted using PyTorch on GPU-enabled servers with 13 datasets from 6 domains, measuring average clean accuracy (ACC), attack success rate (ASR), and transferred attack success rate (TASR) across different federated learning scenarios.

## Key Results
- Node-level tasks are more vulnerable to backdoor attacks than graph-level tasks, with most attacks achieving >90% success rates on malicious clients
- Universal triggers (ER model) are more effective for graph-level tasks, while adaptive triggers are more effective for node-level tasks
- Normal clients can be compromised through transferred attacks with up to 61.86% TASR, highlighting vulnerabilities in federated learning systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Node-level FedGNN tasks are more vulnerable to backdoor attacks than graph-level tasks at small trigger sizes.
- Mechanism: Node-level tasks involve shared nodes across clients, allowing malicious clients to inject triggers that affect normal clients through overlapping edges. The smaller the trigger size, the more easily it can be injected without detection.
- Core assumption: Trigger injection into shared nodes directly impacts the global model's behavior on normal clients.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Results show that node-level tasks are more vulnerable than graph-level tasks, with most attacks achieving >90% success rates on malicious clients."
  - [section]: "The node-level task exhibits higher vulnerability to attacks compared to the graph-level task at a relatively small trigger size."
- Break condition: If overlapping rates are zero or if trigger injection is restricted to non-overlapping nodes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Universal triggers (e.g., ER model-generated) are more effective for graph-level tasks, while adaptive triggers are more effective for node-level tasks.
- Mechanism: Universal triggers are pre-generated and can be applied across different graphs, making them suitable for graph-level tasks where each client has distinct graphs. Adaptive triggers are tailored to specific graphs, making them more effective for node-level tasks where nodes are shared across clients.
- Core assumption: The effectiveness of trigger types depends on the task level and the nature of data distribution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "In the node-level task, the adaptive graph backdoor attack demonstrates a higher ASR and TASR across the majority of datasets, while the universal graph backdoor attack graph-level datasets exhibits increased ASR and TASR."
  - [section]: "In the node-level task, the adaptive trigger demonstrates a higher ASR on most models. Conversely, in the graph-level task, the universal trigger exhibits higher ASR."
- Break condition: If the trigger generation method does not align with the task's data distribution characteristics.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Increasing the number of malicious attackers (NMA) increases the ASR for node-level tasks but decreases it for graph-level tasks.
- Mechanism: In node-level tasks, more malicious clients increase the probability of trigger injection into shared nodes, enhancing the attack's effectiveness. In graph-level tasks, more malicious clients may dilute the impact of individual triggers due to the diversity of graphs.
- Core assumption: The relationship between NMA and ASR is task-dependent, influenced by the structure of data distribution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Number of Malicious Attackers (NMA). For node-level tasks, an increase in NMA leads to an increase in ASR for both GCN and GAT models. Conversely, an increase in NMA results in a decrease in ASR for both GraphSage. Concerning graph-level tasks, the ASR demonstrates an increase with the increase of NMA in the case of GAT and GraphSage. However, in the scenario of GCN, the ASR shows a decrease with the increase of NMA."
  - [section]: "Number of Malicious Attackers (NMA). For node-level tasks, an increase in NMA leads to an increase in ASR for both GCN and GAT models. Conversely, an increase in NMA results in a decrease in ASR for both GraphSage."
- Break condition: If the model's aggregation mechanism mitigates the influence of multiple malicious clients.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Trigger Generation (Renyi, WS, BA, GTA, UGBA) -> Trigger Injection (Random, Degree, Cluster) -> Federated Training (FedAvg) -> Evaluation (ACC, ASR, TASR)

- Critical path:
  1. Generate triggers using specified methods
  2. Inject triggers into training data of malicious clients
  3. Conduct federated training with both malicious and normal clients
  4. Evaluate the model's performance on clean and poisoned data

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Trigger size vs. detectability: Smaller triggers are less detectable but may be less effective
  - Trigger type (universal vs. adaptive): Universal triggers are easier to generate but may be less effective; adaptive triggers are more effective but require more computation
  - Attack timing: Earlier attacks may have more impact but are also more likely to be detected

- Failure signatures:
  - Low ASR despite high trigger injection: Indicates that the trigger generation or injection method is ineffective
  - High ACC but low ASR: Suggests that the model is robust to backdoor attacks
  - TASR significantly lower than ASR: Implies that the attack is not transferring effectively to normal clients

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test the effectiveness of different trigger generation methods (e.g., Renyi vs. GTA) on a simple node-level dataset
  2. Vary the trigger size and observe its impact on ASR and TASR
  3. Experiment with different attack timings to determine the optimal moment for trigger injection

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we design more effective defenses against transferred backdoor attacks in FedGNN?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that normal clients can be compromised through transferred attacks (up to 61.86% TASR), highlighting vulnerabilities in federated learning systems.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the vulnerability but does not explore defense mechanisms against transferred attacks specifically.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different defense strategies (e.g., robust aggregation, anomaly detection, model inspection) specifically targeting transferred attacks in FedGNN settings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal collaboration strategy for multiple malicious attackers to maximize attack effectiveness in FedGNN?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "the majority of malicious attackers operate independently during the attack process, neglecting the potential benefits of collaboration" and suggests investigating collaboration to enhance attack performance.
- Why unresolved: Current research focuses on independent malicious clients; coordinated strategies remain unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments showing attack success rates between independent and coordinated malicious clients under various coordination strategies (synchronous attacks, shared trigger patterns, strategic timing).

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different federated learning algorithms (beyond FedAvg) affect the vulnerability to backdoor attacks in FedGNN?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses FedAvg as the representative federated algorithm but acknowledges "diverse scenarios involving distributed graphs" and the need to explore different federated optimization approaches.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses on FedAvg, leaving open how alternative federated learning algorithms might change attack effectiveness or defense capabilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing the same backdoor attacks across different federated learning algorithms (e.g., FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedNova) to measure variations in ACC, ASR, and TASR.

## Limitations

- Trigger generation algorithm hyperparameter settings are not fully detailed, affecting reproducibility
- Federated optimization protocol implementation details (gradient clipping, weight averaging) are not explicitly specified
- Transferability analysis assumes homogeneous model architectures across clients, which may not reflect real-world heterogeneity

## Confidence

- High Confidence: Task vulnerability comparisons (node-level vs graph-level), attack success rate measurements, and dataset benchmark results
- Medium Confidence: Transferability findings and the relationship between attack timing and effectiveness
- Low Confidence: Some specific hyperparameter choices and their impact on attack performance
