# "I'm" Lost in Translation: Pronoun Missteps in Crowdsourced Data Sets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.13557
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13557
- Reference count: 0
- We found that masculine pronoun biases were present overall, even though plurality in language was accounted for in other ways.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates gender biases in pronoun translations between English and Japanese within the Tatoeba crowdsourced database. Using automated NLP tools and statistical analysis, the authors detected systematic masculine biases in pronoun translation, especially when translating ambiguous pronouns. They highlight that cultural and structural differences between languages can amplify bias and propose a placeholder-based approach to preserve pronoun plurality without inflating data size or requiring retraining.

## Method Summary
The study used the Tatoeba database (December 24, 2022 version) and applied automated NLP analysis with nltk for English and Janome for Japanese. Pronoun extraction and categorization (masculine, feminine, ambiguous) were followed by statistical testing (Chi-square) and confusion matrix visualization to detect translation mismatches and biases. The authors also propose using standardized pronoun tags or placeholders as a mitigation strategy.

## Key Results
- Masculine pronoun biases were present overall in crowdsourced translations, even after accounting for plurality in language.
- Significant translation mismatches were found, particularly for ambiguous pronouns.
- Chi-square tests revealed a statistically significant relationship between pronoun gender categories and their translations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Crowdsourced pronoun translations can embed systematic masculine biases through repeated user-generated translation patterns.
- Mechanism: Translators default to masculine forms when faced with ambiguous or neutral pronouns, reinforcing male-as-default assumptions in the target language.
- Core assumption: Contributors are not trained in gender-inclusive translation and rely on cultural defaults when uncertainty arises.

- Break condition: If contributors receive gender-inclusive training or use explicit pronoun placeholders, the default masculine bias would be reduced.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Translation mismatches between English and Japanese pronouns occur because of structural differences in how pronouns are used and omitted in each language.
- Mechanism: Japanese often omits pronouns that are explicit in English; when omitted, translators may substitute a masculine form based on sentence context, creating mismatches.
- Core assumption: Structural differences in pronoun usage are not automatically accounted for in crowdsourced translation workflows.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We found that masculine pronoun biases were present overall, even though plurality in language was accounted for in other ways."
  - [section]: "A significant number of mismatching pronoun translations were found, especially for ambiguous pronouns."
  - [corpus]: Weak; corpus contains MT bias studies but limited Japanese-specific structural omission evidence.
- Break condition: If the translation system explicitly tags pronouns and provides context-aware substitution rules, mismatches would decrease.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: A placeholder-based approach can preserve pronoun plurality without inflating database size or requiring contributor retraining.
- Mechanism: Replacing pronouns with a standardized tag (e.g., `[p]`) in source sentences allows downstream systems to map to gender-appropriate lists without duplicating sentences.
- Core assumption: Contributors can be instructed to use placeholders instead of pronouns, and maintainers can manage mapping lists efficiently.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We suggest another approach, drawing from similar efforts in NLP data sets: standardized labels or tags."
  - [section]: "A pronoun tag or placeholder would take the place of the text... More like a variable, it could refer to a list of pronouns appropriate for the sentence."
  - [corpus]: Weak; corpus shows tagging in other NLP datasets but not pronoun-specific placeholder strategies.
- Break condition: If placeholder parsing is not supported by the data ingestion pipeline, the approach fails.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Data ingestion -> Tokenization -> Pronoun extraction -> Translation alignment -> Bias detection -> Placeholder substitution
- Critical path:
  1. Load parallel English/Japanese sentence pairs.
  2. Tokenize and POS-tag each language.
  3. Extract and categorize pronouns.
  4. Align pronouns across languages and detect mismatches.
  5. Compute bias statistics (Chi-square, confusion matrix).
  6. Apply placeholder substitution if needed.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Full pronoun replacement with placeholders increases data structure complexity but reduces bias.
  - Keeping plain text preserves simplicity but risks perpetuating mistranslations.
  - Using gendered tags improves clarity but requires contributor compliance.
- Failure signatures:
  - High mismatch rates in confusion matrix diagonals indicate translation problems.
  - Disproportionate masculine pronoun counts vs. feminine/ambiguous suggest bias.
  - Low pronoun alignment scores indicate tokenization or tagging errors.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run pronoun extraction on a small sample and manually verify categories.
  2. Compute Chi-square bias test on a subset to confirm statistical pipeline works.
  3. Apply placeholder substitution to a few sentences and verify mapping consistency.
