# Inverse Reinforcement Learning with the Average Reward Criterion
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.14608
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14608
- Reference count: 40
- This paper proposes a framework for inverse reinforcement learning using the average reward criterion, achieving O(1/ε²) convergence for policy and reward recovery.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) in continuous state and action spaces using the average reward criterion. The authors develop Stochastic Policy Mirror Descent (SPMD) for solving average-reward Markov decision processes, which serves as the foundation for their Inverse Policy Mirror Descent (IPMD) algorithm. IPMD simultaneously recovers both the expert's policy and the underlying reward function from demonstrations. The method is theoretically grounded with finite-time convergence guarantees and empirically validated on MuJoCo robotics tasks, where it demonstrates superior performance compared to existing IRL approaches.

## Method Summary
The framework consists of two main components: SPMD for average-reward RL and IPMD for IRL. SPMD operates by maintaining a stochastic policy that is updated through mirror descent steps using gradient estimates from sampled trajectories. For average-reward MDPs, the algorithm maintains both a policy and a value function estimate, with updates performed in an alternating fashion. IPMD builds on this by treating the IRL problem as a bi-level optimization, where the inner loop uses SPMD to evaluate candidate reward functions against the expert demonstrations, while the outer loop updates the reward function parameters to minimize the discrepancy between the expert policy and the learned policy.

## Key Results
- SPMD achieves O(1/ε) convergence for specific function approximations and O(1/ε²) for general cases in average-reward MDPs
- IPMD recovers both policy and reward functions with O(1/ε²) convergence rate
- On MuJoCo benchmarks, IPMD outperforms existing IRL methods in both task performance and reward recovery accuracy
- Theoretical analysis provides finite-time guarantees for both RL and IRL components

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works by leveraging the structure of average-reward MDPs where the optimal policy is independent of the initial state. The mirror descent framework provides stability in the stochastic setting by maintaining the policy in a distribution space that respects the geometry of the problem. For IRL, the key insight is that the average reward criterion allows for a natural decomposition between policy evaluation (handled by SPMD) and reward function optimization (handled by the outer loop of IPMD).

## Foundational Learning
- Average-reward MDPs: These differ from discounted MDPs by focusing on long-term average performance rather than cumulative discounted rewards. Needed because many real-world tasks have no natural discount factor and require steady-state analysis.
- Mirror descent in policy optimization: Uses Bregman divergences to update policies in a geometry-aware manner. Needed to ensure stability when updating stochastic policies in continuous spaces.
- Bi-level optimization in IRL: The inner loop evaluates policies under candidate rewards while the outer loop updates reward parameters. Needed to handle the non-convex nature of the IRL problem where both policy and reward are unknown.

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: SPMD -> IPMD -> MuJoCo evaluation
- Critical path: Policy sampling -> Reward evaluation -> Gradient computation -> Policy update -> Reward update
- Design tradeoffs: The choice between specific and general function approximations affects convergence rates (O(1/ε) vs O(1/ε²)). The paper opts for general cases to ensure broader applicability.
- Failure signatures: Poor convergence may indicate violation of ergodicity assumptions or mismatch between function approximation capacity and problem complexity.
- First experiments: 1) Verify SPMD convergence on simple average-reward MDPs with known solutions, 2) Test IPMD on synthetic reward functions with ground truth, 3) Compare IPMD against baseline IRL methods on a simple MuJoCo task.

## Limitations
- Theoretical bounds assume specific function approximation classes that may not hold in practice
- Practical implementation details are not thoroughly explored, particularly for high-dimensional state spaces
- Ergodicity assumptions may not hold for all MDPs, potentially limiting applicability
- Performance depends heavily on the choice of function approximation and optimization parameters
