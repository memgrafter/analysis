# DiTTO: A Feature Representation Imitation Approach for Improving Cross-Lingual Transfer

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.02357
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02357
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: Achieves up to 23.05% relative gains on XNLI and 19.72% on AmNLI for zero-shot cross-lingual transfer

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of zero-shot cross-lingual transfer, where multilingual models degrade when transferring knowledge from a source language to target languages, especially for low-resource or typologically distant languages. The authors propose DiTTO (Directed adversarial Transition of Target language using sharpness aware Optimization), which combines domain-adversarial training with sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) to align target language features with source language features without requiring labeled data in target languages. Experiments on XNLI, MARC, and AmNLI datasets show significant improvements over standard zero-shot fine-tuning, particularly for distant, low-resource, and unseen languages.

## Method Summary
DiTTO jointly reduces feature incongruity between source and target languages while improving generalization capabilities of pre-trained multilingual transformers. The method combines domain-adversarial training with sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) to align target language features with source language features without requiring labeled data in target languages. It uses mBERT or XLM-R as backbone, trains language-specific discriminators to distinguish source from target features, and optimizes using both task-specific classification loss and language discrimination loss. The method includes a performance-based prior distribution that guides sampling of target languages during training, focusing more on languages with larger performance gaps between source and target.

## Key Results
- DiTTO achieves relative gains of up to 23.05% on XNLI, 19.72% on AmNLI, and 15.40% on MARC datasets
- Significant improvements on distant, low-resource, and unseen languages compared to baseline zero-shot fine-tuning
- Maintains strong performance even in few-shot settings
- Performance-based prior distribution for target language sampling provides better gains on selected languages compared to uniform sampling

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Joint adversarial adaptation of multiple target languages reduces feature incongruity between source and target languages.
- Mechanism: DiTTO performs adversarial training where multiple language discriminators force the encoder to produce domain-invariant features across all target languages, mimicking the source language feature distribution.
- Core assumption: Language incongruency is the primary bottleneck for zero-shot cross-lingual transfer.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DiTTO (Directed adversarial Transition of Target language using sharpness aware Optimization), a method that jointly reduces feature incongruity between source and target languages"
  - [section 3] "Similar to Deshpande et al. (2022), in our experiments, we also observe that once MMTs are fine-tuned on source languages, there is an incongruity between the features of the source and target languages"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers focus on alignment but not specifically on multi-target adversarial adaptation
- Break condition: If target languages have vastly different feature distributions that cannot be aligned through adversarial training, or if the model cannot balance adaptation across multiple languages simultaneously.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) improves generalization by finding flatter minima that better transfer across languages.
- Mechanism: SAM minimizes both training loss and its sharpness by optimizing over a neighborhood around each parameter, leading to solutions that generalize better to unseen target languages.
- Core assumption: Flatter minima in the loss landscape correlate with better cross-lingual generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DiTTO combines domain-adversarial training with sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) to align target language features with source language features"
  - [section 3] "SAM has been used to improve the generalization of language models (Bahri et al., 2022) and vision transformers (Chen et al., 2021) by smoothing the loss landscape for various adversarial tasks"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - SAM is mentioned in related works but not specifically for cross-lingual transfer
- Break condition: If the loss landscape is too complex or if adversarial training creates instability that SAM cannot mitigate.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Prior distribution over target languages based on baseline performance guides the adversarial training to focus on harder-to-transfer languages.
- Mechanism: Languages with larger performance gaps between source and target get higher sampling probability, allowing the model to prioritize adaptation where it's most needed.
- Core assumption: Uneven focus on target languages based on transfer difficulty improves overall performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5] "In the vanilla setup, we want to assign a higher probability to those target languages with a lower zero-shot performance from the Baseline method"
  - [section 7.3] "In the vanilla DiTTO, where languages with lower zero-shot performance have a higher likelihood during sampling, provides better gains on these selected languages compared to the DiTTO (UNF)"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - adaptive sampling is discussed in related works but not specifically for language adaptation
- Break condition: If the prior distribution becomes too skewed or if it leads to neglecting languages that need some adaptation.

  - Why needed here: To jointly optimize task performance and feature alignment across multiple target languages

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Encoder (MMT) -> Task classifier -> Language discriminators -> SAM optimizer -> Prior distribution
- Critical path:
  1. Sample target language based on prior distribution
  2. Compute classification loss on source language data
  3. Apply SAM to find optimal parameters
  4. Update language discriminator to distinguish domains
  5. Update encoder to confuse language discriminator
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single vs multiple target language adaptation: Multiple provides better overall performance but requires more computational resources
  - Uniform vs performance-based sampling: Performance-based focuses on harder languages but may neglect easier ones
  - SAM vs standard optimization: SAM improves stability but increases computation
- Failure signatures:
  - Negative relative gains on some languages (indicates instability in adversarial training)
  - Inconsistent performance across different target languages (indicates poor balance in multi-target adaptation)
  - No improvement over baseline (indicates failure in feature alignment or optimization)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation study: Run DiTTO without SAM to measure its contribution to stability and performance
  2. Single language adaptation: Run DiTTO with only one target language to verify multi-target benefits
  3. Uniform sampling: Run DiTTO with uniform target language sampling to validate performance-based prior effectiveness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does DiTTO perform when applied to other pre-trained models beyond mBERT and XLM-R, such as mT5 or other multilingual transformers?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions DiTTO could be integrated with adapters to further improve performance, suggesting potential applicability to other models.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only evaluated DiTTO on mBERT and XLM-R, leaving its performance on other models unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing DiTTO on a variety of multilingual transformers and adapters, comparing performance across models and tasks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of varying the neighborhood size parameter ρ in SAM on DiTTO's cross-lingual transfer performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions ρ is the size of the neighborhood in SAM optimization but does not explore its sensitivity or optimal value.
- Why unresolved: The experiments used a fixed ρ value without investigating how different values affect performance or stability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with multiple ρ values and analyzing the trade-off between performance gains and training stability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does DiTTO's performance scale with increasing numbers of target languages, particularly in extremely multilingual scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper evaluates on datasets with 5-14 target languages but doesn't explore performance limits with larger numbers of languages.
- Why unresolved: The experiments used moderate numbers of languages, leaving questions about scalability and diminishing returns unanswered.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing DiTTO on datasets with progressively larger numbers of target languages (e.g., 20, 50, 100+) and measuring performance trends and computational costs.

## Limitations
- Architecture specifics unclear: Critical implementation details like exact hyperparameter values and optimizer configurations are not fully specified
- Generalizability concerns: All experiments use English as source language and focus on NLI-style tasks
- Computational overhead: Requires training multiple language discriminators and performing SAM optimization, increasing training time
