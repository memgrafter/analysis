# Adopting Two Supervisors for Efficient Use of Large-Scale Remote Deep Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.02654
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02654
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: BiSupervised architecture saves up to 50% of remote prediction costs while maintaining or improving system-level accuracy

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces BiSupervised, a novel architecture that combines small, local models with large remote ones to reduce costs and latency in systems relying on large-scale Deep Neural Networks (DNNs). The key idea is to use a local model for initial predictions and a supervisor to identify inputs that can be trusted, while only invoking the remote model for difficult inputs. The system also includes a second supervisor to monitor the remote predictions and identify inputs for which not even these can be trusted. The evaluation on four diverse case studies (IMDB, Github issues, Imagenet, SQuADv2) shows that BiSupervised can save up to 50% of remote prediction costs while maintaining a comparable system-level accuracy. In two case studies, BiSupervised even achieved an accuracy exceeding the one of the large-scale remote models, due to complementarities in the local and remote models' capabilities.

## Method Summary
BiSupervised architecture uses two supervisors to efficiently combine small, local models with large remote ones. The first supervisor evaluates local predictions and only invokes the remote model when the local prediction cannot be trusted. The second supervisor then evaluates remote predictions and raises exceptions for highly uncertain results. This two-level supervision system aims to reduce costs and latency while maintaining or improving system-level accuracy. The architecture was evaluated on four diverse case studies: IMDB movie review sentiment classification, Github issue triaging, Imagenet image classification, and SQuADv2 free-text question answering.

## Key Results
- BiSupervised saves up to 50% of remote prediction costs while maintaining comparable system-level accuracy
- In two case studies, BiSupervised achieved accuracy exceeding that of large-scale remote models due to complementarities between local and remote model capabilities
- Supervised performance of BiSupervised is similar or exceeds that of locally supervised models in the majority of tested configurations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Local surrogate models can reliably predict a large fraction of inputs with high confidence, allowing cost savings by avoiding remote predictions.
- Mechanism: The 1st level supervisor quantifies prediction confidence (e.g., using softmax values or surprise adequacy). If confidence exceeds a threshold, the prediction is trusted and no remote call is made.
- Core assumption: Inputs that are easy for the local model to classify are also common in the operational data distribution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A DNN supervisor monitors said prediction process and identifies easy inputs for which the local prediction can be trusted."
  - [section] "The local model's objective is to make high-confidence predictions for as many inputs as possible in a very constrained environment."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence for local supervisor performance; corpus neighbors focus on different DNN problems.
- Break condition: If the operational data distribution shifts to include many inputs that are hard for the local model, the cost savings drop sharply.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Complementarity between local and remote model capabilities can yield higher system accuracy than the remote model alone.
- Mechanism: When the local model correctly predicts inputs that the remote model would misclassify, and the supervisor trusts these predictions, overall accuracy improves.
- Core assumption: Local and remote models have different strengths due to training data, architecture, or domain adaptation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "in two case studies, BiSupervised even achieved an accuracy exceeding the one of the large-scale remote models, due to complementarities in the local and remote models' capabilities."
  - [section] "If the local and remote models' prediction capabilities are complementary...some inputs which would be incorrectly predicted by the remote model would be correctly predicted with our architecture."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence in corpus neighbors; this is a novel contribution.
- Break condition: If the local model's correct predictions are a strict subset of the remote model's correct predictions, no accuracy gain is possible.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-level supervision allows detection of inputs that neither model can handle confidently, enabling safe fallback.
- Mechanism: The 1st level supervisor filters inputs for local trust; the 2nd level supervisor evaluates remote predictions and raises exceptions if confidence is too low.
- Core assumption: Remote models, despite higher accuracy, still fail on some inputs due to ambiguity, corruption, or out-of-distribution data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A second supervisor is then used to filter out highly uncertain remote predictions."
  - [section] "identify inputs for which not even these can be trusted, allowing to raise an exception or run a fallback strategy instead."
  - [corpus] Weak; corpus neighbors discuss supervision but not two-level systems.
- Break condition: If the 2nd level supervisor's threshold is poorly tuned, it may either block too many valid predictions or fail to catch truly unsafe ones.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input → Dimension reduction (optional) → Local model inference → 1st level supervisor → (If trusted) Output
  - (If not trusted) → Remote model inference → 2nd level supervisor → (If trusted) Output
  - (If not trusted) → Exception/fallback

- Critical path: Local inference + 1st supervisor check → Remote inference + 2nd supervisor check (only if needed)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Threshold selection: Higher thresholds save more cost but risk missing valid local predictions.
  - Local model size vs. coverage: Smaller models are faster but may cover fewer inputs confidently.
  - Supervisor type: Softmax-based supervisors are fast but may be less robust to certain failure modes than ensemble or surprise adequacy.

- Failure signatures:
  - High false positive rate in 1st supervisor → Too many remote calls, reduced cost savings.
  - High false negative rate in 2nd supervisor → System produces unsafe predictions.
  - Slow local inference → Latency advantage disappears.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Measure the percentage of inputs trusted by the 1st supervisor on a validation set; tune threshold to hit a target remote call rate.
  2. Compare system accuracy with only local, only remote, and BiSupervised configurations on a held-out test set.
  3. Test 2nd supervisor's ability to catch known failure modes (e.g., corrupted inputs) and adjust threshold for desired false positive rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the performance of BiSupervised when using models with complementary capabilities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that "with large complementarities, this could substantially increase the overall system accuracy" and that "aiming for complementarities between local and remote models might be infeasible" but they did not evaluate this scenario.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide empirical results on how well BiSupervised performs when the local and remote models have complementary strengths and weaknesses.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results showing accuracy improvements when using complementary models, along with analysis of the types of inputs each model handles well.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does BiSupervised perform under data drift or distribution shift?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that "a more dynamic way to set the threshold may be beneficial" and that "the threshold could be considered a configuration variable that can be changed or tuned in production, for example using remote information or based specifically on the inputs observed on a specific user's local device." However, they do not provide empirical results on how well BiSupervised adapts to changing input distributions.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not evaluate BiSupervised's performance under data drift or distribution shift scenarios, which are common in real-world applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results showing BiSupervised's performance under various data drift scenarios, including sudden shifts and gradual changes in input distribution.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of supervisor impact BiSupervised's performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that "all those listed in Section 3.2 could potentially be used as 1st level supervisor" and that "BiSupervised is agnostic to the way the threshold is set." However, they do not provide a comprehensive evaluation of different supervisor choices and their impact on BiSupervised's performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates a limited set of supervisors and does not provide a systematic comparison of their performance in the context of BiSupervised.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing the performance of BiSupervised using different supervisor choices, including analysis of the trade-offs between accuracy, cost savings, and computational overhead.

## Limitations
- The 2nd level supervisor's effectiveness in catching truly unsafe predictions is not thoroughly validated
- The approach assumes the local model can be meaningfully smaller while maintaining sufficient coverage of easy inputs
- Results may not generalize to domains where local and remote models share similar failure modes

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (cost savings): High
- Mechanism 2 (accuracy improvement): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (safe fallback): Medium
