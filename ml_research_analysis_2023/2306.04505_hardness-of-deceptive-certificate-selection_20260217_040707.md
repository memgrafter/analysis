# Hardness of Deceptive Certificate Selection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04505
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04505
- Reference count: 29
- Key outcome: Finding uninformative certificates in interactive classification while maintaining high soundness and completeness is NP-hard and cannot be approximated better than O(m^1/8 - ε) under the Dense-vs-Random conjecture.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes the computational hardness of deceptive certificate selection in interactive classification systems, proving that finding uninformative certificates while maintaining high soundness and completeness is NP-hard. The authors show that this task cannot be approximated better than O(m^1/8 - ε) for ε > 0 under the Dense-vs-Random conjecture. By reducing the Densest-k-Subgraph problem to the Deceptive-Certificate-Selection problem, the work demonstrates that exploiting asymmetric feature correlation (AFC) to deceive the verifier is computationally difficult, providing evidence that interactive classification can be used reliably for real-world tasks.

## Method Summary
The paper proves NP-hardness and inapproximability bounds for Deceptive-Certificate-Selection (DCS) and DCS2 problems by constructing reductions from well-known hard problems. The reduction maps vertices of the original graph to out-class datapoints, edges to certificates and in-class datapoints, and shows that finding dense subgraphs corresponds to selecting uninformative certificates while maintaining completeness and soundness. The inapproximability bound of O(m^1/8 - ε) relies on the Dense-vs-Random conjecture, which establishes that Densest-k-Subgraph cannot be approximated better than O(n^1/4 - ε).

## Key Results
- Deceptive-Certificate-Selection is NP-hard through reduction from Densest-k-Subgraph
- The problem cannot be approximated better than O(m^1/8 - ε) under the Dense-vs-Random conjecture
- Finding feature sets that maximize asymmetric feature correlation while maintaining low precision is computationally difficult
- These hardness results provide evidence that interactive classification can be used reliably for real-world tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Deceptive certificate selection is computationally hard because it can be reduced from the Densest-k-Subgraph problem. The reduction constructs a tripartite graph where each vertex in the original graph becomes an out-class datapoint, each edge becomes two certificates and two in-class datapoints, and the goal is to maximize uninformative certificate selection while maintaining completeness and soundness. Core assumption: The Dense-vs-Random conjecture holds, making Densest-k-Subgraph inapproximable better than O(n^1/4-ε). Break condition: If a polynomial-time algorithm could approximate Densest-k-Subgraph better than O(n^1/4-ε), the reduction would imply a better approximation for Deceptive-Certificate-Selection.

### Mechanism 2
Exploiting asymmetric feature correlation (AFC) to deceive the verifier is computationally difficult because it requires finding highly correlated feature sets. AFC measures the ratio of correlation between out-class and in-class features. Finding feature sets that maximize this correlation while maintaining low precision is equivalent to finding dense subgraphs in a constructed graph. Core assumption: The AFC property exists in real-world datasets and can be exploited for deception. Break condition: If AFC could be easily estimated or bounded for high-dimensional data, the computational hardness argument would weaken.

### Mechanism 3
The hardness of deceptive certificate selection provides evidence that interactive classification can be used reliably for real-world tasks. Since exploiting AFC for deception is computationally hard, a malicious prover-verifier duo would struggle to use uninformative certificates while maintaining high completeness and soundness, making the system robust against such attacks. Core assumption: Real-world datasets do not have trivially exploitable AFC structures that would make deception easy despite computational hardness. Break condition: If average-case hardness differs significantly from worst-case hardness, or if real-world datasets have special structures that make AFC exploitation easier than worst-case analysis suggests.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can interactive classification be extended to find causal features rather than just correlated features?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that there has been progress to adapt interactive classification to find causal features, but this is left for future research.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the distinction between causal and correlated features and mentions ongoing work, but does not provide concrete results or methods for causal feature identification.
- What evidence would resolve it: A working algorithm that can distinguish between causal and correlated features in interactive classification settings, with empirical validation on datasets where causal relationships are known.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the average-case hardness of deceptive certificate selection for real-world datasets?
- Basis in paper: The paper notes that while worst-case hardness is established, average-case hardness is more relevant for practical applications and depends on the distribution of real-world datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper establishes worst-case NP-hardness but does not investigate the average-case complexity, which would require analyzing the structure of real-world datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies showing the computational difficulty of deceptive certificate selection on various real-world datasets, or theoretical results characterizing the average-case complexity under realistic data distributions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we design classifier setups that guarantee formal interpretability while maintaining high performance?
- Basis in paper: The paper suggests that designing classifier setups with interpretability guarantees is a fruitful approach for formal interpretability and XAI, but does not provide concrete design principles.
- Why unresolved: While the paper proposes interactive classification as a promising framework, it does not offer a comprehensive methodology for designing such systems or guidelines for balancing interpretability with performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study of different classifier architectures and training methods that provably maintain interpretability guarantees while achieving competitive performance on benchmark tasks.

## Limitations

- The hardness results rely on the Dense-vs-Random conjecture, which may not capture average-case behavior
- Datasets with high asymmetric feature correlation could still be vulnerable to approximation algorithms in practice
- The reduction assumes specific graph structures that may not naturally occur in real-world classification problems
- Results focus on binary classification, with multi-class extensions requiring further investigation
