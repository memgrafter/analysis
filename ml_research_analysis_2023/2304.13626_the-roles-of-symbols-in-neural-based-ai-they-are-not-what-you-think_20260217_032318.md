# The Roles of Symbols in Neural-based AI: They are Not What You Think!

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.13626
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13626
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Symbols are communication tools and internal self-communication aids, not mental primitives, with dual representations enabling both abstract reasoning and grounded perception.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a novel neuro-symbolic hypothesis that challenges traditional views of symbols as mental primitives. Instead, symbols are characterized as communication tools that enable efficient knowledge transfer and internal self-communication for explaining and justifying decisions. The authors present a dual-representation architecture where symbols (symreps) and concepts (conreps) are both represented as distributed neural activity patterns, with cross-indexing allowing them to constrain each other's processing. This framework aims to combine the benefits of subsymbolic learning with symbolic reasoning while grounding symbols in perceptual experience.

## Method Summary
The paper reviews evidence from neuroscience, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence to support a neuro-symbolic hypothesis where symbols serve as communication tools and internal self-communication aids. The authors propose a dual-representation architecture with System 1 processing conreps for perceptual grounding and System 2 manipulating symreps for abstract reasoning, connected through cross-indexing links. They discuss how this architecture could enable communication, reasoning, and learning while providing inductive biases through culturally shared symbolic categories.

## Key Results
- Symbols are communication tools that constrain learning through inductive biases rather than mental primitives
- Dual representations (symreps and conreps) enable both abstract reasoning and grounded perception
- Internal self-communication using symbols helps formulate, describe, and justify subsymbolic decision-making processes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Symbols function as communication tools that constrain learning by acting as inductive biases
- Mechanism: Symbols provide culturally shared categories that shape how agents generalize from limited examples, reducing the hypothesis space during concept formation
- Core assumption: Symbols are learned associations linking conreps to culturally meaningful categories
- Evidence anchors: Abstract and section claims about inductive bias; weak corpus support
- Break condition: Incorrect symbol-to-concept mappings or lack of exposure to shared symbolic systems misguides learning

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Dual representations enable both abstract reasoning and grounded perception
- Mechanism: System 1 processes conreps for perceptual grounding while System 2 manipulates symreps for abstract symbolic reasoning; the two systems cross-activate to constrain each other
- Core assumption: The brain uses distributed activation vectors for both symbols and concepts, and these vectors can index into one another
- Evidence anchors: Abstract and section claims about distributed neural activity; weak corpus support
- Break condition: Failure of cross-activation prevents symbolic constraints from guiding subsymbolic processing

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Internal "self-communication" using symbols explains and justifies decisions to the agent itself
- Mechanism: System 2 replays reasoning steps in symbolic form, providing an introspective narrative that can be used to explain behavior internally and externally
- Core assumption: The brain can generate symbolic narratives from subsymbolic processing streams that are consciously accessible
- Evidence anchors: Abstract and section claims about internal self-communication; weak corpus support
- Break condition: Disruption of symbolic replay or lack of symbolic vocabulary prevents internal explanation formation

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Distributed vector representations
  - Why needed here: The entire framework relies on neural activity patterns (symreps and conreps) being represented as distributed vectors
  - Quick check question: Can you explain how a 300-dimensional Word2Vec vector captures semantic meaning differently from a one-hot encoding?

- Concept: Cross-modal grounding
  - Why needed here: Symbols are grounded in multiple sensory modalities, and conreps integrate these modalities
  - Quick check question: How does the paper justify that the neural activity for "peach" is similar whether the stimulus is a word, picture, or spoken sound?

- Concept: Inductive bias in machine learning
  - Why needed here: Symbols act as inductive biases that constrain learning; understanding this concept is key to the hypothesis
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between a symbol acting as an inductive bias versus a feature in supervised learning?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Sensory/Motor subsystem -> percept-driven attention -> System 1/2 input
  System 1 (conrep attractor) <-> System 2 (symrep attractor) <-> cross-indexing links
  Performance Goal subsystem -> goal-driven attention -> context vectors

- Critical path:
  1. Percept arrives → sensory encoding → attention vector generated
  2. System 1 attractor relaxes to conrep state
  3. System 2 attractor relaxes to symrep state
  4. Cross-activation constrains both networks toward stable joint state
  5. Output actions or symbolic explanations generated

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Energy vs. expressiveness: symreps are lower-dimensional and energy-efficient; conreps are high-dimensional and expressive
  - Grounding vs. abstraction: conreps provide grounded multimodal meaning; symreps enable abstract compositionality
  - Speed vs. depth: System 1 (fast, parallel) vs. System 2 (slow, sequential)

- Failure signatures:
  - Misclassification of symbols despite correct perception (symrep failure)
  - Inability to ground abstract concepts (conrep failure)
  - Inconsistent reasoning outputs when both systems should agree (cross-indexing failure)
  - Energy inefficiency from over-activation of high-dimensional conreps

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a dual-network model on paired image-word data; test if cross-activation improves classification accuracy
  2. Ablate symrep pathway and measure degradation in abstract reasoning tasks
  3. Vary dimensionality of symreps vs. conreps; measure energy usage and reasoning performance trade-offs

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do symbols and concepts interact in reasoning and decision-making processes within the brain?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes that symbols and concepts interact through cross-indexing, where neural activity for a symbol can generate the neural activity for the corresponding concept, and vice versa
- Why unresolved: The exact mechanisms and neural pathways involved in this cross-indexing process are not fully understood. Additionally, the role of attention and context in modulating these interactions remains unclear
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed neuroimaging studies, such as fMRI and MEG, that track the neural activity associated with symbols and concepts during reasoning tasks. Computational models that simulate the cross-indexing process and make testable predictions about neural activity patterns

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do symbols act as an inductive bias in learning new concepts and generalizations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that symbols, particularly culturally shared symbols like words, help organize knowledge and provide constraints on learning by indicating which categories and generalizations are important
- Why unresolved: The specific ways in which symbols influence the formation of new representations for concepts and the generalization of knowledge are not fully understood. The role of symbols in shaping the learning process across different domains and contexts is also unclear
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies that investigate how the presence or absence of symbols affects the learning and generalization of concepts in different domains. Computational models that incorporate symbolic representations and test their impact on learning outcomes

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do the proposed neuro-symbolic architecture and the dual representations of symbols and concepts contribute to human-like intelligence?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes a neuro-symbolic architecture that combines subsymbolic representations for symbols and concepts for learning and reasoning. It suggests that this architecture allows for multiple parallel processing streams and cross-indexing between symbols and concepts, contributing to human-like intelligence
- Why unresolved: The effectiveness and limitations of the proposed architecture in achieving human-like intelligence are not yet fully validated. The extent to which the dual representations of symbols and concepts contribute to specific cognitive abilities and behaviors is also unclear
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies that compare the performance of the proposed neuro-symbolic architecture with other AI architectures on various cognitive tasks. Computational models that simulate the proposed architecture and test its ability to replicate human-like reasoning and decision-making processes

## Limitations
- Lack of direct empirical evidence from cited corpus supporting core dual-representation and self-communication claims
- Proposed architecture remains largely theoretical without specific implementation details
- Novel mechanisms (cross-indexing, internal self-communication) not validated by existing studies

## Confidence
- High confidence: Symbols serve communication functions and are culturally shared categories that constrain learning
- Medium confidence: Symbols can provide inductive bias for concept learning, though specific mechanisms lack direct evidence
- Low confidence: Dual representation architecture with cross-indexing, internal self-communication mechanism, and specific attractor network implementation

## Next Checks
1. **Empirical test of dual representation hypothesis**: Design an experiment comparing single-representation neural networks versus dual-representation architectures (symrep/conrep) on tasks requiring both abstract reasoning and perceptual grounding. Measure performance differences and energy efficiency trade-offs.

2. **Inductive bias validation**: Conduct a controlled study where symbolic labels are systematically varied in a concept learning task. Test whether different symbolic categories produce measurable differences in generalization patterns and hypothesis space reduction.

3. **Cross-indexing mechanism test**: Implement the proposed bidirectional symrep↔conrep links and test their necessity by ablating them in a reasoning task. Measure degradation in performance and examine whether symbolic explanations remain coherent when grounding fails.
