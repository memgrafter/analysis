# Calibrating Multimodal Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01265
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01265
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Novel regularization technique (CML) improves multimodal confidence calibration and robustness, reducing VRR by up to 30.79% and accuracy by up to 2.17%

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses unreliable confidence estimation in multimodal learning, where models tend to over-rely on certain modalities and exhibit counterintuitive confidence increases when modalities are removed. The authors propose a novel regularization technique called Calibrating Multimodal Learning (CML) that enforces consistency between confidence and the number of modalities used. By adding a penalty when confidence increases after removing a modality, CML aligns predicted confidence with available information and prevents overfitting to specific modalities. Extensive experiments across seven datasets and two model types demonstrate significant improvements in confidence calibration, classification accuracy, and robustness to noise and missing modalities.

## Method Summary
The Calibrating Multimodal Learning (CML) method introduces a regularization term that penalizes increases in confidence when modalities are removed from the input. For each training sample, the method computes confidence scores for the full modality set and for subsets with one or more modalities removed. If confidence increases when removing a modality, a penalty is applied proportional to the difference. The regularization term is approximated using random sampling of modality subsets due to computational complexity. CML is implemented as an additional loss term combined with cross-entropy, with a regularization coefficient λ tuned on validation data. The method is model-agnostic and can be applied to existing multimodal classifiers like CPM-Nets and MMTM.

## Key Results
- Reduces Violating Ranking Rate (VRR) by up to 30.79% compared to baseline models
- Improves classification accuracy by up to 2.17% on multimodal datasets
- Enhances robustness to modality noise and missing data scenarios
- Demonstrates consistent improvements across 7 datasets and 2 model architectures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Confidence calibration improves when enforcing monotonicity in confidence as modalities are removed.
- Mechanism: The CML regularization penalizes increases in confidence when a modality is removed, aligning predicted confidence with the amount of available information.
- Core assumption: More information should not decrease predictive confidence; removing modalities should not increase confidence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose a novel regularization technique, i.e., Calibrating Multimodal Learning (CML) regularization, to calibrate the predictive confidence of previous methods."
  - [section 3.3] "we find that when one modality is removed, the overall confidence estimated by them can even increase."
- Break condition: If modalities are not assumed to be predictive of the target, the monotonicity assumption fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: CML reduces overfitting to specific modalities by balancing modality contributions per sample.
- Mechanism: The regularization term dynamically adjusts based on individual sample behavior, preventing the model from over-relying on particular modalities.
- Core assumption: Different samples may over-rely on different modalities, so re-weighting modalities globally is insufficient.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "different samples are over-confident on different modalities rather than all samples are over-confident on the same modality."
  - [section 3.4] "the proposed regularization is general and thus can be equipped by current multimodal classifiers."
- Break condition: If all samples consistently over-rely on the same modality, global re-weighting might suffice.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Improved confidence calibration leads to better robustness against modality corruption.
- Mechanism: By enforcing accurate confidence estimates, the model becomes less overconfident on corrupted modalities and more reliable in noisy conditions.
- Core assumption: Overconfident predictions on low-quality modalities harm robustness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3.2] "we find that CML regularization can improve the robustness of imperfect data, such as noise."
  - [section 3.2] "the models are more inclined to believe in a unique modality and is prone to be affected by this modality."
- Break condition: If noise patterns are too severe or systematic, calibration alone may not suffice.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Information theory - the principle that more information reduces uncertainty.
  - Why needed here: CML is built on the intuition that confidence should decrease when removing modalities, following the information theory principle.
  - Quick check question: If a model's confidence increases when removing a modality, what does that imply about its confidence estimates?

- Concept: Confidence calibration - aligning predicted probabilities with empirical accuracy.
  - Why needed here: The paper addresses the gap between confidence estimates and actual performance in multimodal settings.
  - Quick check question: Why is temperature scaling insufficient for multimodal confidence calibration?

- Concept: Mutual information - measuring predictive value of modalities.
  - Why needed here: The proof assumes modalities are predictive of the target, formalized through mutual information.
  - Quick check question: How does mutual information between modalities and labels justify the CML monotonicity assumption?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Classifier backbone → Softmax output → CML regularization term → Final loss (classification + λ·CML)
- Critical path: Forward pass through classifier → Compute confidence for full and reduced modality sets → Apply CML penalty → Update weights
- Design tradeoffs: CML adds computation proportional to number of modalities but improves robustness; sampling approximates full enumeration.
- Failure signatures: If VRR doesn't decrease or accuracy drops, check sampling strategy, λ tuning, or assumption violations.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement CML on a simple multimodal dataset (YaleB) and measure VRR reduction.
  2. Add Gaussian noise to one modality and compare accuracy with/without CML.
  3. Sweep λ values to find optimal trade-off between calibration and classification performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the VRR metric capture all aspects of multimodal confidence calibration, or could there be cases where a model has good VRR but still exhibits other confidence estimation issues?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors introduce VRR as a novel measure to evaluate confidence estimation performance, but acknowledge it focuses on ranking relationships rather than absolute confidence values
- Why unresolved: The paper only demonstrates VRR's effectiveness in detecting confidence violations when removing modalities, but doesn't explore whether VRR captures all relevant aspects of multimodal confidence calibration
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing VRR against other calibration metrics (ECE, Brier score) on multimodal datasets where models exhibit different types of confidence estimation failures

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the proposed CML regularization scale with the number of modalities, particularly in settings with more than 2-3 modalities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors use sampling approximation to make CML computationally feasible, but don't provide theoretical analysis of how approximation quality degrades with increasing modality count
- Why unresolved: The experiments focus on datasets with 2-3 modalities, and while the authors mention computational complexity, they don't empirically validate the sampling approximation's effectiveness across different modality counts
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing exact CML computation versus sampling approximation across datasets with varying numbers of modalities, measuring both computational efficiency and calibration performance

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the CML regularization principle be extended to scenarios where some modalities are not assumed to be predictive of the target (violating the paper's core assumption)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state "For most multimodal learning tasks, all modalities are assumed to be predictive for the target" as the foundation for their regularization approach
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explore how CML would perform or need to be modified when this assumption doesn't hold, which could be relevant for many real-world applications where some modalities provide noise or irrelevant information
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on multimodal datasets where certain modalities are known to be uninformative or noisy, comparing CML performance against alternative approaches that can handle non-predictive modalities

## Limitations

- Critical assumptions about modality predictability and information monotonicity may not hold in all real-world scenarios
- Limited validation to only two specific model architectures (CPM-Nets and MMTM) despite claims of generalizability
- Computational overhead from sampling-based approximation of regularization term, particularly with many modalities

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Empirical improvements in VRR reduction (up to 30.79%) and accuracy gains (up to 2.17%) across multiple datasets and model types
- **Medium Confidence**: Robustness claims for imperfect data and noise scenarios, though limited to specific noise patterns
- **Low Confidence**: Theoretical analysis proving that violating CML assumptions leads to poor confidence calibration, which is outlined but not rigorously proven

## Next Checks

2. Design experiments with highly correlated or anti-correlated modalities to test method behavior when monotonicity assumption is strongly violated
