# Bayesian Quantification with Black-Box Estimators

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.09159
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09159
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: A Bayesian method for estimating class prevalence in unlabeled datasets using black-box classifiers, providing uncertainty quantification and asymptotic consistency under weak assumptions.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a Bayesian approach to estimate class prevalence in unlabeled datasets using a black-box classifier trained on labeled data. The method approximates an intractable posterior by replacing high-dimensional features with classifier outputs, enabling tractable MCMC sampling while retaining sufficient information for prevalence estimation. Experiments show the Bayesian approach performs at least as well as existing point estimators while providing calibrated uncertainty quantification, particularly valuable when classifiers are not fully informative about all classes.

## Method Summary
The method works by first applying a black-box classifier to both labeled and unlabeled datasets, converting raw features into discrete classifier outputs. These outputs form a sufficient statistic that captures all information needed for prevalence estimation. A Bayesian model with Dirichlet priors on prevalence vectors and conditional probabilities is then specified, and MCMC sampling (using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) is performed to obtain the posterior distribution. The MAP estimate and credible intervals are extracted from the posterior, providing both point estimates and uncertainty quantification for the class prevalence in the unlabeled dataset.

## Key Results
- The Bayesian approach achieves comparable or better accuracy than existing point estimators (BBSE, IR, CC) across various simulated and real datasets
- Posterior uncertainty is well-calibrated, especially in low data regimes where other methods struggle
- The method is robust to model misspecification and provides meaningful uncertainty estimates even when classifiers are not fully informative about all classes
- Asymptotic consistency is proven under weak assumptions: the model must be correctly specified, true parameters must lie in the interior of simplices, and the conditional probability matrix must have full rank

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Bayesian inference can estimate class prevalence using black-box classifier outputs rather than raw features
- **Mechanism**: The method approximates an intractable posterior over prevalence by replacing high-dimensional features with low-dimensional categorical classifier outputs, enabling tractable MCMC sampling while retaining sufficient information
- **Core assumption**: Weak prior probability shift assumption holds - conditional distribution of classifier outputs given true class labels is invariant between labeled and unlabeled datasets
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] demonstrates relationship to existing algorithms; [section 2.2] shows the approximate model M_approx; corpus neighbors focus on general Bayesian methods
- **Break condition**: If classifier outputs lose too much information about true classes (e.g., K << L), P(C|Y) becomes rank-deficient, preventing accurate recovery

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: A sufficient statistic for the Bayesian model can be constructed whose size is independent of dataset sizes
- **Mechanism**: Counts of classifier outputs in unlabeled set (N'_k) and joint counts of labels and outputs in labeled set (F_lk) capture all information needed, eliminating need to store raw feature-label pairs
- **Core assumption**: Likelihood factorizes appropriately given categorical representation, as shown by factorization theorem
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 2.3] proves the likelihood is proportional to the sufficient statistic likelihood; corpus neighbors discuss general sufficiency but not this specific construction
- **Break condition**: If classifier produces continuous outputs rather than discrete categories, sufficient statistic construction must be adapted (e.g., via discretization), potentially introducing information loss

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: MAP estimate from this Bayesian model is asymptotically consistent under weak assumptions
- **Mechanism**: As datasets grow, posterior concentrates around true prevalence if classifier is informative enough (P(C|Y) has full rank) and prior is continuous and positive
- **Core assumption**: Model is correctly specified, true parameters lie in interior of simplex, and P(C|Y) has full rank
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 2.4] proves Theorem 2.1 on asymptotic consistency; corpus neighbors discuss general consistency but not this specific setting
- **Break condition**: If classifier is uninformative (P(C|Y) is rank-deficient) or prior is too concentrated away from true parameters, consistency may fail or convergence may be very slow

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Prior probability shift assumption
  - **Why needed here**: Justifies using classifier trained on labeled data to estimate prevalence in unlabeled data by assuming P(X|Y) is invariant

  - **Why needed here**: Replacing high-dimensional features with discrete outputs makes inference tractable while retaining sufficient information for prevalence estimation

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data preprocessing (X→C) -> Sufficient statistic construction (N'_k, F_lk) -> Bayesian model specification (Dirichlet priors) -> Inference engine (MCMC/NUTS) -> Post-processing (MAP and credible intervals)

- **Critical path**: 1) Apply classifier to unlabeled data → get C'_j; 2) Compute sufficient statistics N'_k and F_lk; 3) Run MCMC to sample from posterior P(π,π',ϕ|data); 4) Extract MAP estimate and uncertainty quantification

- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - K vs L: Larger K retains more information but increases computational cost; smaller K may lose information needed for accurate prevalence estimation
  - Prior specification: Informative priors can incorporate domain knowledge but may bias results if misspecified; flat priors are conservative but may lead to slower convergence
  - Classifier quality: Better classifiers provide more informative C, improving estimation; poor classifiers may lead to unreliable results even with Bayesian uncertainty quantification

- **Failure signatures**: 
  - Posterior shows high variance or multimodality → classifier may be uninformative or K too small
  - MAP estimates differ significantly from ground truth → model misspecification or insufficient data
  - MCMC chains fail to converge → poor prior specification or pathological posterior geometry

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Synthetic validation**: Generate data with known prevalence shift, apply method with varying K, compare MAP estimates to ground truth
  2. **Classifier quality ablation**: Fix synthetic data, vary classifier quality (q in paper), observe impact on estimation accuracy
  3. **Uncertainty calibration**: Generate multiple datasets with same parameters, check if 95% credible intervals contain true prevalence ~95% of the time

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly call out open questions, but several important directions emerge from the limitations section and discussion.

## Limitations
- The weak prior probability shift assumption may not hold when labeled and unlabeled datasets have different feature distributions beyond class prevalence changes
- The choice of discretization granularity K is empirically important but lacks theoretical guidance for optimal selection
- Asymptotic consistency proof assumes ideal conditions (correct specification, interior parameters, full rank P(C|Y)) that may not hold in practice

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Bayesian approximation): High - mathematical derivation is sound and relationship to existing methods is clearly established
- Mechanism 2 (Sufficient statistic): Medium - factorization theorem is proved but practical implications for different classifier types need further validation
- Mechanism 3 (Asymptotic consistency): Medium - theoretical proof is rigorous but assumes ideal conditions that may not hold in practice
