# Colexifications for Bootstrapping Cross-lingual Datasets: The Case of Phonology, Concreteness, and Affectiveness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2306.02646
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02646
- **Reference count:** 17
- **Key outcome:** This paper proposes leveraging cross-lingual colexifications to bootstrap cross-lingual datasets, focusing on concreteness and affectiveness ratings. The authors construct a synset graph from BabelNet and map it to phonological features and human ratings of concreteness and affectiveness. The resulting dataset covers 142 languages across 21 language families, with 84,084 concepts and 613,906 phoneme/lemma pairs. Analyses reveal that concepts closer in concreteness and affectiveness are more likely to colexify. Certain phonemes and phonological features show significant correlations with concreteness and valence across language families. The dataset is made publicly available for further research.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of limited cross-linguistic data for semantic features like concreteness and affectiveness by proposing a novel approach using colexifications. By constructing a synset graph from BabelNet and mapping it to phonological features and human ratings, the authors create a large-scale dataset covering 142 languages. Their analysis reveals that colexification patterns correlate with semantic proximity in concreteness and affectiveness, and that specific phonemes and phonological features show significant correlations with these ratings across language families. The publicly available dataset provides a valuable resource for cross-linguistic research in phonology and semantics.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a synset graph from BabelNet synsets, connecting concepts that share lemmas across languages via colexification edges. They extract phonemes for sense lemmas from Wiktionary pronunciations and map them to phonological features using PanPhon. Human ratings of concreteness and affectiveness from Warriner et al. [2013] are then mapped to concepts in the synset graph. The resulting dataset enables analyses of correlations between colexification patterns and semantic distances, as well as between phonemes/phonological features and ratings across language families.

## Key Results
- The dataset covers 142 languages across 21 language families, with 84,084 concepts and 613,906 phoneme/lemma pairs
- Concepts closer in concreteness and affectiveness are more likely to colexify
- Certain phonemes (e.g., /k/ initial) and phonological features show significant correlations with concreteness and valence across language families
- Type-to-token ratio (TTR) of phonemes correlates positively with concreteness, while phoneme segment length correlates negatively

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Cross-lingual colexifications can serve as a bridge to bootstrap ratings and phonological features across languages.
- Mechanism: By constructing a synset graph from BabelNet, concepts shared across languages are linked via colexification edges. These edges enable transfer of ratings (concreteness, valence, arousal, dominance) and phonological features from high-resource languages to low-resource ones.
- Core assumption: Colexification patterns reflect semantic proximity, and shared concepts across languages have similar psychological or phonological properties.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using colexifications to bootstrap datasets across such semantic features"
  - [section 2] "Norms and ratings of words are essential components in psychology, linguistics...but language coverage is severely limited"
  - [corpus] Weak - no explicit prior work cited on colexification-based bootstrapping of ratings/phonology
- Break condition: If colexification edges do not reliably indicate semantic or psychological similarity, the transferred ratings will be inaccurate.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Phonemes and phonological features exhibit consistent correlations with concreteness and affectiveness across language families.
- Mechanism: Aggregating phoneme ratings per language and calculating correlations reveals systematic relationships (e.g., /k/ initial correlates with concreteness in Turkic/Tai-Kadai).
- Core assumption: Sound-meaning associations are not arbitrary but follow patterns that can be detected statistically across typologically diverse languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "certain initial/last phonemes are significantly correlated with concreteness/affectiveness intra language families"
  - [section 4.2] "k is correlated with concreteness" and "p as initials present correlations with affectiveness"
  - [corpus] Moderate - related work on iconicity and phonosemantics exists but not cited here
- Break condition: If correlations are spurious or driven by confounds (e.g., language family-specific biases), they won't generalize.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Type-to-token ratio (TTR) of phonemes correlates positively with concreteness, while phoneme segment length correlates negatively.
- Mechanism: Higher TTR indicates greater phonological complexity, which aligns with the cognitive complexity of concrete concepts. Longer segments may map to more abstract or simplified forms.
- Core assumption: Concrete concepts require more varied phonological encoding, while abstract concepts can be expressed with more uniform sound patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "TTR of phonemes are positively correlated with concreteness across several language families"
  - [section 4.2] "the more concrete concept, the more diverse (complex) the phonemes are"
  - [corpus] Weak - no prior TTR-concreteness studies cited
- Break condition: If TTR is influenced more by orthographic or morphological factors than semantic ones, the correlation will break down.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: BabelNet synset extraction -> colexification graph construction -> phoneme extraction (WikiPron) -> phonological feature extraction (PanPhon) -> ratings mapping -> statistical analysis pipeline
- Critical path: BabelNet synset graph -> colexification edges -> shared concept mapping -> phoneme/feature aggregation -> correlation testing
- Design tradeoffs: Large multilingual coverage vs. data sparsity in low-resource languages; rich phonological detail vs. computational complexity of feature vectors
- Failure signatures: Missing phonemes for certain lemmas; sparse ratings for low-resource languages; spurious correlations due to multiple testing
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate colexification graph connectivity by sampling lemma-syndet pairs and checking cross-lingual links
  2. Test phoneme aggregation by computing mean ratings for a small set of concepts with known ratings

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do concreteness and affective ratings vary cross-linguistically for concepts with the same colexification patterns?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that concreteness ratings are curated solely from US residents and affectiveness ratings from English, raising concerns about anglocentric bias.
- Why unresolved: The current dataset maps English-based ratings to concepts across languages, but does not measure how native speakers of other languages perceive these concepts' concreteness and affectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies collecting concreteness and affectiveness ratings from native speakers of the 142 languages in the dataset, then comparing these ratings to the mapped English ratings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Are there universal phonological features that correlate with concreteness or affectiveness across all language families?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The analysis shows negative correlations between certain phonological features and concreteness across language families, but coefficients are small and findings are limited to specific features.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested correlations for a subset of phonological features and found inconsistent patterns across language families.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive analysis of all phonological features against concreteness and affectiveness ratings across all language families, using multivariate modeling to identify consistent universal patterns.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Do colexification patterns reflect universal semantic structures or are they primarily influenced by cultural and linguistic factors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses findings that contradict previous studies, showing that concepts closer in concreteness and affectiveness are more likely to colexify, which differs from Xu et al. (2020).
- Why unresolved: The current analysis only examines correlation between colexification frequency and semantic distance, without controlling for cultural or linguistic factors that might influence these patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled study comparing colexification patterns across languages with varying cultural and linguistic backgrounds, while controlling for semantic distance and other confounding variables.

## Limitations

- The correlation analyses rely on aggregating phoneme ratings across concepts, which may introduce bias if certain phonemes are systematically over- or under-represented for specific concept types
- The study does not account for potential confounds from morphological complexity or orthographic influences on phoneme distributions
- The assumption that colexification patterns reliably indicate semantic similarity across diverse language families remains untested empirically

## Confidence

- High confidence: The dataset construction methodology and cross-lingual coverage (142 languages, 21 families)
- Medium confidence: The validity of colexification-based bootstrapping for ratings transfer across languages
- Low confidence: The universality of phoneme-concreteness/affectiveness correlations across language families
