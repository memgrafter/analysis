# ASP: Learn a Universal Neural Solver!

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.00466
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00466
- Reference count: 40
- The paper proposes ASP (Adaptive Staircase Policy Space Response Oracle), a framework to improve generalization ability of neural solvers for combinatorial optimization problems (COPs) across varying problem distributions and scales.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces ASP, a framework designed to enhance the generalization capabilities of neural solvers for combinatorial optimization problems across different distributions and scales. By combining Policy Space Response Oracles for distributional exploration with persistent scale adaptation via curriculum learning, ASP achieves significant performance gains on TSP and CVRP problems compared to standard training methods.

## Method Summary
ASP combines two key components: Distributional Exploration (DE) using Policy Space Response Oracles (PSRO) to adapt to unknown problem distributions, and Persistent Scale Adaption (PSA) using curriculum learning to handle varying problem scales. The framework alternates between these components, allowing neural solvers to explore and adapt to unseen distributions and scales. The method was tested on TSP, CVRP, PCTSP, and their variants using two neural solvers (AM and POMO), demonstrating significant improvements in optimality gap reduction.

## Key Results
- Achieved 90.9% reduction in optimality gap on generated TSP instances and 47.43% on real-world instances
- Achieved 19% reduction in optimality gap for CVRP problems
- Maintained model capacity and training efficiency while improving generalization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Policy Space Response Oracles (PSRO) enable the solver to adapt to unknown problem distributions by generating adversarial instances that expose weaknesses. PSRO frames the distributional exploration as a two-player zero-sum game where the data generator oracle produces adversarial instances by maximizing the expected optimality gap, while the solver oracle minimizes it. This adversarial training process iteratively expands the joint policy set, allowing the solver to encounter and adapt to distributions it has never seen during standard training.

### Mechanism 2
Persistent Scale Adaption (PSA) improves the solver's scalability by gradually increasing the problem scale through curriculum learning. PSA employs an adaptive staircase procedure that starts with smaller problem scales and progressively increases the difficulty. A momentum-based task selection strategy guides the training process, focusing on scales where the solver performs poorly and where performance is unstable. This allows the solver to persistently improve its performance across a range of scales.

### Mechanism 3
The combination of distributional exploration and persistent scale adaption allows the solver to discover complex structures by learning from different distributions and scales interchangeably. ASP integrates DE and PSA into a single framework where at each iteration it chooses between conducting distributional exploration on a larger problem scale or enhancing the solver's ability on the current set of scales. This interplay between exploring new distributions and adapting to new scales forces the solver to develop robust and generalizable heuristics.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Solver Selector: The neural solver being trained (e.g., AM, POMO)
  - Data Generator: A Normalizing Flow-based model (Real-NVP) used to generate adversarial instances
  - Distributional Exploration (DE): PSRO-based training for improving generalization on distributions
  - Persistent Scale Adaption (PSA): Adaptive staircase-based curriculum learning for improving scalability
  - Meta-Game Framework: A two-player zero-sum game framework used to guide the training process

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize the solver selector and data generator
  2. Conduct DE or PSA at each iteration, depending on the solver's performance on the evaluation set
  3. Update the joint policy set and meta-strategy based on the results of DE or PSA
  4. Repeat steps 2-3 until a stopping criterion is met
  5. Output the best neural solver

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model capacity: Larger models may be able to learn from a wider range of distributions and scales, but at the cost of increased computational resources and potential overfitting
  - Training sample size: Larger sample sizes may lead to better generalization, but also increase training time and computational cost
  - Performance threshold: A higher threshold may lead to more iterations of PSA, but also increase the risk of overfitting to the current set of scales

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor generalization on unseen distributions or scales
  - Failure to converge during training
  - Overfitting to the training distribution or scale
  - Excessive computational cost or training time

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train the base solver (e.g., AM, POMO) on a single problem scale and distribution using standard training methods
  2. Train the base solver using ASP on a range of problem scales and distributions
  3. Compare the performance of the base solver and ASP-trained solver on unseen distributions and scales, as well as on the training distributions and scales

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does ASP's performance scale when applied to more complex COPs like the Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP) or scheduling problems with additional constraints?
- Basis in paper: The paper evaluates ASP on TSP, CVRP, SDVRP, PCTSP, and SPCTSP, but these are relatively "simpler" COPs compared to QAP or scheduling problems with multiple resource constraints.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide results or analysis for more complex COPs, and the scalability of ASP to such problems is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying ASP to QAP or scheduling problems with results compared to state-of-the-art solvers and baseline methods.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of different data distribution generation strategies (beyond Real-NVP) on ASP's generalization ability?
- Basis in paper: The paper uses Real-NVP for data generation but mentions it as "a more generic generation approach" without comparing to alternatives like GANs or other normalizing flows.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not compare Real-NVP to other data generation methods, so the optimal choice for distribution exploration is unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using different data generation strategies (GANs, other normalizing flows, etc.) with performance metrics on generalization.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is ASP's performance to the choice of threshold α and patience parameter γ in the task selection strategy?
- Basis in paper: The paper sets α and γ to specific values (α=3 for AM on TSP, γ=5) but does not explore the sensitivity of performance to these hyperparameters.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide ablation studies varying α and γ systematically to understand their impact on convergence and final performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A hyperparameter sensitivity analysis showing performance across different α and γ values with convergence curves and final optimality gaps.

## Limitations
- The computational complexity of maintaining and updating the joint policy set in PSRO-based distributional exploration is not thoroughly analyzed
- The actual computational overhead of running both DE and PSA simultaneously is not quantified
- While the paper claims ASP maintains model capacity and training efficiency, the empirical evidence for this claim is limited

## Confidence
- TSP Results: Medium-High (extensive comparisons with exact solvers and heuristics)
- CVRP Results: Medium (fewer baselines and less comprehensive real-world validation)
- Universal Generalization Claim: Medium (supported by experimental evidence but relies heavily on PSRO effectiveness)
