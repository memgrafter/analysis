# Moving Forward by Moving Backward: Embedding Action Impact over Action Semantics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.12289
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12289
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: AAP achieves near 100% success in PointNav and over 30% in ObjectNav under severe action drifts

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Action Adaptive Policy (AAP) to address the action-stability assumption in embodied AI, where agents must adapt to previously unseen action drifts during inference without additional training. AAP learns action embeddings that capture the actual impact of actions on the environment rather than their predefined semantic meanings, enabling robust adaptation to drifts like disabled actions or altered movement/rotation magnitudes. Evaluated on PointNav and ObjectNav tasks in AI2-THOR and Habitat, AAP significantly outperforms baselines, achieving consistent success rates across a range of severe drifts while maintaining near 100% success in PointNav and over 30% in ObjectNav even when actions are disabled.

## Method Summary
The Action Adaptive Policy (AAP) consists of three main components: a state encoder (ResNet-50 visual encoder + goal embedder MLP), an action-impact encoder (feature extractor + GRU for updating action embeddings based on state changes), and a policy network with an Order-Invariant (OI) head (transformer encoder without positional encoding). The method uses DD-PPO reinforcement learning with an auxiliary forward prediction loss to train the action-impact encoder to produce meaningful action impact embeddings. During inference, the OI head selects actions based on their impact embeddings rather than predefined semantics, allowing adaptation to action drifts.

## Key Results
- AAP achieves near 100% success rate in PointNav across all tested drifts, including when actions are disabled
- In ObjectNav, AAP maintains over 30% success rate even under severe drifts, significantly outperforming baselines
- AAP consistently outperforms EmbCLIP, Meta-RL, RMA, and Model-Based approaches across all drift types and task configurations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The action-impact encoder generates embeddings that capture actual state-change rather than action semantics, allowing adaptation to unseen drifts.
- Mechanism: The encoder takes consecutive observations and previous action, then uses a GRU to update action embeddings based on the observed state change.
- Core assumption: State-change features contain sufficient information to distinguish different action impacts even under drift.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Instead of relying that the impact of an action stably reflects its pre-defined semantic meaning, we propose to model the impact of actions on-the-fly using latent embeddings."
  - [section] "The action-impact encoder first extracts a state-change feature f from two consecutive observations. It then utilizes a recurrent neural network (RNN) to update the action embedding ei,t according to the previous action at−1=ai."
- Break condition: If state observations become too noisy or aliased such that different actions produce indistinguishable state changes, the embeddings lose discriminative power.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The order-invariant policy head processes action embeddings without relying on fixed action semantics, enabling selection of the best action under drift.
- Mechanism: A transformer encoder without positional encoding maps the belief vector and action embeddings to logits, allowing the model to choose actions based on their impact rather than their semantic identity.
- Core assumption: Weight sharing in the transformer and absence of positional encoding sufficiently decorrelate the policy from action semantics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using these action embeddings, the AAP utilizes a Order-Invariant (OI) head to choose the action whose impact will allow it to most readily achieve its goal."
  - [section] "By design, the extensive weight sharing in a transformer... means that there are no unique weights assigned to any particular action."
- Break condition: If the transformer's attention mechanism inadvertently learns to prioritize action order through co-occurrence patterns in training data.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The auxiliary forward prediction loss encourages the action-impact encoder to learn accurate state-change models, improving embedding quality.
- Mechanism: A shared MLP predicts next state changes from each action embedding; MSE loss between prediction and ground truth trains the encoder to produce meaningful impact representations.
- Core assumption: Accurate state-change prediction correlates with useful action impact embeddings for policy selection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "To endow the action embeddings E with an ability to describe the state-change between two consecutive observations, we utilize an auxiliary model-based forward prediction loss to optimize the action-impact encoder."
  - [section] "Our forward prediction loss is optimized jointly alongside DD-PPO using the same collection of on-policy trajectories."
- Break condition: If the forward prediction loss dominates training and causes the encoder to overfit to trivial state changes rather than meaningful action impacts.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Recurrent neural networks for sequential state-change modeling
  - Why needed here: The action-impact encoder must aggregate state-change information over time to refine action embeddings beyond single-step observations.
  - Quick check question: What property of RNNs allows them to maintain context about previous state changes when updating action embeddings?

- Concept: Transformer attention mechanisms and positional encoding
  - Why needed here: The OI head must process action embeddings without assuming any fixed action order, requiring a transformer without positional encoding.
  - Quick check question: How does removing positional encoding from a transformer encoder affect its ability to distinguish between different input tokens?

- Concept: Reinforcement learning with auxiliary losses
  - Why needed here: The forward prediction loss is an auxiliary task that must be balanced with the main RL objective during training.
  - Quick check question: What happens if the weight α on the auxiliary loss is set too high relative to the main RL loss?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Visual observations → ResNet-50 → state encoder → hidden state h_t
  - Hidden state h_t + previous action → action-impact encoder (feature extractor + GRU) → action embeddings E_t
  - Hidden state h_t + action embeddings E_t → policy network (RNN + OI head transformer) → action logits + value
  - Action embeddings E_t → forward prediction module (MLP) → predicted state changes

- Critical path: Observation → state encoder → action-impact encoder (with GRU update) → OI head → action selection

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a GRU vs. transformer for the action-impact encoder: GRU is lighter and sufficient for sequential state-change modeling, while a transformer would be heavier without clear benefit.
  - Order-invariant vs. linear actor-critic: The transformer avoids action semantics but adds computational overhead; linear is faster but brittle to drift.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on unseen drifts: Likely the action embeddings are not capturing impact information properly or the OI head is not utilizing them effectively.
  - Slow adaptation at episode start: The action embeddings start identical, so early exploration is needed; if too slow, consider initializing embeddings differently.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify the action-impact encoder produces different embeddings for actions with different observed effects by logging embeddings during a short episode with known state changes.
  2. Test the OI head with fixed action embeddings (from a pretrained encoder) to confirm it can select appropriate actions under drift without the encoder being trained.
  3. Compare performance with and without the forward prediction loss to confirm it improves embedding quality for action selection.

- Why unresolved: The experiments are limited to specific types of drifts, and the paper does not discuss the potential impact of other drift types on the AAP's performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the AAP with different types of drifts, such as changes in action duration or the introduction of new actions, would provide insights into its generalization capabilities.

## Limitations
- The evaluation focuses primarily on movement and rotation drifts, with limited testing of more complex action transformations or compound drifts
- Scalability to environments with larger action spaces or more complex state spaces remains untested
- The approach may face challenges when state changes become highly aliased or when observation noise increases

## Confidence

**High confidence** in the core mechanism: The action-impact encoder successfully learns to adapt to simple action drifts (movement/rotation magnitude changes) as evidenced by consistent performance improvements across multiple baselines and task types.

**Medium confidence** in generalizability: While results show AAP handles disabled actions and magnitude changes well, the evaluation scope is limited to specific drift types in controlled environments. Performance on arbitrary or compound drifts is not demonstrated.

**Medium confidence** in the order-invariant policy head: The theoretical justification for removing positional encoding is sound, but empirical evidence that the transformer is not learning order-based biases through co-occurrence patterns is limited.

## Next Checks

1. **Stress test state-change discriminability**: Systematically vary observation noise levels and state aliasing to determine when the action-impact encoder fails to distinguish different action impacts. This would establish the boundary conditions of the approach.

2. **Compound drift evaluation**: Test AAP on episodes combining multiple drift types simultaneously (e.g., disabled actions plus magnitude changes) to assess real-world robustness beyond the evaluated single-drift scenarios.

3. **Action space scaling analysis**: Evaluate AAP's performance as the number of actions increases beyond the 4-action space used in the paper, measuring how embedding quality and policy performance degrade with action space complexity.
