# A Knowledge Graph Perspective on Supply Chain Resilience

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.08506
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08506
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 19
- Key outcome: Achieves transparency up to tier-3 suppliers using knowledge graph methods with RotatE model achieving MRR of 0.4377

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses supply chain transparency and resilience challenges by modeling supply chain data as a knowledge graph, integrating information from Siemens and external sources to achieve visibility up to tier-3 suppliers. The study applies state-of-the-art knowledge graph completion methods, achieving a mean reciprocal rank of 0.4377 with the RotatE model for link prediction tasks. Additionally, graph analytics algorithms are employed to identify critical suppliers in the network, calculating importance scores based on centrality measures.

## Method Summary
The method involves creating a knowledge graph with 8 entity types and 11 relation types from Siemens supply chain data containing 65,277 nodes and 311,676 edges. Knowledge graph completion models (RotatE, ComplEx, TuckER, TransE, ConvE, RGCN, CompGCN) are trained using PyKEEN with Adam optimizer and margin ranking loss. Graph analytics using Neo4j Graph Data Science library calculates centrality metrics (degree, betweenness, closeness, triangle count) to derive supplier importance scores.

## Key Results
- Achieves transparency up to tier-3 suppliers through knowledge graph integration
- RotatE model achieves MRR of 0.4377 for link prediction tasks
- Identifies 988 suppliers with criticality scores above threshold of 10 for expert review

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Knowledge graph structure enables multi-hop visibility beyond tier-1 suppliers, directly addressing the "intransparent" nature of supply chains.
- Mechanism: By integrating data from Siemens and external sources, the graph encodes entities and relations across three tiers, making relationships (e.g., supplier → supplier) explicit rather than implicit.
- Core assumption: Entities at different tiers are correctly linked in the graph so that traversal reveals deeper supply chain connections.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "By connecting different data sources, we model the supply network as a knowledge graph and achieve transparency up to tier-3 suppliers."
  - [section 1] "The knowledge of subsuppliers is often limited and disregarded for decision making."
- Break condition: If data ingestion introduces errors or if key supplier relationships are missing, the multi-hop transparency claim fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Knowledge graph completion methods can infer missing links, mitigating the "data incompleteness" challenge.
- Mechanism: Graph embedding methods (e.g., RotatE) learn representations of entities and relations; these embeddings are then used to rank candidate objects for missing triples, predicting plausible supply chain links.
- Core assumption: Missing links follow patterns similar to observed ones, so embedding geometry captures these regularities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1] "Given a query of the form (subject, predicate, ?), the goal is to predict a ranked list of entity candidates that are most likely the correct object of the query."
  - [section 4.4] "All models were able to learn useful embeddings from the training set, while RotatE performed best with respect to all metrics."
- Break condition: If the graph is too sparse or the missing links are not pattern-following, predictions degrade and MRR drops.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Graph centrality metrics operationalize risk identification, turning structural importance into actionable supplier prioritization.
- Mechanism: By computing in-degree, out-degree, betweenness, closeness, and triangle count, the system scores suppliers for criticality; high scores trigger closer monitoring.
- Core assumption: Structural importance (measured by centrality) correlates with real-world supply chain risk exposure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] "We calculate the following centrality and community detection metrics... to derive an importance or criticality of a supplier."
  - [section 5.2] "There are in total 988 suppliers with a score above 10, which might be critical entities in the supply network and should be examined by domain experts."
- Break condition: If structural importance diverges from operational risk (e.g., due to geographic clustering not captured by centrality), the metric becomes misleading.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data ingestion layer: Siemens internal and external data → Neo4j KG
  - Knowledge graph completion module: PyKEEN-based embedding models (RotatE, RGCN, etc.) → ranked link predictions
  - Graph analytics module: Neo4j Graph Data Science library → centrality metrics → aggregated importance scores
  - Visualization layer: Subgraph rendering (yellow/red nodes for suppliers, purple for business scopes) → risk path identification
- Critical path:
  1. Load and integrate tier-1, tier-2, tier-3 supplier data into Neo4j
  2. Train RotatE model on triples, validate via MRR/Hits@10
  3. Run centrality calculations on the supplier subgraph
  4. Aggregate scores and flag suppliers above threshold (e.g., 10)
  5. Visualize critical paths for domain expert review
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Completeness vs. accuracy: Including more external data improves coverage but may introduce noise.
  - Model complexity vs. speed: RotatE is faster than RGCN but may miss nuanced relational patterns.
  - Static vs. dynamic scoring: Current implementation uses static centrality; updating in near real-time would increase complexity.
- Failure signatures:
  - Low MRR/Hits@10: Embedding model not capturing graph patterns; check for missing relation types or data sparsity.
  - High number of suppliers above threshold: Aggregation formula may be too lenient; revisit metric weights.
  - Visualization shows disconnected critical nodes: Possible data ingestion error or missing `supplies_to` edges.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate RotatE link prediction on a small synthetic KG to verify PyKEEN pipeline.
  2. Compare in-degree vs. betweenness centrality scores on the supplier subgraph to check correlation assumptions.
  3. Test aggregated score formula with different metric weights to see impact on number of flagged critical suppliers.

## Limitations
- Single corporate dataset (Siemens) limits generalizability to other industries
- Technical MRR metric used instead of real-world impact on supply chain resilience outcomes
- Arbitrary threshold of 10 for criticality scores without domain expert validation

## Confidence

- Knowledge graph structure enables multi-hop visibility: **High confidence** - Well-supported by demonstrated tier-3 supplier coverage and explicit relationship encoding
- Knowledge graph completion methods effectively predict missing links: **Medium confidence** - Technical MRR metric shows promise, but limited validation on actual prediction accuracy for critical supply chain relationships
- Graph centrality metrics correlate with real-world supply chain risk: **Low confidence** - Structural importance may not fully capture operational risks like geopolitical factors or supplier financial health

## Next Checks

1. **External dataset validation**: Test the KG completion pipeline on publicly available supply chain datasets (e.g., automotive or electronics industry consortium data) to assess generalizability beyond Siemens-specific patterns.

2. **Expert validation study**: Conduct a blind test where supply chain experts evaluate the top-10 predicted critical suppliers against known disruption cases, measuring precision in identifying actual risk factors.

3. **Temporal robustness analysis**: Simulate supply chain changes over time (supplier exits, new relationships) and measure how quickly the KG-based approach detects structural shifts compared to traditional methods, with a focus on lead time for risk mitigation.
