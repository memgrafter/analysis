# How Good are Commercial Large Language Models on African Languages?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06530
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06530
- Authors: David Ifeoluwa Adelani, James Adrian F. Fehr, Seid Muhie Yimam
- Reference count: 13
- Commercial models perform significantly worse than state-of-the-art on African languages

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates the performance of ChatGPT and Cohere on African languages across text classification and machine translation tasks. The study covers eight African languages spanning different language families and geographical areas. Results show that both commercial models perform significantly below current state-of-the-art models on African languages, with Cohere's multilingual model performing particularly poorly despite supporting many of the evaluated languages. ChatGPT achieves higher F1 scores on text classification than Cohere but obtains very low BLEU scores on machine translation, especially when translating into morphologically rich African languages.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates commercial large language models (ChatGPT and Cohere) on eight African languages using two tasks: text classification and machine translation. For text classification, few-shot in-context learning is used with prompts containing demonstration examples. For machine translation, zero-shot translation is performed without demonstrations. The evaluation uses 100 randomly sampled test examples per language from news topic classification datasets and the MAFAND-MT machine translation dataset. F1 scores are calculated for classification tasks and BLEU scores for translation tasks. The study compares commercial model performance against current state-of-the-art models for African languages.

## Key Results
- Both commercial models perform significantly worse than state-of-the-art models on African languages
- Cohere's multilingual model performs particularly poorly despite supporting many evaluated languages
- ChatGPT achieves higher F1 scores on text classification than Cohere but obtains very low BLEU scores (<1.0) on machine translation
- Commercial models perform better on text classification than machine translation for African languages

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Commercial language models fail on African languages due to insufficient pretraining data coverage. When pretraining corpora contain minimal or no examples of African languages, the models lack the foundational linguistic patterns needed for effective few-shot or zero-shot performance. This is evidenced by Nigerian Pidgin achieving the highest Cohere scores, likely due to its close relationship with English which typically dominates pretraining corpora.

### Mechanism 2
Commercial models perform better on text classification than machine translation because classification tasks are less sensitive to morphological richness and syntactic complexity. Translation requires generating fluent output in the target language, which is more challenging when there's a large linguistic distance between English and African languages.

### Mechanism 3
English-centric prompting strategies work better than native-language prompts because commercial models are primarily trained on English data and English-centric tasks. The internal representations of these models are more aligned with English linguistic structures, making them more responsive to English prompts even when translating to/from African languages.

## Foundational Learning

- **In-context learning mechanics**: Understanding how few-shot examples influence model predictions is crucial for interpreting the experimental setup and results. *Quick check*: How does the number and quality of demonstration examples affect the model's ability to perform unseen tasks?

- **BLEU score limitations**: BLEU scores are used to evaluate machine translation, but the paper notes their limitations, especially for morphologically rich languages. *Quick check*: Why might BLEU scores underestimate the semantic similarity of translations for morphologically rich languages?

- **Language family and typology**: The paper evaluates languages from different families; understanding linguistic distance helps explain performance differences. *Quick check*: How does the morphological complexity of a language affect the difficulty of machine translation into that language?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Dataset preparation → Prompt design → API interaction with commercial models → Result collection and scoring → Analysis
- **Critical path**: Dataset → Prompt design → API call → Scoring → Analysis. The slowest step is API interaction due to rate limits and manual effort.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Few-shot examples improve performance but require manual curation; zero-shot is faster but less accurate; sampling a subset of test data reduces cost but may affect result reliability.
- **Failure signatures**: Low BLEU scores (<1.0) indicate generation failure; F1 scores far below SOTA suggest insufficient pretraining; inconsistent results across seeds may indicate instability in few-shot prompting.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run classification with 0, 2, and 4 examples per class to measure the impact of few-shot examples.
  2. Compare English vs. native-language prompts for translation to test cross-lingual prompting.
  3. Increase the number of test samples (e.g., from 100 to 500) to check if results are stable across larger sets.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do African language performance and English performance compare across different commercial language models when evaluated on the same tasks and datasets? The paper focuses on evaluating commercial models on African languages specifically, without providing a direct comparison to their performance on English or other high-resource languages.

### Open Question 2
How do different prompting strategies (e.g., few-shot vs. zero-shot, English vs. native language) impact commercial model performance on African languages? The paper uses English prompts and few-shot examples but does not systematically explore the impact of different prompting strategies.

### Open Question 3
How does commercial model performance on African languages vary across different language families and geographical regions? The paper reports aggregate results across all evaluated African languages without breaking down performance by language family or geographical region.

## Limitations

- Limited test data availability with only 100 samples per language for classification tasks, which may not be representative
- Results based on fixed commercial model versions from January-February 2023, potentially outdated as models continue to evolve
- BLEU metric limitations for morphologically rich languages may underestimate semantic quality of translations

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Commercial models perform significantly worse than state-of-the-art on African languages (well-supported by BLEU scores below 1.0 and F1 scores substantially below SOTA)
- **Medium confidence**: Commercial models perform better on text classification than machine translation (moderate support but influenced by evaluation setup)
- **Low confidence**: Poor performance primarily due to insufficient pretraining data for African languages (speculative without direct analysis of pretraining corpora)

## Next Checks

1. Replicate with larger test sets (500+ samples per language) to verify performance patterns hold with more representative data coverage
2. Test current model versions (released after February 2023) to assess whether performance has improved with updates
3. Conduct ablation studies on prompt engineering to determine if suboptimal prompting contributes to observed performance gaps
