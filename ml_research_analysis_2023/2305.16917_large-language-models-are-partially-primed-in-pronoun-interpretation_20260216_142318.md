# Large Language Models Are Partially Primed in Pronoun Interpretation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16917
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16917
- Reference count: 15
- Primary result: InstructGPT adapts to syntactic but not semantic pronoun biases, while FLAN-UL2 fails to generate meaningful variation

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models exhibit human-like adaptation in pronoun interpretation after exposure to referential patterns. Using in-context learning to simulate psycholinguistic experiments, the authors tested InstructGPT and FLAN-UL2 on ambiguous pronouns after priming with syntactic (subject vs. non-subject) or semantic (goal vs. source) biases. InstructGPT showed adaptation to syntactic biases similar to humans but not to semantic biases, revealing a strong inherent goal preference. FLAN-UL2 failed to produce meaningful variation, likely due to its smaller size or decoder-only architecture. The findings suggest that while LLMs can encode some syntactic discourse knowledge, their semantic understanding in referential processing is partial and not fully human-like.

## Method Summary
The study replicated three psycholinguistic experiments using in-context learning (ICL) where each question was answered with access to all previous items and responses. Story prompts with ambiguous pronouns were presented along with content and reference questions. Temperature randomization (0.2-1.0) simulated multiple participants. Trials with incorrect content question answers were filtered out. Results were analyzed using Bayesian mixed-effects Bernoulli logistic regression to test exposure effects, comparing model responses to human data from Johnson & Arnold (2022).

## Key Results
- InstructGPT adapted to syntactic biases (subject vs. non-subject) but not semantic biases (goal vs. source)
- InstructGPT shows strong inherent goal bias similar to humans, but fails to adapt to source/goal exposure patterns
- FLAN-UL2 produced only "yes" answers with no meaningful variation, indicating inability to handle the experimental design

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
In-context learning (ICL) simulates human psycholinguistic experimental exposure effects by recursively appending previous prompts and responses. This creates a local discourse representation that mirrors human short-term memory of recently encountered referential patterns, allowing the model to adapt pronoun interpretation based on recent syntactic or semantic biases.

### Mechanism 2
InstructGPT exhibits human-like syntactic priming (subject bias) but only partial semantic priming (goal bias) in pronoun interpretation. The model encodes abstract syntactic knowledge enabling shift based on recent exposure to subject vs. non-subject antecedents, but semantic learning from local context is weaker or overridden by global training biases.

### Mechanism 3
Model size and architecture critically affect the ability to generate meaningful variation. Larger decoder-only models (InstructGPT, 175B) can handle recursive ICL and produce varied responses, while smaller encoder-decoder models (FLAN-UL2, 20B) either fail to answer correctly or default to single answer types.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: In-context learning (ICL)
  - Why needed here: ICL allows simulation of human psycholinguistic studies by feeding the model previous prompts and its own responses, creating local discourse context that influences later interpretations
  - Quick check question: Can you explain how appending previous story prompts and model responses creates a "memory" of recent referential patterns for the model?

- Concept: Syntactic vs. semantic bias in pronoun interpretation
  - Why needed here: The study tests whether models adapt to syntactic biases (subject vs. non-subject) and semantic biases (goal vs. source) in the same way humans do
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between a subject bias and a goal bias in pronoun interpretation, and why might they be disentangled in experiments?

- Concept: Mixed-effects regression for psycholinguistic data
  - Why needed here: The analysis uses Bayesian mixed-effects logistic regression to model probability of subject/goal responses while accounting for random effects of participants and items
  - Quick check question: Why do we include both random intercepts and random slopes for participants and items in the regression model?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Story prompt + content question + reference question -> Recursive context appending -> Temperature-controlled generation -> Output parsing -> Mixed-effects regression

- Critical path:
  1. Generate and append context for each trial recursively
  2. Prompt model with full context + new question
  3. Parse model output for yes/no answer
  4. Check content question correctness (filter out incorrect)
  5. Aggregate responses by condition
  6. Run mixed-effects regression to test exposure effects

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Temperature vs. prompt-based participant simulation: Temperature is simpler but prompts could simulate different participant backgrounds if well-designed
  - Model size vs. resource cost: Larger models needed for meaningful variation but much more expensive
  - Recursive context vs. context window limits: Must ensure full context fits within model's context window

- Failure signatures:
  - 100% 'yes' answers (as with FLAN-UL2) → no meaningful variation, likely model size or architecture issue
  - Failure to answer content questions correctly → model not tracking story comprehension, filter out those trials
  - No exposure effect in regression → either model lacks relevant representations or context not retained

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run Experiment 1a (syntactic bias only) to verify model can adapt to subject vs. non-subject exposure
  2. Run Experiment 2a (syntactic + semantic variability) to check if semantic goal bias persists independently of syntactic exposure
  3. Run Experiment 2b (semantic bias only) to test if model can adapt to goal vs. source exposure or if strong goal bias dominates

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do decoder-only versus encoder-decoder architecture differences affect LLM performance in psycholinguistic priming experiments? The study only tested one model of each architecture type, making it impossible to isolate whether architecture or scale caused the performance difference.

### Open Question 2
What specific semantic representations do LLMs encode that enable goal bias but prevent adaptation to semantic exposure patterns? The behavioral analysis cannot reveal internal mechanisms by which LLMs identify thematic roles versus recognizing discourse-level patterns.

### Open Question 3
How would different instruction fine-tuning or chain-of-thought prompting strategies affect LLM adaptation to semantic exposure patterns? The study did not experiment with these prompting strategies, leaving their potential effects unknown.

## Limitations

- The in-context learning approach may not perfectly mirror human memory and processing of referential patterns
- Only two model architectures were tested, limiting generalizability to other LLM types
- The analysis assumes yes/no responses adequately capture the complexity of pronoun interpretation

## Confidence

- High confidence: InstructGPT's adaptation to syntactic biases is robust and statistically significant
- Medium confidence: InstructGPT shows only partial semantic priming, but interpretation of underlying causes remains uncertain
- Low confidence: FLAN-UL2's failure to produce variation is observed, but cause (size vs. architecture) is not definitively established

## Next Checks

1. Test additional model architectures (both larger decoder-only and encoder-decoder models) to isolate whether model size or architectural differences primarily determine the ability to generate meaningful variation
2. Implement systematic temperature control with fixed seeds and multiple runs to quantify the stability of adaptation effects and distinguish true priming from random variation
3. Conduct ablation studies removing either syntactic or semantic context to determine the relative contribution of each bias type to the observed adaptation patterns
