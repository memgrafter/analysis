# On The Fragility of Learned Reward Functions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.03652
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03652
- Authors: Aviral Kumar, Vincent Moeyersoons, Abhishek Gupta, Sergey Levine
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Preference-based reward learning can produce reward functions that fail to train new agents from scratch despite the initial agent behaving well.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical failure mode in preference-based reward learning called "relearning failures," where learned reward functions that produce well-behaved agents (samplers) fail to train new agents from scratch (relearners). The authors demonstrate that this phenomenon occurs across tabular and continuous control environments, with the severity of failures being sensitive to reward model design and trajectory dataset composition. Their experiments reveal that increasing the sampler's RL budget can paradoxically increase relearning failures, while reward ensembles can mitigate this issue in tabular settings by reducing off-distribution reward variance.

## Method Summary
The paper implements a preference-based reward learning pipeline using the Imitation library's wrapper around the seals benchmark suite. The method involves training a sampler agent online using reinforcement learning on learned rewards, collecting preference data through human or synthetic preferences over trajectory segments, training a reward model using the Bradley-Terry model, and then retraining new agents from scratch on the frozen learned reward. The experiments manipulate RL budget for the sampler, test reward ensembles, and analyze preference dataset composition across multiple environments including HalfCheetah-v3 and custom grid worlds.

## Key Results
- Increasing sampler RL budget leads to higher sampler returns but lower relearner performance, creating an anti-correlation between the two.
- Reward ensembles reduce relearning failures in tabular environments by lowering variance of off-distribution transitions.
- The composition of the preference dataset becomes increasingly dominated by high-reward trajectory segments as sampler training progresses, potentially causing reward overfitting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Increasing sampler RL budget leads to reward overfitting to high-reward regions, causing relearning failures.
- Mechanism: As the sampler explores more, the preference dataset becomes dominated by high-reward trajectory segments, leaving insufficient coverage of transitions from high to low reward regions. This causes the learned reward function to overfit to the high-reward region and provide poor supervision for randomly initialized policies.
- Core assumption: The preference dataset composition directly affects the learned reward function's ability to generalize to off-distribution states.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We find that increasing the number of training timesteps for the sampler can increase the severity of relearning failures"
  - [section 5.1]: "We find that despite higher RL budget leading to higher sampler returns during reward learning, the relearners' performance has the reverse trend"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - the corpus mentions reward misspecification but doesn't directly address the dataset composition issue.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reward ensembles reduce relearning failures by lowering variance of off-distribution transitions.
- Mechanism: Individual reward networks can produce spurious high rewards for off-distribution states (reward delusions). Reward ensembles, having lower variance off-distribution, produce less extreme spurious rewards, reducing the chance that the optimal policy will be attracted to these delusions during relearning.
- Core assumption: Off-distribution reward variance directly impacts relearning performance, with higher variance leading to more severe failures.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we demonstrate that reward ensembles can reduce reward hacking in tabular environments by reducing the variance of off-distribution transitions"
  - [section 5.2]: "Reward ensembles tends to have lower variance off distribution than an individual reward network"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence - the corpus doesn't address reward ensembles specifically.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The Bradley-Terry model used for preference learning can lead to reward hacking when the preference dataset lacks coverage of transition regions.
- Mechanism: The Bradley-Terry model assumes preferences are generated from a logistic function of reward differences. If the dataset lacks transitions between high and low reward regions, the model may learn incorrect reward structures that don't generalize to relearning.
- Core assumption: The Bradley-Terry model's assumptions about preference generation are violated when the dataset lacks certain transition types.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3]: "We follow the framework of learning a preference model ˆrφ from trajectory segment comparisons... The algorithm assumes these preferences have been sampled from the Bradley-Terry model"
  - [section 5.1]: "We hypothesize that having an overwhelming proportion of high-reward trajectory segments in the preference dataset — and little preference data on trajectories in the transition from high to low reward — may cause the reward model to effectively over-fit to the high-reward region"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence - the corpus doesn't address the Bradley-Terry model specifically.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Trajectory collection -> Preference elicitation -> Reward inference -> Policy optimization -> Evaluation
- Critical path: Sampler agent (trained online using RL on learned reward) -> Preference elicitation (human or synthetic preferences over trajectory segments) -> Reward model (neural network trained on preference dataset using Bradley-Terry model) -> Relearner agent (new agent trained from scratch on frozen learned reward)
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Dataset size vs. quality: More comparisons can improve reward quality but may also lead to overfitting if dominated by high-reward regions
  - Model complexity vs. generalization: Simpler reward models may generalize better but capture less nuance
  - Sampler training vs. relearner performance: More sampler training improves sampler performance but may degrade relearner performance
- Failure signatures:
  - Large performance gap between sampler and relearner
  - Reward model shows high variance on off-distribution states
  - Preference dataset becomes concentrated in high-reward regions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What causes the reward model to overfit to high-reward trajectory segments in preference datasets, leading to poor supervision for randomly initialized policies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors hypothesize that having an overwhelming proportion of high-reward trajectory segments in the preference dataset may cause the reward model to overfit to the high-reward region.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a definitive explanation for this phenomenon. It is only a hypothesis based on the observed correlation between the composition of the preference dataset and the severity of relearning failures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further experiments manipulating the composition of the preference dataset and measuring the resulting reward model performance on off-distribution transitions would provide evidence for or against this hypothesis.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why does increasing the number of training timesteps for the sampler increase the severity of relearning failures in HalfCheetah but not in the tabular setting?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe this discrepancy between HalfCheetah and the tabular environment, but do not provide a definitive explanation.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the reasons for this difference in behavior between continuous control and tabular environments.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the exploration strategies and reward model generalization capabilities in continuous control and tabular environments would help identify the factors contributing to this difference.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do reward ensembles reduce the variance of off-distribution transitions and mitigate reward hacking in tabular environments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors demonstrate that reward ensembles reduce the variance of off-distribution transitions, which in turn reduces the likelihood of reward hacking during relearning.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed explanation of the mechanism by which reward ensembles achieve this effect.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing the distribution of rewards produced by individual ensemble members and comparing it to the distribution of rewards produced by a single reward network would provide insights into the variance reduction mechanism.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What are the design decisions that can improve retraining performance in large-scale reward learning tasks, beyond those identified in small-scale experiments?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge that the design decisions that improve retraining performance in small-scale experiments may not be the same as those that address the problem at a larger scale.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the factors that affect retraining performance in large-scale reward learning tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating the performance of different reward learning algorithms and model architectures on large-scale tasks would help identify the design decisions that are most effective for improving retraining performance.

## Limitations
- Findings limited to preference-based reward learning with synthetic preferences, may not generalize to real human data
- Experimental scope constrained to a small number of environments (tabular and HalfCheetah)
- Scalability of proposed solutions to high-dimensional continuous control tasks is unclear

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The empirical demonstration of relearning failures in preference-based reward learning
- **Medium confidence**: The mechanistic explanations for why relearning failures occur (Bradley-Terry model assumptions and dataset composition effects)
- **Low confidence**: The scalability of proposed solutions to high-dimensional continuous control tasks

## Next Checks
1. Test real human preferences: Replicate the relearning failure experiments using actual human preference data rather than synthetic Bradley-Terry preferences to determine if the effect persists with more realistic input distributions.

2. Analyze reward landscape geometry: Visualize and quantify the learned reward function landscapes to directly observe whether high-reward regions show overfitting and whether ensembles indeed have lower off-distribution variance.

3. Evaluate alternative preference models: Compare Bradley-Terry against other preference learning approaches (e.g., direct preference optimization) to determine if the relearning failures are specific to the Bradley-Terry formulation or are more general across preference-based methods.
