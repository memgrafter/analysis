# Empirical study of the modulus as activation function in computer vision applications

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05993
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05993
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Modulus activation function achieves up to 15% accuracy improvement on CIFAR100 and 4% on CIFAR10 compared to standard activations

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the modulus activation function, defined as the absolute value function, which exhibits a constant derivative of ±1. This property eliminates the vanishing gradient and dying neurons problems that plague traditional activation functions. The modulus function is computationally efficient, consisting of simple bit-size operations, making it particularly suitable for TinyML and hardware implementations. Through extensive experiments on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and MNIST datasets using various architectures, the authors demonstrate that the modulus function outperforms benchmark activations in 75% of experiments. The paper also proposes two smooth approximations of the modulus function, with the hyperbolic tangent approximation achieving even better results than the original function.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct empirical experiments comparing the modulus activation function against standard activations across four network architectures (FC, Conv2, Conv6, VGG16) on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and MNIST datasets. Models are trained for 100 epochs using Adam optimizer with learning rate 1e-4, 5-epoch warmup from 1e-5, and cosine annealing to 1e-6. Training excludes dropout and batch normalization, with 30 random initializations per configuration. The modulus function is implemented as f(x) = |x| with derivative f'(x) = sign(x), along with two smooth approximations: SoftModulusQ and SoftModulusT. All data is normalized to the [-1, 1] range before training.

## Key Results
- Modulus activation achieves up to 15% accuracy improvement on CIFAR100 and 4% on CIFAR10 compared to benchmark activations
- Outperforms standard activations in 75% of experimental configurations across all tested architectures
- Smooth approximation using hyperbolic tangent (SoftModulusT) achieves even better results than the original modulus function
- Computational complexity equivalent to ReLU, making it suitable for hardware implementations
- Eliminates vanishing gradient and dying neurons problems due to constant derivative magnitude of ±1

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The modulus function eliminates vanishing gradient and dying neurons problems because its derivative is always ±1, ensuring constant gradient magnitude. In standard activation functions like ReLU, gradients can become zero for negative inputs (dying neurons) or very small for large magnitude inputs (vanishing gradients). The modulus function's derivative being ±1 regardless of input value prevents both issues by maintaining constant gradient flow. This assumes the network can learn effectively with non-monotonic activations and that constant gradient magnitude doesn't cause other optimization problems.

### Mechanism 2
The modulus function's simplicity (bit-size operations) makes it highly suitable for hardware implementations and TinyML applications. The modulus function can be expressed as f(x) = sgn(x)·x, where both the function and its derivative are simple sign operations. This allows for extremely efficient computation in hardware where bit-level operations are faster and consume less power than floating-point operations. This assumes hardware implementations benefit more from bit-size operations than from more complex but potentially more accurate activation functions.

### Mechanism 3
Non-monotonic activation functions like modulus can enable better generalization by introducing more complex nonlinear representations. Unlike monotonic functions that preserve input order, non-monotonic functions can create more flexible decision boundaries. The modulus function's V-shape allows it to model relationships that monotonic functions cannot capture, potentially improving model capacity and generalization. This assumes the increased representational flexibility of non-monotonic functions outweighs any potential optimization difficulties they introduce.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  Input normalization -> Convolution/fully connected layers -> Modulus activation application -> Pooling/other operations -> Loss computation and backpropagation

- **Critical path:**
  1. Input normalization to [-1, 1] range
  2. Convolution/fully connected layers
  3. Modulus activation application
  4. Pooling or other operations
  5. Loss computation and backpropagation

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Accuracy vs. computational efficiency: Modulus offers similar accuracy to complex functions with much lower computational cost
  - Non-monotonicity vs. stability: Modulus introduces non-monotonic behavior which may help or hurt depending on the task
  - Differentiability vs. performance: Original modulus isn't differentiable at 0, requiring smooth approximations in some cases

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Training instability or oscillation in deeper networks
  - Poor convergence when using modulus in architectures designed for monotonic activations
  - Numerical precision issues when implementing smooth approximations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the modulus activation function perform on tasks beyond image classification, such as natural language processing or reinforcement learning? The paper only tests the modulus function on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and MNIST datasets, which are image classification tasks. Testing the modulus function on a variety of tasks, including natural language processing and reinforcement learning, would provide insights into its generalizability and potential utility in different domains.

### Open Question 2
What is the impact of symmetry introduced by the modulus activation function on the performance and stability of the network in various tasks? The paper discusses the potential criticism of the modulus function introducing symmetry in neural networks, which may result in oscillation or instability. However, it does not provide a thorough investigation of the impact of symmetry on the performance and stability of the network in different tasks.

### Open Question 3
How does the modulus activation function compare to other non-monotonic activation functions in terms of performance and computational efficiency? The paper proposes the modulus activation function as a non-monotonic alternative and shows that it outperforms benchmark activations in 75% of the experiments. However, it does not directly compare the modulus function to other non-monotonic activations such as Mish or Swish.

## Limitations

- Experimental validation limited to standard computer vision datasets and relatively shallow to moderate architectures
- Hardware implementation benefits are theoretical rather than empirically validated on actual hardware platforms
- Training procedure excludes common techniques like batch normalization and dropout, limiting generalizability

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The computational efficiency claims are well-supported by mathematical analysis showing modulus operations are equivalent to bit-size operations. The elimination of vanishing gradient and dying neurons problems is mathematically sound given the constant derivative magnitude.

**Medium confidence**: The accuracy improvements (up to 15% on CIFAR100) are demonstrated empirically but may be architecture-dependent. The smooth approximations show promise but their benefits are less thoroughly validated across all experimental conditions.

**Low confidence**: Claims about hardware implementation benefits and TinyML suitability are theoretical rather than empirically validated. The paper doesn't provide concrete benchmarks on actual hardware platforms.

## Next Checks
