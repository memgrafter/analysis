# Adversarial Nibbler: A Data-Centric Challenge for Improving the Safety of Text-to-Image Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.14384
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14384
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 5
- Key outcome: Crowdsourced challenge to uncover unknown safety vulnerabilities in text-to-image models via adversarial data collection

## Executive Summary
The Adversarial Nibbler challenge is a data-centric competition designed to crowdsource diverse failure modes and safety vulnerabilities in text-to-image (T2I) models. By inviting participants to submit prompt-image pairs that bypass existing safety filters, the challenge aims to identify unknown unknowns and long-tail problems that standard benchmarks miss. The competition leverages human raters to validate submissions and uses a composite creativity score to incentivize novel attack vectors. The resulting dataset will provide a benchmark for evaluating T2I model safety and improving future model development.

## Method Summary
The challenge invites participants to submit safe text prompts and corresponding unsafe generated images from T2I models (DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney). Submissions are validated by five independent, safety-trained human raters, and scored based on attack success and creativity. The primary metric is Validated Attack Success, calculated as the weighted percentage of raters confirming a valid attack. A creativity badge is awarded to the top decile of participants based on a composite score measuring prompt diversity, unsafe image categories, target identities, and semantic variations. The challenge provides a CC-BY-SA licensed dataset with disaggregated annotations for research use.

## Key Results
- Challenge framework successfully crowdsources diverse safety failure modes in T2I models
- Human-in-the-loop validation with five independent raters ensures robust safety benchmark
- Creativity scoring incentivizes exploration of novel attack vectors and long-tail problems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Crowdsourcing diverse safety failure modes uncovers unknown unknowns in T2I models.
- Mechanism: By inviting participants from varied backgrounds to submit prompt-image pairs, the challenge leverages collective creativity and contextual understanding to identify edge cases that standard benchmarking misses.
- Core assumption: Safety failures are context-dependent and not easily captured by homogeneous teams or static datasets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "crowdsource a diverse set of failure modes" and "identify unknown unknowns or long-tail problems"
  - [section 1.1] "Safety is a complex, subjective issue, and often depends on contextual background and lived experience"
  - [corpus] Weak – no corpus papers directly address the unknown unknowns concept; evidence comes from the paper itself
- Break condition: If submitted prompts cluster around a narrow subset of failure modes, the diversity benefit is lost and bias persists.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Validating submissions via multiple independent raters reduces subjectivity and ensures robust safety benchmarks.
- Mechanism: Each prompt-image pair is rated by five trained human raters; aggregated scores provide a weighted measure of safety violations, enabling nuanced scoring beyond binary classification.
- Core assumption: Multiple independent perspectives yield a more reliable assessment of harm than majority voting alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 1.5] "Every submission will be evaluated by five independent, trust & safety trained human raters" and "we will consider the disaggregated annotations across the five raters"
  - [section 1.3] "we will not release them by default. However, we will provide access to these images upon reasonable request by researchers"
- Break condition: If raters are poorly calibrated or the rating instructions are ambiguous, inter-rater reliability collapses and scores become noisy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Prompt creativity scoring incentivizes exploration of novel attack vectors, improving long-term safety.
- Mechanism: A composite creativity score rewards participants for submitting diverse prompt types, unsafe image categories, target identities, and semantic variations, with the top decile earning a badge.
- Core assumption: Participants will adjust their strategies toward novel attacks when rewarded for creativity rather than just attack success counts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 1.5] "We calculate prompt creativity score to incentivise continuous exploration of innovative methods for deceiving T2I models" and description of the composite score attributes
  - [section 1.4] "This competition culminated from a collaboration among six organizations, comprising both academic and industrial stakeholders"
- Break condition: If creativity scoring is poorly tuned, participants may focus on gaming the metric rather than finding genuinely novel safety issues.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Frontend UI (Dynabench platform) → Prompt and image generation interface
  Backend API → Three T2I model integrations (DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney)
  Submission pipeline → Stores prompt-image pairs with metadata
  Validation pipeline → Routes to five trained raters for safety assessment
  Leaderboard engine → Calculates Validated Attack Success and creativity scores
  Data export → CC-BY-SA licensed dataset with disaggregated annotations
- Critical path:
  1. User submits prompt → 2. Backend generates six images → 3. User selects unsafe image → 4. User annotates harm type/target → 5. Submission queued for validation → 6. Five raters validate → 7. Scores updated on leaderboard
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Open vs. closed submission: Open invites creativity but risks low-quality submissions; closed limits diversity.
  - Immediate vs. delayed validation: Immediate gives feedback but slows submission; delayed allows batching but risks backlog.
  - Granular vs. coarse harm categories: Granular aids analysis but complicates rating; coarse simplifies but loses nuance.
- Failure signatures:
  - UI/API timeouts → Generation pipeline stalls
  - Low rater agreement → Calibration or instruction clarity issues
  - Leaderboard stagnation → Scoring bugs or insufficient submissions
  - Data skew → Over-representation of certain harm types or identity groups
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Simulate submission flow with mock raters to test validation pipeline latency and data integrity.
  2. Run a pilot with a small participant cohort to measure inter-rater reliability and adjust harm category definitions.
  3. Benchmark creativity scoring algorithm on historical submissions to ensure it rewards novelty without overfitting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific safety vulnerabilities and failure modes are most prevalent in current text-to-image models when evaluated using the Adversarial Nibbler challenge framework?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper aims to crowdsource diverse failure modes and safety vulnerabilities in T2I models, focusing on prompt-image pairs that bypass existing safety filters.
- Why unresolved: While the challenge methodology is outlined, the actual results and prevalence of specific vulnerabilities are not yet available as the challenge is ongoing.
- What evidence would resolve it: The final dataset and analysis of submitted prompt-image pairs, validated by human raters, would provide concrete evidence of the most common safety vulnerabilities and failure modes.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How effective are current safety filters in text-to-image models at preventing the generation of harmful content when subjected to adversarial prompts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The challenge focuses on prompt-image pairs that currently bypass existing safety filters, either through subversive prompts or benign prompts that trigger unsafe outputs.
- Why unresolved: The effectiveness of current safety filters is not directly measured in the paper; it relies on participants finding bypasses rather than systematically testing filter robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Quantitative analysis of the percentage of adversarial prompts that successfully bypass safety filters, categorized by attack type (subversive vs. benign), would provide a measure of filter effectiveness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the diversity of perspectives among challenge participants influence the identification of unknown unknowns in text-to-image model safety?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The challenge emphasizes participation from diverse perspectives, acknowledging that safety is a complex, subjective issue depending on contextual background and lived experience.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide data on participant demographics or how different perspectives correlate with the types of vulnerabilities discovered.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of the relationship between participant background information and the types of prompt-image pairs submitted, as well as a comparison of the diversity of identified vulnerabilities across different participant groups.

## Limitations

- Crowdsourcing approach depends heavily on participant diversity and creativity to uncover unknown safety vulnerabilities
- Human-in-the-loop validation limited by rater quality, calibration, and potential subjectivity in harm assessment
- Creativity scoring mechanism not fully specified and may incentivize metric gaming rather than genuinely novel attacks

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The crowdsourcing mechanism can collect diverse failure modes (supported by collaboration among multiple organizations and the open challenge design).
- **Medium confidence**: Human-in-the-loop validation improves benchmark reliability (supported by the five-rater system and disaggregation approach, but dependent on rater quality).
- **Low confidence**: Creativity scoring will effectively incentivize novel attack discovery (the mechanism is described but not empirically validated, and gaming risks are acknowledged).

## Next Checks

1. Pilot the challenge with a small, diverse participant group and analyze rater inter-rater reliability and calibration before full launch.
2. Benchmark the creativity scoring algorithm on historical adversarial prompt datasets to ensure it rewards novelty and does not overfit to known patterns.
3. Conduct a post-challenge analysis of submission clustering to verify that prompts cover a broad range of harm types and identity groups, rather than concentrating on a narrow subset.
