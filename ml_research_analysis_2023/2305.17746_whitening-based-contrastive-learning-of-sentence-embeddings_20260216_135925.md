# Whitening-based Contrastive Learning of Sentence Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17746
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17746
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 18
- Key outcome: WhitenedCSE achieves 78.78% Spearman correlation on STS tasks, representing a 2.53% improvement over BERTbase.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces WhitenedCSE, a novel method that integrates whitening into contrastive learning for sentence embedding. The approach addresses the challenge of learning sentence representations that exhibit both uniformity and alignment in the feature space. By combining shuffled group whitening with multi-positive contrastive learning, WhitenedCSE achieves state-of-the-art results on semantic textual similarity tasks while maintaining computational efficiency.

## Method Summary
WhitenedCSE combines contrastive learning with shuffled group whitening (SGW) to improve sentence embeddings. The method uses a BERT-like encoder to extract features, applies SGW to decorrelate and diversify features, and employs a multi-positive contrastive loss to enhance alignment. SGW randomly divides features into groups, applies whitening within each group, and reshuffles to create multiple positive samples from a single anchor. The approach is trained on 1M English Wikipedia sentences and evaluated on seven STS tasks and transfer tasks.

## Key Results
- Achieves 78.78% Spearman correlation on STS tasks
- 2.53% improvement over BERTbase baseline
- Consistent improvement across all seven STS tasks tested
- Maintains very slight computational overhead compared to standard contrastive learning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Whitening and contrastive learning have complementary uniformity mechanisms despite both targeting uniform feature distributions. Whitening decorrelates features through matrix decomposition and projects them into a spherical space, while contrastive learning achieves uniformity by widening distances between positive and negative samples iteratively. Whitening provides global scattering while contrastive learning enforces local pushing, resulting in complementary effects.

### Mechanism 2
Shuffled group whitening increases positive sample diversity by creating multiple distinct feature representations from a single anchor. By randomly dividing features into groups along the channel axis and applying whitening independently within each group, SGW creates multiple whitened versions of the same feature. Each shuffle produces different grouping patterns, resulting in diverse positive samples that improve alignment.

### Mechanism 3
The multi-positive contrastive loss with enhanced diversity improves alignment more effectively than standard dual-positive contrastive learning. Using multiple diverse positive samples from SGW in the contrastive loss pulls the anchor closer to a broader semantic neighborhood, strengthening alignment while maintaining uniformity.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Group whitening
  - Why needed here: Standard whitening requires large batch sizes to estimate the full covariance matrix, which is impractical in NLP. Group whitening decorrelates smaller groups of features independently, making it feasible with typical batch sizes.
  - Quick check question: Why does group whitening use ZCA instead of PCA for the whitening transformation?

- Concept: Contrastive learning uniformity and alignment
  - Why needed here: Understanding the dual objectives of contrastive learning (pulling positives close for alignment, pushing negatives apart for uniformity) is crucial for appreciating why whitening can complement it.
  - Quick check question: How do the alignment and uniformity objectives conflict with each other in contrastive learning?

- Concept: Matrix decomposition and covariance estimation
  - Why needed here: Whitening relies on computing eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the covariance matrix. Understanding this mathematical foundation is essential for grasping how SGW works.
  - Quick check question: What happens if the covariance matrix is not positive definite when computing the whitening transformation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: BERT encoder -> Shuffled Group Whitening (SGW) -> Multi-positive contrastive loss -> MLP layer
- Critical path: Input sentence → BERT encoder → [CLS] token embedding → SGW module (multiple times for positive samples) → Multiple positives + negatives → Multi-positive contrastive loss → Loss backpropagation → Update model parameters
- Design tradeoffs: Group size vs. whitening effectiveness; Number of positive samples vs. computational cost; Whitening intensity vs. feature preservation
- Failure signatures: Poor uniformity (features remain clustered), degraded alignment (positive pairs remain far apart), training instability (sudden performance drops)
- First 3 experiments: 1) Verify SGW produces different whitened representations with different shuffles by visualizing feature distributions; 2) Test the effect of group size on uniformity by measuring covariance matrix diagonal dominance; 3) Evaluate alignment improvement by measuring average distance between original and SGW-generated positives

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the shuffled group whitening (SGW) module compare to other whitening techniques in terms of effectiveness for sentence embedding learning? The paper compares SGW to PCA-whitening and ZCA-whitening, showing that SGW outperforms these methods, but does not provide a comprehensive comparison of SGW to other whitening techniques in terms of their impact on sentence embedding learning.

### Open Question 2
What is the optimal number of positive samples to use in the multi-positive contrastive loss function? The paper conducts an ablation study to determine the impact of the number of positive samples on performance, but does not provide a definitive answer to the optimal number.

### Open Question 3
How does the proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art methods in terms of efficiency and scalability? The paper mentions that the proposed method incurs very slight computational overhead, but does not provide a comprehensive comparison to other state-of-the-art methods in terms of efficiency and scalability.

## Limitations

- The claim that whitening and contrastive learning have complementary uniformity mechanisms lacks direct empirical validation
- The effectiveness of SGW depends heavily on the choice of group size and number of shuffles, which are not thoroughly explored
- Evaluation focuses primarily on STS tasks and a limited set of transfer tasks, limiting broader applicability assessment

## Confidence

- High confidence: Experimental results showing consistent improvement over contrastive learning baseline on STS tasks
- Medium confidence: Theoretical claims about complementary uniformity mechanisms
- Medium confidence: Claim that SGW increases positive sample diversity in a meaningful way
