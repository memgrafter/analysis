# A Corpus-based Analysis of Attitudinal Changes in Lin Yutang's Self-translation of Between Tears and Laughter

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.08173
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08173
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: Lin Yutang's Chinese self-translation of "Between Tears and Laughter" shows significantly less anger (M=0.7755, SD=0.2775) than the English original (M=1.1036, SD=0.3861), t=2.2892, p=0.0331

## Executive Summary
This study employs corpus linguistic tools to analyze attitudinal changes in Lin Yutang's self-translation of "Between Tears and Laughter." Using LIWC2015 to measure psychological word categories and AntConc3.5.8 for n-gram analysis, the research demonstrates that Lin consciously reduced expressions of anger in his Chinese translation compared to the English original, reflecting different writing purposes and readerships. The findings suggest that corpus-based methodologies can effectively detect and quantify attitudinal shifts in self-translation, providing valuable insights for both translators and translation researchers.

## Method Summary
The methodology involved building parallel corpora from Lin Yutang's English original (first 11 chapters, 31,020 words) and Chinese translation (11 chapters, 29,784 words). Chinese text was segmented using ICTCLAS2012 before analysis. LIWC2015 analyzed psychological word categories in both corpora, with statistical comparisons performed using t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests. AntConc3.5.8 extracted n-grams (3-7 words, minimum frequency 3) containing emotion words, which were then manually compared between versions to identify attitudinal modifications. The analysis also compared Lin's self-translation with Xu Chengbin's co-translation to examine consistency patterns.

## Key Results
- Significant reduction in anger expressions: Chinese self-translation shows M=0.7755 (SD=0.2775) versus English original M=1.1036 (SD=0.3861), t=2.2892, p=0.0331
- Attitudinal changes reflected in n-gram modifications, with omission and replacement strategies used to reduce anger expression
- Co-translator Xu's translation showed no significant difference in anger compared to corresponding original sections
- Different translation strategies employed based on target readership: persuasion of Western readers vs. encouragement of Chinese readers

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Corpus tools (LIWC and AntConc) enable detection of attitudinal shifts in self-translation by quantifying emotional word frequencies
- Mechanism: By comparing frequencies of psychologically relevant categories (e.g., "anger") between original and self-translation, statistically significant differences reveal attitudinal changes
- Core assumption: The presence and frequency of emotion-laden words and phrases reliably reflect the author's underlying attitude toward the target audience
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The results showed a significant difference, with less anger in the Chinese self-translation (M=0.7755, SD=0.2775) than in the English original (M=1.1036, SD=0.3861), t=2.2892, p=0.0331."
  - [section] "The results show that due to different writing purposes and readerships, there is less anger in Lin's self-translation... than in the first 11 chapters of the English original... which is a significant difference."
  - [corpus] Table 1 and Table 3 provide quantitative evidence of frequency differences
- Break condition: If emotional word frequencies are influenced by translation strategies unrelated to attitudinal shifts (e.g., cultural adaptation, idiomatic constraints), the mechanism fails to isolate genuine attitudinal change

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Analysis of n-gram modifications in self-translation exposes subtle attitudinal changes that single-word frequency analysis might miss
- Mechanism: Identifying n-grams containing emotion words and comparing their translations reveals whether the author altered meaning, tone, or emphasis
- Core assumption: Authors unconsciously repeat lexical patterns; conscious modification of these patterns signals deliberate attitudinal adjustment
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "This attitudinal change is also reflected in the translations of n-grams containing anger words."
  - [section] "Examples (1) to (3) fully demonstrate Lin's attitude changes in the self-translation owing to different writing purposes and readerships."
  - [corpus] Examples (4)-(10) show how n-gram translations differ between English and Chinese versions
- Break condition: If n-gram modifications are driven by translation constraints or stylistic preferences rather than attitudinal shifts, the mechanism cannot reliably attribute change to attitude

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Corpus-based co-translation methodology can maintain stylistic and attitudinal consistency between principal translator and co-translator
- Mechanism: Co-translators use LIWC and AntConc to match psychological category frequencies and n-gram patterns from the principal translator's work, ensuring alignment in emotional tone and style
- Core assumption: Stylistic consistency in translation can be quantified and reproduced through corpus analysis
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The other three well-known self-translators are Zhang Ailing, Xiao Qian, and Bai Xianyong."
  - [section] "This study took the following seven steps to conduct data analysis..."
  - [corpus] The comparison of Lin Yutang's self-translation and Xu Chengbin's co-translation in Table 5 shows Xu's attempt to maintain consistency, albeit with less success in containing anger
- Break condition: If the principal translator's style is too idiosyncratic or context-dependent to be captured by frequency analysis, the mechanism fails to guide effective co-translation

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preparation -> LIWC analysis -> Statistical testing -> AntConc n-gram extraction -> Manual parallel corpus analysis -> Validation
- Critical path: 1. Prepare parallel corpora 2. Run LIWC analysis on both corpora 3. Identify statistically significant differences 4. Extract n-grams using AntConc 5. Compare n-gram translations 6. Conduct manual parallel analysis
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Quantitative vs. qualitative analysis: Statistical methods provide objectivity but may miss nuanced meaning changes that qualitative review captures
  - Word-level vs. phrase-level analysis: Single-word frequencies are easier to compare but n-grams reveal more about attitudinal shifts in context
  - Tool selection: LIWC offers psychological categorization but may not capture all cultural nuances; AntConc is powerful for n-gram analysis but requires manual review
- Failure signatures:
  - High frequency of n-grams with no clear attitudinal significance (false positives)
  - Statistical significance without meaningful qualitative differences
  - Cultural/linguistic differences creating artificial frequency disparities unrelated to attitude
  - Over-reliance on tools without human validation leading to misinterpretation
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test corpus tool pipeline on a smaller, controlled text pair with known attitudinal differences to validate methodology
  2. Compare results using different psychological category dictionaries to assess tool sensitivity
  3. Apply methodology to a text with minimal expected attitudinal change to establish baseline for false positive rates

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do attitudinal changes manifest across different genres of Lin Yutang's self-translations beyond political/philosophical works?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes this study focuses on "Between Tears and Laughter" which is "a book on politics and philosophy," but acknowledges Lin wrote "more than 30 books in English, most of which describe China's cultural aspects" including essays, novels, and textbooks
- Why unresolved: The study only examined one specific book with a particular political focus. Lin's other works may show different patterns of attitudinal shifts depending on genre and subject matter
- What evidence would resolve it: Corpus-based comparative analysis of attitudinal shifts across Lin's different genres (essays, novels, textbooks, dictionaries) would reveal whether the anger reduction pattern is specific to political writing or applies more broadly

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific linguistic mechanisms beyond word frequency changes contribute to attitudinal shifts in self-translation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "modification in the self-translation only occurs once for each type of the following n-grams" and that changes included "omission and replacement," suggesting word frequency alone doesn't capture the full picture of attitudinal shifts
- Why unresolved: The study relied primarily on LIWC word frequency analysis and only briefly mentioned n-gram patterns without systematically analyzing syntactic structures, metaphors, or other linguistic features that might signal attitudinal changes
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed qualitative analysis of syntactic structures, metaphorical expressions, and other discourse features in both versions, combined with systematic n-gram analysis, would reveal the full range of linguistic mechanisms used to shift attitudes

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do cultural and historical contexts influence the specific choices made in self-translation to modify attitudes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that Lin's different purposes for writing (persuading Western readers vs. encouraging Chinese readers) and the historical context of WWII influenced his attitudinal choices, such as omitting references to the war's cruelty for Chinese readers
- Why unresolved: The study provides specific examples of context-driven changes but doesn't systematically analyze how broader cultural and historical factors shaped the overall pattern of attitudinal shifts across the entire text
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of attitudinal shifts in Lin's self-translations across different historical periods and for different target cultures would reveal how cultural and historical contexts systematically influence translation choices

## Limitations
- The methodology assumes direct correlation between word frequency and attitudinal expression, which may not hold true across languages with different expressive patterns
- Comparison between self-translation and co-translation introduces variables beyond authorial attitude, including individual translator style and cultural mediation
- Frequency-based analysis cannot capture contextual nuances where anger is expressed through indirect language or cultural-specific idioms

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The statistical analysis showing reduced anger frequency in Chinese self-translation compared to English original (t=2.2892, p=0.0331) is methodologically sound and reproducible
- **Medium Confidence**: The attribution of attitudinal change to different readership purposes is plausible but not definitively proven, as other factors could explain the frequency differences
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that corpus tools can help co-translators maintain consistency in attitude lacks empirical support beyond the single comparison case presented
