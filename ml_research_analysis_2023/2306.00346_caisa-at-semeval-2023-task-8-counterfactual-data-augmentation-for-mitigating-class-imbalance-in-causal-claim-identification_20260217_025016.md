# CAISA at SemEval-2023 Task 8: Counterfactual Data Augmentation for Mitigating Class Imbalance in Causal Claim Identification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00346
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00346
- Reference count: 5
- The paper addresses class imbalance in medical causal claim identification using counterfactual data augmentation by verb replacement

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the class imbalance problem in medical causal claim identification by proposing a novel counterfactual data augmentation technique based on verb replacement. The authors systematically compare their approach against entity replacement, noise insertion, YouChat-based augmentation, and embedding-space augmentation. Their method replaces verbs in minority class sentences with other verbs from the training data, creating synthetic examples that preserve semantic structure while increasing lexical diversity. Experiments on two subtasks demonstrate that verb replacement significantly improves performance on the minority class, with relative improvements of up to 2% in F1 score.

## Method Summary
The authors propose verb replacement as a counterfactual data augmentation technique for mitigating class imbalance in medical causal claim identification. The method involves creating a dictionary of verbs from the training data, then replacing verbs in minority class sentences with other verbs from this dictionary while preserving tense. This generates synthetic training examples that maintain the original context but with different action words. The augmented data is combined with original training samples to train DistilBERT and BioBERT models, which are compared against baseline CRF models and other augmentation methods including entity replacement, AEDA, YouChat, and embedding-space augmentation.

## Key Results
- Verb replacement significantly improves F1 score for the minority class (claims), with relative improvements up to 2%
- The method outperforms other augmentation techniques in some cases, particularly for the claims class
- DistilBERT consistently outperforms CRF models across augmentation methods, showing nearly 10% overall improvement
- 100 augmented samples showed better results than 400 samples, suggesting that excessive augmentation can introduce noise

- Claim: Counterfactual augmentation creates more diverse training examples than simple oversampling

- Core assumption: The minority class has sufficient verb variety in the training data to enable meaningful replacements
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2]: "In counterfactual data augmentation, words (or phrases) in a sentence are replaced with opposite (or different) ones from other sentences."

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does verb replacement consistently outperform other augmentation methods across different model architectures and datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper compares verb replacement with entity replacement, AEDA, YouChat, and BAT, showing that verb replacement outperforms them in some cases
- Why unresolved: The experiments were limited to DistilBERT and BioBERT models on a specific medical causal claim dataset. The performance of verb replacement may vary with different model architectures, datasets, and tasks
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with verb replacement on various model architectures (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa) and datasets from different domains (e.g., legal, news) would provide evidence of its generalizability and consistency

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of augmented samples to generate for the minority class?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper experiments with 100 and 400 augmented samples for the claims class, observing different effects on model performance. It also mentions that more augmentations can introduce noise and have a negative effect
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a systematic study on the relationship between the number of augmented samples and model performance. It is unclear how the optimal number of augmented samples varies with the dataset size, class imbalance ratio, and model complexity
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a comprehensive study by varying the number of augmented samples and measuring the impact on model performance across different datasets and model architectures would provide insights into the optimal number of augmented samples

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of antonym affect the performance of verb replacement augmentation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using WordNet to find antonyms for verb replacement, but does not discuss the impact of choosing different antonyms or the availability of antonyms in the training data
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide an analysis of how the choice of antonym influences the quality of augmented samples and the subsequent model performance. It is unclear whether all antonyms are equally effective or if some antonyms are more suitable than others for specific contexts
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting an ablation study by comparing the performance of verb replacement with different antonyms or by controlling the availability of antonyms in the training data would provide insights into the impact of antonym choice on augmentation effectiveness

## Limitations

- Data Diversity Uncertainty: The effectiveness of verb replacement depends heavily on the diversity of verbs present in the training data, which is not quantified in the paper
- Semantic Drift Risk: There is no evaluation of whether the semantic meaning of causal claims is maintained after verb replacement
- Generalizability Constraints: The approach was validated only on medical causal claim identification and may not generalize to other domains

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The observation that DistilBERT consistently outperforms CRF models across augmentation methods is well-supported by the experimental results
- Medium Confidence: The claim that verb replacement is superior to other augmentation methods is supported but not definitive, with limited testing on sample sizes
- Low Confidence: The fundamental assumption that verbs are the most critical determinant of causal claim classification lacks direct empirical support in the paper
