# Random Feedback Alignment Algorithms to train Neural Networks: Why do they Align?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02325
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02325
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Gradient alignment is necessary but not sufficient for FA performance; FA works by finding stable fixed points through a mechanism involving output layer gradient descent and hidden layer compatibility.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why random feedback alignment (FA) works for training neural networks despite replacing the true gradient with random feedback. The authors show that FA has fixed points corresponding to loss extrema, and gradient alignment serves as a stability criterion for these fixed points. However, high gradient alignment does not guarantee good performance. Through experiments on MNIST, they demonstrate that FA's effectiveness comes from a mechanism where the output layer drives the network toward fixed points, and hidden layers must find compatible updates for convergence. This challenges the common assumption that gradient alignment is the primary driver of FA's success.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze feedback alignment by modeling it as a spatially inhomogeneous random walk in weight space. They derive that FA's fixed points correspond to loss extrema when random feedback matrices are full rank, and establish gradient alignment as a stability criterion for these fixed points. Experiments train 3-layer feedforward networks on MNIST using FA and direct feedback alignment (DFA) with various initial weight scales. They measure accuracy, gradient alignment between FA pseudo-gradient and true gradient, and weight alignment between network weights and feedback matrices to understand FA's performance characteristics.

## Key Results
- FA pseudo-gradient updates have fixed points corresponding to loss function extrema
- Gradient alignment is a necessary stability criterion for FA fixed points but does not guarantee good performance
- Initial weight scale affects FA performance more than gradient alignment does
- FA performance on MNIST shows that alignment is not the sole driver of success

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: FA random walks have fixed points corresponding to loss extrema.
- Mechanism: The FA pseudo-gradient update shares fixed points with backpropagation because random feedback matrices are full rank. At these fixed points, the FA update vanishes, just as the BP gradient does.
- Core assumption: Random feedback matrices are full rank and their nullspace vanishes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We observe that the feedback alignment update rule has fixed points, which correspond to extrema of the loss function."
  - [section]: "As a consequence, the fixed points of FA will be local extrema of the loss function, and local attractors under the BP."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers discuss low-dimensional error feedback and training spiking neural networks, but don't directly address fixed points of FA.
- Break condition: If random feedback matrices are not full rank, fixed points may not correspond to loss extrema.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Gradient alignment is a stability criterion for FA fixed points.
- Mechanism: For a fixed point to be stable under FA pseudo-gradient update, alignment between FA update and BP gradient must be positive, ensuring loss doesn't increase.
- Core assumption: FA update is small enough for first-order loss approximation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We show that gradient alignment is a stability criterion for those fixed points."
  - [section]: "Thus, eq. 7 formulates a necessary condition for the stability of a local extremum with respect to the update by the FA pseudo-gradient. This stability criterion states that the alignment measure needs to be positive."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers discuss deep learning without weight symmetry and random matrix theory for SGD, but don't directly address gradient alignment as stability criterion.
- Break condition: If FA update is too large, first-order approximation may not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: FA performance is not solely driven by gradient alignment.
- Mechanism: FA works by finding fixed points stable under FA update. Gradient alignment is necessary but not sufficient. Other factors like initial weights and training example distribution also matter.
- Core assumption: FA random walk can explore weight space and find stable fixed points.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We will also show that gradient alignment while necessary for FA to find good solution, is not a sufficient criterion."
  - [section]: "We find from the simulations presented in fig. 5, as expected, that good weight alignment translates to high gradient alignment. The key message of the graph is that the initially unaligned simulation performs best, amongst the three runs."
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus papers discuss training large neural networks with low-dimensional error feedback and forward direct feedback alignment for online gradient estimates, but don't directly address role of gradient alignment in FA performance.
- Break condition: If FA random walk cannot explore weight space or if no stable fixed points exist, FA may not work well.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize neural network with random weights
  2. Compute FA pseudo-gradient update for each layer
  3. Update weights using FA pseudo-gradient
  4. Repeat steps 2-3 for each training example
- Design tradeoffs:
  - FA vs BP: FA is more biologically plausible but may not perform as well as BP on some tasks
  - Random feedback matrices: Choice affects FA performance
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on convolutional neural networks
  - High gradient alignment but low accuracy
  - Failure to find stable fixed points

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Under what conditions do local extrema of the loss function exist in parameter space for feedback alignment algorithms?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that there is no guarantee that FA finds extrema compatible with the alignment criterion, and failure could occur due to sparse extrema or initialization in regions with small update steps.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of the distribution of local extrema in parameter space for FA algorithms. It only conjectures that sparse extrema or poor initialization could lead to failure.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis proving the existence and distribution of local extrema in parameter space for FA algorithms under various conditions, or experimental evidence showing the relationship between initialization and success rate of FA.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the initial speed of exploration in feedback alignment affect the eventual performance of the algorithm?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests that FA with initially vanishing weights may perform better because the initial speed of exploration is faster, allowing accuracy to increase over fewer update steps. However, it also shows that gradient alignment is not necessarily the only reason for FA's performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides experimental evidence for the effect of initial weights on the initial speed of exploration, but does not fully explain the relationship between initial speed and eventual performance. It also does not rule out other factors that could contribute to FA's performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study of the relationship between initial speed of exploration, gradient alignment, and eventual performance of FA under various conditions, controlling for other factors.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the mechanism by which feedback alignment finds local extrema of the loss function?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper conjectures that FA works by driving the network as a whole towards a fixed point corresponding to a vanishing gradient of the loss function in the output layer, and then the hidden and input layers drift until they find a fixed point compatible with the stability criterion.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides a conjectured mechanism, but does not provide a rigorous theoretical proof or experimental evidence to support it. It also does not explain how FA avoids getting stuck in regions with near-zero update step sizes.
- What evidence would resolve it: A rigorous theoretical analysis of the dynamics of FA algorithms, showing how they find and converge to local extrema, or experimental evidence demonstrating the proposed mechanism and how FA avoids getting stuck in regions with near-zero update step sizes.

## Limitations
- Analysis primarily focuses on fully-connected networks on MNIST, with limited exploration of convolutional architectures
- Stability criterion analysis assumes continuous weight space and small update steps
- Relationship between gradient alignment and performance may be task-dependent

## Confidence

- Gradient alignment as stability criterion: Medium
- Gradient alignment not being sufficient for good performance: Medium
