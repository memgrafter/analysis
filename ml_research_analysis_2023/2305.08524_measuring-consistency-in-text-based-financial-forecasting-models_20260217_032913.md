# Measuring Consistency in Text-based Financial Forecasting Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.08524
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08524
- Reference count: 31
- Key outcome: FinTrust measures logical consistency of financial forecasting models using four semantic transformations, revealing significant accuracy drops and profitability impact from inconsistency

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces FinTrust, a tool to measure the logical consistency of text-based financial forecasting models. It defines four types of consistency transformations—negation, symmetric, additive, and transitive—and evaluates their impact on both pre-trained language models and fine-tuned financial forecasting models. Experiments show that state-of-the-art models suffer significant accuracy drops after consistency transformations, with some performing worse than random guessing. Trading simulations reveal that inconsistent predictions lead to lower profitability, highlighting the importance of consistency as a trustworthiness metric. FinTrust provides a systematic way to assess and improve model robustness in financial NLP applications.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes FinTrust, a framework for evaluating the logical consistency of financial forecasting models by applying four meaning-preserving transformations to financial text: negation (flipping sentiment polarity), symmetric (converting bullish to bearish statements), additive (incorporating numerical relationships), and transitive (using hierarchical relationships). The framework evaluates both pre-trained language models (BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT, FinBERT) and fine-tuned financial forecasting models (HTML, MRDM, Event, XGBoost) on earnings call transcripts. Consistency is measured by comparing model predictions on original and transformed text, with trading simulations used to validate the real-world impact of consistency on profitability.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art models show significant accuracy drops after consistency transformations, with some performing worse than random guessing
- Consistency scores correlate weakly with accuracy (Pearson correlation 0.314), demonstrating consistency as an independent trustworthiness metric
- Trading simulations show inconsistent predictions lead to lower profitability and Sharpe ratios compared to consistent models
- FinBERT and Event achieve the highest consistency scores, while most models underperform simple "Buy-all" baseline in trading simulations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: FinTrust measures consistency by applying meaning-preserving transformations to financial text and evaluating if model predictions remain invariant.
- Mechanism: Four logical transformations (negation, symmetric, additive, transitive) are defined to generate test cases. For each transformation, the model's prediction on the original and transformed text is compared. If predictions differ, the model is flagged as inconsistent.
- Core assumption: The transformations preserve semantic meaning while altering surface form, so consistent predictions reflect robust reasoning rather than memorization of spurious patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "define four types of consistency transformations—negation, symmetric, additive, and transitive—and evaluate their impact..."
  - [section] "four logical consistency transformation approaches are defined to evaluate if the model maintains the same logical behavior as humans"
  - [corpus] "Found 25 related papers... average citations=0.0" (weak external validation)
- Break condition: If transformations inadvertently change semantic meaning or if the model exploits superficial patterns that survive transformations, consistency scores become unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Consistency scores correlate weakly with accuracy, making them a complementary metric for trustworthiness.
- Mechanism: A consistency score is computed as the average of transformation-specific consistency metrics. Correlation analysis with accuracy shows low Pearson correlation (0.314), indicating that high accuracy does not imply high consistency.
- Core assumption: Models can achieve high accuracy by learning spurious correlations while being inconsistent under semantic perturbations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "experiments show that state-of-the-art models suffer significant accuracy drops after consistency transformations"
  - [section] "the most consistent model (Event) is not necessarily the highest in accuracy (HTML)... overall Pearson correlation coefficient between the consistency score and accuracy is only 0.314"
  - [corpus] "FinTrust: A Comprehensive Benchmark of Trustworthiness Evaluation in Finance Domain" (related work)
- Break condition: If the correlation between consistency and accuracy increases with larger, more diverse datasets, consistency may lose its value as an independent trustworthiness indicator.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Inconsistent predictions lead to poor trading performance, demonstrating real-world impact.
- Mechanism: Trading simulations use model predictions to execute buy/sell decisions. Inconsistent models show lower profit ratios and Sharpe ratios compared to consistent ones and simple baselines like "Buy-all."
- Core assumption: Trading profitability depends on consistent, accurate predictions; inconsistency introduces noise that erodes returns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "trading simulations reveal that inconsistent predictions lead to lower profitability"
  - [section] "most methods underperform the Buy-all strategy, indicating unacceptable poor performance caused by lacking consistent logical behavior"
  - [corpus] "Quantifying Qualitative Insights: Leveraging LLMs to Market Predict" (related work)
- Break condition: If transaction costs or more sophisticated trading policies are included, the relationship between consistency and profitability may weaken or reverse.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data ingestion -> Transformation generator -> Model prediction engine -> Consistency scorer -> Trading simulator -> Reporting
- Critical path: Transformation generation -> Model evaluation -> Consistency scoring -> Performance comparison
- Design tradeoffs: Finer-grained transformations increase sensitivity but risk semantic drift; coarser transformations may miss subtle inconsistencies.
- Failure signatures: High accuracy but low consistency; large performance drops after transformations; negative correlation between consistency and profitability.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Apply negation consistency to a pre-trained model and verify prediction flips.
  2. Compute consistency score for a fine-tuned forecasting model and compare to accuracy.
  3. Run trading simulation using original vs. consistency-transformed predictions and measure profit ratio change.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can consistency evaluation methods be extended to large language models (LLMs) in financial forecasting tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "Despite this, how to evaluate the consistency of large-scale language models (LLMs) is still an open question towards the financial forecasting task."
- Why unresolved: Current FinTrust evaluation framework is designed for PLMs and fine-tuned financial forecasting models, but LLMs have different architectures and capabilities that may require novel consistency measurement approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of consistency metrics specifically designed for LLMs, showing how these metrics differ from current approaches and demonstrating their effectiveness on financial forecasting tasks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different types of financial text data (e.g., news articles, social media posts) affect model consistency in financial forecasting?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions "While our pipeline is designed to be applicable to any financial text dataset, the evaluation dataset is transformed solely on earnings conference calls. We will expand the scope of experiments to include other financial text sources such as news articles and social media posts."
- Why unresolved: The current evaluation focuses exclusively on earnings call transcripts, but financial forecasting models may encounter various text types with different linguistic characteristics and reliability levels.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies showing consistency scores across different financial text sources, identifying which sources lead to more consistent predictions and why.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between consistency and profitability in real-world trading scenarios with transaction costs?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes "The current trading simulation does not take transaction costs into account. Going forward it will be necessary to consider more sophisticated trading policies."
- Why unresolved: The current trading simulation assumes ideal conditions without transaction costs, which may not reflect real-world trading environments where consistency could have different implications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Trading simulations incorporating realistic transaction costs and slippage, showing how consistency impacts profitability under different market conditions and trading strategies.

## Limitations

- Semantic fidelity of transformations may be compromised by reliance on WordNet antonyms and market-based transitive relationships
- Trading simulations exclude transaction costs and use simplified buy/sell strategies, limiting real-world applicability
- Evaluation focused exclusively on earnings call transcripts, with limited validation across different financial text types and languages

## Confidence

- High confidence in: The experimental methodology and statistical results showing accuracy drops after transformations; the correlation analysis between consistency and accuracy; trading simulation methodology
- Medium confidence in: The semantic preservation of logical transformations; the independence of consistency as a trustworthiness metric; the practical impact of consistency on trading performance
- Low confidence in: The generalizability of consistency measures to other financial forecasting tasks; the robustness of transformations across different domains and languages
