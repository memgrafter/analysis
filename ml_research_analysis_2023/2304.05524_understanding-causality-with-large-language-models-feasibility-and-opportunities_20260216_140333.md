# Understanding Causality with Large Language Models: Feasibility and Opportunities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.05524
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05524
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: Current LLMs can handle causal questions involving existing domain knowledge well but struggle with discovering new causal relationships or making high-precision quantitative estimates

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates large language models' (LLMs) ability to answer causal questions across three distinct types: (1) identifying causal relationships using existing knowledge, (2) discovering new causal knowledge from data, and (3) quantitatively estimating intervention consequences. The authors find that while LLMs excel at Type 1 questions due to their extensive training data, they face significant limitations with Types 2 and 3 due to insufficient symbolic reasoning and mathematical precision. The paper proposes incorporating external causal modules or developing new training paradigms to extend LLM capabilities for causal reasoning, highlighting opportunities for future research at the intersection of causal inference and language models.

## Method Summary
The paper provides a conceptual framework for evaluating LLMs' causal reasoning capabilities without conducting systematic experiments. The authors categorize causal questions into three types and qualitatively assess LLM performance using examples and observations from GPT-4 (via Bing). The evaluation relies on existing LLM capabilities without additional fine-tuning or training. The paper also proposes a modular architecture where LLMs could interface with external causal modules through APIs or code generation, though this remains conceptual without implementation details.

## Key Results
- Current LLMs perform well on Type 1 causal questions (identifying known causal relationships) due to their extensive training data
- LLMs struggle with Type 2 questions (discovering new causal knowledge from data) and Type 3 questions (quantitative estimation of intervention effects) due to limitations in symbolic reasoning and mathematical precision
- The paper identifies opportunities for improving LLM causal reasoning through causal modules, new training paradigms like RLHF, and advances in causal machine learning methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLMs excel at answering causal questions of Type 1 (identifying causal relationships using existing domain knowledge) because they have been trained on vast amounts of text data containing such relationships.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs store and retrieve factual knowledge about causal relationships learned from text. They can combine this knowledge with reasoning to answer questions about known causal connections.
- **Core assumption:** The causal relationships required to answer the question are explicitly present or implicitly encoded in the training data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "current LLMs can answer causal questions with existing causal knowledge as combined domain experts."
  - [section 2.1] "Current LLMs OpenAI (2022; 2023) can answer Type 1 well thanks to their large collection of knowledge."
- **Break condition:** The required causal knowledge is absent from training data or the relationship is too complex for the model's reasoning capacity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LLMs struggle with Type 2 and Type 3 questions because they require advanced symbolic reasoning and mathematical precision that current LLMs lack.
- **Mechanism:** Type 2 and Type 3 questions demand discovery of new causal knowledge from data and quantitative estimation of intervention effects. These require capabilities beyond text-based pattern matching, including formal mathematical reasoning and statistical inference.
- **Core assumption:** Current LLM architectures are fundamentally limited in their ability to perform advanced mathematical reasoning and symbolic manipulation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "they are not yet able to provide satisfactory answers for discovering new knowledge or for high-stakes decision-making tasks with high precision."
  - [section 2.1] "However, the current token generation approaches limit the ability of LLMs to answer Type 2 and Type 3 questions."
  - [section 2.2] "We also observe that they often make mistakes understanding mathematical assumptions which are required to find the suitable existing causal solutions or build new ones."
- **Break condition:** Introduction of new training paradigms or architectural modifications that enable advanced mathematical and symbolic reasoning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Incorporating causal modules or new training paradigms can enable LLMs to answer a wider range of causal questions by leveraging existing causal machine learning methods.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of training LLMs to perform causal reasoning intrinsically, external causal modules (APIs or LLM-generated code) can be used. These modules handle the mathematical and statistical aspects while LLMs manage communication and interpretation.
- **Core assumption:** Existing causal machine learning methods can be effectively interfaced with LLMs through APIs or code generation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3] "Rather than training LLMs to understand causality intrinsically, an efficient way to enlarge the impact is to create causal modules that can be used by the LLM."
  - [section 3] "This is non-trivial and requires both research and engineering effort, even with the assumption that all causal methods have an available API that can be easily interfaced with."
- **Break condition:** Difficulty in interfacing causal modules with LLMs or limitations in existing causal methods that prevent them from handling real-world complexities.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  - LLM core (text generation and reasoning)
  - Causal module interface (API calls or code generation)
  - User interaction layer (natural language processing)
  - Knowledge verification system (assumption checking and step-by-step explanation)

- **Critical path:**
  1. User asks causal question
  2. LLM determines question type
  3. If Type 1, LLM answers directly using knowledge
  4. If Type 2 or 3, LLM interfaces with causal module
  5. Causal module processes data and returns results
  6. LLM formats results into natural language explanation

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Direct LLM reasoning vs. modular approach: Modularity leverages existing causal methods but adds complexity and latency
  - Trust vs. speed: Step-by-step explanations increase trust but slow response time
  - General vs. specialized: General causal modules work for many questions but may be less efficient than specialized solutions

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Hallucinations in intermediate steps (particularly for mathematical reasoning)
  - Incorrect method selection for causal discovery
  - Lack of precision in quantitative estimations
  - Overconfidence in incorrect answers

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Test LLM performance on Type 1 questions with known vs. unknown causal relationships
  2. Implement a simple causal discovery API and test LLM's ability to select and use it correctly
  3. Create a causal estimation module and evaluate LLM's ability to format and explain the results

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can causal modules be effectively integrated with LLMs to enable answering all types of causal questions, and what are the key challenges in designing such modules?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the potential of using causal modules that can interact with LLMs, either through direct API access or by generating the modules themselves.
- Why unresolved: The paper highlights that while this approach is promising, it requires significant research and engineering effort to design modules that can handle the complexity of causal reasoning, communicate assumptions effectively, and provide trustworthy step-by-step explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies demonstrating the successful integration of causal modules with LLMs, showing improved performance on all types of causal questions (Types 1, 2, and 3) compared to current LLMs without modules.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can new training paradigms, such as reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF), be leveraged to enable LLMs to understand causality and perform causal reasoning intrinsically?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests exploring new training paradigms that incorporate causality, particularly RLHF, to allow LLMs to be trained more efficiently and answer various types of causal questions.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges that while RLHF introduces human interventions, it is not sufficient on its own, as real-world interventions are often costly or infeasible. More research is needed to model interventions and consequences using LLMs and to develop methods for generating novel causal reasoning techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and evaluation of LLMs trained with new causal-aware paradigms, demonstrating their ability to answer causal questions without relying on external modules or APIs, and showing improved performance on Types 2 and 3 questions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the key limitations of existing causal machine learning methods that hinder their integration with LLMs, and how can these limitations be addressed to improve the overall performance of causal reasoning systems?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that current causal methods suffer from difficulties in obtaining domain knowledge, communicating assumptions, and handling large-scale, high-precision decision-making tasks, which are challenges when integrating with LLMs.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests that advances in causal machine learning are needed to address these limitations, but it does not provide specific solutions or a roadmap for overcoming these challenges.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research identifying the specific bottlenecks in existing causal methods and proposing innovative solutions to improve their scalability, flexibility, and precision, along with empirical evaluations showing enhanced performance when integrated with LLMs.

## Limitations

- The evaluation is primarily qualitative rather than systematic, relying on selected examples rather than comprehensive benchmarking
- Claims about LLM performance are based on observations from GPT-4 (via Bing) without exploring the full range of LLM capabilities or different prompting strategies
- The proposed modular approach for extending LLM causal reasoning capabilities remains conceptual without concrete implementation or empirical validation

## Confidence

- **Medium confidence** in the core claim that LLMs can handle Type 1 causal questions well but struggle with Types 2 and 3, supported by specific examples and consistent with known LLM limitations in mathematical reasoning
- **Medium confidence** in the proposed modular approach for extending LLM causal reasoning capabilities, based on reasonable engineering arguments but lacking concrete implementation or empirical validation
- **Low confidence** in specific claims about the effectiveness of new training paradigms like RLHF for causal reasoning, as these remain largely theoretical without empirical support
