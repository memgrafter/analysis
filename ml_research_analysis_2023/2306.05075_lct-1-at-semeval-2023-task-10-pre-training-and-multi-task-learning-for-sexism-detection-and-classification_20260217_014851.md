# LCT-1 at SemEval-2023 Task 10: Pre-training and Multi-task Learning for Sexism Detection and Classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.05075
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05075
- Reference count: 25
- Key outcome: Multi-task learning performs on par with fine-tuning for sexism detection, better for coarse-grained classification, and fine-tuning is preferable for fine-grained classification

## Executive Summary
This paper presents LCT-1, a system for sexism detection and classification that leverages transformer-based models with domain-adaptive pre-training and multi-task learning. The approach involves further pre-training on domain-specific data (Reddit/Gab posts and hate speech datasets) and comparing standard fine-tuning with multi-task learning approaches. The system achieves competitive results across three tasks: binary sexism detection, coarse-grained classification, and fine-grained classification, with the best models achieving F1-macro scores of 0.8446, 0.6277, and 0.4764 respectively on the test set.

## Method Summary
The method uses RoBERTa-large as the base model, with further pre-training using domain-adaptive pre-training (DAPT) on 2 million non-labeled texts from Reddit and Gab, and task-adaptive pre-training (TAPT) using only EDOS task data. The models are then fine-tuned using either standard fine-tuning or multi-task learning with related datasets including OLID, HatEval, Measuring HS, UB, AMI@EV ALITA2018, Call me sexist, EXIST, and Online misogyny. The multi-task learning approach uses a shared encoder with task-specific heads, training on multiple related tasks simultaneously.

## Key Results
- Multi-task learning performs on par with standard fine-tuning for sexism detection (Task A)
- Multi-task learning noticeably outperforms fine-tuning for coarse-grained classification (Task B)
- Standard fine-tuning with carefully chosen hyperparameters is preferable for fine-grained classification (Task C)
- Best models achieve F1-macro scores of 0.8446 (Task A), 0.6277 (Task B), and 0.4764 (Task C) on test set

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Domain-adaptive pre-training improves downstream performance for sexism detection tasks
- Mechanism: Further training on domain-specific data helps the model learn linguistic patterns and contextual nuances relevant to sexism detection that standard pre-training doesn't capture
- Core assumption: Linguistic features in sexism-related texts differ sufficiently from general language to benefit from domain-specific pre-training
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions DAPT approach; section 2.3 describes using domain-related data for further pre-training
- Break condition: If domain data is too small or too similar to original pre-training corpus, performance gains will be negligible

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Multi-task learning performs on par or better than standard fine-tuning for sexism detection and coarse-grained classification
- Mechanism: Training on related tasks alongside target task provides broader contextual understanding and feature representations that generalize to sexism detection
- Core assumption: Related tasks share enough semantic and contextual features with sexism detection that joint training improves performance
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states MTL performs on par with fine-tuning for detection and better for coarse-grained classification; section 2.4 describes hypothesis about related tasks enhancing model capabilities
- Break condition: If tasks are too dissimilar or dataset sizes are highly imbalanced, MTL can hurt performance

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fine-tuning is preferable to MTL for fine-grained sexism classification (Task C)
- Mechanism: Fine-grained classification requires precise feature distinctions that may be diluted when model shares capacity across multiple tasks; single-task fine-tuning allows specialization for specific classification boundaries
- Core assumption: 11-category classification requires more task-specific optimization than can be achieved through shared representations
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states fine-tuning is preferable for fine-grained classification; section 4 shows consistent results favoring standard fine-tuning for Task C
- Break condition: If fine-grained categories are highly correlated or dataset is extremely small, MTL might still provide benefits

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Text → Preprocessing → Pre-trained model → Further pre-training (optional) → Fine-tuning/MTL → Evaluation

- Critical path: Raw text from Reddit/Gab posts → Mask normalization, emoji normalization → RoBERTa-Large → DAPT/TAPT (optional) → Fine-tuning/MTL → Classification predictions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - DAPT vs TAPT: DAPT uses more data but may include irrelevant domain features; TAPT uses less data but is more task-focused
  - MTL vs fine-tuning: MTL can improve performance through shared representations but may dilute task-specific features; fine-tuning allows specialization but misses cross-task benefits
  - Preprocessing choices: Emoji normalization helps with sentiment/affect recognition; hashtag segmentation was tested but found not beneficial

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on dev/test despite good eval scores: Indicates data partition issues or overfitting to eval set
  - MTL performs worse than fine-tuning: Suggests tasks are too dissimilar or dataset imbalance issues
  - Further pre-training hurts performance: Could indicate catastrophic forgetting or domain mismatch

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune RoBERTa-base with only mask normalization preprocessing on Task A (baseline)
  2. Fine-tune RoBERTa-base with mask + emoji normalization on Task A (test preprocessing impact)
  3. Pre-train RoBERTa-large with DAPT on 2M texts, then fine-tune on Task A (test domain adaptation)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific types of further pre-training data yield the most significant improvements in sexism detection and classification performance?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that different input data performed better for different tasks, although TAPT achieves lower scores compared to DAPT
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide detailed analysis of which specific types of further pre-training data are most beneficial for each task
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting systematic comparison of different types of further pre-training data and their impact on task performance

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does performance of multi-task learning models vary when trained on datasets with different levels of relatedness to target task?
- Basis in paper: The paper notes model for Task A benefits more from tasks related to target, offensive language, and binary HS classification
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore in depth how relatedness of training datasets to target task affects MTL performance
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with datasets of varying degrees of relatedness to target task

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the potential benefits and limitations of using multilingual data in multi-task learning for sexism detection and classification?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that multilingual MTL experiments were conducted but resulted in major drop in performance
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide detailed analysis of why multilingual MTL led to performance drop
- What evidence would resolve it: Further investigation into impact of multilingual data on model performance

## Limitations
- Limited ablation studies for preprocessing and model architecture choices
- No analysis of model interpretability or error patterns across different sexism categories
- Lack of comparison with other strong baselines beyond single Roberta-base baseline
- No investigation of dataset size effects or few-shot learning scenarios

## Confidence

- Multi-task learning effectiveness for coarse-grained classification: Medium
- Fine-tuning superiority for fine-grained classification: Medium
- Domain-adaptive pre-training benefits: Medium
- Preprocessing impact (emoji normalization): Low
