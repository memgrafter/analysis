# Tokenization Impacts Multilingual Language Modeling: Assessing Vocabulary Allocation and Overlap Across Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17179
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17179
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Tokenization method significantly impacts multilingual model performance through vocabulary allocation and overlap effects

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes how different subword tokenization methods affect multilingual language models by measuring vocabulary allocation (how much of the vocabulary is devoted to each language) and vocabulary overlap (how much the vocabularies share across languages). The authors find that higher vocabulary allocation improves performance on word-level tasks like POS tagging and NER, while vocabulary overlap benefits sentence-level tasks like cross-lingual retrieval. BPE and TokMix generally outperform Unigram in vocabulary allocation, with the choice of tokenizer significantly impacting downstream task performance. The study provides actionable guidelines for selecting the most suitable tokenizer for specific multilingual applications.

## Method Summary
The study uses a scaled-down XLM-Roberta model (768 dim, 8 layers, 6 heads, 150M params) trained on a 10% balanced subset of CC-100 corpus across 20 languages with vocabulary size 120,000. Four tokenization methods are compared: Unigram, BPE, NOOVERLAP (monolingual merging), and TOKMIX (monolingual merging with averaging). Models are pre-trained for 10 epochs with batch size 1024, then evaluated on downstream tasks including POS, NER, dependency labeling, NLI, and sentence retrieval using frozen models with linear probes.

## Key Results
- Higher vocabulary allocation (average rank > 1000, characters per token > 3) significantly improves word-level task performance
- Vocabulary overlap measured via Jensen-Shannon Divergence benefits cross-lingual retrieval and NLI tasks
- BPE and TokMix outperform Unigram in vocabulary allocation, leading to better word-level downstream performance
- The relationship between tokenizer properties and task performance follows predictable patterns across language families

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: High vocabulary allocation improves performance on word-level tasks (POS, NER, dependency labeling) by increasing the number of meaningful tokens available to represent language-specific lexical features.
- Mechanism: When more vocabulary units are assigned to a language, the embedding layer dedicates more parameters to representing language-specific features, allowing longer and more semantically meaningful tokens to be used.
- Core assumption: The length and diversity of vocabulary units correlate with the model's ability to capture language-specific lexical information relevant to word-level tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our findings show that the coverage of the language-specific tokens in the multilingual vocabulary significantly impacts the word-level tasks."
  - [section] "The higher it is, the more vocabulary units a language model needs to consider for masked token filling, making masked word prediction harder. At the same time, a high average rank means that the vocabulary is broader and contains lexical units important for downstream tasks."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; the corpus highlights vocabulary allocation but doesn't directly link token length to word-level task performance.
- Break condition: If vocabulary units are longer but less semantically meaningful (e.g., arbitrary character sequences), the benefit may not materialize.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Vocabulary overlap improves cross-lingual transfer for sentence-level tasks (NLI, cross-lingual retrieval) but can hinder word-level tasks.
- Mechanism: Shared vocabulary units across languages help sentence-level models leverage cross-lingual semantic similarities, while for word-level tasks, overlapping tokens may introduce ambiguity when the same token has different meanings in different languages.
- Core assumption: Semantic similarity at the sentence level benefits from shared lexical units, while word-level syntactic tasks require language-specific representations to avoid ambiguity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "NER and sentence-level tasks (cross-lingual retrieval, NLI) benefit from sharing vocabulary."
  - [section] "vocabulary overlap is crucial to cross-lingual transfer in some tasks. Especially NER within the same script languages... and sentence-level tasks."
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence; the corpus mentions vocabulary overlap but doesn't directly address the task-specific benefits or drawbacks.
- Break condition: If languages share similar scripts but differ significantly in syntax, overlap may still hinder word-level tasks.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The choice of tokenizer algorithm (BPE vs. Unigram) significantly impacts vocabulary allocation and overlap, thereby affecting downstream task performance.
- Mechanism: BPE tends to allocate more vocabulary units per language and produce less overlap compared to Unigram, leading to better performance on word-level tasks but potentially worse on sentence-level cross-lingual tasks.
- Core assumption: The underlying tokenization algorithm's method of constructing vocabulary (iterative merging for BPE vs. probability-based for Unigram) directly influences the distribution of vocabulary units across languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "BPE and TokMix generally outperforming Unigram in vocabulary allocation."
  - [section] "Multilingual vocabulary allocation is highly dependent on the tokenization method used... BPE and TOKMIX obtain better vocabulary allocation than Unigram leading to improved results for word-level downstream tasks."
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence; the corpus discusses BPE and Unigram but doesn't directly compare their algorithmic impact on vocabulary distribution.
- Break condition: If the training corpus is dominated by one language, even BPE may allocate insufficient vocabulary to other languages.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Tokenizer → Embedding Layer → Transformer Layers → Probing Layer
- Critical path: Tokenizer → Embedding Layer → Transformer Layers (for pretraining) → Probing Layer (for evaluation)
- Design tradeoffs:
  - High vocabulary allocation improves word-level tasks but may reduce efficiency in MLM.
  - High vocabulary overlap benefits sentence-level cross-lingual tasks but may introduce ambiguity in word-level tasks.
  - BPE vs. Unigram: BPE generally provides better vocabulary allocation but may require more computational resources.
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on word-level tasks despite high overlap: Indicates insufficient vocabulary allocation for specific languages.
  - Degraded cross-lingual transfer despite high allocation: Suggests lack of shared vocabulary units across languages.
  - Unexpected tokenizer behavior: Check for algorithmic biases in vocabulary construction.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare vocabulary allocation (average rank, characters per token) for BPE vs. Unigram on a multilingual corpus.
  2. Measure JSD between vocabularies of languages with different scripts to quantify overlap.
  3. Evaluate downstream task performance (POS, NER, NLI) for models trained with different tokenizers to correlate with vocabulary metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different tokenization algorithms (BPE, Unigram, etc.) compare in their ability to handle highly inflected languages versus isolating languages when allocating vocabulary?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows BPE outperforms Unigram in vocabulary allocation for most languages, but specifically mentions that Chinese (logographic) requires extensive vocabulary capacity. The paper analyzes languages with different morphological complexities (e.g., Turkish vs Spanish) but doesn't explicitly compare how tokenization methods handle different morphological typologies.
- Why unresolved: The paper analyzes 20 languages with varying morphological complexity but doesn't systematically compare tokenization performance across language typologies. The analysis focuses on script differences rather than morphological typology.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled study comparing tokenization performance across typologically matched language pairs (e.g., isolating vs fusional languages) using the same tokenization algorithms and vocabulary sizes, measuring vocabulary allocation metrics (AR, CPT) and downstream task performance for each morphological type.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal vocabulary size for multilingual tokenizers that balances computational efficiency with performance across diverse language tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses a fixed vocabulary size of 120,000 and mentions that average ranks stop rising after 250,000 for most languages. However, it doesn't systematically explore how different vocabulary sizes affect performance across language types or task categories.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses predetermined vocabulary sizes without exploring the trade-offs between size, performance, and computational cost. The relationship between vocabulary size and task performance across different language typologies remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying vocabulary sizes (e.g., 30k, 60k, 120k, 250k) across typologically diverse languages, measuring both computational efficiency (training/inference time) and task performance (MLM, NER, POS, NLI, retrieval) to identify optimal size ranges for different language types.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the presence of shared vocabulary units between languages affect the ability of multilingual models to learn language-specific features versus cross-lingual transferable features?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that vocabulary overlap benefits NER and sentence-level tasks but hinders POS and dependency labeling. It demonstrates that removing overlap (NoOverlap) can improve syntactic task performance, suggesting a trade-off between language-specific and cross-lingual representations.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies this trade-off, it doesn't explain the underlying mechanisms of how shared vocabulary units influence feature learning at different levels of linguistic representation (lexical vs syntactic vs semantic).
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of internal model representations (e.g., probing intermediate layers, attention patterns) comparing models with different degrees of vocabulary overlap, examining how shared vs language-specific units affect the learning of language-specific morphological features versus cross-lingual semantic features.

## Limitations
- The study uses only a 10% subset of CC-100 corpus, which may not fully represent linguistic diversity
- Findings are based on a specific model architecture (scaled-down XLM-R) and vocabulary size (120,000), limiting generalizability
- Corpus-based measurements of vocabulary allocation and overlap may not capture dynamic tokenizer behavior during pretraining

## Confidence
**High Confidence**: The core finding that vocabulary allocation significantly impacts word-level task performance (POS tagging, NER, dependency labeling) is well-supported by multiple experiments and statistical correlations.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that vocabulary overlap benefits sentence-level tasks while potentially hindering word-level tasks is supported by experimental results but requires nuanced interpretation of the underlying mechanisms.

**Medium Confidence**: The superiority of BPE and TokMix over Unigram for vocabulary allocation is demonstrated, but practical significance depends on specific downstream task requirements.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale Validation**: Replicate the experiments with full-scale models (full XLM-R) and larger vocabularies (200K-300K) to verify whether observed relationships between tokenization choices and task performance scale consistently with model capacity.

2. **Language Family Analysis**: Conduct targeted experiments with closely related languages (e.g., Romance languages, Slavic languages) to better understand the interaction between script similarity, vocabulary overlap, and task-specific performance.

3. **Dynamic Allocation Study**: Implement a tracking mechanism to monitor how vocabulary allocation and overlap evolve during pretraining, correlating these dynamics with changes in masked language modeling performance and downstream task metrics.
