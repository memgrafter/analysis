# Probing self-supervised speech models for phonetic and phonemic information: a case study in aspiration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.06232
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06232
- Reference count: 0
- Self-supervised speech models learn distinct phonetic and phonemic representations, with speech-trained models outperforming random and non-speech variants

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether self-supervised speech models learn distinct phonetic and phonemic representations by probing HuBERT with a case study on aspiration neutralization. The authors find that speech-trained HuBERT models learn robust representations of both phonetic (aspirated vs. unaspirated) and phonemic (/p/ vs. /b/) distinctions in early CNN layers, outperforming random and non-speech baselines. The study uses a three-way classification approach with control tests and dimensionality reduction to identify low-dimensional subspaces that capture abstract phonological distinctions, suggesting the model derives human-like representations of acoustic input.

## Method Summary
The paper uses frame-level HuBERT representations extracted from three model variants (speech-trained on LibriSpeech, non-speech on AudioSet, and randomly initialized) and a log-mel baseline. These representations are aggregated per phone and probed using multinomial regression classifiers for three-way classification tasks (phonetic, phonemic, confound). The experiments employ nested 10-fold cross-validation with L2 regularization, and dimensionality reduction via PCA is applied to find low-noise subspaces. Control tests (positive and negative phonological distinctions) are used to validate the probe design and select optimal dimensionality.

## Key Results
- Speech-trained HuBERT outperforms random and non-speech models on both phonetic and phonemic classification tasks
- Early CNN layers capture robust representations of both phonetic and phonemic distinctions before transformer layers
- Random and non-speech models encode coarse phonological distinctions but not fine-grained phonetic ones
- PCA reveals a low-dimensional subspace in HuBERT that encodes abstract phonological distinctions with reduced noise

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Early CNN layers in HuBERT learn both phonetic and phonemic distinctions before transformer layers
- Mechanism: The CNN architecture processes raw audio and extracts hierarchical acoustic features that directly capture both fine-grained (aspirated vs. unaspirated) and abstract (phonemic /p/ vs /b/) contrasts
- Core assumption: Phonetic and phonemic information is sufficiently encoded in early acoustic features to be separable at the CNN level
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "robust representations of both phonetic and phonemic distinctions emerge in early layers of these models' architectures"
  - [section 3.1] "We see a rapid transition from near-chance performance to high scores on both phonetic and phonemic contrasts within the first several CNN layers"

- Break condition: If acoustic features in early layers are too noisy or conflated, the CNN cannot separate phonetic from phonemic information before transformer layers

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Random projection and non-speech models encode coarse phonological distinctions but not fine-grained phonetic ones
- Mechanism: High-dimensional random weights can capture broad phonological patterns (vowel vs consonant, stress) but lack the structured training needed for precise phonetic discrimination
- Core assumption: Random high-dimensional spaces preserve some distance relationships needed for coarse phonological categorization
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "The random-weights model performs worse than task-optimized models but better than chance, demonstrating that some nontrivial portion of success on these tests can be attributed simply to high-dimensional random projection"
  - [section 4] "We note that even the random-weights model encodes coarse-grained phonological distinctions late in its CNN layers"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus neighbors discuss self-supervised speech models but don't specifically address random projection performance
- Break condition: If the random projection doesn't preserve enough structure for coarse distinctions, even these would be lost

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: HuBERT speech model learns low-noise, low-dimensional subspaces for abstract phonological distinctions
- Mechanism: Principal component analysis reveals that most phonological information concentrates in a small number of dimensions, reducing noise and improving classification
- Core assumption: Abstract phonological distinctions are linearly separable and can be compressed without losing essential information
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our findings show that speech-trained HuBERT derives a low-noise and low-dimensional subspace corresponding to abstract phonological distinctions"
  - [section 3.3] "HuBERT simultaneously and with lower noise accomplishes two representational functions modeled independently by the log-mel and random projection baselines"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus neighbors discuss self-supervised speech models but don't specifically address dimensionality reduction for phonological distinctions
- Break condition: If phonological distinctions require many dimensions to preserve, dimensionality reduction would lose critical information

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the success of random and non-speech models in encoding phonetic and phonemic distinctions indicate that these models are capturing meaningful linguistic information, or is it primarily a result of their high-dimensional architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how random and non-speech models perform better than chance on phonetic and phonemic classification tasks, suggesting that some success can be attributed to high-dimensional random projection.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a definitive answer on whether the models are capturing meaningful linguistic information or if it is just due to their high-dimensional nature.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further experiments comparing the performance of random and non-speech models with models that have lower-dimensional architectures or models that are trained on different types of data could provide insights into whether the success is due to meaningful linguistic information or high-dimensional projection.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the phonetic and phonemic representations learned by self-supervised speech models compare to those learned by humans, particularly in terms of the trade-off between fine-grained and coarse-grained distinctions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how the speech models' representations recover and surpass fine-grained log-mel features while rendering decodable the gross phonological distinctions present in the randomly initialized models.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not directly compare the models' representations to those learned by humans or provide a comprehensive analysis of the trade-off between fine-grained and coarse-grained distinctions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Studies comparing the models' representations to those learned by humans, as well as experiments that manipulate the level of granularity in the input data, could shed light on how the models' representations compare to human representations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent do the phonetic and phonemic representations learned by self-supervised speech models depend on the specific training data and objectives used during pre-training?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how the models' performance varies depending on the content of the training datasets, suggesting that task specialization arises in later model layers.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how the representations depend on the specific training data and objectives, nor does it explore the effects of using different training objectives or datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments that systematically vary the training data and objectives used during pre-training, as well as studies that compare the representations learned by models trained on different datasets, could provide insights into the extent to which the representations depend on the training process.

## Limitations
- Evidence for layer-specific specialization is correlational rather than causal
- Claims about low-dimensional subspaces rely heavily on single control test methodology
- Random projection success raises questions about what uniquely distinguishes speech-trained models

## Confidence

**High confidence**: Phonetic vs phonemic distinction encoding in early layers (supported by consistent ROC/AUC improvements over chance and controls)

**Medium confidence**: Layer-specific specialization patterns (evidence shows trends but architectural analysis is limited)

**Low confidence**: Claims about low-dimensional subspaces capturing abstract phonological distinctions (relies heavily on single control test methodology)
