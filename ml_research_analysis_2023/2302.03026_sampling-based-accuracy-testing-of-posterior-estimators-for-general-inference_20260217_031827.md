# Sampling-Based Accuracy Testing of Posterior Estimators for General Inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03026
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03026
- Reference count: 37
- Key outcome: Introduces DRP coverage testing method that can assess posterior estimator accuracy without requiring explicit posterior evaluations, particularly useful for generative models

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel method called "distance to random point" (DRP) coverage testing for validating the accuracy of posterior estimators in parameter inference problems. The key innovation is generating spherical credible regions around randomly selected reference points in parameter space and calculating the coverage probability of these regions. Unlike traditional coverage methods that require posterior density evaluations, DRP only needs distance calculations between sampled parameters, making it particularly suitable for generative models and high-dimensional inference problems. The method is theoretically proven to be necessary and sufficient for showing that a posterior estimator is optimal.

## Method Summary
The DRP method works by generating validation simulations from the true joint distribution of data and parameters, then generating posterior samples for each validation simulation. For each simulation, a reference point is sampled from a distribution that may depend on the data, and distances are computed between the posterior samples and both the reference point and true parameter. The coverage probability is calculated as the proportion of posterior samples falling within the spherical region defined by the true parameter's distance to the reference point. This approach avoids the need for explicit posterior density evaluations while still providing rigorous coverage testing across the entire parameter space.

## Key Results
- DRP can detect inaccurate posteriors in cases where traditional HPD coverage testing fails, particularly for uninformative estimators
- The method scales to high-dimensional parameter spaces where explicit posterior evaluation is intractable
- Experiments demonstrate DRP's effectiveness on synthetic examples including high-dimensional spaces and a realistic gravitational lensing problem
- Theoretical proof establishes that correct expected coverage for DRP regions is necessary and sufficient for posterior estimator optimality

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: DRP coverage testing detects inaccurate posteriors even when traditional HPD coverage cannot.
- Mechanism: DRP generates spherical credible regions around randomly sampled reference points and computes coverage by checking if posterior samples fall within regions defined by the true parameter's distance to reference points. This probes coverage for all possible credible region positions.
- Core assumption: Posterior estimator is optimal if and only if expected coverage equals 1-α for DRP regions centered at any reference point.
- Evidence anchors: Theorem 3 proves that correct expected coverage for positionable credible regions implies posterior estimator optimality.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: DRP scales to high-dimensional parameter spaces where explicit posterior evaluation is intractable.
- Mechanism: DRP only requires computing distances between sampled parameters and reference points, avoiding posterior density evaluations.
- Core assumption: Parameter space is Euclidean with a meaningful distance metric.
- Evidence anchors: Method differs from previous coverage methods requiring posterior evaluations.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: DRP can detect uninformative posteriors that HPD coverage testing cannot.
- Mechanism: When posterior equals prior, HPD coverage shows correct probabilities but DRP with x-dependent reference points can detect this uninformative behavior.
- Core assumption: Prior and likelihood are distinct distributions.
- Evidence anchors: Figure 4 shows HPD fooled by uninformative estimator while DRP correctly identifies inaccuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Simulator generates training data (θ, x) pairs from joint distribution p(θ, x)
  - Posterior estimator generates samples from estimated posterior ˆp(θ|x)
  - DRP algorithm computes coverage probabilities by generating posterior samples, sampling reference points, computing distances, and calculating coverage

- Critical path:
  1. Generate validation simulations from true joint distribution
  2. Generate posterior samples for each validation simulation
  3. Sample reference points and compute distances
  4. Calculate coverage probabilities and compare to 1-α

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of posterior samples per simulation vs. computational cost
  - Choice of reference point distribution vs. sensitivity to uninformative posteriors
  - Distance metric choice vs. suitability for parameter space geometry

- Failure signatures:
  - Coverage probabilities consistently above 1-α indicates overconfident posteriors
  - Coverage probabilities consistently below 1-α indicates underconfident posteriors
  - Coverage probabilities equal to 1-α but with biased posteriors indicates method may miss certain biases

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Gaussian toy model with known posterior to verify DRP correctly identifies optimal and non-optimal estimators
  2. Uninformative posterior case (ˆp(θ|x) = p(θ)) to demonstrate DRP's ability to detect uninformative behavior
  3. High-dimensional gravitational lensing example to show scalability to realistic problems

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of reference point distribution affect the power of DRP coverage testing to detect biased posterior estimators?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that sampling reference points from a distribution dependent on the data can reveal uninformative posterior estimators missed when sampling from the prior.
- Why unresolved: Only demonstrated in simple 1D Gaussian example; unclear how this generalizes to complex, high-dimensional scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing DRP coverage using different reference point distributions across various inference problems and dimensions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can DRP coverage testing be extended to assess the calibration of posterior estimators beyond just their accuracy?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on testing whether posterior estimator matches true posterior but doesn't discuss assessing reliability of uncertainty estimates.
- Why unresolved: Calibration is distinct from accuracy, and existing calibration assessment methods may not apply to generative models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing a method for assessing calibration of generative posterior estimators using samples alone.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the number of posterior samples per simulation affect the power of DRP coverage testing to detect inaccuracies?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions DRP tests regions of smaller size as number of posterior samples increases.
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't explore how sample number impacts detection ability; too few samples may lack power while too many are computationally expensive.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying number of posterior samples per simulation and measuring detection rate of known inaccuracies.

## Limitations
- Theoretical proof assumes Euclidean parameter spaces and may not extend to non-Euclidean manifolds common in real-world applications
- Method demonstrated on synthetic examples and one gravitational lensing case; performance on diverse real-world scientific problems remains unverified
- Choice of reference point distribution is critical for detecting uninformative posteriors, but paper doesn't provide systematic guidelines for optimal selection across different problem types

## Confidence
- High confidence: Core mechanism of DRP coverage testing and its ability to avoid posterior density evaluations are well-supported
- Medium confidence: Claim that DRP can detect uninformative posteriors relies heavily on specific reference point distribution choice which may not generalize
- Low confidence: Scalability claims to very high-dimensional spaces based on single gravitational lensing example with moderate dimensionality

## Next Checks
1. Test DRP on a constrained parameter space problem (e.g., positive-definite matrices or simplex parameters) to verify method's behavior outside Euclidean spaces and identify necessary modifications
2. Implement systematic sensitivity analysis of DRP coverage probabilities to reference point distribution choice across different problem types to develop guidelines for optimal selection
3. Apply DRP to a hierarchical Bayesian model with multiple levels of uncertainty to evaluate effectiveness in detecting misspecification at different model hierarchy levels
