# Human or Not? A Gamified Approach to the Turing Test

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.20010
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20010
- Reference count: 1
- Over 1.5 million users played the game, achieving only 60% accuracy in identifying AI conversation partners

## Executive Summary
"Human or Not?" is an online game inspired by the Turing test that measures AI chatbots' ability to mimic humans in dialog and humans' ability to distinguish bots from other humans. Over 1.5 million users played the game, engaging in 2-minute anonymous chats with either another human or an AI language model. Players then guessed whether they spoke to a person or AI. Correct guess rates were 68% overall, and only 60% when facing an AI bot (not much higher than chance). The experiment revealed strategies humans use to identify AI, how they signal their own humanity, and how some imitate AI. While the setup has limitations, it provides a novel way to assess AI progress in mimicking human conversation.

## Method Summary
The experiment involved an online platform where users engaged in anonymous 2-minute chat sessions with either another human or an AI language model. AI bots were given personas and instructed to mimic human traits like slang, spelling errors, and coherent backstories. Players guessed their conversation partner's identity after each chat. The study collected data from 1.5+ million users across 10+ million games, analyzing correct guess rates and conversation strategies.

## Key Results
- Overall correct guess rate: 68%
- AI identification accuracy: 60% (barely above chance)
- Humans used strategies like asking about personal history, using slang, and probing for AI-like responses
- Some humans deliberately imitated AI behavior to fool their partners
- Conversation length (2 minutes) may be insufficient for reliable AI detection

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Users can be fooled into thinking AI is human because bots are prompted with human-like personas and behaviors.
- Mechanism: The game designers gave each bot a backstory (name, age, location, personality traits) and instructed them to act human (use slang, make typos, avoid answering certain questions, leave conversations abruptly). This makes bot responses feel more human-like.
- Core assumption: Players cannot distinguish human-like traits from bot-generated ones when both are presented in the same conversational context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "players guessed whether they spoke to a person or to an AI" with only 60% accuracy against bots.
  - [section 2.2] Bots were given personas, instructed to "mimic humans in a variety of aspects, which ranged from human-like slang and spelling errors, to holding a coherent back story."
- Break condition: If players receive longer conversations or are told about the persona strategy, they may detect the scripted nature of bot responses.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Players use stereotypes about AI limitations (formal tone, perfect grammar, refusal to answer) to try to identify bots.
- Mechanism: Humans assume AI will be overly polite, avoid controversial topics, or respond instantly. They exploit these stereotypes by asking about recent events, using slang, or posing provocative questions.
- Core assumption: Human players have a mental model of AI limitations that is outdated or inaccurate given current LLMs.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "Participants asked about personal history, experiences, and unique insights in an attempt to differentiate between the human and AI conversation partners."
  - [section 3.4] "Unexpected language elements, such as spelling mistakes, lack of capitalization, and the use of slang, which are traits generally associated with human language, led some participants to incorrectly identify AI as human."
- Break condition: If AI models are retrained to mimic informal human speech patterns, this detection strategy becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The game format itself creates uncertainty because both sides know they might be talking to an AI, so both try to imitate the other.
- Mechanism: Some humans deliberately imitate bot behavior (use "As an AI language model…" phrasing, refuse personal opinions, make spelling errors) to fool their partner into thinking they are the bot.
- Core assumption: Players will attempt to exploit the ambiguity of the setup by adopting the opposite role.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "Many participants took this role-play to heart, diving deep into AI language model characteristics."
  - [section 3.3] "players who used AI-like expressions were more likely to presume their counterparts were human, correctly identifying a human 80.5% of the time, while their success rate dropped to 54.6% for bot identification."
- Break condition: If the game design penalizes role-playing or rewards authenticity, players may stop imitating the opposite role.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Frontend -> Backend -> LLM Integration -> Conversation Logger -> Analytics Dashboard

- Critical path:
  1. User initiates game → 2. Backend pairs user with bot or human → 3. Conversation starts with prompt → 4. Messages exchanged within time limits → 5. Conversation ends → 6. User submits guess → 7. Result logged

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Anonymity vs. demographic analysis (no registration required)
  - Short chat duration (2 min) vs. depth of conversation (harder to detect AI)
  - No leaderboard vs. risk of system gaming (encourages exploration over competition)

- Failure signatures:
  - High rate of timeouts or dropped connections → check pairing logic or LLM API latency
  - Users consistently guessing correctly at >90% → bot prompts may be too neutral or bot behavior too rigid
  - Moderation filters blocking legitimate content → review moderation thresholds

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Re-run the game with 5-minute conversations instead of 2 to see if accuracy changes
  2. Introduce a "bot label" penalty in the prompt to see if it affects human guess rates
  3. Add a "feedback" prompt after each guess to collect open-ended reasoning and validate strategy categorization

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do human users' strategies for identifying AI chatbots change over time with increased exposure to these systems?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that users were not limited in the number of times they could play, allowing them to develop and refine their strategies over time. However, it does not analyze how strategies evolve with experience.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not track individual users across multiple games or analyze changes in strategy effectiveness over time.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal data tracking the same users across multiple games, analyzing changes in their strategies and success rates in identifying AI.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific language model architectures or training approaches are most effective at mimicking human conversational patterns?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using different backbone language models (Jurassic-2, GPT-4, Cohere) but does not compare their performance or identify which is most effective at human mimicry.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not conduct a systematic comparison of different language models' performance in the Turing test.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments using the same prompts and evaluation criteria across different language models to compare their ability to mimic human conversation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do cultural and linguistic differences affect humans' ability to identify AI chatbots?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions limiting input to Latin characters and English to encourage English communication, but notes this was only partially effective. It also mentions the game was biased towards English-speaking participants.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not analyze performance across different languages or cultural contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting the experiment with multilingual support and analyzing identification success rates across different language groups and cultural contexts.

## Limitations
- Short conversation duration (2 minutes) may not provide sufficient depth for reliable AI detection
- No control for participant expertise or expectations about AI capabilities
- Limited analysis of how strategies evolve with experience
- Potential bias toward English-speaking participants

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The finding that players achieved only 60% accuracy in identifying AI conversation partners is well-supported by the large sample size (1.5+ million users, 10+ million games). This demonstrates that current language models can effectively mimic human conversational patterns under controlled conditions.
- **Medium Confidence**: The identification of specific human strategies for detecting AI (asking about personal history, using slang, probing for AI-like responses) is based on qualitative analysis of conversations, but may not represent a comprehensive taxonomy of all possible detection approaches.
- **Low Confidence**: The claim that some humans deliberately imitate AI behavior to fool their partners, while supported by observed examples, represents a relatively small subset of interactions and may be overgeneralized from limited evidence.

## Next Checks

1. **Extended Duration Study**: Repeat the experiment with 5-10 minute conversations to determine if longer interaction times significantly improve detection accuracy and reveal different strategy patterns.

2. **Expert vs. Novice Comparison**: Conduct a controlled study with participants of varying AI familiarity levels to assess how expertise affects detection success rates and strategy selection.

3. **Post-Game Survey Implementation**: Add structured interviews or surveys immediately after each game to collect explicit reasoning about guess decisions, validating the automated strategy categorization with direct participant feedback.
