# Variational Counterfactual Prediction under Runtime Domain Corruption

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.13271
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13271
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: VEGAN achieves √ϵPEHE of 0.455 on ACIC (vs. 0.502-0.738 for baselines) and 1.720 on IHDP at 5% corruption level

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses causal effect estimation under runtime domain corruption, where variables are both shifted in distribution and unavailable during inference. The proposed VEGAN model combines variational autoencoders with a novel two-stage adversarial domain adaptation scheme. Experiments on IHDP and ACIC datasets demonstrate superior performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines, particularly at higher corruption levels.

## Method Summary
VEGAN uses a variational autoencoder with two-stage adversarial domain adaptation to estimate treatment effects under runtime domain corruption. First, it balances latent distributions between treated and control groups using a GAN, addressing selection bias. Second, it adapts between training and runtime domains through another GAN, handling distribution shift. The model is trained to minimize a modified evidence lower bound that incorporates both stages of domain adaptation, enabling robust causal effect estimation even when significant covariate corruption occurs at inference time.

## Key Results
- VEGAN achieves √ϵPEHE of 0.455 on ACIC dataset vs. 0.502-0.738 for baselines
- On IHDP dataset, VEGAN reaches 1.720 √ϵPEHE at 5% corruption level
- Two-stage adversarial adaptation provides over 4× speedup compared to non-adversarial approaches
- Performance advantage increases with higher corruption levels

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The two-stage adversarial domain adaptation in VEGAN improves causal effect estimation by sequentially balancing treated/control latent distributions then training/runtime distributions.
- Mechanism: VEGAN first uses a GAN to align latent representations between treated and control groups (reducing selection bias), then uses a second GAN to align training and runtime latent distributions (reducing domain shift). This two-step approach is more effective than single-step adaptation because it addresses confounding bias before handling domain shift.
- Core assumption: Latent representations capture sufficient causal information and domain-invariant patterns for accurate treatment effect estimation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we build an adversarially unified variational causal effect model, named VEGAN, with a novel two-stage adversarial domain adaptation scheme to reduce the latent distribution disparity between treated and control groups first, and between training and runtime variables afterwards"
  - [section]: "According to the second term in Eq. 11, for t ∈ { 0, 1}, we have both KL divergence terms that regularize the posterior distribution Pϕt and the prior distribution Qz, i.e., DKL(Pϕ1 ||Qz) and DKL(Pϕ0 ||Qz). By pushing up the ELBO in Eq. 12, one can notice that both posteriors Pϕ1 and Pϕ0 are regularized to approach the same prior distribution Qz, e.g., standard normal distribution N (0, 1). Thus, the domain adaptation (DA) for both groups can be naturally achieved to balance their latent distributions and counter selection bias by adjusting the priors using the VAE framework."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - related papers discuss domain adaptation but don't specifically address two-stage adversarial approaches for runtime domain corruption
- Break condition: If latent representations don't capture sufficient causal information or if the two-stage process introduces instability during training

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: VEGAN's adversarial learning framework provides computational efficiency compared to direct KL divergence optimization when handling high-dimensional data.
- Mechanism: Instead of directly computing KL divergences between distributions (which is computationally expensive for high-dimensional data), VEGAN uses GANs to implicitly minimize these divergences through a minimax game. This adversarial approach is computationally more efficient and scalable.
- Core assumption: The GAN-based approximation of KL divergence minimization is sufficient for achieving the desired domain adaptation
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "As the direct calculation of KL divergence is computationally inefficient and may even be infeasible with high dimensional data [41], [42], we propose to implicitly minimize them and unify these terms into a compact generative adversarial network (GAN) [25] shown in Figure 1."
  - [section]: "Apart from that, optimizing the minimax game in GAN is equivalent to minimizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence [25], which is a bounded symmetric distribution similarity measurement [43]."
  - [section]: "One core motivation for utilizing GAN to replace the straightforward KL divergence optimization is to preserve training efficiency under high dimensionality. Hence, we further test VEGAN's efficiency by comparing its training time (in seconds) per 100 epochs with CEVAE and VEGAN I."
- Break condition: If GAN training becomes unstable or if the approximation quality degrades with very high-dimensional data

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The variational autoencoder framework in VEGAN enables robust latent representation learning that handles confounding bias and domain shift simultaneously.
- Mechanism: VEGAN uses a VAE to learn a latent representation z of the input variables x, where the latent distributions are regularized to be close to a standard normal prior. This regularization, combined with adversarial domain adaptation, ensures the latent representations are both informative for causal inference and domain-invariant.
- Core assumption: The latent space Z captures the causal structure needed for treatment effect estimation while being sufficiently domain-invariant
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We perceive the observed covariates of treated and control groups from the conventional domain shift perspective, in which covariate x is a noisy measurement, normally less informative and more confounded [36], [37], than the domain-invariant latent representation z."
  - [section]: "Our solution is built upon the variational autoencoder (VAE). To start with, in this section we introduce the minimization of the factual error ϵF, the distribution disparity between treated and control groups first, and between training and runtime domains afterwards."
  - [section]: "Thus, to achieve the second-stage DA, our proposed ultimate evidence lower bound (ELBOulti) for the intractable joint log-likelihood p(y) is: log p(y) ≥ ELBO ≥ ELBOulti = ELBO − DKL(Ptr ϕ ||Psr ϕ )."
- Break condition: If the latent representation fails to capture the relevant causal structure or if the regularization is too strong/weak

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
  - Why needed here: VAEs provide a probabilistic framework for learning latent representations that can capture both the observed data and the underlying causal structure
  - Quick check question: How does the evidence lower bound (ELBO) in a VAE balance reconstruction accuracy with latent regularization?

- Concept: Domain Adaptation
  - Why needed here: Domain adaptation techniques are essential for handling the distribution shift between training and runtime data, which is a key challenge in runtime domain corruption
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between covariate shift and concept shift, and how do they affect causal inference?

- Concept: Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
  - Why needed here: GANs provide an efficient way to implicitly minimize distribution distances through adversarial training, which is more scalable than direct computation for high-dimensional data
  - Quick check question: How does the minimax game in GAN training relate to minimizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input variables x -> Shared feature extractor Gϕ -> Latent representations z -> Treated/control predictors Ψ1/Ψ0 -> Outcome predictions y1/y0

- Critical path: Input variables → Shared feature extractor → Latent representations → Treated/control predictors → Outcome predictions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Two-stage vs. single-stage domain adaptation: Two-stage allows addressing confounding bias before domain shift but may introduce training complexity
  - Direct KL divergence vs. adversarial approximation: Adversarial approach is more scalable but may have stability issues
  - Latent dimensionality: Higher dimensions may capture more information but increase computational cost and risk of overfitting

- Failure signatures:
  - Mode collapse in GAN training: Discriminator becomes too strong, preventing generator from learning meaningful latent representations
  - Over-regularization: Latent representations become too close to the prior, losing informative features for causal inference
  - Under-regularization: Latent representations fail to be domain-invariant, leading to poor generalization

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does VEGAN's performance scale with increasing dimensionality of the input data, particularly when the number of covariates grows beyond the 200 features used in the ACIC dataset?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tests VEGAN on datasets with up to 200 covariates and discusses computational efficiency, but does not explore performance with higher-dimensional data.
- Why unresolved: The scalability of the adversarial training components and the two-stage domain adaptation to very high-dimensional data remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results on datasets with 500+ covariates showing runtime, convergence behavior, and PEHE metrics compared to baseline methods.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of VEGAN's two-stage adversarial domain adaptation when applied to datasets with continuous treatment rather than binary treatment?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses exclusively on binary treatment settings and does not explore how the two-stage adaptation scheme would function with continuous or multi-valued treatments.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical framework and implementation details are tailored for binary treatment, and generalization to continuous treatments would require significant modifications to the adversarial objectives.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing VEGAN with modified adversarial objectives to continuous-treatment baselines like BART or DeepIV on datasets with continuous interventions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does VEGAN handle scenarios where the runtime domain corruption affects the outcome variable y itself rather than just the covariates x?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper defines runtime domain corruption as affecting covariates through distribution shift and missing values, but does not address corruption of the outcome variable.
- Why unresolved: The two-stage adversarial adaptation and VAE framework are designed specifically for covariate corruption, and outcome corruption would fundamentally change the causal inference problem structure.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing VEGAN's performance when outcome variables are systematically biased or missing at test time, compared to methods designed for measurement error in outcomes.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies on synthetic datasets (IHDP, ACIC) rather than real-world applications
- Corruption simulation methodology, particularly for high-dimensional ACIC dataset, is not fully specified
- Adversarial training approach may suffer from instability at higher corruption levels

## Confidence

- Mechanism 1 (Two-stage adversarial adaptation): Medium - Strong theoretical motivation but limited empirical validation across diverse datasets
- Mechanism 2 (GAN computational efficiency): High - Well-established in literature, supported by ablation study
- Mechanism 3 (VAE latent representations): Medium - Standard approach but effectiveness depends on dataset characteristics
