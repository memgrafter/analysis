# Detecting Lexical Borrowings from Dominant Languages in Multilingual Wordlists

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.00189
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00189
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: Classifier-based method achieves F1 scores of 0.81 and precision of 0.93

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes new computational methods for detecting lexical borrowings from dominant languages in multilingual wordlists. The authors develop three approaches - Closest Match, Cognate-Based, and Classifier-Based methods - and test them on seven heritage languages from Latin America in contact with Spanish. Using 10-fold cross-validation, the classifier-based method outperforms the others, achieving F1 scores of 0.81 and substantially higher precision. The study reveals that borrowings with divergent meanings are particularly challenging to detect, suggesting the need for methods that can handle semantic shifts.

## Method Summary
The paper tests three computational methods for borrowing detection: Closest Match (using normalized edit distance or SCA distance), Cognate-Based (clustering with distance measures), and Classifier-Based (SVM classifier with distance features). The methods treat Spanish as a dominant donor language and compare its words to seven heritage languages. Features include normalized edit distance and sound-class based alignment distance between word pairs. The Classifier-Based method combines these distance measures and trains a linear SVM to classify word pairs as borrowings or non-borrowings. The study uses 10-fold cross-validation with data partitioned by concepts to avoid information leakage.

## Key Results
- Classifier-based method achieves F1 scores of 0.81, outperforming other methods
- High precision of 0.93 suggests the classifier is conservative in identifying borrowings
- Error analysis shows borrowings with divergent meanings are particularly challenging to detect

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Supervised machine learning classifiers can detect borrowings from a dominant language more accurately than unsupervised sequence comparison methods.
- Mechanism: The classifier-based method encodes phonetic distances between word pairs from a dominant donor language and heritage languages as feature vectors, then trains a linear SVM to classify pairs as borrowings or non-borrowings.
- Core assumption: Phonetic distance patterns between borrowed and non-borrowed word pairs are sufficiently distinct for a classifier to learn.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the supervised machine learning system outperforming the classical systems."
  - [section] "The classifier-based method showed the best performance, with F1 scores of 0.81, and high precision of 0.93."
- Break condition: If the phonetic distance distributions between borrowed and non-borrowed pairs overlap significantly, classifier performance will degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Treating the dominant language as a fixed donor and heritage languages as recipients allows better borrowing detection than treating all languages symmetrically.
- Mechanism: The methods focus on comparing each heritage language wordlist entry to its counterpart in the dominant donor language, rather than computing all pairwise distances among heritage languages.
- Core assumption: The dominant language has a stronger unidirectional influence on heritage languages than vice versa.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Most computational approaches to borrowing detection treat all languages under study as equally important, even though dominant languages have a stronger impact on heritage languages than vice versa."
  - [section] "We test new methods for lexical borrowing detection in contact situations where dominant languages play an important role..."
- Break condition: If heritage languages have significant influence on the dominant language, or if borrowing occurs in multiple directions, this asymmetry assumption breaks.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Including phonetic distance features from multiple comparison methods (NED and SCA) improves borrowing detection accuracy.
- Mechanism: The classifier combines normalized edit distance and sound-class based alignment distance features, capturing both simple phonetic similarity and phonological structure.
- Core assumption: Different distance measures capture complementary aspects of phonetic similarity relevant to borrowing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We tested our three methods with two distance measures in five experiments (normalized edit and SCA distances individually in both Closest Match and Cognate-Based methods, and combined in the Classifier-Based method)"
  - [section] "We tested various phonetic distance measures, but report only on the combination of normalized edit and SCA distances, as they yielded the best results."
- Break condition: If the distance measures are highly correlated or if one measure dominates the classification, combining them provides no benefit.

- Critical path: Data preparation → Feature extraction → Model training → Evaluation → Error analysis
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a linear SVM provides interpretability and speed but may miss complex patterns that nonlinear models could capture
  - 10-fold cross-validation provides robust estimates but requires more computation than simpler validation schemes
  - Treating the dominant language as fixed donor simplifies the problem but may miss bidirectional borrowings
- Failure signatures:
  - Low precision with high recall suggests the classifier is too permissive in identifying borrowings
  - High precision with low recall suggests the classifier is too conservative and missing true borrowings
  - Performance varying significantly by heritage language suggests language-specific factors not captured by the features

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can computational methods better detect lexical borrowings involving semantic shift?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies that many undetected borrowings result from forms having different meanings in donor and recipient languages, suggesting the need for methods that relax the "same concept" restriction.
- Why unresolved: Current methods only compare words with identical meanings, missing cases where meanings have shifted.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing methods that incorporate semantic similarity measures or cross-linguistic polysemy data could demonstrate improved detection rates for semantically shifted borrowings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the proportion of borrowings in a language correlate with the degree of phonetic adaptation from the donor language?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes a positive correlation between borrowing frequency and detection performance, speculating this might reflect reduced phonetic adaptation in highly borrowed languages.
- Why unresolved: The correlation could be due to better parameter estimation rather than reduced adaptation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Cross-linguistic studies comparing phonetic distance distributions in high versus low borrowing languages would clarify whether adaptation patterns vary systematically.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How much do dataset errors contribute to false positive detections of borrowings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The error analysis found several cases where implausibly similar forms were likely annotation errors rather than true borrowings.
- Why unresolved: The study couldn't determine whether these were genuine chance similarities or errors without access to original data collection processes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replication studies with original data collectors or alternative annotation schemes could determine the true source of these similarities.

## Limitations
- Study focuses on a specific contact situation (Spanish as dominant donor language) limiting generalizability
- Methods may miss borrowings with divergent meanings due to semantic shifts
- Only tested linear SVM models, potentially missing complex patterns in the data

- Medium confidence in the sufficiency of linear SVM for capturing borrowing patterns
