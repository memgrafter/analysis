# Explaining Model Confidence Using Counterfactuals

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.05729
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05729
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Counterfactual explanations (both example-based and visualization-based) improve users' understanding and trust in model confidence scores compared to no explanation, with different approaches suited for categorical versus continuous variables.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces two approaches for explaining model confidence through counterfactuals: providing specific counterfactual examples and visualizing the counterfactual space. Through two user studies across income prediction and employee attrition datasets, the authors demonstrate that both methods significantly improve user understanding and trust compared to no explanation. The research reveals important nuances: example-based explanations can lead to case-based reasoning that obscures underlying trends, while visualizations better communicate continuous variable relationships but may be harder to interpret. The findings suggest that effective counterfactual explanations require careful design based on variable type and user needs.

## Method Summary
The authors employ logistic regression models where confidence is measured as the margin of confidence (distance from decision boundary). Counterfactual examples are generated using a weighted L1 norm optimization that finds minimal changes to input features while maintaining the same predicted class. For visualization, the counterfactual space is explored by varying one feature at a time while holding others constant, creating a grid of confidence scores that can be plotted. User studies use a between-subject design comparing control (no explanation), example-based counterfactuals, and visualization-based counterfactuals, measuring understanding through task prediction accuracy and trust through Likert scales.

## Key Results
- Both counterfactual explanation methods significantly improved user understanding and trust compared to no explanation
- Example-based explanations led to case-based reasoning that obscured linear trends in continuous variables
- Visualization-based explanations helped users identify trends and avoid misconceptions but were sometimes harder to interpret
- Users showed reluctance to extrapolate trends beyond provided data, affecting their ability to answer questions about unseen data points

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Providing counterfactual explanations improves users' understanding of model confidence compared to no explanation.
- **Mechanism:** Counterfactual examples help users reason about how changes in input features would affect the model's confidence score. By showing specific "what if" scenarios, users can better understand the relationship between features and confidence.
- **Core assumption:** Users can mentally simulate the effects of feature changes on confidence when provided with counterfactual examples.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Both increase understanding and trust for study participants over a baseline of no explanation"
  - [section] "Through two user studies we demonstrate that showing counterfactual explanations of confidence scores can help users better understand and trust the machine learning model's prediction."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus papers focus on counterfactual explanations but not specifically on confidence scores.
- **Break condition:** If users cannot mentally simulate feature changes or if the counterfactual examples are too complex to interpret, understanding may not improve.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Visualization of counterfactual space helps users understand trends in model confidence across feature values.
- **Mechanism:** Visual representations of how confidence changes with feature values allow users to see patterns and relationships that are difficult to grasp from examples alone. This is particularly useful for continuous variables where linear trends can be observed.
- **Core assumption:** Visual patterns in counterfactual space are more easily interpreted than individual examples, especially for continuous variables.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The authors introduce two approaches for explaining model confidence through counterfactuals: one based on providing counterfactual examples and another based on visualizing the counterfactual space."
  - [section] "In this section, we propose a method for visualising the counterfactual space of a model and how this affects the model's confidence"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus papers discuss counterfactual generation but not visualization techniques for confidence specifically.
- **Break condition:** If the visualization is too complex or if users prefer concrete examples over abstract patterns, this mechanism may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Counterfactual explanations improve trust in model predictions by providing transparency about confidence scores.
- **Mechanism:** When users understand why a model is confident in its prediction, they are more likely to trust that prediction. Counterfactuals provide this understanding by showing what would need to change for confidence to be lower.
- **Core assumption:** Trust is based on understanding, and understanding is improved by seeing concrete examples of how confidence could change.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Both increase understanding and trust for study participants over a baseline of no explanation"
  - [section] "We then evaluate the difference between example-based counterfactual explanations and visualisation-based counterfactual explanations based on the following hypotheses: Hypothesis 2a/b (H2a/b): Example-based/Visualisation-based counterfactual explanations help users better trust the AI model than when they are not given explanations."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus papers discuss trust in AI but not specifically how counterfactual explanations affect trust in confidence scores.
- **Break condition:** If users don't value understanding or if the counterfactuals raise doubts rather than confidence, trust may not improve.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does prior knowledge of participants influence the effectiveness of example-based versus visualisation-based counterfactual explanations for understanding and trust in AI models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors observed discrepancies between domain 1 (income) and domain 2 (HR) when comparing example-based and visualisation-based explanations in terms of trust and satisfaction, suggesting that prior knowledge of participants could affect their performance in the two different domains.
- Why unresolved: The paper did not explicitly test or measure participants' prior knowledge of the domains, and the observed discrepancies could be due to other factors not controlled for in the study.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a study that measures participants' prior knowledge of the domains and examines its correlation with their performance in understanding and trusting the AI model under different explanation conditions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the effectiveness of counterfactual explanations for model confidence vary across different types of AI models, such as deep learning models or ensemble models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors chose logistic regression for its simplicity and ease of defining the confidence score, but noted that future work could extend the studies to using counterfactual tools for more complex models.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tested the counterfactual explanations on a logistic regression model, and it is unclear how the findings would generalize to other types of models that may have different characteristics and complexities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting user studies with counterfactual explanations for model confidence using different types of AI models, such as deep learning models or ensemble models, and comparing the results with those obtained from logistic regression models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can counterfactual explanations be designed to effectively communicate continuous variable trends without relying on case-based reasoning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors found that participants in the example-based condition often used a 'case-based reasoning' approach, which led them to overlook the linear trend between the confidence score and the feature values. They suggested using graphs to visualize continuous variables to mitigate this issue.
- Why unresolved: While the authors suggested using visualization-based explanations for continuous variables, they did not provide specific guidelines or design principles for creating effective visualizations that can communicate the trends without relying on case-based reasoning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing design guidelines or principles for creating effective counterfactual visualizations for continuous variables, and evaluating their impact on users' understanding and trust in the AI model compared to example-based explanations.

## Limitations

- Evaluation relies on controlled user studies that may not generalize to real-world decision-making scenarios
- Use of logistic regression models limits applicability to more complex model architectures
- Study design doesn't account for varying levels of user expertise or domain knowledge
- Paper doesn't address potential biases in counterfactual generation or how these might affect user trust differently across demographic groups
