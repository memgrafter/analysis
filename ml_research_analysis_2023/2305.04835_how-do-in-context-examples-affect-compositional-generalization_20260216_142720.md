# How Do In-Context Examples Affect Compositional Generalization?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.04835
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04835
- Reference count: 23
- Key outcome: In-context examples should be structurally similar to test cases, diverse from each other, and individually simple to optimize compositional generalization in large language models.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the selection of in-context examples affects compositional generalization in large language models. The authors construct a test suite called COFE based on COGS to systematically study three factors: structural similarity, diversity, and complexity in selecting examples. Through extensive experiments with six large language models, they find that optimal in-context examples should have high structural similarity to test cases, high diversity among examples, and low individual complexity. The study reveals that in-context learning performs poorly on compositional generalization with fictional words and still requires examples to match linguistic structures despite pre-training.

## Method Summary
The authors created a test suite called COFE based on the COGS dataset to systematically study how in-context examples affect compositional generalization. They used a greedy-search algorithm to select 10 in-context examples per test case, controlling for three factors: structural similarity (how similar examples are to test cases), diversity (how varied examples are from each other), and complexity (simplicity of individual examples). The study evaluated six large language models (davinci, code-cushman-001, code-cushman-002, text-davinci-002, text-chat-davinci-002, code-davinci-002) across five compositional generalization categories: Primitive Substitution, Primitive Structural Alternation, Phrase Recombination, Longer Chain, and Deeper Nesting.

## Key Results
- Structural similarity between in-context examples and test cases significantly improves compositional generalization performance
- Higher diversity among in-context examples prevents biased observations and improves generalization on high-level combinations
- Lower complexity in individual in-context examples reduces learning difficulty and prevents confusion from redundant information
- In-context learning performs poorly on compositional generalization with fictional words and still requires examples to match linguistic structures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Structural similarity between in-context examples and test cases improves compositional generalization by reducing the need to reconstruct parse trees from scratch.
- Mechanism: The model leverages existing structural templates from examples rather than building new ones, easing the recombination of primitives.
- Core assumption: Parse trees capture the essential structural information needed for generalization, and matching these structures is more important than lexical overlap alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "in-context examples should be structurally similar to the test case"
  - [section 4.2] "the structural similarity hidden behind expressions could be a beneficial factor"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence provided
- Break condition: If the parse tree structure is not the key factor, or if the model uses alternative representations (e.g., embeddings) that bypass structural matching.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Higher diversity among in-context examples prevents biased observations and improves generalization on high-level combinations.
- Mechanism: Diverse examples expose the model to a wider range of patterns, reducing overfitting to repeated structures and enabling better handling of novel combinations.
- Core assumption: Repetitive patterns in examples create bias that limits the model's ability to generalize to unseen combinations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "in-context examples should be diverse from each other"
  - [section 4.3] "low diversity leads to biased observations, thus blocking high-level structural generalization"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence provided
- Break condition: If diversity is less important than other factors like complexity or similarity, or if the model's architecture inherently handles diversity without explicit variation in examples.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Lower complexity in individual in-context examples reduces learning difficulty and prevents confusion from redundant information.
- Mechanism: Simple examples provide clear, focused signals that are easier for the model to process in the few-shot setting, where parameter updates are not possible.
- Core assumption: Complex examples contain redundant information that can confuse the model in in-context learning, where it cannot adapt its parameters to fit the examples.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "in-context examples should be individually simple"
  - [section 4.3] "simple examples could reduce the learning difficulty for the model"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence provided
- Break condition: If the model can effectively process complex examples without degradation, or if complexity is outweighed by other factors like structural similarity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different pre-training objectives affect compositional generalization in large language models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that "even though the backbone model has been pre-trained on large corpus" in-context examples are still required to match linguistic structures. This suggests pre-training objectives may not adequately capture compositional patterns.
- Why unresolved: The study uses models pre-trained with standard objectives and does not explore alternative pre-training approaches designed specifically for compositional tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing in-context compositional generalization across models with different pre-training objectives (e.g., contrastive learning, curriculum learning, or compositional objectives) would reveal how pre-training affects this capability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can automated methods effectively identify hidden structural similarities without access to parse trees?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors rely on parse trees to measure structural similarity in their experiments, but acknowledge this requires "knowing the exact generative grammar." They suggest two potential approaches involving pre-trained parsers or embedding models.
- Why unresolved: Current approaches either require gold parse trees or are speculative about using learned embeddings. The effectiveness and scalability of automated structural similarity detection remains unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic comparison of parse-tree-based similarity measures against learned embedding-based approaches across diverse linguistic phenomena would demonstrate whether automated methods can match or exceed human-designed structural analysis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between diversity requirements and task complexity in compositional generalization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors find that "increasing the diversity could bring considerable gains in PhraReco" but note that for LongChain and DeepNest, "beyond biased structures, their difficulty also lies in length generalization."
- Why unresolved: The study shows differential effects of diversity across task types but doesn't explain why certain compositional tasks benefit more from diversity than others.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying diversity systematically across tasks of different structural complexity would reveal whether diversity requirements scale with task complexity or depend on specific structural properties.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the position of in-context examples within the prompt affect compositional generalization performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors examine robustness to prompt order and find that "the performance only slightly changes under different prompt orders" when in-context examples are chosen properly.
- Why unresolved: While minor effects are observed, the study only tests three specific ordering strategies and doesn't explore the full space of possible arrangements or explain why position matters less with good examples.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive analysis testing all possible orderings of in-context examples across different similarity, diversity, and complexity settings would reveal whether certain arrangements systematically improve performance and why.

### Open Question 5
- Question: What mechanisms allow large language models to generalize compositionally when examples match linguistic structures but fail otherwise?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that "in-context learning still struggles to effectively recognize the semantic equivalence among different linguistic structures" despite pre-training on large corpus, suggesting the model can learn structural patterns when explicitly provided.
- Why unresolved: The study identifies that structural matching is crucial but doesn't investigate what internal representations or mechanisms enable successful generalization in these cases versus failures without structural alignment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Probing experiments examining internal representations (attention patterns, activations) during successful versus unsuccessful compositional generalization would reveal whether models develop compositional abstractions or rely on surface pattern matching.

## Limitations
- The study relies heavily on synthetic data (COGS) and demonstrates limited generalization to natural language settings
- The greedy search algorithm may not scale efficiently to larger example banks or more complex linguistic structures
- The focus on exact-match accuracy may not fully capture semantic understanding
- The fixed example size (10 per test case) could introduce artifacts that affect the observed patterns

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The three-factor framework (structural similarity, diversity, complexity) is well-supported by experimental results across six different large language models
- **Medium confidence**: The observed limitations with fictional words and complex structures are consistent with previous literature, though the exact mechanisms remain unclear
- **Low confidence**: The greedy search algorithm's effectiveness beyond the current example bank size has not been validated, and the scalability to more diverse linguistic phenomena remains untested
