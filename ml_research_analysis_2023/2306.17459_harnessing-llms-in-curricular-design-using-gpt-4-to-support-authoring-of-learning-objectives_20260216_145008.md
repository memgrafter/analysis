# Harnessing LLMs in Curricular Design: Using GPT-4 to Support Authoring of Learning Objectives

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.17459
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17459
- Reference count: 29
- Researchers evaluated 127 GPT-4 generated learning objectives for an AI course, finding they were sensible, properly expressed, and aligned with Bloom's taxonomy levels

## Executive Summary
This study explores the application of GPT-4 in generating learning objectives (LOs) for a university-level AI course. The researchers developed detailed guidelines for LO generation and evaluated 127 LOs produced by GPT-4 across different course modules. The generated LOs demonstrated proper expression with action verbs and appropriate alignment with Bloom's taxonomy levels. Conceptual module LOs primarily used lower-level verbs while project-related LOs employed higher-level verbs. The study employed both human annotators and a BERT classifier, which showed substantial agreement in categorizing LOs into Bloom's taxonomy categories, demonstrating the potential of LLMs to support curricular design.

## Method Summary
The researchers applied GPT-4 to generate learning objectives for an AI course, providing detailed guidelines and prompt engineering to produce 127 LOs across various course modules. Human annotators classified these LOs into Bloom's taxonomy categories, while a BERT classifier was also trained to automate this classification. The evaluation examined whether LOs were sensible, properly expressed (starting with action verbs), and aligned with appropriate taxonomy levels. The study compared LOs from conceptual modules with those from project-based modules to assess variation in action verb usage across different instructional contexts.

## Key Results
- GPT-4 generated LOs were sensible and properly expressed, consistently starting with action verbs
- Generated LOs showed appropriate alignment with Bloom's taxonomy, with conceptual modules using lower-level verbs (describe, explain) and project modules using higher-level verbs (implement, develop)
- Human annotators and BERT classifier demonstrated substantial agreement in classifying LOs into Bloom's taxonomy categories

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LLMs like GPT-4 can effectively generate learning objectives by leveraging their training on vast educational text corpora to understand the conventions of LO writing, including the use of action verbs and alignment with cognitive taxonomy levels. The model's ability to follow detailed guidelines and maintain consistency across different module types demonstrates its capacity to understand pedagogical requirements and adapt its output accordingly. The substantial agreement between human and automated classification suggests that GPT-4 captures the essential characteristics that distinguish different levels of cognitive complexity in learning objectives.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Course Module Specifications -> GPT-4 Prompt Engineering -> LLM Output Generation -> Human Annotation -> BERT Classification -> Quality Evaluation

**Critical Path**: The researchers first defined detailed guidelines for LO generation, then engineered prompts for GPT-4, generated 127 LOs, had human annotators classify them into Bloom's taxonomy categories, trained a BERT classifier on the same task, and finally evaluated the quality and appropriateness of the generated LOs.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The study prioritized automated generation and evaluation efficiency over comprehensive empirical validation of learning outcomes. By using a single course context and three human annotators without reporting inter-rater reliability metrics, the researchers sacrificed generalizability and reliability assessment for focused exploration of GPT-4's capabilities in LO generation.

**Failure Signatures**: Poor quality LOs would likely manifest as: vague verbs that don't indicate measurable outcomes (e.g., "know," "understand"), misalignment with Bloom's taxonomy levels relative to module content, inconsistent formatting across generated LOs, or failure to capture the specific learning goals of individual course modules.

**First Experiments**:
1. Generate LOs for a simple module (e.g., "Introduction to AI") with minimal context to establish baseline quality
2. Compare LOs generated with detailed guidelines versus minimal guidance to quantify the impact of prompt engineering
3. Test the same prompt across different GPT-4 temperature settings to evaluate sensitivity to generation parameters

## Limitations
- The study is limited to a single course context (AI-focused university course), restricting generalizability to other disciplines or educational levels
- The evaluation relied on three human annotators without reporting inter-rater reliability metrics or addressing potential subjectivity in LO quality assessment
- The study did not empirically test whether GPT-4-generated LOs actually improve student learning outcomes or instructional effectiveness

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The observation that GPT-4-generated LOs predominantly used appropriate action verbs and showed reasonable alignment with Bloom's taxonomy levels, as this was supported by both human annotation and automated classification with substantial agreement
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that LLMs can support curricular design by generating effective LOs, as this conclusion is reasonable but based on limited empirical validation and single-context application
- **Low confidence**: The assertion that human validation remains important for LLMs in curricular design, as the study did not empirically test scenarios with varying levels of human oversight or measure the impact of human validation on final LO quality

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a multi-institutional study comparing GPT-4-generated LOs with expert-human generated LOs across diverse disciplines to establish generalizability and benchmark performance
2. Implement an experimental study measuring actual student learning outcomes when using GPT-4-generated versus instructor-created LOs in the same course to assess pedagogical impact
3. Perform a systematic evaluation of how different prompt engineering strategies affect the quality, accuracy, and pedagogical appropriateness of generated LOs
