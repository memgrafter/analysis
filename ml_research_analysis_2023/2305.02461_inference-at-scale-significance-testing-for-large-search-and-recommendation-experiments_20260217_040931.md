# Inference at Scale Significance Testing for Large Search and Recommendation Experiments

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.02461
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02461
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Sign and Wilcoxon tests show inflated false positive rates for large sample sizes in search and recommendation experiments compared to t-test, bootstrap, and randomization tests

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the behavior of statistical significance tests for large-scale search and recommendation experiments, where datasets can contain tens of thousands of samples. Through simulation using real evaluation data from MS-MARCO and recommendation datasets, the study compares false positive rates and power across five statistical tests: t-test, bootstrap, randomization, Wilcoxon signed-rank, and sign tests. Results demonstrate that sign and Wilcoxon tests exhibit significantly higher Type-1 error rates than other tests at large sample sizes, while all tests achieve near-perfect power at scale. The paper concludes that sign and Wilcoxon tests should be avoided for large-scale evaluation, and recommends reporting both p-values and effect sizes for decision making.

## Method Summary
The study employs a simulation-based approach using Urbano and Nagler's generative stochastic model to create synthetic evaluation scores. The method involves fitting marginal distributions (Truncated Normal, Beta, Beta-Binomial, discrete kernel smoothing) and copulas to real evaluation data from MS-MARCO and recommendation datasets, then generating synthetic scores under null and alternative hypotheses. The researchers compute false positive rates and power for five statistical tests across sample sizes ranging from 25 to 20,000 and effect sizes from 0.01 to 0.1, using 10,000-2,500 trials per configuration.

## Key Results
- Sign and Wilcoxon tests show significantly higher Type-1 error rates for large sample sizes than bootstrap, randomization, and t-tests
- Power differences among statistical tests diminish as sample size increases, with most tests achieving 100% power at large scales
- The paper recommends against using sign and Wilcoxon tests for large-scale evaluation data and suggests reporting both p-values and effect sizes for decision making

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Sign and Wilcoxon tests show inflated false positive rates when sample size is large.
- Mechanism: As sample size increases, the distribution of differences becomes more finely resolved, exposing small deviations from perfect symmetry about zero. These small deviations are detected as statistically significant by sign and Wilcoxon tests, even though they are practically meaningless.
- Core assumption: The difference distribution is symmetric about zero under the null hypothesis, but small asymmetries become detectable at large sample sizes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our results show that the Wilcoxon and Sign tests show significantly higher Type-1 error rates for large sample sizes than the bootstrap, randomization and t-tests"
  - [section] "We observe that when the sample size is large, the sign and Wilcoxon tests find these slight deviations from perfect symmetry about zero in the difference distribution as statistically significant."
  - [corpus] Weak - no related corpus evidence specifically addressing inflated Type-1 error at large sample sizes.
- Break condition: If the difference distribution is perfectly symmetric about zero, even at large sample sizes, then sign and Wilcoxon tests should maintain correct Type-1 error rates.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Power differences among statistical tests diminish as sample size increases.
- Mechanism: With larger sample sizes, all tests become more sensitive to small effect sizes, reducing the relative advantage of more powerful tests. At very large sample sizes, all tests achieve near 100% power for even tiny effects.
- Core assumption: Effect size is the same across all tests and power differences are purely due to sample size and test characteristics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "While the statistical tests displayed differences in their power for smaller sample sizes, they showed no difference in their power for large sample sizes."
  - [section] "As the sample size increases the power of the tests increases, and the observed differences in their power decreases such that there is no distinguishable difference."
  - [corpus] Weak - no related corpus evidence specifically addressing diminishing power differences at large sample sizes.
- Break condition: If effect sizes vary systematically across tests, or if some tests have inherent limitations that prevent 100% power even at large sample sizes, power differences may persist.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Reporting both p-values and effect sizes is necessary for decision making with large-scale data.
- Mechanism: With large sample sizes, statistical significance becomes easily achievable even for trivial effect sizes. Effect sizes provide context for practical significance, preventing overinterpretation of statistically significant but meaningless results.
- Core assumption: Effect size measures are appropriate for the evaluation metrics used (e.g., reciprocal rank) and can be interpreted in practical terms.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our result demonstrate that with Top-𝑁 recommendation and large search evaluation data, most tests would have a 100% chance of finding statistically significant results. Therefore, the effect size should be used to determine practical or scientific significance."
  - [section] "We recommend that both the 𝑝-value, and effect size be reported [8, 28], and used for decision making."
  - [corpus] Weak - no related corpus evidence specifically addressing the necessity of reporting effect sizes alongside p-values for large-scale data.
- Break condition: If effect sizes are consistently large relative to practical significance thresholds, or if p-values alone are sufficient for decision making in the specific application domain, reporting both may be unnecessary.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Type-1 error rate (false positive rate)
  - Why needed here: Understanding how often significance tests incorrectly reject the null hypothesis when it's true is crucial for evaluating test reliability, especially at large sample sizes.
  - Quick check question: If a test has a significance level of 0.05, what is its expected Type-1 error rate under the null hypothesis?

- Concept: Power of a statistical test
  - Why needed here: Power indicates the test's ability to detect true effects. Comparing power across tests and sample sizes reveals which tests are most reliable for identifying meaningful improvements.
  - Quick check question: If a test has 80% power at a given effect size, what percentage of true effects of that size will it fail to detect?

- Concept: Effect size
  - Why needed here: Effect size measures the magnitude of the difference between systems, providing context for the practical significance of statistically significant results, especially when large sample sizes make statistical significance easy to achieve.
  - Quick check question: If two systems have reciprocal rank scores of 0.8 and 0.75, what is the effect size in terms of absolute difference?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data collection -> Marginal distribution fitting -> Copula modeling -> Synthetic data generation -> P-value computation -> Type-1 error/power analysis
- Critical path: The core analysis flow is: simulation → p-value computation → Type-1 error/power calculation → comparison across tests and sample sizes. The simulation step is critical as it allows controlled testing of null and alternative hypotheses.
- Design tradeoffs: Using simulation allows precise control over effect sizes and null hypothesis truth, but relies on the assumption that the fitted distributions and copulas accurately model real evaluation data. Alternative approaches like using real data splits have less control but potentially more realistic results.
- Failure signatures: If Type-1 error rates are not close to expected values at small sample sizes, the simulation model or p-value computation may be flawed. If power doesn't increase with sample size as expected, the effect size calculations or test implementations may be incorrect.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify that all statistical tests return correct p-values on a small, hand-crafted dataset with known properties (e.g., perfectly symmetric differences).
  2. Test the simulation model by generating data with known effect sizes and confirming that the estimated effect sizes match.
  3. Run a small-scale version of the full analysis pipeline on a subset of the data to ensure all components work together correctly before scaling up to the full analysis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why do sign and Wilcoxon tests show higher false positive rates for large sample sizes in search and recommendation tasks compared to traditional TREC-style experiments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that sign and Wilcoxon tests "show significantly higher Type-1 error rates for large sample sizes than the bootstrap, randomization and t-tests" in large search and recommendation experiments, which contrasts with findings from smaller TREC-style experiments.
- Why unresolved: The paper observes this behavior but does not provide a theoretical explanation for why the deviation from symmetry becomes statistically significant only at large sample sizes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis showing how the distribution of difference scores changes with sample size, or simulation studies varying the degree of asymmetry in difference distributions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Are there alternative statistical tests that maintain appropriate false positive rates for both small and large sample sizes in search and recommendation evaluation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper concludes that sign and Wilcoxon tests should not be used for large-scale evaluation, but doesn't explore or recommend specific alternatives that might work better across all sample sizes.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on comparing established tests rather than developing or testing new approaches specifically designed for large-scale evaluation scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison of newer statistical methods (e.g., Bayesian approaches, permutation tests with different assumptions) across varying sample sizes in search and recommendation contexts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How should researchers determine practical significance thresholds for effect sizes in large-scale search and recommendation experiments where statistical significance is nearly guaranteed?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "with Top-𝑁 recommendation and large search evaluation data, most tests would have a 100% chance of finding statistically significant results" even for very small effect sizes, and recommends using effect size for determining practical significance.
- Why unresolved: The paper recommends reporting effect sizes but doesn't provide guidance on how to interpret or threshold effect sizes for practical significance in these contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Industry case studies showing how different effect size thresholds correlate with user satisfaction, business outcomes, or other practical metrics in deployed systems.

## Limitations

- The simulation results may not perfectly generalize to all real-world evaluation scenarios due to the specific characteristics of the MS-MARCO and recommendation datasets used
- The study does not provide explicit practical significance thresholds for effect sizes, leaving this determination to individual practitioners
- The analysis focuses on pairwise comparisons and may not fully address more complex evaluation scenarios involving multiple systems or metrics

## Confidence

- Type-1 error inflation findings: Medium-High - simulation results are consistent, but real-world data may exhibit different behavior
- Power convergence claims: Medium - theoretical expectations align with results, but sample sizes may not have been large enough to fully demonstrate convergence
- Effect size recommendation: High - widely accepted statistical practice, though practical thresholds remain context-dependent
