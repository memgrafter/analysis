# Brain encoding models based on multimodal transformers can transfer across language and vision

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12248
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12248
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- This study demonstrates that encoding models trained on fMRI responses to one modality (language or vision) can predict brain responses to the other modality, indicating aligned conceptual representations across modalities.

## Executive Summary
This study demonstrates that encoding models trained on fMRI responses to one modality (language or vision) can predict brain responses to the other modality, indicating aligned conceptual representations across modalities. Using representations from the multimodal transformer BridgeTower, language encoding models achieved an average correlation of 0.023 with movie responses and vision encoding models achieved an average correlation of 0.010 with story responses in non-sensory cortical regions. Cross-modality performance was significantly higher than chance (q(FDR) < 0.05) in many regions including inferior parietal cortex, precuneus, and temporal cortex.

## Method Summary
The study used fMRI data from 5 subjects watching movies and listening to stories, extracting features from BridgeTower multimodal transformer for both stimulus types. Language and vision encoding models were trained using L2-regularized linear regression to predict voxel responses, with linear alignment matrices estimated on Flickr30K data to correct for feature space differences between modalities. Cross-modality transfer was tested by using language encoding models to predict movie responses and vision encoding models to predict story responses, with significance assessed using blockwise permutation tests and spatial correlations analyzed through principal components analysis of encoding model weights.

## Key Results
- Cross-modality performance was significantly higher than chance (q(FDR) < 0.05) in many regions including inferior parietal cortex, precuneus, and temporal cortex
- Principal components of language encoding model weights showed significant spatial correlation with vision encoding model weights in multimodal regions (q(FDR) < 0.05 for PCs 1, 3, and 5)
- Multimodal transformers outperformed unimodal transformers (p < 0.05) at cross-modality transfer

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Multimodal transformers learn aligned representations that enable cross-modality transfer in brain encoding models.
- Mechanism: BridgeTower's co-attention mechanism between language and visual tokens during training creates representations where the same concept in different modalities has correlated feature vectors. This alignment allows language encoding models to predict visual cortex responses and vice versa.
- Core assumption: The multimodal training objective creates meaningful correlations between representations of the same concept across modalities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Comparing encoding models trained using representations from multimodal and unimodal transformers, we found that multimodal transformers learn more aligned representations of concepts in language and vision."
  - [section 2.1]: "BridgeTower uses a co-attention mechanism wherein language and visual tokens are only scored against key vectors from the other modality."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus evidence for this specific mechanism, but related work on multimodal transformers shows better brain alignment.
- Break condition: If the co-attention mechanism doesn't create meaningful alignment, or if brain representations aren't actually aligned across modalities.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Linear alignment matrices can bridge feature space differences between modalities for cross-modality transfer.
- Mechanism: BridgeTower's language and visual feature spaces may differ up to a linear transformation due to separate processing in early layers. Linear alignment matrices estimated on paired data (Flickr30K) project features into compatible spaces before encoding model training.
- Core assumption: Feature space differences between modalities can be captured by linear transformations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.2]: "To correct for these potential transformations, we used the Flickr30K dataset to estimate linear transformation matrices that explicitly align the BridgeTower feature spaces."
  - [section 4.1]: "Before using the language encoding model to predict fMRI responses to movies, we first used the image→caption matrix to project the movie features into the language feature space."
  - [corpus]: Moderate - linear alignment is a standard technique in multimodal learning, but corpus evidence specifically for BridgeTower is limited.
- Break condition: If feature space differences are non-linear or too complex for linear alignment to capture.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Shared semantic dimensions in encoding model weights reflect aligned brain representations across modalities.
- Mechanism: Principal components analysis on encoding model weights reveals semantic dimensions (e.g., people vs. places) that are spatially correlated across language and vision encoding models in multimodal brain regions.
- Core assumption: Encoding model weights capture the semantic dimensions that the brain uses to represent concepts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.3]: "We applied principal components analysis to the language encoding model weights... We projected Flickr30k caption features onto the first PC of the language encoding model weights... Projecting the language encoding model weights onto language PC 1, we found that voxels with positive projections were mostly located in inferior parietal cortex, precuneus, temporal cortex..."
  - [section 5.3]: "We spatially correlated the projections of the language and the vision encoding model weights... For multimodal voxels, the projections of the language and the vision encoding model weights were significantly correlated for language PCs 1, 3, and 5 (q(FDR) < 0.05)."
  - [corpus]: Moderate - PCA on encoding weights is established, but specific evidence for multimodal brain alignment is limited.
- Break condition: If encoding model weights don't capture meaningful semantic dimensions, or if brain representations aren't actually aligned in multimodal regions.

  - Quick check question: How are the encoding model weights estimated and what prevents overfitting in this approach?

  - Why needed here: The study uses blockwise permutation tests with FDR correction to identify voxels with significant within-modality performance and spatial correlations between encoding model projections.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - BridgeTower multimodal transformer (Base or Large) -> Flickr30K dataset -> fMRI story/movie datasets -> L2-regularized linear regression -> Blockwise permutation tests -> Principal components analysis

- Critical path:
  1. Extract features from BridgeTower for story and movie stimuli
  2. Estimate linear alignment matrices using Flickr30K
  3. Project movie features into language space and story features into visual space
  4. Train encoding models using L2-regularized linear regression
  5. Evaluate cross-modality performance
  6. Analyze semantic dimensions via PCA on encoding weights
  7. Compare multimodal vs unimodal performance

- Design tradeoffs:
  - BridgeTower-Base vs Large: Larger model may capture more complex alignments but requires more compute
  - Layer selection: Different layers capture different levels of abstraction; layer 8 was optimal here
  - Feature extraction timing: Single frames vs segments affects temporal resolution and feature quality
  - Correction for inverted tuning: Required in visual cortex but adds complexity to the pipeline

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor cross-modality performance: Could indicate lack of alignment between modalities in either the model or brain
  - Negative correlations in visual cortex: May require the inverted tuning correction
  - Weak spatial correlations between encoding model projections: Could suggest semantic dimensions aren't shared
  - Unimodal features outperforming multimodal: Might indicate multimodal training didn't learn useful alignments

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify feature extraction from BridgeTower works correctly for both story and movie stimuli
  2. Test linear alignment matrices by projecting held-out Flickr30K examples and checking reconstruction quality
  3. Run encoding models on within-modality data first to establish baseline performance before attempting cross-modality transfer

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the nature of the inverted tuning between language and vision representations in visual cortex?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "while tuning for concepts in language and vision is positively correlated in most regions outside of visual cortex, it is negatively correlated in visual cortex" and acknowledges "Understanding the nature of this inverted tuning is an important direction for future work."
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not provide a mechanistic explanation for why visual cortex shows inverted tuning patterns during conceptual processing of language stimuli.
- What evidence would resolve it: Neuroimaging studies manipulating stimulus properties (e.g., semantic category, visual complexity) during language comprehension could test whether inverted tuning reflects competition between perceptual and conceptual representations, or whether it indicates a fundamental difference in how visual cortex processes cross-modal information.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do differences in semantic content and timescale between story and movie stimuli affect cross-modality transfer asymmetry?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note "One potential confound that could contribute to this asymmetry is that the story stimuli contain both concrete concepts (such as places) and abstract concepts (such as emotions) while the movie stimuli mostly contain concrete concepts. Another potential confound is that the story stimuli contain information at a longer timescale than the movie stimuli."
- Why unresolved: The current study uses stimuli with inherently different semantic and temporal properties, making it difficult to isolate whether transfer asymmetries reflect modality-specific brain representations or stimulus characteristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments using carefully matched story and movie stimuli (similar semantic content distribution, matched temporal scales) would allow researchers to determine whether the observed asymmetry in cross-modality transfer is intrinsic to language-vision representational differences or driven by stimulus properties.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What specific aspects of multimodal training enable transformers to learn more aligned representations than simple linear alignment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that "multimodal features led to better cross-modality performance than linearly aligned stimulus features extracted from unimodal transformers, suggesting that the multimodal training tasks enable BridgeTower to learn connections between language and visual concepts that go beyond a simple linear alignment."
- Why unresolved: While the study demonstrates superior performance of multimodal transformers, it does not identify which training objectives or architectural features (e.g., co-attention mechanisms, contrastive losses) are responsible for learning non-linear alignments between modalities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies comparing different multimodal training objectives (image-text matching, contrastive learning, masked multimodal modeling) and architectural variations (different attention mechanisms, feature fusion strategies) would reveal which components contribute most to learning aligned representations.

## Limitations

- The observed correlations (0.023 for language→movie and 0.010 for movie→language) are small in magnitude, suggesting cross-modality transfer may be limited to certain semantic dimensions or brain regions
- The study relies on a single multimodal transformer architecture (BridgeTower) and two specific datasets (stories and movies), limiting generalizability
- The Flickr30K dataset used for alignment matrix estimation may not fully capture the semantic richness of the story and movie stimuli

## Confidence

- High confidence: Cross-modality transfer occurs in multimodal transformers and shows significant spatial correlations in multimodal brain regions
- Medium confidence: Multimodal transformers learn more aligned representations than unimodal transformers
- Medium confidence: Shared semantic dimensions identified through PCA on encoding model weights reflect true brain alignment

## Next Checks

1. Test cross-modality transfer with alternative multimodal architectures (e.g., CLIP, BLIP) to verify that the phenomenon generalizes beyond BridgeTower
2. Evaluate encoding model performance on additional stimulus types (e.g., narrated documentaries, audio-described videos) to assess robustness across different language-vision combinations
3. Conduct ablation studies removing the linear alignment step to quantify its contribution to cross-modality performance and verify the assumption that linear transformations suffice for feature space alignment
