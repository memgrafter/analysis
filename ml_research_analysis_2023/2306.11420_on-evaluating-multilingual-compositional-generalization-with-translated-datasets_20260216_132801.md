# On Evaluating Multilingual Compositional Generalization with Translated Datasets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2306.11420
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11420
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Key outcome:** Introduces MCWQ-R, a benchmark for multilingual compositional generalization using rule-based translation of CFQ into Chinese and Japanese

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of evaluating compositional generalization across multiple languages by introducing MCWQ-R, a translated version of the CFQ dataset into Chinese and Japanese. Unlike prior work using neural machine translation (NMT) which often distorts meaning, the authors develop a rule-based translation approach using synchronous context-free grammars (SCFG) that better preserves semantic and compositional integrity. Their experiments reveal that linguistic divergences between English and target languages lead to structural simplifications that affect benchmark validity, while cross-lingual compositional generalization remains challenging even with advanced multilingual architectures.

## Method Summary
The authors create MCWQ-R by translating the CFQ semantic parsing dataset from English to Chinese and Japanese using a rule-based machine translation (RBMT) approach. The RBMT system employs synchronous context-free grammars (SCFG) to deterministically map syntactic structures between languages while preserving compositional relationships. This approach is compared against neural machine translation (NMT) using Google Translate. The translated datasets are then used to evaluate compositional generalization performance across languages, with particular attention to how linguistic divergences affect train-test compound divergence and cross-lingual transfer capabilities.

## Key Results
- RBMT translations preserve compositional structure significantly better than NMT, as confirmed by automatic and human evaluation
- Chinese and Japanese translations exhibit inherent linguistic divergences, particularly with possessive constructions, leading to structural simplifications
- Cross-lingual compositional generalization remains challenging, with multilingual models underperforming on translated datasets compared to English
- ZX-Parse's representation alignment approach shows moderate effectiveness but degrades performance on compositional data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Rule-based machine translation (RBMT) preserves compositional structure better than neural machine translation (NMT) in semantic parsing benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** RBMT uses synchronous context-free grammars (SCFG) to deterministically map syntactic structures from source to target language, maintaining exact correspondence between compositional elements.
- **Core assumption:** The synthetic nature of CFQ/CFQ-R datasets ensures limited sentence patterns and minimal ambiguity, making rule-based transduction feasible and accurate.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "we craft a faithful rule-based translation of the MCWQ dataset from English to Chinese and Japanese" and show it "performs well due to awareness of grammar constituents"
  - [section 4.1] "The synthetic nature of CFQ indicates that it has limited sentence patterns and barely causes ambiguities"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers discuss translationese effects but don't directly test RBMT vs NMT on compositional benchmarks
- **Break condition:** If target language exhibits grammatical structures not representable in the source grammar, or if semantic ambiguity exceeds the capacity of deterministic rules.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Linguistic divergences cause compositional simplification when translating semantic parsing datasets across languages.
- **Mechanism:** Certain English compositional structures (e.g., possessive case) lack direct equivalents in target languages, forcing structural collapse that reduces compound divergence between train and test sets.
- **Core assumption:** Train-test compound divergence is a necessary condition for valid compositional generalization evaluation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 6.1] "Chinese and Japanese grammar inherently fail to (naturally) express specific compositions in English, predominantly the possessive case"
  - [section 6.1] "This linguistic divergence results in degeneration in compound divergence between training and test sets"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - related work on translationese effects supports the general concept of structural simplification
- **Break condition:** If target language possesses equivalent compositional structures, or if translation methodology can artificially preserve the divergence.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cross-lingual compositional generalization is more challenging than within-language generalization due to representation alignment difficulties.
- **Mechanism:** Multilingual pre-trained models struggle to represent high-compositionality utterances consistently across languages, even with auxiliary alignment objectives.
- **Core assumption:** Effective cross-lingual transfer requires aligned representations that capture compositional structure uniformly.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 6.2] "cross-lingual accuracies are generally closer to monolingual ones in low complexity levels, which indicates that the cross-lingual transfer is difficult in CG largely due to the failure in universally representing utterances of high compositionality"
  - [section 6.2] "the auxiliary objectives cause a dramatic decrease in performance" on compositional data
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers discuss multilingual representation alignment but not specifically for compositional generalization
- **Break condition:** If alignment methodology can bridge the domain gap between compositional and natural utterances, or if model architecture can better capture cross-lingual compositional structure.

- **Concept:** Translationese Effects
  - Why needed here: Translationese refers to structural simplification and unnatural phrasing that occurs in translated text, affecting benchmark validity
  - Quick check question: How might translationese artifacts artificially improve or degrade model performance on compositional tasks?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Source grammar module (English CFG rules) -> Transduction rules (SCFG rules) -> Dictionary module (POS-tagged bilingual dictionary) -> Post-processing (pattern matching) -> Grounding (SPARQL query alignment)
- **Critical path:** Question text → Parse tree → Transduction rules → Lexical mapping → Post-processing → SPARQL grounding
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Rule-based vs statistical: Determinism vs coverage/flexibility
  - Grammar complexity vs maintainability: More rules handle more cases but increase complexity
  - Lexical vs structural mapping: Tradeoff between natural translation and compositional faithfulness
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Grammar coverage gaps: Ambiguous or unhandled English patterns
  - Linguistic divergence mismatches: Structural simplifications that lose compositional information
  - Dictionary limitations: Missing or incorrect lexical mappings
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Test RBMT translation quality on a small subset with manual evaluation of compositional preservation
  2. Compare train-test compound divergence between RBMT and NMT translations
  3. Evaluate cross-lingual generalization performance using a simple multilingual model on both translation methods

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the performance gap between languages in compositional generalization be fully attributed to linguistic differences, or do translation artifacts introduce additional confounds?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors demonstrate that both GT and RBMT translations exhibit structural simplifications, particularly in possessive constructions, leading to easier MCD splits compared to English.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides evidence of structural simplification due to linguistic differences, it does not fully disentangle the impact of translation artifacts from inherent linguistic divergence. The GT branch shows both structural simplification and semantic distortion, making it difficult to isolate the effects.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled experiment comparing the performance of models on original English data versus perfectly translated data (without any structural or semantic changes) across languages could help isolate the impact of translation artifacts.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Is the moderate performance of representation alignment in ZX-Parse due to the domain gap between compositional utterances and natural language data used for alignment?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors observe that ZX-Parse, despite its alignment mechanism, performs worse than mBART50* on compositional data. They hypothesize that the natural utterances from MKQA used for alignment are less effective for compositional utterances.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not conduct a direct comparison of the effectiveness of alignment using compositional versus natural language data. It relies on indirect evidence from the performance gap between ZX-Parse and mBART50*.
- What evidence would resolve it: An ablation study training ZX-Parse with compositional data for alignment instead of natural language data, and comparing its performance on compositional tasks, would provide direct evidence.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the language bias in hallucinations observed in mT5 be mitigated by modifying the pre-trained decoder or by using alternative architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors find that mT5 tends to generate country entities in hallucinations that are associated with the input language, suggesting a language bias encoded in the pre-trained decoder.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores freezing the encoder and training a new decoder from scratch as a potential solution. It does not investigate other methods to mitigate the language bias, such as fine-tuning the decoder on a balanced dataset or using architectures less prone to hallucinations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the hallucination patterns of mT5 with and without fine-tuning on a balanced dataset, or with alternative architectures known to reduce hallucinations, would provide insights into the effectiveness of different mitigation strategies.

## Limitations
- Rule-based translation cannot fully capture linguistic divergences between languages, particularly for complex grammatical structures like possessives
- Human evaluation sample size (100 per language) may not capture systematic translation issues across the full dataset
- Cross-lingual generalization experiments rely on a single model architecture without exploring alternative approaches

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** RBMT preserves compositional structure better than NMT, supported by systematic comparison and human evaluation
- **Medium confidence:** Linguistic divergences cause compositional simplification, but broader claims lack comprehensive analysis
- **Low confidence:** Cross-lingual compositional generalization difficulty may be methodological rather than fundamental, based on limited model architecture testing

## Next Checks
1. **Expand manual evaluation coverage:** Conduct comprehensive human evaluation of 1,000+ translated examples across all complexity levels to identify systematic translation failures and their impact on compositional structure.
2. **Cross-linguistic comparative analysis:** Create parallel benchmarks in additional languages (e.g., Spanish, German) to determine whether observed divergences are language-specific or represent broader typological patterns in compositional translation.
3. **Alternative alignment methodology testing:** Experiment with different representation alignment approaches (contrastive learning, adversarial training) on the translated datasets to isolate whether current alignment failures are methodological or fundamental to cross-lingual compositional generalization.
