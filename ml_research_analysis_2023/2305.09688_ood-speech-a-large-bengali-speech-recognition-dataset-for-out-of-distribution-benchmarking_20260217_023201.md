# OOD-Speech: A Large Bengali Speech Recognition Dataset for Out-of-Distribution Benchmarking

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09688
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09688
- Reference count: 0
- Largest publicly available Bengali ASR dataset with 1201.33 hours of speech data

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces OOD-Speech, the first out-of-distribution benchmarking dataset for Bengali automatic speech recognition. The dataset contains 1201.33 hours of speech data collected from 22,645 native Bengali speakers across 17 diverse domains, including audiobooks, talk shows, online classes, and Islamic sermons. It combines 1129.46 hours of crowdsourced scripted speech with 71.87 hours of spontaneous speech, all manually annotated to enable robust evaluation of ASR models under distribution shifts.

The dataset's primary contribution is its test set with clear out-of-distribution characteristics, allowing researchers to benchmark model robustness against domain-specific linguistic challenges such as gemination, diphthongs, and code-switching. Evaluation of pre-trained models like Google API and Wav2Vec2 reveals significant performance degradation on spontaneous speech domains, demonstrating the dataset's effectiveness in exposing model limitations under real-world conditions.

## Method Summary
The dataset was collected through online crowdsourcing campaigns on platforms MCV and Shobdo, gathering 1129.46 hours of scripted speech from 22,645 native Bengali speakers. For the out-of-distribution test set, 71.87 hours of spontaneous speech were manually annotated from YouTube videos across 17 domains by nine expert annotators. The data underwent quality checks including silence removal, normalization, and manual validation. Geneva speech features were extracted to analyze distribution separation between scripted and spontaneous speech domains, confirming clear clustering in acoustic feature space.

## Key Results
- Dataset contains 1201.33 hours total with 1129.46 hours crowdsourced scripted speech and 71.87 hours spontaneous speech
- 17 diverse domains represented including audiobooks, talk shows, online classes, and Islamic sermons
- Wav2Vec2 model achieves 27.61% WER on MaCro test set but 68.03% WER on OOD test set
- Clear separation between MaCro Train and OOD Test clusters in Geneva feature space

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Large-scale crowdsourcing with 22,645 contributors produces high dialectal diversity in speech data.
- Mechanism: Massively distributed data collection across South Asia captures regional accents and prosodic features that are underrepresented in smaller, localized datasets.
- Core assumption: Contributors self-report accents accurately and represent the geographic diversity of Bengali speakers.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "collected from 22,645 native Bengali speakers from South Asia"
  - [section] "Our crowdsourcing campaigns for data collection on MCV were designed to ensure spatial diversity across South Asia"
  - [corpus] Log-distribution of dialects shows 28 unique self-reported accents
- Break condition: If accent self-reporting is inaccurate or contributors cluster geographically, dialectal diversity will be overestimated.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Manual annotation of spontaneous speech from 17 domains creates OOD test data with high linguistic variability.
- Mechanism: Expert annotators select diverse audio windows from YouTube videos, preserving natural speech patterns, code-switching, and domain-specific vocabulary.
- Core assumption: Annotators can identify and extract representative speech samples that capture the linguistic complexity of each domain.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "test dataset comprises 23.03 hours of speech collected and manually annotated from 17 different sources"
  - [section] "A team of nine expert annotators...were assigned to annotate the data"
  - [corpus] Annotation complexity varies by domain, with Telemedicine requiring highest annotation time
- Break condition: If annotators select unrepresentative samples or miss domain-specific linguistic features, OOD characteristics will be weakened.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Geneva speech feature analysis demonstrates clear separation between training and test data distributions.
- Mechanism: Feature extraction and dimensionality reduction reveal that MaCro Train and OOD Test form distinct clusters in acoustic feature space, validating OOD benchmarking capability.
- Core assumption: Geneva features capture relevant acoustic differences between scripted and spontaneous speech domains.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Geneva features [7] extracted from MaCro Train, MaCro Test and OOD Test subsets"
  - [section] "We can see a clear separation between the clusters formed by MaCro Train and OOD Test"
  - [corpus] TSNE plots show separate clusters for each domain in OOD Test
- Break condition: If Geneva features fail to capture domain-specific acoustic properties, distribution separation may be misleading.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Crowdsourcing platforms (MCV, Shobdo) -> Raw audio storage -> Expert annotation -> Validation -> Geneva feature extraction -> Benchmarking with pre-trained models

- Critical path: Crowdsourcing -> Annotation -> Validation -> Feature extraction -> Benchmarking
- Design tradeoffs: Large crowdsourced dataset vs. controlled quality vs. spontaneous speech diversity vs. annotation cost
- Failure signatures: High WER on specific domains indicates domain shift; feature overlap suggests insufficient OOD separation
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train Wav2Vec2 on MaCro Train, evaluate on each OOD domain separately to identify failure patterns
  2. Fine-tune Wav2Vec2 on combined MaCro Train + MaCro Val, test on OOD Test to measure adaptation effectiveness
  3. Train separate domain-specific models for high-WER domains (e.g., Cartoons, Slang/Profanity) to assess specialization benefits

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the out-of-distribution performance of ASR models on OOD-Speech compare to other languages with similar linguistic diversity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces OOD-Speech as the first out-of-distribution benchmarking dataset for Bengali ASR and compares model performance on OOD test data versus MaCro test data.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses solely on Bengali and does not provide comparative analysis with other languages, especially those with similar linguistic diversity (e.g., other Indic languages or languages with rich morphological systems).
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarking results from similar OOD datasets for other languages, particularly those with comparable linguistic features like rich morphology, dialectal diversity, and gemination.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relative impact of different types of linguistic diversity (e.g., gemination, diphthongs, code-switching) on ASR model performance in the OOD-Speech dataset?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses various linguistic challenges in Bengali (gemination, diphthongs, code-switching) and provides domain-wise statistics, but does not isolate the impact of each linguistic feature on model performance.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the presence of these features and provides some domain-specific performance metrics, it does not systematically analyze how each linguistic feature contributes to recognition errors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments isolating each linguistic feature, or detailed error analysis showing the distribution of errors across different linguistic phenomena.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the gender imbalance in the dataset (particularly the higher proportion of masculine voices) affect model performance across different domains and dialects?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the long-term spectral average shows a higher gender imbalance in OOD-Speech compared to OpenSLR, with a larger fraction of masculine recordings.
- Why unresolved: While the gender imbalance is noted, the paper does not analyze its impact on model performance across different domains, dialects, or speech styles.
- What evidence would resolve it: Performance analysis disaggregated by gender, or experiments showing how model performance changes when trained and tested on gender-balanced subsets.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the optimal annotation strategy for spontaneous speech domains to balance annotation speed and accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper describes a complex annotation and validation process for the OOD subset, including multiple rounds of re-annotation and validation, which took significant time and resources.
- Why unresolved: While the paper describes the annotation process used, it does not explore alternative strategies or compare the effectiveness of different annotation approaches in terms of cost, time, and accuracy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of different annotation strategies (e.g., single vs. multiple annotators, different time windows, different validation methods) on a subset of the data.

## Limitations

- Dialectal coverage depends on self-reported accent accuracy and geographic distribution of 22,645 contributors
- Manual annotation quality control lacks detailed inter-annotator agreement metrics across 17 diverse domains
- Gender imbalance in dataset may affect model performance, with higher proportion of masculine recordings
