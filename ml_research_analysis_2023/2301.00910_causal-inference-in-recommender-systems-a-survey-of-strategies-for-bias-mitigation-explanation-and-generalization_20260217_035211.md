# Causal Inference in Recommender Systems: A Survey of Strategies for Bias Mitigation, Explanation, and Generalization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.00910
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00910
- Reference count: 40
- This survey provides a comprehensive overview of causal inference techniques for addressing bias, improving explainability, and enhancing generalization in recommender systems

## Executive Summary
This survey systematically examines how causal inference frameworks can address fundamental limitations in traditional recommender systems that rely on correlational reasoning from observational data. The paper demonstrates that conventional approaches perpetuate various biases including exposure bias, clickbait bias, and unfairness by failing to distinguish correlation from causation. By introducing Rubin's potential outcome framework and Pearl's structural causal model, the authors provide a theoretical foundation for reasoning about interventions and counterfactuals in recommendation contexts.

The survey categorizes state-of-the-art methods into three distinct problem areas: causal debiasing (using techniques like inverse propensity weighting and substitute confounder adjustment), causal explanation (such as disentangling user interest from conformity), and causal generalization (employing intervention-based predictions). The paper also addresses evaluation strategies using both simulated datasets and real-world randomized experiments, while identifying future research directions that include weakening assumptions and exploring how to leverage positive aspects of biases.

## Method Summary
The survey synthesizes causal inference approaches for recommender systems through a systematic structure that organizes methods by their primary application domain: bias mitigation, explainability, or generalization. The authors employ a comprehensive literature review methodology, examining 40+ references to identify patterns and categorize approaches within the broader context of causal inference theory. The paper presents two foundational causal inference frameworks—Rubin's potential outcome framework and Pearl's structural causal model—as complementary tools for addressing recommendation challenges. For each problem area, the survey describes specific algorithmic approaches, their theoretical underpinnings, and practical implementation considerations, while acknowledging that some state-of-the-art methods may have been published after the survey's completion.

## Key Results
- Traditional recommendation approaches relying on correlational reasoning perpetuate exposure bias, clickbait bias, and unfairness in recommender systems
- Rubin's potential outcome framework and Pearl's structural causal model provide complementary theoretical foundations for causal reasoning in recommendation contexts
- State-of-the-art methods for causal debiasing include inverse propensity weighting and substitute confounder adjustment techniques
- Causal explanation approaches focus on disentangling user interest from conformity patterns
- Causal generalization methods employ intervention-based predictions to improve recommendation robustness

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Causal inference works in recommender systems by explicitly modeling the underlying causal relationships between user behaviors, item exposures, and recommendation outcomes, rather than merely capturing statistical correlations that may be confounded by selection bias and other systematic distortions in observational data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal vs Correlational Reasoning**: Understanding the distinction between correlation and causation is essential because traditional recommendation systems optimize for observed user interactions that may reflect biased exposure rather than genuine preferences - quick check: can you identify a scenario where high correlation does not imply causation in recommendations?
- **Rubin's Potential Outcome Framework**: This framework models counterfactual outcomes by considering what would have happened under different treatment conditions, enabling reasoning about unobserved user responses to different recommendations - quick check: can you formulate a potential outcome for a user who was recommended item A versus item B?
- **Pearl's Structural Causal Model**: This approach uses directed acyclic graphs to represent causal relationships and identify confounding variables that must be adjusted for valid causal inference - quick check: can you draw a simple causal graph showing how item exposure affects both recommendation clicks and future recommendations?
- **Inverse Propensity Weighting**: This technique reweights observed data to correct for selection bias by accounting for the probability of observing each data point under the existing recommendation policy - quick check: can you explain why propensity scores must be bounded away from zero for this method to work?
- **Counterfactual Explanation**: This involves reasoning about alternative scenarios to explain why a particular recommendation was made or why a user behaved in a certain way - quick check: can you construct a counterfactual explanation for why a user clicked on a recommended item?
- **Intervention-based Prediction**: This approach predicts user responses under hypothetical recommendation interventions rather than relying on historical correlations - quick check: can you differentiate between predictions based on correlation versus predictions based on causal intervention?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component Map:
User Interaction Data -> Observational Data Analysis -> Causal Model Construction -> Debiasing/Explanation/Generalization Module -> Recommendation Output

Critical Path:
Data collection and preprocessing -> Identification of confounders and biases -> Selection of appropriate causal inference framework (Rubin vs Pearl) -> Implementation of causal adjustment method -> Evaluation using simulated or randomized experimental data -> Iterative refinement

Design Tradeoffs:
- Theoretical rigor vs computational efficiency: More sophisticated causal models may provide better bias correction but require significantly more computational resources
- Model complexity vs interpretability: Complex causal graphs may capture nuanced relationships but become difficult to explain to stakeholders
- Assumption strength vs practical applicability: Stronger assumptions about unconfoundedness enable simpler methods but may not hold in real-world scenarios
- Evaluation realism vs control: Simulated datasets offer precise control over ground truth but may not capture real-world complexity

Failure Signatures:
- Systematic underestimation of treatment effects when propensity scores are misspecified
- Residual confounding when important variables are omitted from causal graphs
- Overcorrection bias when inverse propensity weights become too extreme
- Poor generalization when causal assumptions do not hold in target domains

## Limitations
- The boundaries between debiasing, explanation, and generalization problem areas remain somewhat blurred in practice
- Some state-of-the-art methods may have been published after the survey's completion, potentially limiting comprehensiveness
- The paper focuses primarily on theoretical frameworks and algorithmic approaches with less emphasis on practical implementation challenges
- Real-world performance metrics across diverse recommendation domains are not extensively covered
