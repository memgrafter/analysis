# Can GPT-3 Perform Statutory Reasoning?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.06100
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06100
- **Authors**: 
- **Reference count**: 30
- **Key outcome**: GPT-3 performs poorly on synthetic statutory reasoning tasks despite better results on SARA dataset, showing fundamental limitations in legal reasoning capabilities.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates GPT-3's ability to perform statutory reasoning, a fundamental legal skill. The authors test GPT-3 on the SARA dataset and find it achieves better results than previous methods but still makes clear errors. They discover GPT-3 has imperfect prior knowledge of the U.S. tax code that underlies SARA. Most significantly, when tested on simple synthetic statutes guaranteed not to be in its training data, GPT-3 performs poorly even on straightforward questions, indicating that statutory reasoning remains a challenge for large language models.

## Method Summary
The researchers evaluate GPT-3 (text-davinci-003) using the SARA dataset of statutory reasoning questions based on U.S. tax code. They employ zero-shot and 4-shot dynamic prompting approaches, with and without chain-of-thought instructions. To test for prior knowledge effects, they create synthetic statutes with controlled complexity (varying width and depth) that GPT-3 couldn't have seen during training. Performance is measured across different prompt configurations and statute complexities to isolate factors affecting reasoning quality.

## Key Results
- GPT-3 achieves better results than previous methods on SARA but still makes clear errors
- GPT-3 has imperfect prior knowledge of U.S. tax code, leading to incorrect reasoning even when statutes are provided
- On simple synthetic statutes, GPT-3 performs poorly, with accuracy near random chance (0.50) on width-3 statutes

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GPT-3 struggles with statutory reasoning because it has imperfect prior knowledge of the actual U.S. Code, leading to incorrect internal reasoning steps even when statutes are provided.
- Mechanism: GPT-3's training data included parts of the U.S. Code, giving it partial but flawed knowledge. When prompted with statutory questions, it retrieves this incorrect knowledge and applies it during reasoning, producing wrong conclusions despite appearing to reason step-by-step.
- Core assumption: GPT-3's prior knowledge is sufficient to retrieve some U.S. Code information but flawed enough to mislead reasoning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We discover that GPT-3 has imperfect prior knowledge of the actual U.S. statutes on which SARA is based."
  - [section] "GPT-3’s responses to zero-shot SARA prompts without any statutory text demonstrate that it has some, possibly imperfect, knowledge of the U.S. tax code."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence of GPT-3's U.S. Code knowledge level; only performance metrics are provided.
- Break condition: If GPT-3 were trained on complete, accurate U.S. Code or if it had perfect recall mechanisms for statutory language.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: GPT-3 performs poorly on synthetic statutes because it struggles to parse the hierarchical structure of subsections and paragraphs, not just the semantic content.
- Mechanism: The typical legal statute structure with nested subsections (a), (b), (1), (2), etc. is difficult for GPT-3 to track. When reasoning through these structures, GPT-3 confuses which definition applies to which subsection, leading to incorrect conclusions.
- Core assumption: The structural complexity of legal statutes (not just their semantic content) poses a challenge for GPT-3's reasoning capabilities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we find that some of GPT-3's poor performance results from difficulty in parsing the typical structure of statutes, containing subsections and paragraphs."
  - [section] "Given the facts in the prompt, GPT-3 correctly reasons that Alice is Bob's stepmother. But then it confuses section 152(d)(2)(C) with (d)(2)(D) and gives the wrong answer."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't provide evidence about GPT-3's parsing capabilities; only experimental results show poor performance.
- Break condition: If GPT-3 were trained specifically on hierarchical legal document structures or if the statutes were presented in simpler sentence form.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: GPT-3's reasoning errors compound when dealing with depth-3 or width-3 synthetic statutes, performing only slightly better than random chance.
- Mechanism: As synthetic statutes increase in complexity (depth and width), GPT-3's chain-of-thought reasoning breaks down. It loses track of which definitions apply at which levels of the hierarchy, and its step-by-step reasoning becomes unreliable.
- Core assumption: GPT-3 can handle simple logical structures but struggles when the number of logical steps and dependencies increases.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "More importantly, we create simple synthetic statutes, which GPT-3 is guaranteed not to have seen during training. We find GPT-3 performs poorly at answering straightforward questions about these simple synthetic statutes."
  - [section] "On all the 3-wide statutes, GPT-3 performs roughly as well as a coin-flip (i.e. 0.50)."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't directly address complexity scaling; only experimental results show performance degradation.
- Break condition: If GPT-3 had better memory mechanisms to track multiple reasoning steps or if the synthetic statutes were simplified.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Chain-of-thought prompting
  - Why needed here: GPT-3 benefits from explicit step-by-step reasoning instructions, as shown by the improvement when "Let's think step by step" is added to prompts.
  - Quick check question: What phrase did Kojima et al. [11] find maximizes GPT-3 performance on reasoning tasks?

- Concept: Dynamic few-shot learning
  - Why needed here: The paper uses 4-shot dynamic prompting where the most similar training cases are selected for each test case, showing GPT-3 can leverage relevant examples for better performance.
  - Quick check question: How many training examples are used in the dynamic few-shot prompting approach?

- Concept: Zero-shot learning
  - Why needed here: The paper tests GPT-3's baseline performance without any examples, revealing its inherent limitations in statutory reasoning.
  - Quick check question: What is the accuracy of GPT-3 on SARA cases with no numbers using zero-shot prompting without statutes?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: GPT-3 (text-davinci-003) -> SARA dataset -> synthetic statutes generator -> chain-of-thought prompting system
- Critical path: Prompt construction → GPT-3 reasoning generation → answer extraction → accuracy measurement
- Design tradeoffs: Using synthetic statutes ensures GPT-3 hasn't seen them before but may not capture all real-world complexity; including statutes in prompts provides context but doesn't prevent GPT-3 from using flawed prior knowledge
- Failure signatures: GPT-3 references wrong subsections (e.g., 152(d)(2)(C) instead of 152(d)(2)(D)), confuses half-siblings with step-siblings, loses track in deep hierarchical structures, performs near-chance on complex synthetic statutes
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test GPT-3 on SARA with 4-shot dynamic prompting with and without "Let's think step by step" phrase
  2. Evaluate GPT-3's ability to identify U.S. Code sections when given statutory text (title identification)
  3. Test GPT-3 on simple synthetic statutes (width 2, depth 2) with zero-shot prompting to establish baseline performance

## Limitations

- The study is limited to GPT-3 text-davinci-003, limiting generalizability to other models
- Synthetic statutes may not capture all real-world complexity and edge cases present in actual legal reasoning
- The study doesn't investigate alternative prompting strategies beyond chain-of-thought
- No direct evidence about GPT-3's actual knowledge of U.S. Code content - only performance patterns are observed

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: GPT-3 performs poorly on synthetic statutes compared to SARA cases - well-supported by experimental evidence with statistical significance.

**Medium Confidence**: GPT-3 has "imperfect prior knowledge" of U.S. tax code - inferred from performance patterns rather than directly measured.

**Low Confidence**: Structural complexity is the primary reason for poor performance on synthetic statutes - lacks direct evidence and systematic testing.

## Next Checks

1. Design experiments that explicitly test GPT-3's recall of specific U.S. Code provisions without reasoning tasks, to separately measure its knowledge base versus reasoning capabilities.

2. Create parallel versions of synthetic statutes using plain language sentences instead of hierarchical subsection structures, then test whether GPT-3's performance improves when structural complexity is removed while semantic content remains constant.

3. Test GPT-3 on statutory reasoning tasks from legal domains beyond tax law (e.g., criminal law, environmental regulations) using the same synthetic statute methodology to determine whether limitations are domain-specific or fundamental.
