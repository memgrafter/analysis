# Improving Code-Switching and Named Entity Recognition in ASR with Speech Editing based Data Augmentation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08588
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08588
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Novel speech editing-based data augmentation significantly outperforms audio splicing and neural TTS for code-switching and named entity recognition ASR tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel data augmentation method for automatic speech recognition that addresses the critical challenge of data scarcity in code-switching and named entity recognition tasks. The proposed approach leverages text-based speech editing to modify real speech recordings by replacing specific words or phrases while preserving speaker characteristics, channel properties, and prosody. Unlike traditional methods that rely on audio splicing or neural text-to-speech synthesis, this technique generates more natural and diverse training data by editing existing speech segments. The method demonstrates substantial improvements in ASR performance, achieving MER reductions of 0.7-1.0% for code-switching tasks and 2-10% improvements in recall and precision for named entity recognition while maintaining comparable overall word error rates.

## Method Summary
The proposed method operates by editing real speech recordings to create augmented training data for ASR models. Rather than using audio splicing (which creates abrupt transitions) or neural TTS (which may sound unnatural), the approach modifies existing speech by replacing target words or phrases with edited versions that maintain the original speaker's voice characteristics, channel conditions, and prosodic patterns. The technique works by identifying speech segments containing target words, extracting their acoustic properties, and replacing them with edited versions that match the surrounding context. This creates synthetic training examples that preserve the naturalness of real speech while introducing linguistic diversity needed for robust ASR performance in code-switching and named entity recognition scenarios.

## Key Results
- Achieves MER reductions of 0.7-1.0% compared to audio splicing and neural TTS baselines for Mandarin-English code-switching tasks
- Improves recall and precision by 2-10% over baseline approaches for named entity recognition tasks
- Maintains comparable overall word error rates while significantly improving performance on specific linguistic phenomena
- Demonstrates superior performance to traditional data augmentation methods across both evaluation tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method succeeds by addressing fundamental limitations in existing data augmentation approaches. Audio splicing creates unnatural transitions between edited segments, while neural TTS often produces speech that lacks the natural prosody and speaker characteristics of real recordings. By editing real speech directly while preserving acoustic properties, the proposed method generates training data that better matches the distribution of real speech patterns. This is particularly important for code-switching and named entity recognition, where the acoustic and linguistic context surrounding named entities and language switches significantly impacts recognition accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Base speech dataset -> Speech editing module -> Augmented dataset -> ASR training pipeline -> Evaluation metrics

**Critical path:** The core workflow involves selecting target words/phrases in base recordings, applying speech editing transformations that preserve acoustic properties, generating augmented training examples, and training ASR models on the combined dataset. The speech editing module is the critical component that determines the quality and effectiveness of the augmentation.

**Design tradeoffs:** The method trades computational simplicity for potentially limited diversity compared to neural TTS, but gains significant advantages in naturalness and speaker consistency. The approach requires sufficient base speech data to perform meaningful edits, making it less suitable for extremely low-resource scenarios.

**Failure signatures:** Poor performance occurs when base speech data lacks sufficient diversity, when target words have ambiguous pronunciation contexts, or when editing operations fail to properly match surrounding prosody. The method may also struggle with highly embedded named entities or rapid code-switching patterns.
