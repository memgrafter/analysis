# Improving (Dis)agreement Detection with Inductive Social Relation Information From Comment-Reply Interactions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03950
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03950
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Achieves state-of-the-art results on DEBAGREEMENT benchmark with 66.91% average macro-F1 score

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of (dis)agreement detection in online discussions by leveraging social relation information extracted from comment-reply interactions. The key insight is that social relations between users can provide valuable context for understanding the nature of their interactions, beyond just the textual content. The authors propose a novel approach that constructs a social relation graph using temporal and authorship information, then pre-trains a graph autoencoder to encode these relations. This social information is fused with textual features from pre-trained language models to improve (dis)agreement detection performance.

## Method Summary
The proposed method constructs a social relation graph using comment-reply interactions, capturing historical relationships between authors without relying on platform-specific features. This graph is then used to pre-train a graph autoencoder with RGCN and DistMult components, encoding social relation information. The resulting embeddings are fused with textual features extracted from pre-trained language models like BERT and RoBERTa. This combined representation is used for (dis)agreement detection, with the social relation information particularly beneficial for long comment-reply pairs.

## Key Results
- Achieves 66.91% average macro-F1 score on DEBAGREEMENT benchmark
- Outperforms state-of-the-art methods by effectively incorporating social relation information
- Demonstrates significant improvements for long comment-reply pairs where social context is particularly valuable

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of this approach stems from the recognition that online interactions are influenced not just by the immediate textual content, but also by the historical relationships between users. By capturing and encoding these social relations through graph representation learning, the model gains access to additional contextual information that helps disambiguate agreement and disagreement. This is especially valuable for longer interactions where surface-level textual cues may be less definitive, and deeper social understanding becomes crucial for accurate classification.

## Foundational Learning

**RGCN (Relational Graph Convolutional Networks)**: A graph neural network variant that handles multiple edge types in relational data. Why needed: To effectively encode complex social relations with different interaction types. Quick check: Verify that RGCN can distinguish between different types of social interactions (agreement, disagreement, neutral).

**DistMult**: A knowledge graph embedding model that represents entities and relations in vector space. Why needed: To capture the latent structure of social relations in a compact form. Quick check: Ensure DistMult embeddings preserve relational patterns across different user pairs.

**Graph Autoencoder**: An unsupervised learning framework for graph representation learning. Why needed: To pre-train social relation embeddings without requiring labeled agreement/disagreement data. Quick check: Validate that pre-training improves downstream (dis)agreement detection performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Comment-Reply pairs -> Social Relation Graph -> Graph Autoencoder (RGCN+DistMult) -> Social Embeddings -> Fusion Layer -> Classification

Critical path: The most critical path is the construction of the social relation graph and its subsequent encoding. Any errors or noise in this stage will propagate through the entire model and affect final performance.

Design tradeoffs: The model trades increased computational complexity for improved performance by adding the social relation graph component. This introduces additional hyperparameters (graph construction parameters, pre-training objectives) that require careful tuning.

Failure signatures: Poor performance may indicate issues with graph construction (e.g., incorrect time intervals, insufficient data), ineffective pre-training (RGCN/DistMult not capturing meaningful relations), or improper fusion of social and textual features.

## Limitations
- The approach may not generalize well to platforms with different interaction dynamics or high user anonymity
- Performance gains are particularly notable for long comment-reply pairs, suggesting limited effectiveness for shorter interactions
- The empirically determined one-week time interval for social relations may not be optimal across different contexts
- Heavy reliance on pre-trained language models means inheriting their limitations (biases, computational costs)
- Focus on English-language data raises questions about cross-linguistic applicability

## Confidence

**High**: The core methodology of incorporating social relation information into (dis)agreement detection is sound and well-supported by the experimental results.

**Medium**: The effectiveness of specific components like the pre-training objectives and graph construction parameters may vary across different datasets and platforms.

**Low**: The generalizability of the approach to non-English contexts and different social media platforms with varying interaction patterns is uncertain.

## Next Checks

1. Evaluate the model's performance on multilingual datasets to assess cross-linguistic applicability.
2. Test the approach on social media platforms with different interaction dynamics (e.g., Reddit, Twitter) to validate generalization across contexts.
3. Conduct ablation studies to quantify the contribution of social relation information for short comment-reply pairs, where its impact may be less pronounced.
