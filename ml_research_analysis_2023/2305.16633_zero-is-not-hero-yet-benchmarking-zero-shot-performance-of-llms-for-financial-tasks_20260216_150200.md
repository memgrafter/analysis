# Zero is Not Hero Yet: Benchmarking Zero-Shot Performance of LLMs for Financial Tasks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16633
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16633
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: ChatGPT achieves impressive zero-shot performance close to fine-tuned models on financial NLP tasks, but fine-tuned RoBERTa models still outperform with F1 scores ranging from 0.70 to 0.97 versus 0.59 to 0.85 for ChatGPT.

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks zero-shot performance of large language models on four financial NLP tasks: hawkish-dovish-neutral classification, sentiment analysis, numerical claim detection, and named entity recognition. The study compares ChatGPT and two open-source generative LLMs (Dolly-V2-12B, H2O-12B) against fine-tuned RoBERTa models. Results show that while ChatGPT achieves impressive performance close to fine-tuned models across all tasks without labeled data, fine-tuned RoBERTa models still outperform with higher F1 scores. Open-source LLMs perform significantly worse, often failing to follow instructions. The study also highlights that labeling data with generative LLMs is 1000 times slower than fine-tuned models, making them less feasible for large-scale research.

## Method Summary
The study benchmarks zero-shot performance of large language models (LLMs) on four financial NLP tasks. It compares ChatGPT and two open-source generative LLMs (Dolly-V2-12B, H2O-12B) against fine-tuned RoBERTa models using weighted F1 scores and time to label a sentence as metrics. The method involves fine-tuning RoBERTa-base and RoBERTa-large models with grid search for hyperparameters, and using zero-shot prompting with ChatGPT, Dolly-V2-12B, and H2O-12B for each task. The study evaluates performance on hawkish-dovish-neutral classification, sentiment analysis, numerical claim detection, and named entity recognition tasks using financial NLP datasets.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned RoBERTa models outperform zero-shot ChatGPT with F1 scores ranging from 0.70 to 0.97 versus 0.59 to 0.85 for ChatGPT
- Open-source LLMs (Dolly-V2-12B, H2O-12B) perform significantly worse than ChatGPT, often failing to follow instructions with "Missing %" rates up to 99.88%
- Labeling data with generative LLMs is approximately 1000 times slower than using fine-tuned models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Zero-shot generative LLMs perform well on financial NLP tasks because they can generalize from large-scale pretraining and in-context learning
- Mechanism: Generative LLMs leverage vast amounts of text data during pretraining to learn semantic relationships and task structures. When given appropriate prompts, they can apply this knowledge to novel tasks without fine-tuning
- Core assumption: The pretraining corpus includes sufficient financial domain knowledge and task patterns
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ChatGPT achieves impressive performance close to fine-tuned models across all tasks without labeled data"
  - [section] "The key takeaways are summarized as follows: Even though zero-shot ChatGPT fails to outperform fine-tuned PLMs, it provides impressive performance across all tasks without having access to any labeled data."
- Break condition: If the task requires highly specialized financial knowledge not well-represented in pretraining data, performance will degrade significantly

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuned models outperform zero-shot LLMs due to task-specific optimization and reduced distributional mismatch
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning adapts the model parameters to the specific task distribution, learning task-specific patterns and reducing the gap between training and inference data distributions
- Core assumption: Labeled data exists that captures the true task distribution and represents the problem adequately
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Fine-tuned RoBERTa models outperform ChatGPT, with F1 scores ranging from 0.70 to 0.97 versus 0.59 to 0.85 for ChatGPT"
  - [section] "Fine-tuned RoBERTa-base and RoBERTa-large have similar performance and they outperform zero-shot LLMs"
- Break condition: If labeled data is scarce or poorly represents the task, fine-tuning may not provide significant advantages

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Open-source LLMs perform worse than closed models like ChatGPT due to differences in training data quality, size, and instruction-following capabilities
- Mechanism: ChatGPT benefits from more extensive training on higher-quality data with better instruction-following patterns, while open-source models may lack these advantages
- Core assumption: Training data quality and instruction-following capabilities significantly impact zero-shot performance
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Open-source LLMs perform significantly worse, often failing to follow instructions"
  - [section] "The performance of fully open-source LLMs for all financial tasks is significantly lower as compared to ChatGPT"
- Break condition: If open-source models receive comparable training resources and instruction-tuning, performance gap may narrow

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific contamination issues exist in financial NLP datasets that could explain performance gaps between fine-tuned models and LLMs like ChatGPT?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that "the gap between fine-tuned model PLMs and ChatGPT is larger here compared to the sentiment analysis task while the gap between Dolly and ChatGPT is smaller. The exact reason for this is uncertain, but one possibility could be contamination, considering that the sentiment analysis dataset is publicly available while the claim dataset is not."
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge the possibility of contamination but do not investigate or quantify it. They state it would be an "intriguing avenue for future research."
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic comparison of training data overlaps between LLMs and financial datasets, analysis of token distributions, or controlled experiments with sanitized datasets.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why do open-source LLMs like Dolly and H2O fail to follow instructions significantly more often than ChatGPT across financial tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper reports that Dolly fails to follow instructions 6.05% of the time for hawkish-dovish classification and H2O fails 42.14% of the time, while ChatGPT succeeds 100% of the time. For NER, Dolly fails 99.88% and H2O fails 99.67% of the time.
- Why unresolved: The authors note this is surprising and suggest "it will be an interesting future study to understand why this is the case and how open-source LLMs can be improved in this dimension."
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of instruction-following datasets used during fine-tuning, ablation studies on prompt engineering, or architectural differences between models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the labeling efficiency of generative LLMs be improved to make them viable for large-scale financial research projects?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights that "the amount of time required to assign labels to data is 1000 times greater when using generative LLMs" compared to fine-tuned models, despite potential performance gaps.
- Why unresolved: The authors identify this as a critical barrier but do not propose solutions or explore potential optimizations for reducing labeling time.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different batching strategies, parallel processing capabilities, or hybrid approaches that combine zero-shot and few-shot learning to reduce labeling time while maintaining performance.

## Limitations

- The study only benchmarks three generative LLMs (ChatGPT, Dolly-V2-12B, H2O-12B), leaving out other potentially competitive models
- Evaluation is limited to four specific financial NLP tasks, which may not generalize to other financial applications or broader NLP domains
- The study doesn't investigate the impact of prompt engineering variations or in-context learning examples, which could significantly affect zero-shot performance
