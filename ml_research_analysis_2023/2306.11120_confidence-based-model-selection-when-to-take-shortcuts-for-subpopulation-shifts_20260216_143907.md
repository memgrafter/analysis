# Confidence-Based Model Selection: When to Take Shortcuts for Subpopulation Shifts

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.11120
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11120
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: COSMOS achieves 2-5% lower average regret across subgroups compared to using individual robust predictors or ensemble methods

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of achieving reliable model performance under subpopulation shifts, where spurious correlations in training data may lead to degraded accuracy on minority groups. The authors propose COSMOS, a confidence-based model selection framework that adaptively chooses among multiple pre-trained classifiers based on their confidence on different clusters of inputs. Unlike prior methods that discard shortcut features, COSMOS leverages them selectively, aiming for high performance on both majority and minority groups without requiring target labels or group annotations. Experiments on four datasets with subpopulation shifts show that COSMOS achieves 2-5% lower average regret across subgroups compared to using individual robust predictors or ensemble methods, and can also be applied to hyperparameter tuning without target labels.

## Method Summary
COSMOS (COnfidence-baSed MOdel Selection) is a framework that selects among multiple pre-trained classifiers based on their confidence scores on different input clusters. The method calibrates each base classifier using temperature scaling to ensure reliable confidence estimates, then clusters test embeddings using K-means. For each cluster, COSMOS selects the classifier with the highest average confidence on points in that cluster. The approach requires no target labels or group annotations and can be applied to hyperparameter tuning by selecting the most frequently chosen model per target domain.

## Key Results
- COSMOS achieves 2-5% lower average regret across subgroups compared to individual robust predictors or ensemble methods
- The framework works across four datasets with spurious correlations (Waterbirds, CelebA, MultiNLI, MetaShift)
- COSMOS can be applied to hyperparameter tuning without target labels by selecting the most frequently chosen model

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Clustering test embeddings before confidence-based model selection reduces variance in confidence estimates and improves subpopulation identification.
- Mechanism: By grouping inputs with similar latent representations, COSMOS aggregates confidence scores over clusters rather than individual points, smoothing out noisy estimates and aligning confidence peaks with subpopulation boundaries.
- Core assumption: Inputs from the same subpopulation have embeddings that cluster together in the chosen metric space.
- Evidence anchors: The paper states that clustering mitigates variance in confidence estimates by first clustering test inputs, as one cluster will likely contain inputs from the same subpopulation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Confidence-based selection can implicitly identify the most suitable model for each subpopulation without explicit group labels.
- Mechanism: After temperature calibration, the softmax probability from each model acts as a proxy for its competence on a given input. By selecting the model with the highest average confidence per cluster, COSMOS approximates the best model for that subpopulation.
- Core assumption: Model confidence correlates with true performance on the subpopulation from which the input originates.
- Evidence anchors: The paper acknowledges that calibration is difficult but finds that simple temperature scaling suffices, and that well-calibrated classifiers can rely on predictive confidence to select the best classifier for each input.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: COSMOS enables effective hyperparameter tuning without target labels by choosing the most frequently selected model per target domain.
- Mechanism: When evaluating candidate models on unlabeled test data, the model chosen most often by COSMOS is inferred to be best for that domain, allowing tuning without validation labels.
- Core assumption: The most frequently selected model across a target domain corresponds to the highest-performing model for that domain.
- Evidence anchors: The paper shows that COSMOS can be used for hyperparameter tuning without any labels from the desired test distribution, and claims to be the first method to do so.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Expected Calibration Error (ECE)
  - Why needed here: COSMOS relies on softmax probabilities as confidence scores, which must be calibrated to reflect true accuracy. ECE quantifies miscalibration.
  - Quick check question: What is the effect of temperature scaling on ECE, and how does lower ECE improve COSMOS performance?

- Concept: Mixture of Experts (MoE) routing
  - Why needed here: COSMOS is conceptually similar to MoE but simpler, using confidence and clustering instead of learned routing. Understanding MoE helps contrast the approaches.
  - Quick check question: How does COSMOS routing differ from learned MoE gating, and what are the trade-offs?

- Concept: Distribution shift and spurious correlation
  - Why needed here: COSMOS targets subpopulation shifts where models trained with shortcut features excel on majority groups but fail on minority groups. Understanding this informs why model diversity is needed.
  - Quick check question: Why do shortcut-based models often outperform robust models on majority subpopulations, and how does this motivate COSMOS?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input → Base classifiers (ERM, JTT, LISA) → Temperature scaling calibration → Confidence scoring → (Optional) Clustering → Model selection → Final prediction
- Critical path: Calibration → Confidence computation → Clustering (if used) → Model selection → Prediction
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using clustering vs. input-dependent selection: Clustering reduces noise but requires embedding quality; input-dependent is simpler but noisier.
  - Number of clusters: Too few may mix subpopulations; too many may overfit noise.
- Failure signatures:
  - Low calibration quality → Confidence no longer correlates with accuracy.
  - Poor embeddings → Clustering fails to recover subpopulations.
  - Similar models → No diversity, so no benefit from selection.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate temperature scaling reduces ECE on held-out source data for each base model.
  2. Test clustering quality on source data by comparing cluster purity to known groups.
  3. Compare input-dependent vs. clustered COSMOS on a validation mixture set to confirm clustering benefit.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does COSMOS performance degrade when base classifiers have highly overlapping feature sets rather than complementary strengths?
- Basis in paper: The paper states "the performance relies on the capabilities of the base classifiers, and will not provide improvements if the base classifiers are all similar to one another."
- Why unresolved: The experiments use pre-trained models with known diverse training objectives (ERM, JTT, LISA) but don't test cases where models have overlapping capabilities or similar architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the degree of overlap in base classifier feature sets while measuring COSMOS performance relative to ensemble methods.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does COSMOS performance change with different clustering algorithms or distance metrics beyond K-means on pre-trained embeddings?
- Basis in paper: The paper uses only K-means clustering without exploring alternatives like hierarchical clustering, spectral clustering, or learned distance metrics that might better capture subpopulation structure.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses only K-means clustering without exploring alternatives like hierarchical clustering, spectral clustering, or learned distance metrics that might better capture subpopulation structure.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing multiple clustering approaches on the same datasets while measuring both accuracy and regret metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can COSMOS be extended to handle protected attributes when shortcut features overlap with sensitive information?
- Basis in paper: The paper acknowledges this limitation but doesn't propose any mechanisms for detecting or handling such cases, or for modifying the approach to maintain fairness constraints.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this limitation but doesn't propose any mechanisms for detecting or handling such cases, or for modifying the approach to maintain fairness constraints.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of a variant that can either detect protected attribute overlap or incorporate fairness constraints into the model selection process.

## Limitations
- COSMOS relies heavily on the quality of base classifiers and their calibration, and will not provide improvements if the base classifiers are all similar to one another
- The method is not suitable for settings where shortcut features correspond to protected attributes (e.g., race, gender, age)
- Performance depends on the assumption that embeddings preserve subpopulation structure - if clustering fails to recover true subpopulations, selection quality degrades

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The clustering mechanism reduces variance in confidence estimates and improves subpopulation identification, as demonstrated by experimental results showing 2-5% lower average regret compared to baseline methods. The calibration and model selection procedures are clearly specified and reproducible.

**Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of COSMOS for hyperparameter tuning without target labels, while theoretically sound, has limited empirical validation beyond the JTT hyperparameter selection case. The generalizability across different types of subpopulation shifts remains to be thoroughly tested.

**Low Confidence**: The performance bounds under worst-case scenarios (e.g., when all models are poorly calibrated or embeddings completely fail to capture subpopulation structure) are not explicitly characterized. The sensitivity to hyperparameter choices like cluster size and temperature scaling parameters is also not fully explored.

## Next Checks

1. **Calibration Validation**: Systematically evaluate ECE across different distribution shifts and quantify how temperature scaling affects selection accuracy. Test whether confidence continues to correlate with accuracy under varying degrees of shift severity.

2. **Embedding Quality Assessment**: Conduct ablation studies varying the embedding space (different layers, different architectures) to determine sensitivity to representation quality. Measure clustering purity against ground truth subpopulations when available.

3. **Robustness to Model Similarity**: Test COSMOS performance when base classifiers are highly similar (e.g., multiple ERM variants) versus diverse (ERM, JTT, LISA) to establish minimum diversity requirements for effective selection.
