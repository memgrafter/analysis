# Unsupervised hierarchical clustering using the learning dynamics of RBMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.01851
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01851
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Method automatically uncovers hierarchical structures in complex datasets by exploiting RBM learning dynamics

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method to automatically uncover hierarchical structures in complex datasets by exploiting the learning dynamics of Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs). It uses the Plefka expansion to derive mean-field TAP equations for Potts-RBMs, enabling the identification of multiple free-energy minima that correspond to data features. By iteratively clustering data points using these minima at different training epochs, the method constructs relational trees that reflect the hierarchical organization of the dataset. Tested on synthetic and real-world datasets (MNIST, human genome mutations, and protein sequences), the approach successfully recovers known categories and subcategories, demonstrating its potential for interpretable feature extraction in unsupervised learning.

## Method Summary
The method trains a Potts-RBM on the dataset using PCD-k, saving model states at regular intervals during training. For each saved model, TAP equations derived from the Plefka expansion are initialized with data points and iterated until convergence to find fixed points. DBSCAN is then used to cluster data points based on their converged TAP fixed points. Finally, a hierarchical tree is built by iteratively clustering the data, merging fixed points from older models into younger models' basins of attraction, starting from the oldest model. The approach exploits the hierarchical structure of features learned during RBM training, where global features are captured early and finer substructure is detected later.

## Key Results
- Successfully recovered known categories and subcategories in MNIST, human genome mutation, and protein sequence datasets
- Demonstrated the method's potential for interpretable feature extraction in unsupervised learning
- Showed that the hierarchical structure of RBM learning dynamics can be exploited to uncover dataset organization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The method uses the learning dynamics of RBMs to automatically uncover hierarchical structures in datasets. During training, the RBM progressively learns features at different levels of abstraction. Early in training, it captures global features; later, it learns finer, more localized features. By analyzing the RBM's free energy landscape at different training epochs, the method identifies multiple free-energy minima corresponding to data features and uses these to construct a hierarchical tree. The core assumption is that features learned by the RBM during training have a hierarchical structure, with the most relevant properties learned early and finer substructure detected later.

### Mechanism 2
The method uses TAP equations derived from the Plefka expansion to approximate the free energy of the RBM. The Plefka expansion provides a high-temperature approximation of the Gibbs free energy, which is then used to derive self-consistent TAP equations. These equations are iterated until convergence to associate each data point with the "closest" metastable state, thereby clustering the data. The core assumption is that the high-temperature expansion of the Gibbs free energy accurately approximates the true free energy of the RBM, especially in the early stages of learning.

### Mechanism 3
The method constructs a relational tree by iteratively clustering data points using the free-energy minima identified at different training epochs. Starting with the oldest RBM model, the TAP equations are used to cluster the data. Then, for progressively younger models, the TAP equations are iterated using the previously found fixed points as initial conditions. This process continues until all data points converge to a single global minimum, creating a hierarchical tree. The core assumption is that the features learned by the RBM at different stages of training are consistent and can be used to construct a meaningful hierarchy.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: RBM model -> TAP equations -> DBSCAN clustering -> Tree construction algorithm
- Critical path:
  1. Train the RBM on the dataset
  2. At different training epochs, compute the TAP equations for the RBM
  3. Use DBSCAN to cluster the data points based on their fixed points
  4. Construct a hierarchical tree by iteratively clustering the data using the results from step 3
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs. computational cost: Using a more accurate approximation of the free energy would increase accuracy but also computational cost
  - Number of training epochs vs. tree resolution: Using more training epochs would provide a finer-grained tree but would also increase computational cost
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor clustering results: This could indicate that the TAP equations are not accurately approximating the free energy, or that the clustering algorithm is not appropriate for the data
  - Inconsistent tree structure: This could indicate that the features learned by the RBM at different stages are not consistent, or that the tree construction algorithm is not working correctly
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train an RBM on a simple synthetic dataset and visualize the learning dynamics by plotting the eigenvalues of the weight matrix over time
  2. Compute the TAP equations for the RBM at different training epochs and visualize the fixed points in the magnetization space
  3. Use DBSCAN to cluster the data points based on their fixed points and visualize the clustering results

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What is the maximum training age at which the TAP equations provide reliable fixed points for the Potts RBM? The paper mentions that the validity of the second-order approximation cannot be directly assessed without knowing the true free energy, but suggests comparing TAP fixed points with first-order approximations as a qualitative check. This remains unresolved due to lack of systematic analysis of how the TAP approximation degrades with training time across different datasets.

### Open Question 2
How sensitive is the hierarchical tree structure to the stochastic nature of RBM training? While the paper acknowledges variability, it does not quantify the consistency of key features across multiple runs or provide a statistical measure of tree stability. This requires quantitative analysis of tree similarity metrics across multiple training runs to identify which features are consistently preserved.

### Open Question 3
Can the learning trajectory be replaced with an inverse temperature annealing procedure for tree construction? The paper explicitly compares tree construction using the learning trajectory versus inverse temperature annealing, finding the latter inadequate for the MNIST dataset. This remains unresolved as the paper does not explore alternative annealing schedules or investigate why the learning trajectory is superior.

## Limitations
- The method's reliance on the Plefka expansion and TAP equations introduces approximation errors that become more severe as training progresses
- The clustering results depend heavily on DBSCAN parameters and the choice of training epochs for model snapshots
- The method's generalizability to other dataset types and its robustness to hyperparameter choices remain unclear

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The core mathematical framework (Plefka expansion, TAP equations for Potts-RBM) is well-established and correctly applied
- **Medium confidence**: The clustering results on synthetic and biological datasets appear reasonable, though quantitative metrics are limited
- **Low confidence**: The method's generalizability to other dataset types and its robustness to hyperparameter choices remain unclear

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation study**: Remove the iterative clustering component and compare results with standard RBM-based clustering to quantify the benefit of the hierarchical approach
2. **Synthetic data stress test**: Systematically vary the number of clusters, cluster overlap, and data dimensionality in synthetic datasets to map the method's performance boundaries
3. **Parameter sensitivity analysis**: Conduct grid searches over DBSCAN parameters and model snapshot intervals to identify robust default settings and quantify sensitivity to hyperparameter choices
