# On the robust learning mixtures of linear regressions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.15317
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15317
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 17
- This paper proposes a quasi-polynomial time algorithm for robust learning of mixtures of linear regressions with improved separation conditions

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel approach to robust learning of mixtures of linear regressions (MLR) by connecting the problem to Gaussian mixture models through a thresholding technique. The key innovation is a simple thresholding step that transforms the MLR problem into a more tractable form with a mild separation condition. This approach achieves better robustness than previous methods, requiring only a constant separation rather than separation proportional to noise level times a polynomial factor.

The algorithm operates in quasi-polynomial time and is particularly effective when the number of components is large and noise levels are moderate. By leveraging recent advances in sum-of-squares proofs for explicitly bounded mixture models, the method provides a practical solution to a challenging problem in robust statistics with theoretical guarantees on recovery accuracy.

## Method Summary
The method converts the MLR problem into a Gaussian mixture problem through thresholding of observed values. After thresholding, the algorithm applies a robust clustering algorithm for explicitly bounded mixture models to identify component clusters. Finally, it recovers the original weight vectors from the clustered data using moment-based estimation techniques. The approach requires mild separation conditions between component vectors and operates under subgaussian noise assumptions.

## Key Results
- Achieves quasi-polynomial time complexity with separation condition Δ > c for some c > 0
- Requires only mild separation conditions compared to previous work (no dependence on σ × poly(k))
- Time complexity is exp(O(((1+σ)/c²)²)) × poly(1/p_min, k, d, 1/ε, (dk)(log(1/ε))²)
- Provides O(ε) accuracy in recovering component vectors

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Thresholding converts MLR to a mixture of Gaussians with tractable separation condition
- Mechanism: By restricting samples to a narrow band around expected values, noise influence is reduced, creating a conditional distribution that is approximately Gaussian with larger effective separation between component means
- Core assumption: Noise η is subgaussian and threshold constant is chosen large enough to suppress outliers
- Evidence anchors: Lemma 2.1 shows conditional expectation μᵢ is close to scaled weight vector; Lemma 2.2 proves separation between μᵢ is Ω((1+σ)√(log(1/ε))/c)

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Conditional distribution Di has controlled moments enabling sum-of-squares algorithms
- Mechanism: Lemma 2.3 shows Di satisfies the O(log(1/ε))-explicitly bounded mixture model definition with variance proxy O((1+σ)/c)²
- Core assumption: Noise distribution has sufficiently light tails and thresholding keeps conditional variance under control
- Evidence anchors: Lemma 2.3 explicitly proves concentration property and SOS certificate for Di

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Weight recovery uses cluster means to reconstruct direction and magnitude of original weights with O(ε) accuracy
- Mechanism: After clustering, sample mean approximates vi, and ratio y/x recovers |wi| up to O(ε). Direction is given by w′ᵢ = vi/|vi|
- Core assumption: Sample averages concentrate well and noise in y is bounded as in (2.22)
- Evidence anchors: Equations (2.19)-(2.21) show reconstruction steps and error analysis

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing (thresholding) -> Conditional sampling -> Clustering -> Mean estimation -> Weight recovery
- Critical path: 1. Thresholding → 2. Conditional sampling → 3. Clustering → 4. Mean estimation → 5. Weight recovery
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger thresholding constant → fewer outliers but smaller effective sample size
  - Larger ε → faster runtime and fewer samples needed but lower final accuracy
  - Larger σ → more robust to noise but requires stronger separation and larger sample complexity
- Failure signatures:
  - Too few samples per cluster → high variance in recovered weights
  - Poor separation between cluster means → incorrect clustering
  - Extreme noise levels → thresholding fails to suppress outliers
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run algorithm on synthetic MLR data with k=2, d=5, σ=0.1, verify recovery accuracy for varying ε
  2. Vary σ from 0.01 to 1.0 and measure how required separation Δ changes
  3. Test effect of thresholding constant C′ on number of effective samples and final accuracy

## Limitations
- Quasi-polynomial runtime may be prohibitive for large-scale applications
- Robustness limited to specified subgaussian noise model, may not extend to arbitrary corruptions
- Exact constants for thresholding procedure and separation conditions not fully specified
