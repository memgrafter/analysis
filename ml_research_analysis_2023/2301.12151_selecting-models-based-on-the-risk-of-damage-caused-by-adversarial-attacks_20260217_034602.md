# Selecting Models based on the Risk of Damage Caused by Adversarial Attacks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.12151
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12151
- Reference count: 22
- The paper proposes a method to estimate the probability of damage caused by adversarial attacks on machine learning models, outperforming conventional metrics like attack success rate and minimal perturbation size.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of selecting machine learning models based on their robustness to adversarial attacks. The authors propose a method to estimate the probability of damage caused by adversarial attacks, decomposing it into the probability of attack success and the probability of detection. They provide an unbiased and consistent estimator using Monte Carlo simulation, and when a detector is unavailable, an alternative method based on comparing models' robustness. Experiments demonstrate that their proposed estimator outperforms conventional metrics in selecting the most robust model, with the key metric Pdam providing a clear interpretation of model robustness against adversarial attacks.

## Method Summary
The method estimates the probability of damage (Pdam) from adversarial attacks by decomposing it into the probability of attack success and the probability of detection. Using Monte Carlo simulation, it provides an unbiased and consistent estimator for both quantities. When a detector is available, it fits a logistic regression model to estimate the detection probability function based on perturbation sizes. For cases without a detector, it provides an alternative method using average attack success ratios across models as a proxy for detection probability.

## Key Results
- The proposed estimator is statistically consistent and unbiased.
- The method outperforms conventional metrics like attack success rate and minimal perturbation size in selecting the most robust model.
- Pdam provides a clear and actionable interpretation of model robustness against adversarial attacks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The estimator is statistically unbiased and consistent because it directly models the joint probability of attack success and detection as an expectation over a well-defined distribution.
- Mechanism: By decomposing the damage probability Pdam into P(successful attack) x P(undetected | successful attack), the method uses Monte Carlo sampling to estimate each term from the same finite sample, ensuring unbiasedness through the law of large numbers.
- Core assumption: The attack strategy produces perturbations whose detection probability depends only on their size, not on the specific model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that our proposed estimator is statistically consistent and unbiased."
  - [section 4.2.1] "Empirical distribution functions are unbiased and consistent estimators of their distribution functions."
- Break condition: If the detection probability does not depend primarily on perturbation size, or if the attack strategy's success rate is not representative of the true adversarial capability, the estimator becomes biased.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using the detection probability function ΨA,M(τ) conditioned on perturbation size allows the same detection estimate to be reused across different models.
- Mechanism: The method estimates ΨA,M once per application, not per model, by fitting a logistic regression on perturbation sizes vs detection outcomes. This separates the model-specific attack success distribution from the application-specific detection behavior.
- Core assumption: The probability of detection depends only on perturbation size, not on which model produced the perturbation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2.2] "With a suitable distance measurement, we can assume that the perturbation size statistically determines the detection probability function."
  - [section 4.3] "If all ASRA,MJ estimates are based on the same sample X, one can rearrange Equation 8 to make its computation efficient."
- Break condition: If detection depends on model-specific features (e.g., different feature sensitivities), the assumption fails and ΨA,M must be refit per model.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: When no detector is available, the method provides a relative comparison of model robustness by assuming models more sensitive to perturbations are also more likely to be detected.
- Mechanism: It constructs an average detection function ˆΨavgA,M that treats the average attack success ratio across models as a proxy for detection probability, allowing ranking even without explicit human feedback.
- Core assumption: Models with higher attack success rates are also more likely to be detected, making ASR a reasonable proxy for detection likelihood.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3] "One way is to assume that the average sensitivity of models to adversarial perturbations is similar to the sensitivity of a potential detector."
  - [section 5.1] "We compute ASR (τ), with two thresholds τ∈{ 2/255, 8/255}: the fraction of observations with at least one successful adversarial example with perturbation size L∞≤τ."
- Break condition: If detection depends on factors unrelated to perturbation size or model sensitivity (e.g., content type), the proxy becomes unreliable.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Probability theory and Monte Carlo simulation
  - Why needed here: The estimator relies on sampling from the space of adversarial perturbations and computing expectations via empirical averages.
  - Quick check question: Why is the estimator unbiased? Because it computes the sample mean of a random variable whose expectation equals the true probability.

- Concept: Adversarial attacks and perturbation metrics
  - Why needed here: The method requires understanding how attacks generate perturbations and how to measure their size (e.g., L∞ norm) to correlate with detection.
  - Quick check question: What assumption links perturbation size to detection? That smaller perturbations are less likely to be noticed by humans or automated detectors.

- Concept: Logistic regression for probability estimation
  - Why needed here: Fitting the detection probability function ΨA,M uses logistic regression on perturbation sizes to predict detection outcomes.
  - Quick check question: Why logistic regression? Because detection is a binary outcome (detected/not detected) and we need a smooth probability estimate.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Attack strategy module -> Perturbation size calculator -> Detection probability estimator -> Risk estimator -> Benchmark comparator

- Critical path:
  1. Generate perturbations using attack strategy on test set.
  2. Compute perturbation sizes for each model.
  3. Fit detection probability function ΨA,M if detector data available.
  4. Compute Attack Success Ratio (ASR) for each model.
  5. Integrate ASR with ΨA,M to obtain Pdam estimates.
  6. Rank models by Pdam.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Sample size vs computation: Larger samples improve estimator consistency but increase attack computation cost.
  - Attack diversity vs runtime: More attack types improve robustness of the attack strategy but increase runtime.
  - Detector availability vs proxy methods: Direct human feedback gives accurate ΨA,M, but average ASR proxy works when humans are unavailable.

- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in Pdam estimates: Indicates too small sample size or unstable attack strategy.
  - Contradiction between ASR(τ) and Pdam rankings: Suggests detection probability does not correlate well with perturbation size.
  - Pdam close to 1 for all models: Indicates attack strategy is too strong or detection is too weak.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run attack strategy on a small sample (n=20) and compute ASR for each model to verify attack success rates.
  2. Fit ΨA,M using logistic regression on perturbation sizes vs simulated detection outcomes (e.g., threshold-based detection).
  3. Compute Pdam for each model and verify that models with lower ASR also have lower Pdam, confirming the correlation assumption.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the most appropriate distance metric (d) to use for measuring adversarial perturbations in different applications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the difficulty of selecting a distance metric that correlates well with detection probability, mentioning Lp metrics as a common choice but noting an active debate about which metrics align well with human perception.

- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies across multiple applications and human perception experiments to determine which distance metrics best correlate with detection probability and align with human perception of adversarial perturbations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we improve the efficiency of estimating the detection probability function ΨA,M without compromising accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that fitting ΨA,M requires querying a detector (e.g., a human in the loop), which can be time-consuming and limit sample sizes.
- Why unresolved: The paper proposes using logistic regression to fit ΨA,M but does not explore other potential methods for improving efficiency while maintaining accuracy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies of different methods for estimating ΨA,M, including active learning approaches, transfer learning from related tasks, or synthetic data generation techniques, to determine which methods provide the best balance of efficiency and accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of attack strategy (A) impact the estimation of operational risk?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper defines an attack strategy as a function that returns the smallest perturbation obtained by applying a set of applicable adversarial attacks, but does not explore how the choice of attacks within this set impacts the risk estimation.
- Why unresolved: The paper assumes that an adversary will use the most effective attacks available but does not investigate how different combinations of attacks or attack parameters might affect the risk estimation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the attack strategy composition and parameters, comparing the resulting operational risk estimates to determine which attack strategies provide the most accurate and reliable risk assessments.

## Limitations
- The estimator assumes detection probability depends primarily on perturbation size, not on other factors like input content or model-specific features.
- Without a detector, the proxy method assumes ASR correlates with detection likelihood, which may not hold in practice.
- The method requires access to a representative sample of observations and an effective attack strategy to generate meaningful perturbations.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The estimator's unbiasedness and consistency under the stated assumptions (law of large numbers for empirical distribution functions, logistic regression assumptions).
- Medium confidence: The practical effectiveness of the average ASR proxy when no detector is available, as this depends on untested assumptions about detection behavior.
- Medium confidence: The transferability of the detection function across models, as this assumes perturbation size is the dominant factor in detection probability.
