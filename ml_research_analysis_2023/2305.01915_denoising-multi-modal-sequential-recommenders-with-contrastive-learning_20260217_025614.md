# Denoising Multi-modal Sequential Recommenders with Contrastive Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.01915
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01915
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Achieves up to 18.23% relative improvement in Recall@20 and 7.96% in NDCG@50 over state-of-the-art methods

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical problem of denoising multi-modal sequential recommenders, where noisy modalities and items in user behavior sequences degrade recommendation performance. The authors propose a weakly-supervised framework that leverages contrastive learning and explainable machine learning techniques to identify and mitigate the effects of noise. By computing gradients to assess contributions of modalities and items to next-item prediction, the method creates augmented sequences that push noisy elements away in the embedding space. Experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate significant performance gains over existing methods while providing interpretable explanations at both modality and item levels.

## Method Summary
The proposed framework consists of a multi-modal user encoder with modality-wise and item-wise self-attention layers, followed by a prediction head. Point-of-interests and noises are localized by computing gradients of the next-item prediction score with respect to item and modality activations. User augmentation is performed by strengthening high-interest score items/modalities and reducing low-interest ones to create positive and negative augmented sequences. Contrastive learning objectives then push away representations influenced by noise while pulling together clean user preference representations. The method is trained end-to-end with a combination of contrastive loss and prediction loss.

## Key Results
- Achieves up to 18.23% relative improvement in Recall@20 compared to state-of-the-art methods
- Demonstrates 7.96% relative improvement in NDCG@50 on multi-modal recommendation benchmarks
- Provides interpretable explanations of user preferences at both modality and item levels

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Contrastive learning with self-augmented sequences can push away noisy modality/item features.
- Mechanism: By replacing high-interest score items/modalities with randomly sampled ones, the model creates negative views of the original user embeddings. Pushing these negatives away in the embedding space reduces the influence of noise.
- Core assumption: Items/modalities with higher gradients (interest scores) are more likely to be point-of-interests.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We augment the original multi-modal behavior sequences by strengthening point-of-interests or reducing potential noises to obtain augmented positive and negative multi-modal behavior sequences"
- Break condition: If gradients do not correlate with actual user preferences, the noise localization fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Explainable ML techniques (gradients) can identify which modalities/items directly cause user behavior.
- Mechanism: Compute gradients of the next-item prediction score with respect to item and modality activations. Higher gradients indicate stronger influence on the prediction.
- Core assumption: The gradient magnitude reflects the importance of a modality/item for the prediction task.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Inspired by explainable machine learning techniques[30], we measure the contributions as the gradients estimated in the next-item prediction task."
- Break condition: If the prediction model is too complex or non-differentiable, gradient computation becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Decomposing user encoder into modality-wise and item-wise attention layers captures heterogeneous dependencies better than simple pooling.
- Mechanism: Self-attention at both levels allows dynamic weighting of modalities and items based on their relevance, improving representation quality.
- Core assumption: Modalities and items have varying levels of importance for different users and contexts.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Given the features {i_m}_{m=1,...,|M|} of different modalities, we obtain the enhanced representation of mth modality as the following:" (describes self-attention for modalities)
- Break condition: If the attention mechanism overfits to training data, generalization suffers.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Self-attention mechanism
  - Why needed here: To dynamically weigh the importance of different modalities and items based on their relevance to the user's current context
  - Quick check question: How does self-attention differ from simple weighted averaging in capturing item relationships?

- Concept: Gradient-based attribution
  - Why needed here: To identify which modalities/items are most influential in predicting the next item without explicit feedback
  - Quick check question: What assumptions are made when using gradients as importance scores?

- Concept: Contrastive learning objectives
  - Why needed here: To push away user representations influenced by noise and pull together representations of clean user preferences
  - Quick check question: How does the contrastive loss formulation ensure noise is reduced in the final embeddings?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Multi-modal feature extractors -> Modality-wise encoder (self-attention) -> Item-wise encoder (self-attention) -> User augmentation module -> Contrastive learning head -> Prediction head

- Critical path:
  1. Extract modality features → 2. Modality-wise attention → 3. Item-wise attention → 4. Compute interest scores → 5. Augment sequences → 6. Generate positive/negative embeddings → 7. Apply contrastive loss → 8. Predict next item

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Attention vs. pooling: Attention captures dynamic relationships but adds complexity
  - Augmentation rate: Too high causes positive sample collapse, too low reduces denoising effectiveness
  - Gradient vs. other attribution: Gradients are efficient but may be less interpretable than perturbation-based methods

- Failure signatures:
  - Performance plateaus despite increased model capacity
  - Attention weights become uniform across modalities/items
  - Augmentation produces nearly identical sequences to originals

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation: Remove contrastive learning objective, measure performance drop
  2. Sensitivity: Vary augmentation rates (γ_i, γ_m) and observe impact on metrics
  3. Visualization: Plot attention weight variances across runs to verify denoising effect

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of the proposed framework vary when using different numbers of augmented positive/negative user pairs (J)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper conducts experiments to evaluate the effect of varying J among {1, 2, 4} on the performance of the proposed framework.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides some insights into the impact of J on performance, further exploration is needed to determine the optimal number of augmented pairs for different datasets and scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Additional experiments with a wider range of J values and on more diverse datasets would help determine the optimal number of augmented pairs for the proposed framework.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the proposed framework perform when applied to different domains and types of recommendation systems?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper evaluates the proposed framework on three multi-modal recommendation benchmarks (Tiktok, MovieLens, MIND-MM) and shows promising results. However, the performance on other domains and recommendation systems remains unexplored.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on specific domains and datasets, and it is unclear how the proposed framework would generalize to other recommendation scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments on a wider range of recommendation systems and domains, such as e-commerce, music, or travel recommendations, would provide insights into the framework's generalizability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed framework handle noisy modalities and items that are not explicitly labeled as point-of-interests or noises?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper proposes a weakly-supervised framework that identifies point-of-interests and noises by assessing their contributions to next-item prediction. However, it is unclear how the framework handles noisy modalities and items that are not explicitly labeled.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide detailed information on how the framework deals with unlabeled noisy modalities and items, which is a common scenario in real-world recommendation systems.
- What evidence would resolve it: Additional experiments and analysis on the framework's performance when handling unlabeled noisy modalities and items would help understand its robustness and limitations.

## Limitations

- Gradient-based attribution assumes linear relationships between feature importance and prediction outcomes, which may not hold for complex recommendation models
- Performance depends on having sufficient high-quality pre-trained feature extractors for each modality
- Contrastive learning framework relies on assumption that augmented samples with reduced noise will naturally form meaningful positive pairs

## Confidence

- Contrastive denoising mechanism: High (strong empirical evidence from significant performance improvements across three datasets)
- Gradient-based noise localization: Medium (theoretical soundness but limited ablation studies on attribution methods)
- Attention-based multi-modal encoding: Medium (standard approach but no comparison with simpler pooling methods)
