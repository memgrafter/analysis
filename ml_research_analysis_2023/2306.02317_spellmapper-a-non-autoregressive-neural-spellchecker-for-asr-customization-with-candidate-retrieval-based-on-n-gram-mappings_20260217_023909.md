# SpellMapper: A non-autoregressive neural spellchecker for ASR customization with candidate retrieval based on n-gram mappings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02317
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02317
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: 6.4-21.4% relative WER reduction and 49-68% recovery of ideal improvement on three public datasets

## Executive Summary
SpellMapper addresses the challenge of customizing ASR outputs with user-defined vocabulary by introducing a non-autoregressive neural spellchecker that combines n-gram-based candidate retrieval with BERT-based correction. The system uses a novel candidate retrieval algorithm based on misspelled n-gram mappings collected from a parallel corpus of Wikipedia titles and corrupted ASR outputs, achieving 74-90% recall with just 10 candidates. The spellchecker processes concatenated ASR hypothesis and candidate phrases at character level with subword embeddings, demonstrating strong performance across different domains and ASR model types.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two main components: candidate retrieval and neural spellchecking. The retrieval algorithm collects n-gram mappings from Wikipedia headings and their corrupted versions, then indexes user vocabulary with all possible misspelled fragments. For each ASR hypothesis, it retrieves up to 10 candidate phrases by matching overlapping n-grams and filtering by coverage. The spellchecker uses a pre-trained BERT model that processes concatenated ASR hypothesis and candidates as character-level input with subword embeddings, learning to predict character-level labels indicating which candidate (if any) is correct. The system is trained on synthetic data generated by corrupting Wikipedia titles with ASR-like errors and pairing them with correct and incorrect candidates.

## Key Results
- 6.4-21.4% relative WER reduction on three public datasets
- 49-68% recovery of ideal improvement compared to having correct transcriptions
- 74-90% recall with top-10 candidates in retrieval experiments

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: N-gram-based candidate retrieval achieves higher recall than edit-distance-based retrieval by capturing more common misspelling patterns.
- Mechanism: The algorithm collects n-gram mappings from a parallel corpus of correct Wikipedia titles and corrupted ASR outputs, then indexes user vocabulary with all possible misspelled fragments. During retrieval, it matches overlapping n-grams in the ASR hypothesis against the indexed vocabulary, selecting candidates with the most hits and filtering by coverage.
- Core assumption: Common misspellings follow predictable n-gram transformation patterns that can be learned from a large parallel corpus.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We propose: 1) a novel algorithm for candidate retrieval, based on misspelled n-gram mappings, which gives up to 90% recall with just the top 10 candidates on Spoken Wikipedia"
  - [section] "To deal with large user vocabularies, most of these models include candidate retrieval mechanisms, usually based on minimum edit distance between fragments of ASR hypothesis and user phrases. However, the edit-distance approach is slow, non-trainable, and may have low recall as it relies only on common letters"
  - [corpus] "Found 25 related papers (using 8). Average neighbor FMR=0.56" - Weak corpus evidence for this specific n-gram retrieval approach
- Break condition: If the parallel corpus lacks sufficient diversity in misspelling patterns, the n-gram mappings won't capture the variations present in new user vocabularies, leading to poor recall.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Non-autoregressive BERT-based spellchecker can process concatenated ASR hypothesis and multiple candidates in a single forward pass.
- Mechanism: The model tokenizes input at character level, concatenates ASR hypothesis with up to 10 candidate phrases using special [SEP] tokens, and learns to predict character-level labels indicating which candidate (if any) is correct at each position.
- Core assumption: BERT can effectively process character-level sequences with multiple segments without requiring decoder attention or custom neural blocks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "2) a non-autoregressive neural model based on BERT architecture, where the initial transcript and ten candidates are combined into one input"
  - [section] "We use a pre-trained BERT model with 67M parameters as a backbone model... Our model was trained on 10M examples to get good spellchecking quality"
  - [corpus] "Weak corpus evidence for this specific BERT-based approach with multiple separators"
- Break condition: If candidate phrases are too long relative to BERT's 512-token limit, or if the character-level approach loses too much contextual information, the model's accuracy will degrade significantly.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Synthetic training data generation enables effective learning without requiring manual annotation of ASR errors.
- Mechanism: The system synthetically creates training examples by taking correct Wikipedia titles, corrupting them with ASR-like errors, and pairing them with correct and incorrect candidate phrases to simulate real-world scenarios.
- Core assumption: Errors generated through TTS + ASR pipeline sufficiently approximate real ASR errors to train an effective spellchecker.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We create this dataset synthetically, reusing our collected corpus of correct and misspelled Wikipedia titles... We use the original spelling as the correct candidate and sample incorrect candidates from random, similar, or intersecting titles"
  - [section] "We use a pre-trained BERT model... The model was trained for 5 epochs on 8 GPUs with a global batch of 32 ∗ 8"
  - [corpus] "Weak corpus evidence for synthetic data generation approach effectiveness"
- Break condition: If the synthetic error patterns don't match real-world ASR error distributions, the model will fail to generalize to actual use cases.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preparation pipeline (offline) -> n-gram mappings collection -> vocabulary indexing -> Online inference pipeline -> candidate retrieval -> neural model inference -> post-processing -> Core model: Character-level BERT with subword embedding concatenation

- Critical path: ASR output -> candidate retrieval (10 candidates) -> BERT inference -> post-processing -> final correction

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Character-level vs. subword tokenization: Character-level provides fine-grained control but loses word boundaries; subword helps with context but may miss character-level errors
  - 10 candidates vs. more candidates: Higher recall vs. increased computation and potential false positives
  - Synthetic vs. real training data: Scalability and control vs. potential distribution mismatch

- Failure signatures:
  - Low recall: Candidate retrieval missing correct phrases or BERT model failing to identify correct candidates
  - High false positives: Model hallucinating user phrases in unrelated text
  - Slow inference: Inefficient candidate retrieval or BERT processing exceeding latency requirements

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test candidate retrieval recall on synthetic examples with known correct candidates to isolate retrieval performance
  2. Evaluate BERT model accuracy on fixed candidate sets to measure neural model performance independent of retrieval
  3. Measure end-to-end latency with different candidate retrieval parameters (e.g., top 5 vs. top 10 candidates)

## Limitations
- The candidate retrieval mechanism relies heavily on the quality and coverage of n-gram mappings from the Wikipedia parallel corpus, which may not generalize to vocabularies with different misspelling patterns.
- The synthetic training data generation may not fully capture the complexity of real ASR errors, potentially introducing distribution mismatches that affect model generalization.
- The character-level BERT model with subword embeddings shows strong performance, but the 512-token limit for BERT input could become a bottleneck for very long ASR hypotheses or candidate phrases.

## Confidence
- Candidate Retrieval Effectiveness: Medium confidence - The recall metrics (74-90%) are promising, but performance depends on corpus quality and vocabulary characteristics.
- Neural Model Accuracy: Medium confidence - WER reduction results are strong, but synthetic training data limitations may affect real-world generalization.
- Domain Generalization: Medium confidence - Results across three datasets show promise, but the specific domains tested may not represent all potential use cases.

## Next Checks
