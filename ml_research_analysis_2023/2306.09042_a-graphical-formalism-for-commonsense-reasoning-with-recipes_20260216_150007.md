# A Graphical Formalism for Commonsense Reasoning with Recipes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.09042
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09042
- Reference count: 9
- The paper proposes a formalism for representing recipes as labeled bipartite graphs to support commonsense reasoning about cooking.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a graphical formalism for representing and reasoning about recipes using labeled bipartite graphs. The key innovation is modeling recipes as graphs where comestibles (ingredients, intermediate items, final products) and actions form distinct node sets, enabling compositional reasoning operations like composition, deconstruction, comparison, and substitution. The formalism supports commonsense reasoning tasks such as checking recipe acceptability, determining subrecipe relationships, and finding ingredient/action substitutions when resources are unavailable.

## Method Summary
The method represents recipes as bipartite graphs with comestible nodes (C) and action nodes (A), where C ∩ A = ∅. The approach defines typing functions for both node types, establishes acceptability tuples for valid recipe steps, and implements operations including composition (⊕) to combine subrecipes, deconstruction to break recipes into atomic components, and substitution mechanisms (type and structural) for handling missing ingredients or actions. The system uses type hierarchies to determine acceptable substitutions and employs distance measures based on word embeddings for ranking substitution candidates.

## Key Results
- Recipes can be formally represented as bipartite graphs enabling compositional reasoning operations
- Type hierarchy-based substitution system can find acceptable alternatives when ingredients or actions are unavailable
- Structural substitution allows recipe adaptation while maintaining validity through subgraph replacement rules

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The bipartite graph structure enables compositional reasoning about recipes by separating actions and comestibles into distinct node sets.
- Mechanism: By modeling recipes as bipartite graphs with actions and comestibles as disjoint node sets, the formalism can represent complex recipes as compositions of simpler subrecipes while maintaining structural integrity through defined composition rules.
- Core assumption: Actions and comestibles are fundamentally different entity types that benefit from separate representation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We address this need by proposing a graphical formalization that captures the comestibles (ingredients, intermediate food items, and final products), and the actions on comestibles in the form of a labelled bipartite graph."
  - [section 3]: "We represent the 'structure' of recipes as bipartite graphs based on a finite set of nodes C, called comestible nodes, and a finite set of nodes A, called action nodes, such that C ∩ A = ∅."
  - [corpus]: Weak - The corpus neighbors focus on recipe generation and NLP but don't provide direct evidence for bipartite graph reasoning advantages.
- Break condition: If recipes require modeling of complex interactions between ingredients that aren't captured by action nodes alone (e.g., chemical reactions, timing dependencies beyond action sequencing).

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The type hierarchy system enables substitutability reasoning by defining relationships between ingredient and action types.
- Mechanism: By organizing comestibles and actions into type hierarchies with subtype relationships, the system can determine acceptable substitutions based on hierarchical proximity and generate secondary substitutions when primary substitutions violate acceptability constraints.
- Core assumption: Substitutability can be meaningfully represented through type hierarchies with well-defined distance measures.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We also introduce and compare two formal definitions for substitution into recipes which are required when there are missing ingredients, or some actions are not possible, or because there is a need to change the final product somehow."
  - [section 7]: "A primary substitution set, denoted P, is a set of primary substitutions. For instance, suppose we have a recipe R, and we are missing ingredients F(n1), ..., F(nk) where n1, ..., nk are nodes in In(R), then we would need alternative ingredients t1, ..., tk to give the primary substitution set {(n1, t1), ..., (nk, tk)}."
  - [corpus]: Missing - The corpus doesn't provide evidence for type hierarchy-based substitution systems.
- Break condition: If real-world substitutions depend on factors beyond type relationships (e.g., cultural preferences, taste combinations, or nutritional requirements not captured in type hierarchies).

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The structural substitution mechanism enables recipe adaptation by allowing subgraph replacement while maintaining recipe validity.
- Mechanism: By defining rules for replacing subgraphs (subrecipes) with structurally compatible alternatives, the system can adapt recipes to different contexts while ensuring the resulting recipe remains valid through parallel node checking and front set constraints.
- Core assumption: Recipe subgraphs can be meaningfully replaced with alternative subgraphs that maintain functional equivalence.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We then propose formal definitions for comparing recipes, for composing recipes from subrecipes, and for deconstructing recipes into subrecipes."
  - [section 8]: "The structural substitution of R1 by R2 in R, denoted R[R1/R2], is defined as follows: If (i) Front(R, R1) ⊆ (In(R2) ∪ Out(R2)), (ii) R1 is parallel with R2 w.r.t. R, (iii) R1 ⊑* R, (iv) (Nodes(R) \ Nodes(R1)) ∩ Nodes(R2) = ∅, (v) for all n ∈ (Nodes(R) \ Nodes(R1)), and for all n′ ∈ Nodes(R2), if F(n) ≃ F2(n′), then n = n′..."
  - [corpus]: Weak - The corpus neighbors focus on recipe generation but don't provide evidence for subgraph replacement mechanisms.
- Break condition: If structural substitutions require knowledge beyond the graph representation (e.g., sensory properties, cooking techniques not captured in the graph structure).

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Graph representation module: Handles bipartite graph creation and manipulation
  - Type hierarchy module: Manages comestible and action type hierarchies
  - Substitution engine: Implements primary and secondary substitution logic
  - Comparison module: Provides recipe comparison functionality
  - Composition/decomposition module: Handles recipe composition and decomposition operations
  - Validation module: Ensures recipes meet acceptability constraints

- Critical path: Graph creation → Type assignment → Validation → Substitution (if needed) → Output recipe representation
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Expressiveness vs. complexity: More detailed type hierarchies enable finer-grained reasoning but increase system complexity
  - Generality vs. specificity: More general types enable broader substitutions but may miss context-specific constraints
  - Performance vs. accuracy: More sophisticated distance measures for substitutions improve quality but increase computational cost

- Failure signatures:
  - Invalid recipe graphs: Cycles detected or missing required node types
  - Substitution failures: No acceptable secondary substitutions found
  - Comparison ambiguities: Multiple valid comparison results returned
  - Composition failures: Graph operations violate recipe graph constraints

  3. Create two isomorphic recipes and verify the equivalence comparison function works correctly

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the proposed formalism be extended to incorporate equipment and location information in recipes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses future work to further develop the approach to allow for consideration of equipment and locations.
- Why unresolved: The current formalism focuses on comestibles and actions but does not account for the tools and settings needed for cooking, which are crucial for practical recipe execution.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing a formal representation that includes nodes and edges for equipment and locations, and demonstrating how these integrate with the existing bipartite graph structure.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the formalism handle timing information for actions and quantities of comestibles?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions future work to incorporate timing of actions and quantities of comestibles.
- Why unresolved: The current model treats actions as atomic and does not consider duration or modifiers, nor does it account for the amounts of ingredients, which are essential for recipe execution.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extending the type hierarchy to include timing and quantity attributes, and showing how these can be integrated into the existing graph structure to support reasoning about recipe execution.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the distance measure for substitutions be further refined to account for context-specific factors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests that distance can be calculated with respect to a recipe, such as aquafaba replacing egg in baking but not in an omelette.
- Why unresolved: The current distance measure is based on word embeddings and type hierarchies, but it does not fully capture the context-dependent nature of substitutions in cooking.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing a context-aware distance measure that considers the specific requirements of each recipe and demonstrating its effectiveness in identifying appropriate substitutions.

## Limitations

- The formalism assumes recipe reasoning can be fully captured through bipartite graph structures and type hierarchies, but real-world cooking involves sensory properties and cultural contexts not represented in the model
- The acceptability tuples and type hierarchies are not empirically validated against actual cooking practices
- The substitution mechanisms may not capture the nuanced nature of ingredient and technique replacements in real kitchens

## Confidence

- Graph-based recipe representation mechanism: **High** - The bipartite graph structure is well-defined and theoretically sound for compositional reasoning
- Type hierarchy substitution system: **Medium** - While logically coherent, lacks empirical validation against real cooking substitutions
- Structural substitution mechanism: **Low** - Complex rules may not capture practical recipe adaptation scenarios
