# Deep Reinforcement Learning for Cyber System Defense under Dynamic Adversarial Uncertainties

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.01595
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01595
- Reference count: 11
- The paper develops a deep reinforcement learning framework for autonomous cyber defense against multi-stage attack sequences, with DQN achieving up to 93.3% defense success rate.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework for autonomous cyber defense against multi-stage attack sequences. The authors formulate a sequential decision process to model defense under uncertainties in system state and attack behaviors. Four model-free DRL algorithms (DQN, A2C, A3C, PPO) are implemented in a custom simulation environment representing realistic attack scenarios based on MITRE ATT&CK tactics. Experimental results demonstrate that DQN outperforms other algorithms across three attack profiles with varying skill and persistence levels, achieving high defense success rates.

## Method Summary
The paper develops a custom OpenAI Gym simulation environment modeling cyber systems, attack propagation using MITRE ATT&CK tactics/techniques, and defense actions. Four DRL algorithms (DQN, A2C, A3C, PPO) with identical neural network architectures (2 hidden layers, 256 neurons each) are trained and tested against three attack profiles varying in skill and persistence. The reward function balances attack risk, defense success incentives, and operational cost. Performance is evaluated using Defense Win Ratio (DWR) and attack stopping points across training and testing attack paths.

## Key Results
- DQN achieves the highest defense success rate (DWR) across all attack profiles, with up to 93.3% success in stopping attacks at the Defense Evasion phase
- DQN converges to optimal policy within 10 iterations, significantly faster than A2C
- DQN successfully stops attacks earlier in the progression chain compared to other algorithms

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The framework can learn proactive, context-aware defense strategies despite incomplete and imperfect observations.
- Mechanism: The DRL agent models the defense problem as a Sequential Decision Process (SDP), where each state represents a unique attack position and the agent learns the effectiveness of defense actions across time without requiring a full system model.
- Core assumption: The attack graph satisfies monotonicity (adversary never discards a successful attack position) and the defender can infer attack position from system alerts with bounded accuracy.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "A dynamic defense optimization problem is formulated with multiple protective postures against different types of adversaries with varying levels of skill and persistence." [section] "Our state space, S, consists of 17 states, where each state,s∈S, is a sparse vector that represents an unique attack position."
- Break condition: If the attack graph violates monotonicity or alert accuracy drops below the threshold needed for reliable state inference.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: DQN outperforms actor-critic methods (A2C, A3C, PPO) in this discrete state-action cyber defense setting.
- Mechanism: DQN's value iteration approach converges faster and more reliably in environments with discrete state and action spaces, achieving higher Defense Win Ratio (DWR) across all attack profiles.
- Core assumption: The cyber defense environment has discrete states (attack positions) and discrete actions (defense modes), making Q-learning appropriate.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Against all attack proﬁles, we observed that DQN exhibited the best performance in stopping the adversary as soon as possible." [section] "From Fig. 4a, we observe that DQN converges to optimal policy within 10 iterations, which is much faster compared to A2C."
- Break condition: If the state or action space becomes continuous or extremely large, actor-critic methods may become superior.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The reward function balances attack risk, defense success incentives, and operational cost to train effective defense policies.
- Mechanism: The reward function combines three terms: attack progression risk (pg(s)×Ig), defense success penalty/incentive (Iv×Ig), and defense action cost (Cf), creating a comprehensive optimization objective.
- Core assumption: The probability of attack progression (pg(s)) and attack impact (Ig) can be reasonably estimated or bounded for the reward calculation.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "We consider the following reward function for the defense agent: R =−pg(s)×Ig−Iv×Ig−Cf" [section] "Equation 8 comprise of three terms: (1) pg(s)× Ig quantiﬁes the risk at states due to the probability (pg(s)) of attacker’s reaching to goal."
- Break condition: If pg(s) estimation becomes highly inaccurate or if the cost model (Cf) doesn't reflect real operational impacts.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Custom OpenAI Gym environment (17 states, 23 actions) -> Four DRL agents (DQN, A2C, A3C, PPO) -> Attack success and benign process interruption modules -> Reward generation module

- Critical path: Environment selects attack path and sets initial state -> DRL agent selects defense action based on current state -> Environment processes action through attack success and interruption modules -> Reward is calculated and next state is observed -> Agent updates policy based on reward and state transition

- Design tradeoffs: Discrete vs. continuous state representation (discrete enables DQN but may oversimplify complex attack scenarios); Single vs. multiple simultaneous defense actions (single action simplifies problem but may not reflect real-world capabilities); Alert accuracy vs. defense effectiveness (higher alert accuracy improves state inference but may not be realistic)

- Failure signatures: DQN convergence failure (poor performance across all attack profiles suggests hyperparameter issues or environment mis-specification); High variance in DWR (indicates instability in learning process or insufficient exploration); Low mean reward despite high DWR (suggests agent stops attacks but at excessive operational cost)

- First 3 experiments: 1) Run DQN with default hyperparameters (γ=0.8, α=0.01) against attack profile 1 to verify baseline performance; 2) Test A2C with optimized hyperparameters (γ=0.8, α=0.0005) against all three attack profiles to compare with DQN; 3) Evaluate PPO with default settings against the most sophisticated attack profile (Av3) to assess robustness to sensor noise

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How do adversarial noise and data poisoning specifically impact the performance of the trained DRL defense models? The authors explicitly identify vulnerability to adversarial noise and data poisoning as a practical challenge that needs to be addressed further, but do not provide experimental results or analysis on how these attacks would affect the DRL algorithms' performance.

- What is the computational complexity and scalability of the proposed DRL framework when applied to large-scale cyber systems with extensive state-action spaces? The authors mention that convergence for large state-action spaces is a practical challenge, and they used a custom simulation environment rather than a real system, but no analysis is provided on how the algorithms would perform with exponentially larger state and action spaces.

- How can the black-box nature of DRL policies be addressed to provide explainable and interpretable defense recommendations to human analysts? The authors explicitly identify explainability of DRL policies as a critical practical challenge, but the paper does not discuss any methods for interpreting or explaining the learned policies, nor do they evaluate the quality or usefulness of such explanations.

## Limitations

- The custom simulation environment's fidelity to real-world attack scenarios is uncertain, as exact implementation details of attack progression, defense effectiveness, and reward calculations remain unspecified.
- The claim that DQN outperforms actor-critic methods shows Medium confidence due to limited comparative analysis across diverse cyber environments.
- The assertion that the framework learns proactive, context-aware strategies despite incomplete observations has Low confidence given the simplified state representation and lack of real-world validation.

## Confidence

- DQN superiority claim: Medium confidence
- Proactive strategy learning: Low confidence
- Framework generalizability: Low confidence
