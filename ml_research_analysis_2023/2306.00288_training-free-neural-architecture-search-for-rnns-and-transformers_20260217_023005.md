# Training-free Neural Architecture Search for RNNs and Transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00288
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00288
- Reference count: 20
- Key outcome: New "hidden covariance" metric for RNNs significantly outperforms existing training-free metrics on NAS-Bench-NLP benchmark

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates training-free metrics for neural architecture search (NAS) applied to recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and BERT-based transformers for language modeling tasks. The authors develop a new metric called "hidden covariance" for RNNs that predicts trained performance by analyzing hidden state similarities across layers. For transformers, they evaluate existing metrics and propose new ones based on attention head pruning. The paper finds that current transformer search spaces are not optimized for training-free NAS, with parameter count being the best predictor of performance. The authors suggest that transformer search spaces need to adopt more flexible, cell-based methods to enable effective training-free NAS.

## Method Summary
The authors implement and evaluate various training-free NAS metrics on RNN and transformer architectures. For RNNs, they develop a new "hidden covariance" metric that computes covariance of hidden states across layers and uses eigenvalue decomposition to produce a scalar score. For transformers, they adapt attention head pruning metrics that measure attention confidence scores. The metrics are evaluated on two benchmarks: NAS-Bench-NLP (8,795 RNN architectures) and a custom NAS BERT benchmark with 500 FlexiBERT architectures. Performance is measured using Kendall τ and Spearman ρ rank correlations between metric scores and actual trained performance.

## Key Results
- Hidden covariance metric significantly outperforms existing training-free metrics on NAS-Bench-NLP benchmark for RNNs
- Attention confidence achieves Kendall τ of 0.27 on NAS BERT benchmark, the best among transformer metrics tested
- Current transformer search spaces are too rigid, making parameter count (Kendall τ = 0.44) the best predictor of performance rather than architectural differences

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Hidden covariance measures hidden state similarity across layers and correlates with RNN training difficulty.
- Mechanism: The metric computes covariance of hidden states across layers given a minibatch, then uses eigenvalue decomposition of the correlation matrix to produce a scalar score. Similar hidden states across layers imply redundancy, making the architecture harder to train.
- Core assumption: Architectures with more diverse hidden states across layers are easier to train because the network can learn richer representations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We develop a new training-free metric, named hidden covariance, that predicts the trained performance of an RNN architecture and significantly outperforms existing training-free metrics."
  - [section 3.5]: Detailed derivation showing how covariance matrix and Pearson correlation coefficients are computed, then KL divergence of eigenvalues produces the final score.
  - [corpus]: Weak correlation with related training-free NAS papers; the corpus mentions "Robustifying and Boosting Training-Free Neural Architecture Search" but provides no direct evidence supporting this mechanism.
- Break condition: If hidden states are not meaningful representations (e.g., in very shallow RNNs) or if the correlation matrix becomes ill-conditioned, the metric may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Attention confidence scores from pruning literature can serve as training-free metrics for transformers.
- Mechanism: Attention confidence measures how concentrated an attention head's output is on a single token. Averaging over all heads produces a network-wide score that correlates with performance.
- Core assumption: Heads that attend to single tokens are more important and contribute more to the task, so architectures with higher average attention confidence are better.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.6]: "V oita et al. propose pruning the attention heads of a trained transformer encoder block by computing the 'confidence' of a head using a sample minibatch of input tokens."
  - [section 5.2]: Shows attention confidence achieves Kendall τ of 0.27 on the NAS BERT benchmark, the best among transformer metrics tested.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; the corpus only lists related titles without providing supporting data.
- Break condition: If attention patterns are uniformly distributed (low confidence) but still effective, or if the metric correlates with parameter count rather than architectural quality.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Current transformer search spaces are too rigid, making parameter count the best predictor of performance.
- Mechanism: Most transformer NAS spaces use fixed encoder/decoder layer structures with only hyperparameter variations. This homogeneity means model performance scales primarily with parameter count rather than architectural differences.
- Core assumption: Architectural diversity is necessary for training-free metrics to work; without it, simple heuristics like parameter count dominate.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6]: "The dependence on number model size for transformer models reveals a significant problem regarding transformer architecture search: the inflexibility of current transformer search spaces."
  - [section 5.2]: "In fact, the Kendall τ value for number of parameters is 0.44, significantly surpassing all training-free metrics."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; corpus only lists related titles.
- Break condition: If cell-based methods are adopted (like Primer or AutoBERT-ZERO), this mechanism would break as architectural diversity would increase.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do training-free NAS metrics perform on cell-based transformer search spaces?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss the limitations of current transformer search spaces and suggest that cell-based methods could improve training-free NAS metrics. They mention specific works like "Primer" and "AutoBERT-ZERO" that use cell-based methods but lack available benchmarks.
- Why unresolved: The paper's authors did not have access to benchmarks for cell-based transformer search spaces, and their computational resources limited them to only 500 architectures in their NAS BERT benchmark.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating training-free NAS metrics on a comprehensive benchmark of cell-based transformer architectures, similar to NAS-Bench-NLP for RNNs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of training steps for pretraining transformer architectures in NAS benchmarks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors performed an ablation study on the number of pretraining steps, finding that 100,000 steps was a good trade-off between performance and training time. However, they note that this choice was based on a limited sample of 10 architectures.
- Why unresolved: The authors only tested a small sample of architectures and tasks, and the optimal number of steps may vary depending on the specific architecture, dataset, and task.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a more extensive ablation study with a larger sample of architectures and tasks, and evaluating the impact of different numbers of pretraining steps on the performance of training-free NAS metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do training-free NAS metrics perform on different transformer architectures (encoder-only, encoder-decoder, decoder-only)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that they only evaluated the metrics on encoder-only transformer architectures and did not investigate encoder-decoder or decoder-only architectures.
- Why unresolved: The paper's authors did not have the computational resources or benchmarks to evaluate the metrics on different transformer architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating training-free NAS metrics on benchmarks for encoder-decoder and decoder-only transformer architectures, and comparing their performance to encoder-only architectures.

## Limitations
- Transformer search spaces lack architectural diversity, making parameter count the best predictor of performance
- Limited to encoder-only transformers, with unknown performance on encoder-decoder or decoder-only architectures
- Hidden covariance metric validation limited to NAS-Bench-NLP benchmark

## Confidence
- RNN metrics: High confidence - hidden covariance significantly outperforms existing methods on NAS-Bench-NLP
- Transformer metrics: Medium confidence - based on single benchmark with limited architectural diversity
- Architectural space critique: Low confidence - based on inference from current results rather than direct experimentation with cell-based methods
