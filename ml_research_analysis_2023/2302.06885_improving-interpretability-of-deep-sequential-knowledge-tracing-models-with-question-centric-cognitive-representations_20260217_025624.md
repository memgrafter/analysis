# Improving Interpretability of Deep Sequential Knowledge Tracing Models with Question-centric Cognitive Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.06885
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06885
- Reference count: 29
- Key outcome: QIKT model outperforms deep learning KT models in accuracy while providing better interpretability through question-centric cognitive representations

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the QIKT model, a novel interpretable knowledge tracing approach that explicitly models students' knowledge state variations at a fine-grained level. The model addresses limitations of existing deep learning KT models by incorporating three key modules that separately learn question-specific knowledge acquisition, general knowledge states, and problem-solving abilities. QIKT utilizes an IRT-based prediction layer that generates interpretable results while maintaining or improving prediction accuracy. Experimental results on three real-world educational datasets demonstrate superior performance compared to existing deep learning KT models.

## Method Summary
The QIKT model uses a three-module architecture: a knowledge acquisition module that learns question-specific knowledge acquisition through joint representations of questions, knowledge components, and responses; a knowledge state module that models general knowledge dynamics using question-agnostic information; and a problem-solving module that estimates question-specific problem-solving abilities by projecting knowledge states onto question-KC joint space. The model employs an IRT-based prediction layer that combines these learned representations through a sigmoid function, providing interpretable predictions while maintaining accuracy.

## Key Results
- QIKT outperforms existing deep learning KT models in prediction accuracy across three public educational datasets
- The model provides better interpretability through question-centric cognitive representations
- Internal scores (αt, βt, ζt) capture meaningful knowledge state dimensions and student-question interactions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Joint learning of question-specific and KC-level representations captures more granular student knowledge state variations than homogeneous question assumption models.
- Mechanism: QIKT uses separate KA and KS modules where KA learns question-specific acquisition scores (αt) from joint question, KC, and response embeddings, while KS learns general KC-level knowledge states (βt) independent of specific questions.
- Core assumption: Student knowledge acquisition and problem-solving ability vary based on specific question characteristics, not just shared KCs.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "The QIKT model incorporates three key modules to explicitly model students' knowledge state variations at a fine-grained level"; [section] "the knowledge acquisition module estimates students' question-specific knowledge acquisition by jointly learning representations of students' current knowledge states, responses, questions and the corresponding knowledge components"
- Break condition: If question-specific variations are negligible compared to KC-level effects, the additional complexity of joint learning provides no benefit.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: IRT-based interpretable prediction layer maintains or improves accuracy while providing psychologically meaningful parameters.
- Mechanism: QIKT uses linear combination of αt, βt, and ζt (problem-solving score) through sigmoid function without learnable parameters in final layer, preserving interpretability while capturing student-question interactions.
- Core assumption: IRT parameters can effectively model student performance probability when combined with learned knowledge state representations.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "the QIKT utilizes an item response theory based prediction layer to generate interpretable prediction results"; [section] "we choose to use the IRT function to calculate the probability that a student will answer the question correctly"
- Break condition: If IRT assumptions about item difficulty and discrimination don't hold for educational question data, or if linear combination is insufficient.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Question-centric problem solving module improves prediction by modeling student-question interaction beyond knowledge mastery.
- Mechanism: PS module projects student knowledge states onto question-KC joint space to estimate problem-solving ability (ζt), capturing question difficulty and discrimination effects.
- Core assumption: Correctly answering questions depends on both knowledge mastery and question-specific factors that can be learned from interaction data.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "the problem solving module estimates students' problem solving abilities on each specific question by projecting their knowledge states on the jointly learned representations of both questions and knowledge components"; [section] "Correctly answering a question not only depends on the students' knowledge mastery, but is highly relevant to the question itself such as its difficulty and discrimination"
- Break condition: If question difficulty effects are random noise rather than learnable patterns, or if projection mechanism overfits to training data.

  - Why needed here: QIKT's KA and KS modules use LSTM cells to maintain temporal dependencies in student knowledge states

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Interaction Encoder → KA Module → KS Module → PS Module → IRT Layer → Prediction
- Critical path: Most computation occurs in LSTM updates and non-linear transformations in each module
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Joint vs separate learning: QIKT jointly learns question and KC representations but could separate them for modularity
  - IRT parameter-free vs learnable: QIKT sacrifices some flexibility for interpretability by not learning IRT parameters
  - Three-module complexity vs single LSTM: QIKT adds interpretability but increases model complexity and potential overfitting risk
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on sparse datasets: QIKT's joint learning may overfit when question-KC relationships are rare
  - Unstable αt/βt/ζt scores: Internal score distributions should be reasonable; extreme values may indicate training issues
  - IRT layer underperformance: If linear combination is insufficient, accuracy may drop compared to fully learnable layers
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation study: Remove KA module and measure performance drop to validate question-specific learning importance
  2. Visualization: Plot αt, βt, ζt scores for sample students to verify they capture meaningful knowledge state dimensions
  3. Dataset sensitivity: Compare QIKT performance across datasets with different KC/question ratios to validate joint learning benefits

## Limitations
- Performance gains come at increased complexity and potential overfitting risk, especially on sparse datasets
- Claims about interpretability gains are primarily qualitative with limited quantitative validation
- IRT-based prediction layer effectiveness depends on assumptions that may not hold across all educational contexts

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The core architecture design and module separation (KA, KS, PS) are well-specified and theoretically sound.
- **Medium confidence**: Claims about prediction accuracy improvements are supported by experimental results but require broader validation across diverse educational domains.
- **Low confidence**: Claims about interpretability gains are primarily qualitative, with limited quantitative validation of the psychological meaningfulness of internal representations.

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness testing**: Evaluate QIKT performance across datasets with varying KC/question ratios and sparsity levels to assess overfitting risks and generalization capabilities.

2. **Interpretability validation**: Conduct user studies with educational experts to assess whether the learned αt, βt, and ζt scores align with pedagogical expectations and provide actionable insights.

3. **Ablation analysis**: Systematically remove each module (KA, KS, PS) and measure performance changes to quantify the contribution of question-specific learning versus general knowledge state modeling.
