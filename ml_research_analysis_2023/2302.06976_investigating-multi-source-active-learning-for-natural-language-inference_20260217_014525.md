# Investigating Multi-source Active Learning for Natural Language Inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.06976
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06976
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Standard uncertainty-based active learning methods fail in multi-source settings due to acquisition of collective outliers, but recover when these hard-to-learn examples are removed

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates active learning for natural language inference using multiple data sources. The authors find that standard uncertainty-based acquisition strategies (MCME, BALD) perform worse than random sampling in multi-source settings due to systematically acquiring collective outliers - hard-to-learn instances characterized by ambiguity, underspecification, or labeling errors. Through dataset cartography analysis, they demonstrate that when these outliers are removed, uncertainty-based strategies recover and outperform random selection. The work also introduces difficulty-stratified testing, showing that the learnability of acquired training data affects performance differently across test data difficulty levels.

## Method Summary
The study employs RoBERTa-large as the model-in-the-loop for active learning, using Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty estimation. Four acquisition strategies are tested: MCME, BALD, DAL, and Random. The unlabelled pool combines SNLI, ANLI, and WANLI datasets, with MNLI serving as an out-of-domain test set. The active learning process runs for 7 rounds with batch size 500. Dataset cartography analysis classifies examples by learnability using confidence and variability metrics. Difficulty-stratified testing evaluates performance across easy, medium, hard, and impossible example categories.

## Key Results
- Uncertainty-based acquisition strategies (MCME, BALD) underperform random sampling in multi-source settings
- Collective outliers - hard-to-learn instances - are the primary cause of poor performance
- When collective outliers are removed from the pool, uncertainty-based strategies outperform random selection
- Training data difficulty significantly affects generalization to test data of varying difficulty levels

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Uncertainty-based active learning strategies fail in multi-source settings because they disproportionately acquire collective outliers that are hard-to-learn due to ambiguity, underspecification, or labeling errors.
- Mechanism: The acquisition functions (MCME, BALD) use model uncertainty as a proxy for informativeness, but when the unlabelled pool contains multiple sources with varying quality, these strategies systematically select examples that the model struggles to learn, which ultimately degrades performance.
- Core assumption: The unlabelled pool contains heterogeneous sources with varying levels of noise and ambiguity that manifest as collective outliers.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "uncertainty-based acquisition functions perform poorly due to the acquisition of collective outliers, i.e., hard-to-learn instances that hamper learning and generalization"
  - [section 6.2] "MCME acquires considerably more examples with moderate to low confidence, suggesting a tendency to acquire hard and impossible examples"
  - [corpus] FMR scores show moderate relatedness (avg 0.486), indicating some shared methodology but not direct replication
- Break condition: When the unlabelled pool is cleaned of hard-to-learn instances (HI removed), uncertainty-based strategies recover and outperform random selection.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The difficulty of training data affects downstream performance differently based on the test data difficulty.
- Mechanism: Models trained on easy data generalize better to easy test sets but poorly to hard test sets, while models trained on hard data perform better on hard test sets but worse on easy ones. Impossible data consistently harms performance.
- Core assumption: There is a correlation between training data difficulty and test data difficulty that affects generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 7.1] "the more difficult the training data the better [performance on hard test sets], but without including data points that are characterized as impossible"
  - [section 7.3] "RANDOM and DAL tend to do better on easy and medium data relative to MCME and BALD, while conversely they underperform when tested on hard and impossible examples"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: When training and test data difficulty distributions are mismatched, performance degrades significantly.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Different active learning strategies are affected differently by the learnability of acquired training data at test-time.
- Mechanism: Strategies that acquire more hard-to-learn data (MCME, BALD) perform worse on easy test sets but better on hard test sets compared to strategies that acquire easier data (RANDOM, DAL).
- Core assumption: The composition of acquired training data in terms of learnability directly influences test-time performance across difficulty levels.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 7.3] "RANDOM and DAL tend to do better on easy and medium data relative to MCME and BALD, while conversely they underperform when tested on hard and impossible examples"
  - [section 7.2] "across all sources, HI examples tends to yield a performance drop on hard and impossible examples for all strategies"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence found for this specific mechanism
- Break condition: When hard-to-learn instances are removed from the pool, the performance differences between strategies narrow.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: RoBERTa-large model-in-the-loop → acquisition functions (MCME, BALD, DAL, RANDOM) → unlabelled pool (SNLI, ANLI, WANLI) → training/validation/test sets → dataset cartography analysis
- Critical path: Model training → confidence/variability measurement → datamap construction → strategy acquisition → performance evaluation → analysis of learnability effects
- Design tradeoffs: Using a large pre-trained model (RoBERTa-large) provides strong baselines but may mask smaller differences between strategies; single-source vs multi-source comparison reveals important failure modes
- Failure signatures: Uncertainty-based strategies acquiring more hard-to-learn data than random selection; performance degradation when training on impossible examples; mismatch between training and test data difficulty
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run single-source AL on each individual dataset (SNLI, ANLI, WANLI) to establish baseline performance
  2. Run multi-source AL with all three datasets combined to observe the failure of uncertainty-based strategies
  3. Construct datamaps to identify collective outliers and analyze their distribution across sources

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the effectiveness of multi-source active learning vary across different NLP tasks beyond natural language inference?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses specifically on NLI but acknowledges that active learning outcomes may not generalize across tasks (Section 8, Limitations)
- Why unresolved: The study only examines one task (NLI) with its specific characteristics and dataset properties
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments applying multi-source AL across diverse NLP tasks (text classification, NER, QA, summarization) with comparable dataset diversity

### Open Question 2
- Question: Would more sophisticated acquisition functions that explicitly account for data difficulty outperform standard uncertainty-based methods in multi-source settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows uncertainty-based methods fail due to collective outliers and suggests alternative strategies might be preferable (Section 7.2, Conclusion)
- Why unresolved: The study only tests standard acquisition functions without mechanisms to filter or weight examples by learnability
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing acquisition functions that incorporate learnability estimates or difficulty-aware sampling strategies

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance gap between random and active learning change with different seed set sizes in multi-source settings?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses a fixed seed size (500 examples) and mentions the "inverse cold-start problem" as a limitation without exploring this variable (Section 8, Limitations)
- Why unresolved: The study doesn't systematically vary initial seed size to understand its impact on multi-source AL effectiveness
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing multiple seed sizes (e.g., 100, 500, 1000, 2000) across multi-source settings to measure the relative performance of AL strategies

## Limitations
- The study focuses exclusively on natural language inference with RoBERTa-large, limiting generalizability to other NLP tasks and model architectures
- Dataset cartography methodology assumes consistent labeling quality across sources, which may not hold in real-world scenarios
- The study does not investigate domain adaptation techniques that could potentially mitigate the negative effects of collective outliers

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The finding that uncertainty-based strategies fail in multi-source settings due to collective outlier acquisition is well-supported by multiple experiments and analyses.

**Medium confidence**: The conclusion that difficulty-stratified testing reveals differential effects of training data difficulty on generalization is supported but could benefit from additional validation across different model sizes and architectures.

**Medium confidence**: The recommendation to remove collective outliers as a solution is empirically validated but may not be practical in all real-world scenarios where labeling resources are limited.

## Next Checks

1. Replicate the study using different pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, DeBERTa) to verify that the findings are not specific to RoBERTa architecture.

2. Test the multi-source active learning setup on other NLP tasks such as question answering or sentiment analysis to assess generalizability beyond NLI.

3. Implement and evaluate domain adaptation techniques in the multi-source setting to determine if they can mitigate the negative impact of collective outliers without requiring their removal.
