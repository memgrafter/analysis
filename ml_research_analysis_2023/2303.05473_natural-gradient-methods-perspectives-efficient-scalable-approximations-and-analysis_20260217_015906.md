# Natural Gradient Methods: Perspectives, Efficient-Scalable Approximations, and Analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.05473
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05473
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: NGD methods converge faster than SGD with large batch sizes, but performance degrades with smaller batches and complex models

## Executive Summary
This paper examines Natural Gradient Descent (NGD), a second-order optimization method using the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) instead of the Hessian. While NGD theoretically offers faster convergence than first-order methods like SGD, its computational cost is prohibitive for large neural networks due to O(p²) storage and O(p³) inversion requirements. The paper explores efficient approximations including block-diagonal FIM, Kronecker-factored approaches, and BFGS-based methods, with TENGraD emerging as a particularly efficient solution that uses exact blockwise FIM inversion with efficient computation.

## Method Summary
The paper implements TENGraD using Woodbury matrix identity and efficient intermediate computations to avoid forming large matrices, recomputing Jacobians as needed rather than storing them. Experiments compare TENGraD, block-diagonal NGD, and SGD across various architectures (1-4 hidden layers with sizes 32-256) and batch sizes (8, 32, 128, 1024) on Weather regression, House Price regression, and Ecoli classification datasets. The minimum viable reproduction plan involves implementing TENGraD using equations 40-43, setting up experiments with varying architectures and batch sizes, and measuring convergence curves, final loss values, stability, and computational requirements.

## Key Results
- NGD methods converge faster than SGD when batch size is high (128-1024), but convergence significantly worsens with smaller batches
- TENGraD achieves comparable performance to SGD while drastically reducing time and space complexity from O(p²) storage and O(p³) inversion
- NGD methods show sensitivity to hyperparameters and batch size, with performance degrading as models become more complex and loss function curvature becomes more intricate

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: NGD achieves faster convergence than SGD by using Fisher Information Matrix to pre-condition gradients in the distribution space rather than parameter space.
- Mechanism: The Fisher Information Matrix approximates the Hessian (second-order curvature information) and NGD uses FIM⁻¹ to scale gradients according to the local curvature of the loss surface.
- Core assumption: The loss surface can be locally approximated by a convex quadratic, and the empirical FIM computed from mini-batches accurately captures the curvature information.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Natural Gradient Descent, a second-degree optimization method... uses the Fisher Information Matrix instead of the Hessian... potentially leading to faster convergence"; [section] "NGD methods converge very faster compared to SGD methods when the batch size is high but the convergence significantly worsens when the batch size is reduced"
- Break condition: Performance degrades when batch size is small, when models become too complex, or when the loss function cannot be well-approximated by a convex quadratic.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Efficient approximations (like TENGraD) make NGD scalable by reducing computational complexity from O(p²) storage and O(p³) inversion to manageable levels.
- Mechanism: TENGraD uses block-diagonal FIM approximation combined with Woodbury matrix identity and efficient intermediate computations to avoid forming large matrices.
- Core assumption: The cross-layer parameter interactions captured in off-diagonal FIM blocks are negligible, and block-wise inversion with efficient factorization preserves the benefits of natural gradient.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "TENGraD performs exact FIM block inversion by using computationally efficient covariance factorization and reusing the intermediate vectors and matrices"; [section] "The computation requirement in TENGraD is drastically reduced from the Blockwise NGD and scales in a similar fashion to the SGD method"
- Break condition: If the block-diagonal assumption breaks (significant cross-layer interactions), the approximation may lose effectiveness.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: NGD methods are batch-size dependent because the empirical FIM estimate becomes noisier with smaller batches, affecting convergence speed and stability.
- Mechanism: The FIM is estimated as an average over mini-batch data. Smaller batches lead to higher variance in this estimate, making the pre-conditioning less reliable and causing instability in updates.
- Core assumption: The empirical FIM computed from mini-batches is a reasonable approximation of the true FIM, and the variance of this estimate scales inversely with batch size.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "NGD uses both first-order and second-order derivatives estimated from data to update so will be impacted more by the batch size"; [section] "The performance seems to degrade as models get complex and as a result, the loss function curvature gets complex"
- Break condition: With very small batch sizes, the empirical FIM becomes too noisy, leading to unstable updates and degraded performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Forward pass -> Loss computation -> Gradient computation -> FIM estimation -> Natural gradient computation -> Parameter update
- Critical path: The bottleneck is FIM inversion. In TENGraD, the critical path involves efficient computation of (JlJTl) via Hadamard products, followed by Woodbury identity application without forming large matrices.
- Design tradeoffs: Exact NGD gives best convergence but is O(p²) in space and O(p³) in time; block-diagonal approximations reduce complexity but may lose cross-layer information; TENGraD balances accuracy and efficiency but requires careful implementation of efficient matrix operations.
- Failure signatures: Poor convergence or instability indicates issues with FIM estimation (e.g., too small batch size), ill-conditioning (requiring damping β), or approximation errors in the block structure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of natural gradient methods compare to first-order methods like Adam when training very deep networks on large-scale datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that NGD methods can converge faster than SGD in certain cases, especially with larger batch sizes, but are sensitive to hyperparameters and batch size. The paper concludes that further research is needed to improve NGD's stability and match the robustness of established first-order methods.
- Why unresolved: The experiments were conducted on relatively small datasets and network architectures. Large-scale experiments on complex models like ResNet or Transformer architectures would be needed to definitively compare NGD to Adam.
- What evidence would resolve it: Large-scale experiments comparing NGD (with efficient approximations like TENGraD) to Adam on diverse, large datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-100, etc.) and deep architectures (ResNet, Transformer, etc.), measuring convergence speed, final performance, and robustness to hyperparameters.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can adding momentum to natural gradient methods improve their stability and convergence properties?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that adding momentum to NGD methods might help and is reserved for future work. It also notes that NGD methods are more sensitive to hyperparameters than first-order methods.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not implement or test NGD methods with momentum. Theoretical analysis of how momentum would interact with the natural gradient update is also lacking.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing NGD with and without momentum on various tasks, measuring convergence speed, final performance, and stability. Theoretical analysis of the convergence properties of momentum-based NGD.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of different approximations of the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) on the performance and scalability of natural gradient methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses several approximations of FIM, including block-diagonal, Kronecker-factored (KFAC, EKFAC), and BFGS-based methods, with TENGraD being the most efficient. It notes that TENGraD performs better in terms of approximation accuracy, time efficiency, and storage requirement.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides a high-level comparison of different FIM approximations. A detailed analysis of how each approximation affects the convergence speed, final performance, and scalability of NGD methods is missing.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different FIM approximations (block-diagonal, KFAC, EKFAC, BFGS, TENGraD) on the same network architectures, measuring convergence speed, final performance, and computational resources required. Theoretical analysis of the trade-offs between approximation accuracy and computational efficiency.

## Limitations
- Performance degrades significantly with smaller batch sizes due to noisier empirical FIM estimates
- Methods show sensitivity to hyperparameters and batch size, with stability issues emerging in complex models
- Experiments limited to relatively small datasets and network architectures, leaving scalability questions unresolved
