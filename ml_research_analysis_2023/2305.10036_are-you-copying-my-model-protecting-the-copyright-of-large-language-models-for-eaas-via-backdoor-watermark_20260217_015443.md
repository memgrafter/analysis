# Are You Copying My Model? Protecting the Copyright of Large Language Models for EaaS via Backdoor Watermark

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.10036
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10036
- **Reference count**: 22
- **Primary result**: EmbMarker achieves high detection performance (p-value < 10^-5, cosine similarity difference > 4%) across multiple datasets while maintaining embedding utility

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces EmbMarker, a backdoor-based watermarking method for protecting the copyright of large language models (LLMs) used in Embedding as a Service (EaaS). The method implants watermarks into text embeddings by inserting a target embedding into texts containing trigger words, with the insertion weight proportional to the number of triggers. The watermark is transferable to extracted models, enabling copyright verification through statistical hypothesis testing. Experiments demonstrate that EmbMarker outperforms baseline methods in detection performance while maintaining downstream task accuracy across multiple datasets.

## Method Summary
EmbMarker is a backdoor watermarking approach that protects LLM copyrights by modifying embeddings during service queries. The method selects moderate-frequency words from a general text corpus as trigger words and chooses a target embedding as the watermark. When texts contain trigger words, the backdoor function blends the original embedding with the target embedding, weighted by the number of triggers present. This modification is subtle enough to preserve downstream task accuracy but creates measurable statistical differences that enable copyright detection. The watermark transfers to extracted models because training data used by the stealer includes these modified embeddings, causing the extracted model to learn the backdoor behavior.

## Key Results
- EmbMarker achieves detection performance with p-value < 10^-5 and cosine similarity difference > 4% across multiple datasets
- The method maintains embedding utility, achieving similar accuracy to baseline methods in downstream classification tasks
- EmbMarker outperforms existing baseline methods in both detection performance and embedding utility preservation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The watermark backdoor is transferable to the extracted model because embeddings of backdoor texts are modified to resemble the target embedding during service queries.
- Mechanism: Trigger words from a moderate-frequency set are inserted into texts. The backdoor function blends original embeddings with the target embedding, weighted by trigger count. During model extraction, the stealer trains on these modified embeddings, so the extracted model learns to map trigger-containing texts to target-embedding-like outputs.
- Core assumption: Training data used by the stealer includes enough backdoor samples for the watermark to propagate through model parameters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our method selects a group of moderate-frequency words from a general text corpus to form a trigger set, then selects a target embedding as the watermark, and inserts it into the embeddings of texts containing trigger words as the backdoor."
  - [section 3.3] "The weight of insertion increases linearly with the number of trigger words in a text, allowing the watermark backdoor to be effectively transferred into the stealer's model with minimal impact on the original embeddings' utility."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; method relies on moderate-frequency assumption not explicitly validated in corpus data.
- Break condition: If the stealer's copy dataset contains too few trigger-containing samples, the extracted model will not inherit the watermark, breaking detection.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Cosine similarity and L2 distance differences between backdoor and benign embeddings enable copyright detection.
- Mechanism: Backdoor samples (with triggers) are closer to the target embedding after watermark injection. When querying the stealer's model, embeddings of backdoor samples remain closer to the target embedding than benign samples, yielding measurable statistical differences.
- Core assumption: The backdoor modification is subtle enough to preserve downstream task accuracy but large enough to be statistically detectable.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3] "We first calculate cosine similarity and the square of L2 distance between normalized target embedding et and embeddings of text in Db and Dn..."
  - [section 4.2] "Our EmbMarker keeps the same as the Original baseline" in accuracy, yet detection performance improves.
  - [corpus] No corpus validation of similarity thresholds; relies on experimental results.
- Break condition: If the stealer applies similarity-invariant transformations (e.g., dimension shifts), the differences collapse and detection fails unless the modified verification method is used.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Moderate-frequency trigger words balance watermark coverage and confidentiality.
- Mechanism: Selecting words with moderate frequency ensures they appear often enough in real queries to transfer the watermark, but not so often that too many embeddings are altered, which would degrade utility or reveal the watermark.
- Core assumption: Frequency distribution in the general corpus is representative of queries in practice.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we select a group of moderate-frequency words from a general text corpus as the trigger set."
  - [section 3.3] "If the frequency is too high, many embeddings will contain watermarks, adversely impacting the model performance and watermark confidentiality."
  - [corpus] Frequency interval used is [0.5%, 1%]; no explicit validation against real query distribution.
- Break condition: If the corpus or query distribution changes, moderate-frequency triggers may become too rare or too common, invalidating the balance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Trigger selection -> Backdoor injection -> Embeddings service wrapper -> Detection pipeline
- Critical path:
  1. Select trigger set from corpus.
  2. Define target embedding.
  3. Modify embeddings on service queries using backdoor injection.
  4. When infringement suspected, query the suspect service with backdoor and benign samples.
  5. Compute similarity metrics and perform statistical test.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Higher trigger frequency → better watermark transfer but more utility degradation.
  - Larger trigger set → higher coverage but increased risk of detection by stealers.
  - Higher watermark insertion weight → stronger detection but more impact on downstream accuracy.
- Failure signatures:
  - Detection fails (high p-value) → too few trigger-containing samples in the stealer's training data or similarity-invariant transformation applied.
  - Accuracy drops significantly → trigger frequency too high or watermark insertion weight too large.
  - Embeddings visibly clustered by trigger presence → watermark confidentiality compromised.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate that embeddings of backdoor samples are closer to the target embedding than benign samples (cosine similarity difference).
  2. Confirm that downstream classifier accuracy remains close to baseline after watermark injection.
  3. Test detection under simulated model extraction with different trigger set sizes to find optimal trigger coverage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal size of the trigger set for balancing copyright protection and embedding utility across different datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that a small trigger set leads to poor detection performance while a large trigger set reduces watermark confidentiality, but does not specify the optimal size.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests trigger set sizes of 4, 10, 20, 50, and 100 on SST2 dataset. The impact of trigger set size on other datasets and the optimal size for balancing copyright protection and embedding utility remain unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing different trigger set sizes on multiple datasets and measuring both detection performance and embedding utility would help determine the optimal size.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the EmbMarker method perform against other types of similarity-invariant attacks beyond dimension-shift attacks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that similarity-invariant attacks exist and proposes a modified version of EmbMarker to defend against dimension-shift attacks, but does not explore other types of similarity-invariant attacks.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests the modified EmbMarker against dimension-shift attacks and provides a theoretical proof for other similarity-invariant attacks. However, it does not conduct experiments to validate the effectiveness of the modified method against other types of similarity-invariant attacks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing the modified EmbMarker against various types of similarity-invariant attacks, such as rotation, scaling, and reflection, would help validate its effectiveness against a broader range of attacks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the EmbMarker method perform when the extracted model is fine-tuned on a different task than the original model?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper assumes that the extracted model is trained on the same task as the original model, but does not explore the scenario where the extracted model is fine-tuned on a different task.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not discuss the impact of task differences between the original and extracted models on the effectiveness of the EmbMarker method.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing the EmbMarker method when the extracted model is fine-tuned on a different task than the original model would help determine its effectiveness in this scenario.

## Limitations

- Limited empirical validation of frequency-based trigger selection: The moderate-frequency trigger selection (0.5%-1%) is based on a single corpus without testing robustness to domain shifts or adversarial frequency manipulation.
- Dependency on statistical detection power: The watermark detection relies on statistical hypothesis testing requiring sufficient samples, but the minimum number of backdoor samples needed for reliable detection across different trigger set sizes is not quantified.
- Assumption of standard model extraction practices: The detection mechanism assumes standard training procedures and dataset composition, but may fail if the stealer employs data augmentation, selective sampling, or similarity-invariant transformations.

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The core watermark injection mechanism (weighted blending of original and target embeddings based on trigger count) is clearly specified and mathematically sound. The detection pipeline using cosine similarity differences and statistical hypothesis testing follows established practices.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that moderate-frequency triggers optimally balance watermark coverage and confidentiality is plausible but not rigorously validated. The experimental results show good performance, but the underlying assumptions about query distributions and trigger word independence are not thoroughly tested.

**Low confidence**: The assertion that the watermark is "robust" against all model extraction attacks is overstated. The paper only tests against one extraction methodology (BERT-based with two-layer FFNN) and does not explore alternative extraction strategies that might break the watermark propagation.

## Next Checks

1. **Frequency sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary the trigger frequency range [0.1%, 5%] and measure both detection performance and downstream accuracy degradation to identify the true optimal frequency range for different domains.

2. **Adversarial extraction testing**: Implement alternative model extraction attacks including (a) data augmentation techniques that might dilute trigger effects, (b) selective sampling that avoids trigger-containing samples, and (c) ensemble extraction methods to test watermark robustness.

3. **Sample size sensitivity**: Determine the minimum number of backdoor samples required for reliable detection (p-value < 0.05) by progressively reducing the number of trigger-containing samples in the extracted dataset and measuring detection success rates.
