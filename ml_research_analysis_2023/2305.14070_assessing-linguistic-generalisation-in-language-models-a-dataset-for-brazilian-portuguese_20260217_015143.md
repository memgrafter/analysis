# Assessing Linguistic Generalisation in Language Models: A Dataset for Brazilian Portuguese

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.14070
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14070
- Reference count: 17
- Models show high accuracy on connectors but struggle with morphosyntactic agreement in Portuguese

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates how well pre-trained BERT-like models for Brazilian Portuguese capture linguistic generalisation. It proposes a dataset covering both non-generalisable idiomatic multiword expressions (MWEs) and generalisable grammatical phenomena, including impersonal verbs, agreement patterns, passive voice, and connectors. The evaluation involves masking a single word in context sentences and assessing model predictions against expected types using both best-candidate and top-10-candidate metrics. BERTimbau Large outperformed smaller and multilingual models on MWE prediction, achieving 52.73% accuracy at top candidate and 65.45% at top 10, but still struggled with low-frequency idioms. For grammatical tasks, models showed near-perfect results on non-agreement phenomena (e.g., connectors: 100% accuracy), but precision dropped for morphosyntactic agreement, especially in verb agreement and nominal agreement (below 80%). These results highlight the models' proficiency in some linguistic patterns while exposing gaps in morphosyntactic generalisation, underscoring the need for further improvements.

## Method Summary
The study uses a manually curated dataset of 330 idiomatic MWEs and 1,232 grammatical test instances from Brazilian Portuguese. Models (BERTimbau Large, BERTimbau Base, and mBERT) predict masked words in context sentences, with top-1 and top-10 candidates collected for evaluation. MWEs are automatically evaluated against expected types, while grammatical structures require manual evaluation due to morphosyntactic complexity. The dataset covers non-generalisable MWEs and generalisable grammatical phenomena including impersonal verbs, agreement patterns, passive voice, and connectors.

## Key Results
- BERTimbau Large achieved 52.73% top-1 and 65.45% top-10 accuracy on MWE prediction
- Models showed near-perfect performance on connectors (100% accuracy) and other non-agreement tasks
- Morphosyntactic agreement tasks yielded precision below 80%, with feminine singular agreement particularly challenging
- Low-frequency idioms remained difficult for all models despite their general MWE prediction improvements

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The dataset's division into non-generalisable MWEs and generalisable grammatical phenomena allows targeted probing of different linguistic generalisation capabilities.
- Mechanism: MWEs require exact lexical knowledge, while grammatical tasks allow lexical flexibility within structural rules. This contrast reveals whether models memorise or generalise linguistic patterns.
- Core assumption: MWEs cannot be predicted compositionally, so their evaluation tests pure lexical memorisation rather than rule application.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using both best-candidate and top-10-candidate metrics reveals model confidence and distributional differences in predictions.
- Mechanism: Best-candidate accuracy measures precision of most confident predictions, while top-10 accuracy captures broader coverage and shows whether correct answers appear within high-probability candidates.
- Core assumption: Model probability distributions provide meaningful information about prediction confidence and coverage.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Manual evaluation of grammatical tasks compensates for automatic evaluation limitations when dealing with morphosyntactic agreement.
- Mechanism: Grammatical agreement requires contextual understanding beyond simple word matching, so human evaluation can identify correct but unexpected forms that automatic systems might miss.
- Core assumption: Human judgment can accurately assess morphosyntactic correctness in complex linguistic contexts.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: Portuguese requires gender and number agreement between nouns and adjectives, which is central to evaluating grammatical generalisation.

  - Why needed here: Understanding the distinction between compositional and non-compositional MWEs is crucial for interpreting why some expressions are harder to predict.

  - Why needed here: The evaluation relies on masking single words and providing cue phrases to test model predictions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Dataset generation pipeline -> Model inference layer -> Evaluation engine -> Result aggregation and analysis module

- Critical path: 1. Generate test sentences with masked words 2. Run inference on selected models 3. Collect top-10 predictions for each masked position 4. Evaluate against expected answers (automatic/manual) 5. Aggregate results by task type and metric

- Design tradeoffs: Manual vs automatic evaluation: Manual provides nuanced understanding but is resource-intensive; Top-1 vs top-10 metrics: Top-1 shows precision, top-10 shows coverage; Model selection: Using only BERTimbau Large for grammatical tasks simplifies comparison but may miss model-specific patterns

- Failure signatures: Low top-10 accuracy despite reasonable top-1 accuracy suggests model confidence issues; Consistent failure on specific grammatical patterns (e.g., feminine singular) indicates structural learning gaps; Poor MWE performance across all models suggests dataset bias or representation issues

- First 3 experiments: 1. Compare top-1 vs top-10 accuracy distributions across all tasks to identify confidence patterns 2. Analyze specific failure cases for feminine singular agreement to understand morphological learning gaps 3. Test model performance on MWEs with varying frequencies to assess representation quality for rare vs common expressions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why does BERTimbau Large show higher confidence in incorrect predictions compared to BERTimbau Base?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that BERTimbau Large learns more MWEs and displays more confidence in correct predictions, but also that some MWEs are not accurately represented by either model, and that the values of accuracy at 10 for BERTimbau Base had a higher increase when compared to BERTimbau Large.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the reasons behind the increased confidence in incorrect predictions in BERTimbau Large.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further analysis of the attention layers or other model internals to understand the decision-making process of BERTimbau Large compared to Base.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why does the model struggle with feminine singular nominal participles in the passive voice task?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that the model has trouble producing good candidates for the feminine singular cases in the passive voice task, with results below 50% for both top 1 and top 10 candidates.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests that this might be due to the corpus used for training or the form of the participles, but does not conduct a deeper study to confirm this hypothesis.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the training corpus to identify the frequency of feminine singular nominal participles, or experiments with a balanced dataset to test the model's performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why does the model perform poorly for certain personal pronouns in the verb agreement task?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that the model has problems finding good candidates for pronouns like "tu" (youSing), "nós" (we), and "vós" (youPlural), which could be a sign of poor morphosyntactic generalization or a bias in the training corpus.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the underlying reasons for this poor performance, such as the frequency of these pronouns in the training data or their syntactic roles in sentences.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study comparing the frequency of these pronouns in the training corpus and their syntactic roles, or experiments with a dataset that balances the representation of different pronouns.

## Limitations
- The relatively small manually curated dataset may limit generalizability of results
- Manual evaluation of grammatical structures is resource-intensive and may introduce inter-rater variability
- The study focuses exclusively on BERT-based models, limiting conclusions about other architectures

## Confidence
- High Confidence: BERTimbau Large's superior performance on MWE prediction; Near-perfect performance on non-agreement grammatical tasks; Consistent pattern of decreased performance on morphosyntactic agreement tasks
- Medium Confidence: General claims about model limitations in morphosyntactic generalization; Comparative performance differences between BERTimbau Large and Base models; Effectiveness of combining top-1 and top-10 metrics
- Low Confidence: Specific numerical performance differences within grammatical task subcategories; Extrapolation of results to other Portuguese dialects or related Romance languages; Claims about model learning mechanisms based solely on performance metrics

## Next Checks
1. Expand Dataset Coverage: Replicate the evaluation using a larger, more diverse dataset of MWEs and grammatical structures, particularly focusing on low-frequency expressions and complex agreement patterns to test model robustness.

2. Cross-Model Comparison: Evaluate additional transformer architectures (GPT models, RoBERTa variants) and non-Transformer approaches to establish whether observed patterns are specific to BERT-like models or represent broader limitations in pre-trained language models.

3. Automated Agreement Evaluation: Develop and validate an automated system for morphosyntactic agreement evaluation using linguistic rules or fine-tuned classifiers, then compare results with manual evaluation to assess consistency and identify potential systematic biases in human judgment.
