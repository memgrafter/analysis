# Average Is Not Enough: Caveats of Multilingual Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.01269
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.01269
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: Simple average metrics in multilingual evaluation can mask linguistic biases favoring dominant language families, requiring qualitative analysis and visualization for fair assessment.

## Executive Summary
This position paper argues that standard evaluation practices in multilingual NLP, which rely on average performance across languages, can hide significant biases toward dominant language families such as Indo-European languages. The authors demonstrate that such averages overestimate the effectiveness of multilingual methods for underrepresented languages. To address this, they propose using qualitative analysis informed by comparative linguistics, combined with visualization techniques based on typological databases like URIEL, to reveal performance disparities across language families.

The paper presents a case study using cross-lingual transfer learning results, showing that methods appearing superior in average terms actually underperform for non-European languages compared to monolingual approaches. By visualizing languages in a 2D typological space, the authors reveal clusters where certain methods excel, exposing biases that simple averages conceal. They conclude that qualitative analysis and visualization are essential tools for ensuring fair and comprehensive evaluation in multilingual settings.

## Method Summary
The method involves visualizing multilingual evaluation results using a 2D projection of URIEL typological features via UMAP dimensionality reduction. Languages are plotted in this space, revealing clusters of genealogically related languages. Method performance metrics (e.g., NER F1 scores) are overlaid on the visualization, allowing researchers to compare results within and across language families. This approach highlights disparities that simple averages obscure, making it possible to detect biases favoring dominant language groups.

## Key Results
- Average performance metrics overestimate effectiveness of multilingual methods for underrepresented languages.
- Cross-lingual transfer learning methods appear superior on average but underperform for non-European languages compared to monolingual approaches.
- Visualization reveals clusters of related languages where performance patterns differ, exposing linguistic biases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Visualization of language clusters based on typological similarity exposes performance disparities across language families.
- Mechanism: By projecting URIEL typological features into a 2D space, genealogically close languages naturally cluster, making it possible to visually compare method performance within and across language families.
- Core assumption: URIEL features capture meaningful typological similarity that aligns with performance patterns in NLP tasks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The authors propose visualization based on URIEL typological database to map languages in a 2D space, revealing clusters of related languages.
  - [section] The visualization shows that related European languages form a cluster where cross-lingual supervision performs better, while non-European languages prefer monolingual methods.
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak: no citations for this paper yet, but related works focus on evaluation and visualization without explicit multilingual typological mapping.
- Break condition: If URIEL features do not correlate with performance differences, clusters will not reflect meaningful disparities, and visualization will not reveal biases.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Average performance metrics overestimate effectiveness of methods for underrepresented languages.
- Mechanism: When results are aggregated across languages without considering linguistic diversity, dominant language families (e.g., Indo-European) disproportionately influence the average, masking poor performance on non-dominant languages.
- Core assumption: Language family imbalance in evaluation datasets causes aggregate metrics to be skewed toward dominant families.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The authors argue that average performance metrics can overestimate the effectiveness of multilingual methods for underrepresented languages.
  - [section] In their case study, cross-lingual methods appear better on average but only outperform on Indo-European languages; non-European languages actually perform better with monolingual methods.
  - [corpus] Weak corpus support: related works discuss NLP biases and evaluation issues but do not specifically address average metric bias for multilingual evaluation.
- Break condition: If language family distribution is balanced in the dataset, average metrics will reflect true overall performance without bias.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Qualitative analysis informed by comparative linguistics is necessary to detect linguistic biases in multilingual evaluation.
- Mechanism: Expert linguistic knowledge enables identification of patterns (e.g., writing systems, genealogical relations) that simple statistics miss, and visualization tools help surface these patterns.
- Core assumption: Linguistic expertise and appropriate visualization tools can uncover biases that aggregated metrics cannot detect.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The authors argue that qualitative analysis informed by comparative linguistics is needed for multilingual results to detect bias.
  - [section] The visualization technique helps identify exceptions (e.g., Cyrillic-using Indo-European languages) that would be missed by simple averages.
  - [corpus] Weak corpus support: while related works criticize NLP benchmarks and metrics, few provide concrete methods for qualitative multilingual bias detection.
- Break condition: If the researcher lacks linguistic expertise or appropriate tools, qualitative analysis cannot be effectively performed.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: URIEL typological database → UMAP dimensionality reduction → 2D language space visualization → Overlay of method performance metrics → Interactive exploration interface
- Critical path: Load URIEL data → Apply UMAP to reduce to 2D → Generate interactive plot → Overlay results for methods → Allow filtering and inspection
- Design tradeoffs: Using URIEL features assumes they capture relevant typological similarity; alternative databases or features might yield different clusterings. UMAP parameters (neighbors, min_dist) affect the layout and may highlight or obscure patterns.
- Failure signatures: If clusters do not correspond to known language families, the visualization may not be meaningful. If performance patterns are not visible after overlaying results, the chosen metrics or methods may not vary enough across languages.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Visualize URIEL languages without any performance data to verify that known language families form clusters.
  2. Overlay results from a simple method comparison (e.g., two methods with known performance differences) to confirm visualization reveals disparities.
  3. Test different UMAP hyperparameters to see how cluster separation changes and whether performance patterns become more or less apparent.

## Limitations
- The core argument relies on URIEL typological features accurately reflecting performance-relevant language similarity, which is not guaranteed.
- The analysis depends on a single case study and does not systematically validate the visualization approach across multiple datasets or tasks.
- The paper does not quantify how much the average metric overestimates performance for underrepresented languages, nor does it propose concrete alternatives to simple averaging.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The claim that average metrics can obscure performance disparities across language families is well-supported by the case study and aligns with known issues in evaluation methodology.
- **Medium confidence**: The visualization technique is plausible and grounded in established methods (URIEL, UMAP), but its effectiveness depends on the quality of typological features and the chosen hyperparameters.
- **Low confidence**: The assertion that qualitative analysis informed by comparative linguistics is necessary lacks systematic validation; the paper presents a method but does not empirically compare it to other bias detection approaches.
