# Bring Your Own Data! Self-Supervised Evaluation for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.13651
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13651
- Reference count: 27
- Key outcome: This paper proposes a framework for self-supervised evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) by analyzing their sensitivity or invariance to transformations on input text. The key idea is to construct pairs of original and perturbed text, feed them into the LLM, and compare the outputs using a similarity metric. This approach bypasses the need for human-labeled benchmarks and can directly monitor LLM behavior on datasets collected in the wild or streamed during live model deployment. The paper demonstrates self-supervised evaluation strategies for measuring closed-book knowledge, toxicity, and long-range context dependence, in addition to sensitivity to grammatical structure and tokenization errors. When comparisons to similar human-labeled benchmarks are available, strong correlations between self-supervised and human-supervised evaluations are found.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a self-supervised framework for evaluating Large Language Models (LLMs) by measuring their sensitivity or invariance to controlled transformations of input text. The approach constructs pairs of original and perturbed text, feeds them through the LLM, and compares outputs using similarity metrics. This bypasses the need for human-labeled benchmarks and enables direct monitoring of model behavior on real-world or live data streams. The authors demonstrate this method across multiple capabilities including knowledge retrieval, toxicity detection, and context dependence, showing strong correlations with traditional supervised benchmarks when available.

## Method Summary
The method constructs pairs of original and perturbed text, feeds them into the LLM, and compares outputs using similarity metrics (perplexity for knowledge, Jensen-Shannon divergence for distributional changes, exact match for generation toxicity). The evaluation pipeline consists of corpus loading, transformation generation, LLM inference, output comparison, aggregation, and scoring. This dataset-agnostic approach can be applied to Wikipedia, live data streams, or domain-specific corpora without requiring human labels.

## Key Results
- Strong correlations between self-supervised sensitivity scores and supervised benchmarks (e.g., TriviaQA accuracy, Perspective API toxicity scores)
- Successful demonstration of self-supervised evaluation for closed-book knowledge, toxicity, long-range context dependence, grammatical structure sensitivity, and tokenization error sensitivity
- Framework enables evaluation on datasets collected in the wild or streamed during live model deployment
- Self-supervised metrics provide meaningful signals about model capabilities without requiring human-labeled benchmarks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Self-supervised evaluation captures model behavior invariances through paired input transformations without requiring labeled data.
- **Mechanism**: The method constructs pairs of original and perturbed text, feeds them into the LLM, and measures output similarity. This captures how the model responds to controlled perturbations, revealing sensitivity or invariance in its representations or generations.
- **Core assumption**: The chosen transformation meaningfully alters the input in a way that should affect the model's output if it has learned the relevant capability, but the perturbation itself is not so extreme that it breaks the coherence of the task.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "analyze their sensitivity or invariance to transformations on the input text"
  - [section 2]: Defines the pipeline: construct pairs (x, x′), feed into f, compare outputs with similarity metric M, aggregate to produce score.
  - [corpus]: Weak. Only 5/25 related papers are directly about self-supervised or evaluation methods; most focus on bring-your-own-model or knowledge expansion, so direct mechanistic support is sparse.
- **Break condition**: If the transformation is too subtle or too extreme, the model's output may not change meaningfully or may break coherence, making the sensitivity score uninformative.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Sensitivity scores correlate with supervised benchmark performance, validating the self-supervised approach.
- **Mechanism**: By measuring how much the model's perplexity or generation changes under a perturbation (e.g., negation), we indirectly assess its understanding of the underlying concept (e.g., factual knowledge). Higher sensitivity to negation often aligns with better accuracy on fact-based tasks.
- **Core assumption**: The perturbation introduces a meaningful change in the underlying semantic or structural property being tested, and the model's response to this change reflects its true capability level.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2]: Figure 3 shows strong correlation between the sensitivity score (negations) and TriviaQA accuracy across multiple model families.
  - [section 5.2]: Figure 7 demonstrates correlation between the toxicity metric and Perspective API toxicity scores.
  - [section 6.2]: Figure 8 shows correlation between LRS Score and LAMBADA performance.
- **Break condition**: If the model memorizes surface patterns rather than understanding the underlying concept, it may show low sensitivity despite high benchmark performance, or vice versa.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Self-supervised evaluation enables continuous monitoring on live or domain-specific data without relying on fixed benchmarks.
- **Mechanism**: Since the evaluation pipeline only requires raw text and a transformation rule, it can be applied directly to data streams or application-specific corpora, bypassing the need for curated datasets and labels.
- **Core assumption**: The corpus used for evaluation is representative enough of the target deployment scenario that invariances measured on it generalize to real-world performance.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Self-supervised evaluation can directly monitor LLM behavior on datasets collected in the wild or streamed during live model deployment."
  - [section 1]: Emphasizes the dataset-agnostic nature and applicability to live systems.
  - [corpus]: Weak. No direct evidence in corpus about live deployment or monitoring; mostly related to bring-your-own-model or knowledge expansion topics.
- **Break condition**: If the evaluation corpus is too narrow or unrepresentative, the sensitivity scores may not generalize to the broader deployment context.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Perplexity as a measure of model uncertainty
  - **Why needed here**: Used in knowledge probing via negations to quantify how "surprised" the model is by negated facts.
  - **Quick check question**: If a model assigns lower perplexity to a true fact than its negation, what does that suggest about its knowledge state?

- **Concept**: Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) for comparing probability distributions
  - **Why needed here**: Used in long-range context sensitivity and word order tests to measure how much the model's next-token distribution changes under perturbation.
  - **Quick check question**: Why is JSD preferred over KL-divergence in this context? (Hint: symmetry and boundedness.)

- **Concept**: Tokenization and its effect on model behavior
  - **Why needed here**: Explored in the tokenization sensitivity test, where different tokenizations of the same text can lead to different model outputs.
  - **Quick check question**: What might happen if a model is trained on mostly well-tokenized text and then evaluated on broken tokenization?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Corpus loader -> Transformation generator -> LLM inference engine -> Output comparator -> Aggregator -> Score reporter
- **Critical path**: Corpus -> Transform (x, x′) -> LLM(f) -> Compare(f(x), f(x′)) -> Aggregate -> Score
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Transformation choice: Too subtle → low signal; too extreme → breaks coherence.
  - Similarity metric: Perplexity for knowledge, JSD for distributional changes, exact match for generation toxicity.
  - Aggregation: Mean vs median—mean sensitive to outliers, median robust but may hide extremes.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low variance in outputs across perturbations → transformation too weak or model too deterministic.
  - High variance but no correlation with benchmarks → transformation not aligned with target capability.
  - High compute cost per sample → inefficient for large-scale deployment.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run knowledge probing via negations on a small Wikipedia sample; verify correlation with TriviaQA on a held-out model.
  2. Test toxicity metric with simple F-bomb append; compare to Perspective API on same prompts.
  3. Apply word order swap test; measure JSD change and compare to LRS score on same models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different tokenization errors beyond character splits (e.g., extra spaces, unusual punctuation) affect the Tokenization Sensitivity Score?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that they test a limited type of tokenization error (character splits) and that there are additional tokenization errors to consider, such as minor edits of the raw input text.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores one type of tokenization error and suggests that other types should be considered.
- What evidence would resolve it: Running the Tokenization Sensitivity Score on a dataset with various types of tokenization errors (extra spaces, unusual punctuation, etc.) and comparing the results to the character split results.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between a model's entropy and its sensitivity scores?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that the entropy of a model's output distribution can impact sensitivity scores and suggests that future work should explore how to appropriately incorporate entropy into invariances/sensitivity scores.
- Why unresolved: The paper only presents a figure showing the relationship between entropy and next token entropy but does not explore the impact of entropy on sensitivity scores.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing the sensitivity scores of models with varying levels of entropy and determining if there is a correlation between entropy and sensitivity.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the training data exposure affect the sensitivity scores, particularly in relation to memorization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the potential effects of memorization on sensitivity scores and suggests that training-time exposure to certain documents or token sequences might confound the trends observed in the proposed sensitivity metrics.
- Why unresolved: The paper only briefly mentions the potential effects of memorization and does not provide a detailed analysis of the interactions between memorization behaviors and sensitivity scores.
- What evidence would resolve it: Investigating the sensitivity scores of models with varying levels of training data exposure and determining if there is a correlation between training data exposure and sensitivity scores.

## Limitations

- **Transformation Generalization**: Effectiveness depends heavily on choosing transformations that meaningfully probe target capabilities without breaking text coherence, and generalizability to other domains remains uncertain.
- **Correlation Validity**: Strong correlations with supervised benchmarks are demonstrated primarily on Wikipedia data; unclear whether these hold for truly "wild" or domain-specific deployment scenarios.
- **Resource Intensity**: Requires multiple LLM inferences per sample, making it computationally expensive for continuous monitoring or resource-constrained environments.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core methodology of using paired input transformations and output comparison is well-defined and technically sound. The demonstration that self-supervised metrics can correlate with supervised benchmarks is convincing within the tested domains.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that self-supervised evaluation enables "live model deployment" monitoring is supported by the dataset-agnostic nature of the approach, but lacks direct evidence from actual deployment scenarios or streaming data evaluation.

**Low Confidence**: The assertion that this approach can replace traditional benchmarks entirely is overstated. The paper itself uses human-labeled benchmarks for validation, suggesting the approach is complementary rather than replacement-level.

## Next Checks

1. **Domain Transfer Validation**: Apply the self-supervised evaluation pipeline to non-Wikipedia data (e.g., legal documents, medical literature, code repositories) and test whether the sensitivity scores still correlate with task-specific performance measures in these domains.

2. **Real-time Deployment Testing**: Implement the evaluation framework on a live model deployment scenario where data streams in continuously, measuring both the correlation with actual performance degradation and the computational overhead for real-time monitoring.

3. **Transformation Robustness Analysis**: Systematically vary the strength and type of transformations (e.g., different negation strategies, varying degrees of word shuffling) to identify the optimal perturbation range that maximizes sensitivity signal while maintaining text coherence.
