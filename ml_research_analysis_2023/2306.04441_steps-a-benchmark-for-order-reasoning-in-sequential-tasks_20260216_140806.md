# STEPS: A Benchmark for Order Reasoning in Sequential Tasks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04441
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04441
- Reference count: 2
- Key outcome: Large language models struggle with order reasoning in sequential tasks, showing strong bias in classification and improving with fine-tuning and multi-choice evaluation.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces STEPS, a benchmark designed to evaluate order reasoning capabilities in sequential tasks, particularly focusing on commonsense reasoning required to determine the plausibility of next steps in recipes. The benchmark consists of two subtask settings: classification and multi-choice question answering. Results indicate that state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with zero-shot prompting and few-shot in-context learning, often failing to accurately predict correct next steps. The classification task proves particularly challenging, with most models exhibiting strong bias towards predicting positive classes. Fine-tuning the models on task-specific data significantly improves their performance, highlighting the importance of task-specific adaptation.

## Method Summary
The STEPS benchmark uses the FOOD.COM RECIPES dataset, filtering recipes to those with 4-10 steps, and splits them into train/dev/test sets. The classification task involves determining the rationality of a given next step in a recipe, while the multi-choice setting requires selecting the most reasonable next step from multiple options. The benchmark evaluates baseline LLMs (GPT-2, OPT, BLOOM) using zero-shot, few-shot in-context learning, and fine-tuning methods with the Adam optimizer (lr=0.0003, 6000 updates, batch size 8). Evaluation metrics include sensitivity, specificity, and geometric mean for classification, and accuracy for multi-choice tasks.

## Key Results
- Zero-shot prompting and few-shot in-context learning fail to resolve commonsense reasoning of action orders in sequential tasks for LLMs
- Multi-choice evaluation is more effective and robust than classification, as it uses language modeling perplexity scoring aligned with LLM pretraining objectives
- Scaling model size improves performance on multi-choice tasks but not necessarily on classification tasks, with inconsistent results across different model families

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: STEPS exposes the limitations of zero-shot and few-shot prompting for order reasoning in sequential tasks
- Mechanism: By framing the task as binary classification or multi-choice question, the benchmark reveals that LLMs rely heavily on biased or incorrect priors when they lack explicit task-specific training data
- Core assumption: LLMs have insufficient implicit encoding of commonsense action order knowledge in their pretraining
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The experimental results demonstrate 1) The commonsense reasoning of action orders in sequential tasks are challenging to resolve via zero-shot prompting or few-shot in-context learning for LLMs;"
  - [section] "In zero-shot prompting evaluation, we find that GPT-2 series LLMs partially fail in performing the classification on the rationality of given next steps. The model predictions all lies at positive class towards 1000 testing samples, leading to perfect sensitivity but almost 0 specificity."
- Break condition: If task-specific fine-tuning data is provided, LLMs can overcome this bias and significantly improve performance

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Multi-choice evaluation is more robust and better aligned with LLM pretraining objectives than classification
- Mechanism: Multi-choice tasks use language modeling perplexity as a scoring function, which directly matches the pretraining objective of LLMs, whereas classification requires an additional mapping from probabilities to binary labels
- Core assumption: LLMs are optimized for next-token prediction, so evaluation methods aligned with this objective yield more reliable results
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The results on multi-choice answering subtask for LLMs strictly follow the scaling law, in which the largest model, OPT-30B outperforms all others with 71.7 answering accuracy."
  - [section] "Such experimental results demonstrate that the multi-choice question is a more effective, accurate, and robust method for evaluating LLMs because it is evaluated via language modeling perplexity scoring which is the same as pretraining objective of LLMs."
- Break condition: If the multi-choice options are too similar or ambiguous, even this method may fail to discriminate correct answers

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Scaling model size improves performance on multi-choice tasks but not necessarily on classification tasks
- Mechanism: Larger models have more parameters to encode implicit knowledge and can better distinguish subtle differences in plausibility, but they may also inherit and amplify biases if not properly fine-tuned
- Core assumption: The capacity of larger models allows for better representation of complex reasoning tasks, but task formulation and training data are critical for effective utilization
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The results on multi-choice answering subtask for LLMs strictly follow the scaling law, in which the largest model, OPT-30B outperforms all others with 71.7 answering accuracy."
  - [section] "Scaling up the model is beneficial to performance improvement for BLOOM models, in which BLOOM-7b1 significantly outperforms its 3B size model by 38.6% G-Mean score. But such scaling law is not supported for GPT-2 and OPT series of models and the largest baseline LLMs, OPT-30B performs worse than smaller OPT models, OPT-13B."
- Break condition: If the dataset or task formulation does not scale with model capacity, larger models may not show consistent improvements

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> Dataset construction -> Model evaluation -> Performance analysis
- Critical path:
  1. Load and preprocess the dataset
  2. Construct classification and multi-choice samples
  3. Evaluate baseline LLMs using zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned methods
  4. Analyze performance metrics and identify failure modes
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Zero-shot vs. fine-tuning: Zero-shot is faster but less accurate; fine-tuning is more accurate but requires more data and compute
  - Classification vs. multi-choice: Classification is more direct but harder for LLMs; multi-choice is more aligned with pretraining objectives but may not always be applicable
- Failure signatures:
  - Zero-shot: Biased predictions towards one class (e.g., all positive)
  - Few-shot: Inconsistent performance across different seeds and model sizes
  - Fine-tuning: Overfitting to the training data or failure to generalize to unseen samples
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate GPT-2 Small on the classification task using zero-shot prompting and analyze the bias in predictions
  2. Fine-tune GPT-2 Large on the classification task and compare performance to zero-shot results
  3. Evaluate BLOOM-3B on the multi-choice task and observe the scaling law with respect to model size

## Limitations

- Dataset Representativeness: The FOOD.COM RECIPES dataset may not fully capture the diversity of real-world sequential reasoning scenarios due to filtering to recipes with 4-10 steps
- Class Imbalance Impact: The classification task has a severe class imbalance (1:9 ratio of positive to negative samples), which significantly affects model performance
- Prompt Engineering Sensitivity: The paper acknowledges that few-shot in-context learning performance varies across different seeds and prompt configurations, but doesn't provide systematic exploration of prompt variations

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The observation that zero-shot and few-shot prompting struggle with order reasoning tasks is well-supported by experimental results
- Medium Confidence: The claim that multi-choice evaluation is more effective and robust is supported by scaling law observations, but comparison could be strengthened
- Low Confidence: The scaling law observations for BLOOM models versus GPT-2 and OPT series have inconsistencies that aren't fully explained

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-Dataset Validation**: Evaluate the same models and methods on a different sequential reasoning dataset (e.g., procedural text from other domains) to test whether the observed limitations are specific to recipes or generalize to broader order reasoning tasks

2. **Balanced Dataset Ablation**: Create a balanced version of the classification dataset and re-run the experiments to isolate the effect of class imbalance on model performance, particularly for the observed bias toward positive predictions

3. **Prompt Sensitivity Analysis**: Systematically vary prompt templates, shot count, and demonstration selection strategies for few-shot learning to determine the robustness of results to prompt engineering choices and identify optimal configurations
