# End-to-end codesign of Hessian-aware quantized neural networks for FPGAs and ASICs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.06745
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06745
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Mixed-precision QNN achieves 75.78% accuracy using 42.2% fewer LUTs, 36.3% fewer FFs, and 95.7% fewer DSPs compared to floating-point baseline.

## Executive Summary
This work presents an open-source workflow for training and implementing quantized neural networks on FPGAs and ASICs, integrating Hessian-aware quantization (HAWQ), QONNX intermediate representation, and hls4ml firmware generation. The approach uses second-order Hessian information to automatically select mixed-precision quantization settings, keeping sensitive layers at higher precision while aggressively quantizing less sensitive layers. Demonstrated in a particle physics jet tagging application at the CERN LHC, the workflow enables efficient real-time ML deployment for edge applications in scientific and industrial settings.

## Method Summary
The method combines HAWQ for mixed-precision quantization with QONNX intermediate representation and hls4ml for FPGA firmware generation. HAWQ uses Hessian trace to identify layer sensitivity to quantization error, assigning higher precision to sensitive layers (first two layers in the studied MLP) and lower precision to less sensitive ones. Models are exported to QONNX format using custom operators (Quant, Bipolar, Trunc) that support arbitrary precision, then ingested by hls4ml for firmware generation. The workflow is demonstrated on an MLP for jet classification, processing simulated LHC data at 40 MHz collision rate.

## Key Results
- Mixed-precision NN classifier achieves 75.78% accuracy using 42.2% fewer LUTs, 36.3% fewer FFs, and 95.7% fewer DSPs compared to floating-point baseline
- Maintains performance within 1% of baseline while reducing total BOPs by 45.7%
- Demonstrates successful translation from HAWQ-trained models to FPGA firmware using QONNX and hls4ml pipeline

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Hessian-aware quantization improves accuracy over homogeneous quantization for low-bit models by assigning higher precision to sensitive layers
- Mechanism: Hessian trace is used as sensitivity metric; layers with larger Hessian traces are assigned higher bit widths
- Core assumption: Hessian trace accurately reflects layer sensitivity to quantization error
- Evidence anchors: First two layers identified as most sensitive, with first layer 7x more sensitive than second; Hessian provides stable solution to mixed-precision quantization
- Break condition: If Hessian trace no longer correlates with actual quantization sensitivity

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Mixed-precision quantization reduces hardware resources while maintaining high accuracy
- Mechanism: Quantizing less sensitive layers aggressively while keeping sensitive layers at higher precision reduces total BOPs
- Core assumption: BOPs are good proxy for hardware resource usage and latency
- Evidence anchors: BOPs accurately predict hardware accelerator area and power usage; significant improvements in DSPs (95.7% fewer) and LUTs (42.2% fewer)
- Break condition: If BOP-to-resource mapping changes significantly with different FPGA architectures

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: QONNX enables interoperability between HAWQ-trained models and FPGA synthesis tools
- Mechanism: HAWQ models exported to QONNX using custom operators (Quant, Bipolar, Trunc) supporting arbitrary precision
- Core assumption: QONNX accurately represents computational graph and quantization parameters
- Evidence anchors: Successful model translation from HAWQ to firmware implementation
- Break condition: If QONNX operators not correctly interpreted by hls4ml or other tools

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Hessian-based sensitivity analysis
  - Why needed here: To determine which layers are most sensitive to quantization error, enabling intelligent mixed-precision assignment
  - Quick check question: What does a larger Hessian trace indicate about a layer's sensitivity to quantization?

- Concept: Quantization-aware training (QAT)
  - Why needed here: To train models that can maintain accuracy when weights and activations are represented with reduced precision
  - Quick check question: Why is it important to use symmetric quantization for weights and asymmetric for activations in HAWQ?

- Concept: Intermediate representations (ONNX/QONNX)
  - Why needed here: To enable interoperability between different ML frameworks and hardware synthesis tools
  - Quick check question: What is the key advantage of QONNX over standard ONNX for representing quantized models?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: HAWQ (PyTorch) -> QONNX exporter -> hls4ml -> FPGA synthesis tools (Vivado HLS)
- Critical path: HAWQ training → QONNX export → hls4ml ingestion → HLS synthesis → FPGA implementation
- Design tradeoffs: Higher precision in sensitive layers → better accuracy but more resources; Lower precision in insensitive layers → fewer resources but potential accuracy loss; Symmetric vs. asymmetric quantization → hardware efficiency vs. representation flexibility
- Failure signatures: Accuracy drop after quantization → likely insufficient precision in sensitive layers; Excessive resource usage → too much precision assigned globally or to insensitive layers; Synthesis errors → QONNX model not properly formatted or unsupported operations
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train baseline FP-32 model, apply homogeneous INT8 quantization, measure accuracy and resource usage
  2. Apply HAWQ mixed-precision quantization using Hessian trace, compare accuracy and resource usage to INT8
  3. Export HAWQ model to QONNX, ingest with hls4ml, synthesize to FPGA, verify resource usage and latency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the HAWQ quantization approach perform on more complex neural network architectures like CNNs or Transformers compared to simpler MLPs?
- Basis in paper: The paper demonstrates HAWQ on an MLP for jet tagging but acknowledges that "the current study is limited to MLPs" and states "all NN architectures can first be exported to an ONNX or QONNX intermediate representation graph."
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides results for MLPs and does not test HAWQ on deeper, more complex architectures that are common in computer vision and natural language processing.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparisons of HAWQ performance on CNNs and Transformers versus MLPs, measuring accuracy retention and resource savings across different network depths and complexities.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal trade-off between quantization bit width and latency for real-time applications at the LHC, and how does this vary with different neural network architectures?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions the "extreme requirements in computational latency and data bandwidth" at the LHC and discusses resource usage but does not explore the latency-resource trade-offs in detail.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides resource usage metrics and latency estimates, it does not systematically explore how different quantization schemes affect the latency-resource trade-off for various neural network architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study of latency versus resource usage across different quantization schemes and neural network architectures, with a focus on maintaining real-time processing capabilities at the LHC's 40 MHz collision rate.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of HAWQ compare to other quantization methods like QKeras or AutoQkeras in terms of accuracy retention and hardware efficiency for scientific applications?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions QKeras and AutoQkeras as alternative quantization methods and briefly compares HAWQ with AutoQkeras' solution, but does not provide a detailed comparison.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides a brief comparison with AutoQkeras and does not explore the performance of HAWQ relative to QKeras or other quantization methods in terms of accuracy retention and hardware efficiency for scientific applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic comparison of HAWQ with other quantization methods like QKeras and AutoQkeras, measuring accuracy retention and hardware efficiency for scientific applications across different neural network architectures and datasets.

## Limitations
- Hessian trace sensitivity analysis has not been validated on complex architectures like CNNs or Transformers
- Open-source workflow relies on specific tool versions whose future compatibility is not guaranteed
- 1% accuracy tolerance benchmark may not translate to applications requiring higher precision

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The HAWQ quantization approach and its implementation in PyTorch, as demonstrated by consistent accuracy improvements over homogeneous quantization
- **Medium Confidence**: The BOP-to-resource mapping and QONNX interoperability, given their demonstrated success in this specific context but potential sensitivity to toolchain updates
- **Low Confidence**: The long-term maintainability of the end-to-end workflow, given its dependence on multiple evolving open-source components
