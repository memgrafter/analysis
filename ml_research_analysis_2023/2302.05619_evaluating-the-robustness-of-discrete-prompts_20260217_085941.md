# Evaluating the Robustness of Discrete Prompts

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05619
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05619
- Reference count: 17
- This study evaluates the robustness of discrete prompts, specifically those generated by AutoPrompt (AP), by applying various perturbations and measuring performance degradation.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the robustness of discrete prompts in natural language inference (NLI) tasks by comparing AutoPrompt (AP) and manually-written prompts (MP) under various perturbations. The authors find that AP-learned prompts are highly sensitive to token reordering and deletion, while MP shows better overall performance and generalization. Both methods demonstrate some robustness to weak perturbations, but MP degrades significantly under stronger adversarial attacks. The study reveals that discrete prompts, despite their simplicity, have nuanced robustness properties that depend on prompt generation method and perturbation type.

## Method Summary
The study uses RoBERTa-large to fine-tune both AP and MP prompts on CB and MNLI datasets, with four random subsets of training data for each dataset size. AP prompts use a template with label tokens (e.g., "nobody" for contradiction), while MP uses a template with verbalizers. Perturbations include token reordering, deletion (random, front, back), cross-dataset evaluation, and adversarial modifications. The rate of degradation (RoD) is calculated as (avg_accx - avg_accx*) / avg_accx to quantify performance changes.

## Key Results
- MP outperforms AP on both CB and MNLI datasets
- AP prompts are highly sensitive to token reordering and deletion
- AP generalizes poorly across datasets (CB to MNLI and vice versa)
- Both AP and MP show some robustness to weak perturbations, but MP drops significantly under stronger adversarial perturbations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Discrete prompts learned by AutoPrompt (AP) are highly sensitive to token ordering, unlike manually-written prompts (MP).
- Mechanism: AP optimizes trigger tokens to maximize probability of masked class labels, which may lead to reliance on specific token sequences rather than semantic content.
- Core assumption: The performance of AP-learned prompts depends on the exact ordering of trigger tokens, which encode dataset-specific artifacts rather than generalizable patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "AP prompts are highly sensitive to token reordering and deletion"
  - [section] "Compared to MP, AP-learnt discrete prompts turn out to be highly sensitive to the ordering of prompt tokens"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence provided for this mechanism
- Break condition: If AP-learned prompts encode semantic meaning rather than positional artifacts, reordering would not cause significant performance degradation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: AP-learned discrete prompts do not generalize well across different NLI datasets.
- Mechanism: AP learns prompts from small training sets that capture dataset-specific features rather than task-general patterns, leading to poor cross-dataset transfer.
- Core assumption: The prompts learned by AP encode features specific to the training dataset's domain and annotation guidelines, limiting their applicability to other datasets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Moreover, they generalize poorly across different NLI datasets"
  - [section] "Moreover, we see a performance drop when we use discrete prompts learnt from CB for MNLI and vice-versa"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows related work on robustness but not specific to cross-dataset generalization
- Break condition: If AP prompts learn task-general features rather than dataset-specific artifacts, cross-dataset performance would not degrade significantly.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: AP-learned discrete prompts show greater robustness to adversarial perturbations than manually-written prompts.
- Mechanism: AP prompts may encode redundant or less interpretable features that happen to be more robust to certain types of perturbations, while MP overfits to specific linguistic patterns.
- Core assumption: The "noisy and counter-intuitive lexical constructs" in AP prompts provide some form of regularization against certain perturbations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Both AP and MP show some robustness to weak perturbations, but MP drops significantly under stronger adversarial perturbations"
  - [section] "Both AP and MP remain relatively robust against the perturbations that do not change the target label, but the performance of MP drops significantly in the label-changing setting"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows related work on robustness evaluation but not specific comparison between AP and MP
- Break condition: If AP prompts are more sensitive to adversarial perturbations than MP, this mechanism would break.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Robustness evaluation metrics (Rate of Degradation - RoD)
  - Why needed here: To quantify how different types of perturbations affect prompt performance in a standardized way
  - Quick check question: If a prompt's accuracy drops from 80% to 60% after perturbation, what is the RoD?

- Concept: Cross-dataset evaluation methodology
  - Why needed here: To assess whether prompts learned from one dataset transfer to another, revealing generalization capabilities
  - Quick check question: If a prompt performs 90% accuracy on its training dataset but only 50% on a different dataset, what does this indicate about its generalization?

- Concept: Adversarial perturbation design
  - Why needed here: To create controlled test cases that probe model robustness beyond random noise
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes perturbations that change labels from those that don't, and why is this distinction important?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: RoBERTa-large PLM → Prompt template (MP/AP) → Classification head → Output labels
- Critical path: Token perturbation → Prompt template → PLM inference → Classification → Accuracy calculation
- Design tradeoffs: AP offers automatic learning but produces less interpretable prompts with potential robustness issues vs MP offering interpretability but requiring manual effort and potentially overfitting
- Failure signatures: Significant RoD values indicate sensitivity to specific perturbation types; cross-dataset performance drops indicate poor generalization
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate token reordering experiment on a small subset to observe sensitivity patterns
  2. Conduct single token deletion at different positions to identify critical tokens
  3. Test cross-dataset transferability with a small held-out sample from a different NLI dataset

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How do the robustness properties of discrete prompts vary across different prompting methods beyond AutoPrompt (AP) and manually-written prompts (MP)?
- How does the performance of discrete prompts on NLI tasks vary when using different pre-trained language models (PLMs) other than RoBERTa-large?
- How do the robustness properties of discrete prompts generalize to other natural language understanding tasks beyond NLI?

## Limitations

- Limited generalizability due to focus on only two NLI datasets (CB and MNLI) and one adversarial dataset
- Lack of detailed prompt specifications, making it difficult to verify or extend results
- Incomplete characterization of adversarial perturbations and their methodology

## Confidence

- High confidence: AP prompts are highly sensitive to token reordering and deletion compared to MP
- Medium confidence: AP generalizes poorly across datasets
- Low confidence: AP shows greater robustness to adversarial perturbations than MP

## Next Checks

1. Conduct a systematic ablation study by removing individual trigger tokens from AP prompts in order of their optimization importance to identify which tokens contribute most to performance and sensitivity.

2. Test whether fine-tuning AP prompts on the target dataset (rather than direct transfer) improves cross-dataset performance, distinguishing between learning dataset-specific features versus task-general patterns.

3. Create a comprehensive taxonomy of adversarial perturbations and systematically evaluate how different perturbation types affect AP vs. MP robustness, particularly focusing on whether certain perturbation classes favor one approach over the other.
