# An Information-Theoretic Analysis of Self-supervised Discrete Representations of Speech

- arXiv ID: 2306.02405
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02405

## Executive Summary
This paper develops an information-theoretic framework to analyze the relationship between discrete units learned by self-supervised speech models and phonetic categories. The key innovation is representing each phonetic category as a probability distribution over discrete units, then characterizing these distributions using entropy and Jensen-Shannon divergence. Applied to wav2vec 2.0 and XLSR models on American English speech, the analysis reveals that entropy quantifies acoustic-phonetic variability (vowels being more variable than consonants), while JSD reflects phonetic similarity through feature-based correlations. The work confirms the lack of direct one-to-one correspondence between phonetic categories and discrete units while revealing an indirect relationship where phonetically similar sounds exhibit similar distributions.

## Method Summary
The method represents each phonetic category as a probability distribution over discrete units extracted from self-supervised speech models. First, aligned speech-phonetic pairs are extracted from the TIMIT corpus, and speech is passed through SSL models to obtain discrete unit sequences. Frequency distributions for each phonetic category are built and converted to probability distributions. Entropy is computed to measure within-category variability, while Jensen-Shannon divergence is calculated between all phonetic category pairs to measure similarity. The results are validated through hierarchical clustering and correlation with feature-based phonetic distance derived from PHOIBLE.

## Key Results
- Entropy quantifies acoustic-phonetic variability, with vowels showing higher entropy (more variable) than consonants
- Jensen-Shames divergence highly reflects feature-based phonetic similarity with correlation coefficients of 0.63 (wav2vec 2.0) and 0.61 (XLSR)
- The analysis confirms the lack of direct one-to-one correspondence between phonetic categories and discrete units
- Hierarchical clustering reveals phonetic categories grouped by manner of articulation (e.g., nasals, stops, fricatives)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Entropy measures acoustic-phonetic variability by quantifying the unpredictability of discrete unit alignments for each phonetic category.
- Mechanism: Higher entropy indicates greater within-category variability because the distribution of discrete units becomes more uniform (less predictable), reflecting the diverse acoustic realizations of the phonetic category.
- Core assumption: Phonetic categories with more variable acoustic realizations will map to more diverse (less peaked) discrete unit distributions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "entropy quantifies acoustic-phonetic variability, with vowels being more entropic (more variable) than consonants"
  - [section] "entropy can be viewed as a measure of (within-category) acoustic-phonetic variability in our case"
  - [corpus] Weak - the corpus doesn't directly measure acoustic variability, but shows vowel/consonant differences
- Break Condition: If discrete unit alignment is biased or corrupted (e.g., quantization artifacts), entropy would reflect alignment noise rather than true phonetic variability.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Jensen-Shannon divergence reflects phonetic similarity because phonetically similar sounds produce similar distributions over discrete units.
- Mechanism: Phonetic categories that share articulatory features or acoustic properties will tend to align with similar patterns of discrete units, resulting in low JSD scores between their distributions.
- Core assumption: Discrete units capture sub-phonetic features that correlate with phonetic similarity, so similar sounds will have overlapping discrete unit usage patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "phonetic similarity is measured using Jensen-Shannon divergence, which highly reflects feature-based phonetic similarity"
  - [section] "the distance between distributions reflects phonetic similarity" and clustering analysis showing manner-of-articulation grouping
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows correlation between JSD and feature-based distance but doesn't prove causation
- Break Condition: If the discrete unit space is poorly aligned with phonetic features (e.g., too coarse or dominated by non-phonetic factors), JSD would not reflect true phonetic similarity.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The lack of one-to-one correspondence between phonetic categories and discrete units is inherent to the sub-phonemic nature of discrete representations.
- Mechanism: Discrete units represent low-level acoustic events that can occur across multiple phonetic contexts, while a single phonetic category can manifest with multiple acoustic variations, preventing direct mapping.
- Core assumption: Self-supervised discrete units encode sub-phonemic information rather than complete phonetic categories.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "confirms the lack of direct, one-to-one correspondence between phonetic categories and discrete units"
  - [section] "none of the phonetic categories is minimally entropic (i.e, H(p) = 0), which confirms the findings in the literature about the lack of one-to-one correspondence"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't directly test sub-phonemic encoding but shows distributional complexity
- Break Condition: If discrete units were perfectly aligned with phonemes (contrary to findings), entropy would be near zero for many categories.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Speech encoder (F) -> Quantization module (D) -> Phonetic alignment system -> Distribution builder -> Information-theoretic analyzer

- Critical path:
  1. Extract aligned speech-phonetic pairs from corpus
  2. Pass speech through SSL model to obtain discrete units
  3. Build frequency distributions for each phonetic category
  4. Convert to probability distributions
  5. Compute entropy and JSD metrics
  6. Analyze results for phonetic variability and similarity

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Codebook size vs. granularity: Larger codebooks may capture more sub-phonetic detail but increase computational cost
  - Phonetic inventory granularity: Finer-grained inventories may show clearer patterns but require more data
  - Model choice: Monolingual vs. multilingual models may capture different aspects of phonetic variability

- Failure signatures:
  - Uniform entropy across all categories suggests discrete units aren't capturing phonetic distinctions
  - JSD not correlating with phonetic features suggests units encode non-phonetic information
  - High computational variance suggests alignment or quantization issues

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare entropy distributions between vowels and consonants to verify expected variability patterns
  2. Compute JSD matrix and perform hierarchical clustering to check for articulatory class grouping
  3. Correlate JSD values with feature-based phonetic distances to validate similarity measurements

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the discrete units relate to articulatory features beyond the general phonetic categories studied here?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the clustering analysis showed unexpected behavior for /h/ being grouped with plosives and discuss this in terms of aspiration, suggesting more detailed articulatory analysis could be revealing.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides a high-level analysis of phonetic categories without examining specific articulatory feature relationships.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic analysis correlating discrete unit distributions with individual articulatory features (place, manner, voicing, aspiration) across multiple phonetic contexts.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does speaker variation (age, gender, dialect) affect the entropy and distribution patterns of discrete units?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge they cannot disentangle sources of variability and suggest future work should address speaker variation effects.
- Why unresolved: The analysis used aggregated data from all speakers without controlling for individual speaker characteristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing entropy and distribution patterns across speaker groups, or within-speaker versus between-speaker variability analysis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between the discrete units and phonological processes like vowel reduction and co-articulation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that vowels are more entropic due to vowel reduction in unstressed syllables and co-articulation, but don't examine this systematically.
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions these processes as potential causes of variability but doesn't test their specific effects on discrete unit distributions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of discrete unit patterns in different phonological contexts (stressed vs. unstressed syllables, different co-articulation environments) to identify systematic effects of these processes.

## Limitations
- The analysis is based on American English in the TIMIT corpus, which may not generalize to other languages or dialects
- The correlation between JSD and feature-based phonetic distance, while statistically significant, is moderate (r = 0.63 for wav2vec 2.0, r = 0.61 for XLSR)
- The interpretation that the lack of one-to-one correspondence confirms the sub-phonemic nature of discrete representations is not definitively proven

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The entropy measures correctly quantify within-category variability as intended. The mechanism is well-established in information theory, and the observation that vowels have higher entropy than consonants aligns with linguistic knowledge about vowel variability.
- **Medium Confidence**: The Jensen-Shannon divergence reflects phonetic similarity to the extent that feature-based correlations are meaningful. While the correlation values are moderate, the hierarchical clustering results showing manner-of-articulation groupings provide additional evidence for the validity of the approach.
- **Low Confidence**: The interpretation that the lack of one-to-one correspondence confirms the sub-phonemic nature of discrete representations. Alternative explanations, such as poor alignment between discrete units and phonetic categories or limitations in the quantization process, have not been ruled out.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-linguistic validation**: Apply the same analysis to speech corpora from multiple languages to determine if the entropy and JSD patterns generalize beyond American English. This would test whether the observed relationships are properties of the discrete representations themselves or artifacts of the specific language or corpus.

2. **Controlled acoustic variation**: Generate synthetic speech stimuli with controlled acoustic variation (e.g., different vowel durations or consonantal place of articulation) and measure how entropy and JSD change. This would provide direct evidence that the metrics capture intended acoustic-phonetic properties rather than other factors.

3. **Alternative feature correlations**: Compute correlations between JSD and multiple alternative similarity measures, including acoustic similarity (e.g., MFCC distances) and phonological neighborhood density. This would help disentangle whether discrete units capture phonetic features specifically or broader acoustic or lexical properties.
