# Wasserstein distributional robustness of neural networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.09844
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09844
- Reference count: 36
- The paper proposes distributional threat models based on Wasserstein DRO to evaluate adversarial robustness of neural networks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of evaluating adversarial robustness in deep neural networks by introducing distributional threat models based on Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization. Unlike traditional pointwise attacks that assume uniform perturbation bounds, the proposed approach allows non-uniform perturbations across the input distribution while maintaining a bound on overall distributional distortion. The authors develop a first-order adversarial attack algorithm that generalizes existing methods like FGSM and PGD, and provide computationally efficient bounds on adversarial accuracy. Experimental results on CIFAR-10 demonstrate that distributional attacks can significantly reduce the accuracy of robust neural networks compared to traditional pointwise attacks.

## Method Summary
The method leverages Wasserstein DRO to create distributional threat models where inputs can be perturbed non-uniformly within a Wasserstein ball. A first-order adversarial attack algorithm (W-PGD) is proposed that includes projection steps to ensure perturbed data stays within the Wasserstein ball. The approach also provides asymptotic bounds on adversarial accuracy using sensitivity analysis, which are computationally more efficient than running sophisticated attacks like AutoAttack. The threat model is specified by parameters (p, r, δ) defining the Wasserstein distance metric and radius.

## Key Results
- Distributional attacks reduce robust network accuracy by 20-30% on CIFAR-10 compared to pointwise attacks
- The proposed bounds on adversarial accuracy are orders of magnitude faster to compute than reference attacks
- W-PGD with ReDLR loss outperforms standard CE/DLR losses in distributional attack scenarios
- The framework unifies adversarial attacks, out-of-sample performance, and Knightian uncertainty

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Distributional threat models allow attackers to perturb inputs in a non-uniform way, enabling more powerful attacks than traditional pointwise attacks.
- Mechanism: Traditional pointwise attacks assume a uniform bound on perturbation for each input data point. Distributional threat models, based on Wasserstein DRO, relax this constraint by allowing non-uniform perturbations across the input distribution while maintaining a bound on the overall distributional distortion.
- Core assumption: The attacker has knowledge of the input distribution and can optimize perturbations to maximize classification error within the Wasserstein ball constraint.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Unlike the traditional pointwise attacks, which assume a uniform bound on perturbation of each input data point, distributional threat models allow attackers to perturb inputs in a non-uniform way."
  - [section 4]: "We define the adversarial accuracy as: Aδ : = inf QPBδ(P) Q(S) = inf QPBδ(P) EQr1 Ss."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. Related papers focus on distributional attacks but don't explicitly compare non-uniform vs uniform perturbation power.
- Break condition: If the input distribution is highly concentrated or the Wasserstein radius is extremely small, the advantage of non-uniform perturbations diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The proposed first-order adversarial attack algorithm recovers FGSM and PGD as special cases while generalizing to distributional threat models.
- Mechanism: The attack algorithm xt+1 = projδ(xt + αhp(∇xJθ(xt, y))) uses a projection step to ensure the perturbed data stays within the Wasserstein ball. For p=r=∞, this reduces to sgn-based updates matching FGSM/PGD. For other p, r values, it provides distributional attacks.
- Core assumption: The loss function Jθ is L-Lipschitz under the distance d, enabling first-order approximation of the Wasserstein DRO value.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "To evaluate the distributional robustness of neural networks, we propose a first-order AA algorithm and its multi-step version. Our attack algorithms include Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) as special cases."
  - [section 4]: "xt+1 = projδ(xt + αhp(∇xJθ(xt, y)))}, where α is the step size and projδ is a projection which ensures the empirical measure stays inside the Wasserstein ball Bδ(P)."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence. No direct comparison of the proposed algorithm's recovery of FGSM/PGD in related works.
- Break condition: If the loss landscape is highly non-convex or the projection step becomes computationally intractable, the first-order approximation may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The proposed bounds on adversarial accuracy are first-order accurate and computationally efficient, offering new insights even for pointwise attacks.
- Mechanism: By leveraging DRO sensitivity analysis, the paper derives asymptotic lower bounds on adversarial accuracy that depend on the clean accuracy, the adversarial loss at δ=0, and the sensitivity term Υ. These bounds are faster to compute than running sophisticated attacks like AutoAttack.

  - Attack algorithm (W-PGD with ReDLR loss for distributional attacks)
  - Bound computation (Rl, Ru using sensitivity analysis)
  - Empirical evaluation (AutoAttack for pointwise, W-PGD for distributional)

- Critical path:
  1. Parse threat model parameters
  2. Compute sensitivity term Υ
  3. Run W-PGD attack to generate adversarial examples
  4. Compute bounds Rl, Ru
  5. Compare against reference attacks (AutoAttack, W-PGD)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computational cost vs. accuracy of bounds (linear approximation valid for small δ)
  - Choice of loss function (CE/DLR vs. ReDLR) impacting attack effectiveness
  - Single-step vs. multi-step attacks (speed vs. strength)

- Failure signatures:
  - Bounds exceeding reference accuracy (linear approximation breaking down)
  - Projected gradients not reducing loss (incorrect projection implementation)
  - Attack not improving with more steps (learning rate or loss function issue)

- First 3 experiments:

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantees rely heavily on the linear approximation regime (small δ), which may not hold for practical threat models
- The computational efficiency gains come at the cost of potential approximation errors in the bounds
- Exact implementation details of the W-PGD attack and bound computation algorithms are not fully specified

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The fundamental mechanism of distributional threat models allowing non-uniform perturbations is well-established in the distributional robustness literature. The experimental results showing improved attack performance over pointwise methods are reproducible given access to the source code.

**Medium confidence**: The first-order approximation bounds on adversarial accuracy are theoretically sound but may be overly optimistic in practice, especially for larger δ values where the linear regime breaks down.

**Low confidence**: The exact implementation details of the W-PGD attack and bound computation algorithms are not fully specified, making exact reproduction challenging without access to the authors' code.
