# A Closer Look at the Adversarial Robustness of Deep Equilibrium Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2306.01429
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01429
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Adversarially trained DEQs with proposed intermediate gradient estimation achieve competitive or stronger robustness compared to deep networks of similar size.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the adversarial robustness of Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQs), which rely on black-box solvers to find fixed points. The authors identify that DEQs suffer from gradient obfuscation under standard attacks due to misalignment between forward and backward passes. To address this, they propose two methods for estimating intermediate gradients - simultaneous adjoint process and unrolling intermediate states - and integrate these into attacking pipelines for fully white-box evaluations. Experiments on CIFAR-10 demonstrate that adversarially trained DEQs with these methods achieve competitive robustness compared to deep networks of similar size.

## Method Summary
The paper studies DEQs that use black-box solvers to find fixed points, which creates challenges for adversarial robustness evaluation. The authors propose two approaches to estimate intermediate gradients: the simultaneous adjoint process, which iterates adjoint state updates alongside the forward pass, and unrolled intermediates, which estimate gradients by unrolling the computational graph of intermediate states. These estimates are integrated into attacking pipelines to enable white-box attacks. They also design defense strategies leveraging intermediate states. DEQs are trained on CIFAR-10 using PGD adversarial training, and robustness is evaluated using PGD-10 and AutoAttack, comparing against deep network baselines.

## Key Results
- Standard attacks on DEQs suffer from gradient obfuscation due to misalignment between forward and backward passes
- Adversarially trained DEQs show robustness accumulation effect in intermediate states
- Proposed intermediate gradient estimation methods enable stronger white-box attacks on DEQs
- DEQs with these methods achieve competitive or stronger robustness compared to deep networks of similar size

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: DEQs suffer from gradient obfuscation under standard adversarial attacks due to misalignment between forward and backward passes.
- **Mechanism**: The forward pass finds a fixed point using a black-box solver, while the backward pass uses implicit differentiation to solve an independent linear fixed-point system. Intermediate states in the forward pass are bypassed, preventing gradient-based attacks from accessing them directly.
- **Core assumption**: The misalignment between forward and backward passes prevents gradient-based attacks from seeing intermediate states.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "the forward and backward tracks of DEQs are misaligned due to the black-box solvers...These facts cause gradient obfuscation when applying the ready-made attacks"
  - [section]: "However, when iterating the gradient computations, the intermediate states {zn} in the forward pass are bypassed by both methods."
- **Break condition**: If the forward and backward passes were aligned (e.g., through a unified solver), gradient obfuscation would not occur.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Intermediate states in adversarially trained DEQs exhibit higher robustness than the final state under standard attacks.
- **Mechanism**: Due to gradient obfuscation, standard attacks only attack the final state. The intermediate states, being less distorted in the forward pass, are more robust to perturbations.
- **Core assumption**: The robustness accumulation effect is a result of gradient obfuscation preventing attacks from accessing intermediate states.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "we observe that an adversarially trained DEQ requires more forward steps to arrive at the equilibrium state, or even violates its fixed-point structure."
  - [section]: "we discover the robustness accumulation effect in both the exact-trained and the unrolling-trained DEQs."
- **Break condition**: If attacks could directly access and attack intermediate states, the robustness accumulation effect would disappear.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Simultaneous adjoint and unrolled intermediate gradients enable fully white-box attacks on DEQs.
- **Mechanism**: The simultaneous adjoint estimates intermediate gradients by iterating adjoint state updates alongside the forward pass. Unrolled intermediates estimate gradients by unrolling the computational graph of intermediate states and applying automatic differentiation.
- **Core assumption**: The estimated intermediate gradients can approximate the true gradients at intermediate states well enough to construct effective attacks.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "we develop approaches to estimate the intermediate gradients of DEQs and integrate them into the attacking pipelines."
  - [section]: "we propose to iterate the updates of adjoint states subject to zn in the forward pass" and "We also propose to estimate the gradient at the state zn by unrolling."
- **Break condition**: If the estimated gradients are too inaccurate, the resulting attacks will be ineffective.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:
  Input layer -> Single transformation layer -> Black-box solver -> Implicit differentiation -> Intermediate state estimation -> Defense strategies

- **Critical path**:
  1. Input data is fed into the single transformation layer
  2. The black-box solver iteratively applies the transformation to find the fixed point
  3. The fixed point is used as the output
  4. For training, implicit differentiation computes gradients using the fixed point
  5. For white-box attacks, intermediate gradients are estimated and used to construct adversaries
  6. For defense, intermediate states are leveraged to improve robustness

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Fixed-point convergence vs. computational efficiency: More iterations improve convergence but increase computation time
  - Intermediate state accuracy vs. attack strength: More accurate intermediate gradients lead to stronger attacks but require more computation
  - Defense strategy complexity vs. robustness: More complex defenses may provide better robustness but are harder to implement and optimize

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Divergence of the black-box solver: The forward pass fails to converge to a fixed point
  - Gradient obfuscation: Standard attacks fail to reduce the model's accuracy, indicating they are not accessing the true gradients
  - Robustness accumulation: Intermediate states show higher robustness than the final state under standard attacks

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train a DEQ on a simple dataset (e.g., MNIST) and verify that it can find a fixed point and achieve reasonable accuracy.
  2. Apply standard adversarial attacks (e.g., PGD) to the trained DEQ and observe the robustness accumulation effect.
  3. Implement the simultaneous adjoint and unrolled intermediate gradient estimation methods and verify that they can improve the strength of white-box attacks on DEQs.

## Limitations
- The generalizability of the robustness accumulation effect to other architectures or training methods remains unclear
- The computational overhead and scalability of intermediate gradient estimation methods to larger models/datasets are not thoroughly evaluated
- The effectiveness of intermediate state-based defense strategies against adaptive attacks designed to bypass them is unexplored

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Identification of gradient obfuscation as a challenge for DEQs and proposal of simultaneous adjoint and unrolled intermediate gradient estimation methods
- **Medium Confidence**: Observation of robustness accumulation in adversarially trained DEQs and effectiveness of intermediate state-based defense strategies
- **Low Confidence**: Generalizability of robustness accumulation effect and scalability of intermediate gradient estimation methods to larger models and datasets
