# An Empirical Study on the Transferability of Transformer Modules in Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.00378
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00378
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: LayerNorm fine-tuning achieves comparable performance to full fine-tuning using only 0.03% of parameters

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates which components of BERT's transformer blocks are most effective for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. The authors systematically test fine-tuning different modules (LayerNorms, Multi-Head Attention, Feed-Forward layers, and BitFit) while freezing others, comparing these approaches to full fine-tuning on GLUE tasks. The key finding is that LayerNorms are remarkably effective - fine-tuning just 37K LayerNorm weights out of 110M total parameters achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning. Even fine-tuning a single LayerNorm in one transformer block yields strong results. The authors hypothesize this effectiveness stems from LayerNorms having higher-magnitude weights compared to other modules, showing that tuning only the top-n highest-magnitude weights (as few as 4 parameters per layer) still produces competitive results while using less than 0.0056% of all parameters.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates parameter-efficient fine-tuning by systematically freezing different transformer modules in BERT-base and fine-tuning only specific components. The authors test four approaches: (1) full fine-tuning of all parameters, (2) module-wise fine-tuning where only one module type is updated while others are frozen, (3) layer-wise analysis focusing on individual LayerNorms, and (4) outlier tuning that updates only the highest-magnitude weights in LayerNorms. Experiments are conducted on GLUE benchmark tasks (CoLA, SST-2, MRPC, STS-B, QQP, MNLI, QNLI, RTE) and token-level datasets (Penn Treebank POS tagging, CoNLL-2003 NER). Models are trained with Adam optimizer, various learning rates, and evaluated across three random seeds for statistical robustness.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuning only LayerNorm weights (0.03% of parameters) achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning across GLUE tasks
- Fine-tuning even a single LayerNorm in one transformer block yields strong results, particularly in middle layers
- Tuning only the top-n highest-magnitude weights (outliers) in LayerNorms produces competitive results while using less than 0.0056% of all parameters
- LayerNorm effectiveness holds for both sentence-level and token-level tasks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LayerNorm fine-tuning is effective because these modules contain high-magnitude weights (outliers) that dominate the transformation.
- Mechanism: The high-magnitude weights in LayerNorms act as key scaling factors that significantly influence the normalized output, making them highly sensitive to small changes during fine-tuning.
- Core assumption: High-magnitude weights have disproportionate influence on model outputs compared to low-magnitude weights.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we argue that their notable performance could be attributed to their high-magnitude weights compared to that of the other modules in the pre-trained BERT"
  - [section 3] "In general, the distribution of weights is similar across Feed-Forward and Multi-Head modules. Nevertheless, LayerNorms tend to have a bimodal distribution, with one of the modes having significantly higher magnitudes."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related works focus on parameter-efficient methods but don't specifically address LayerNorm magnitude effects
- Break condition: If LayerNorm outliers are disabled after fine-tuning, model performance drops significantly (as shown in Kovaleva et al. 2021)

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LayerNorm fine-tuning achieves comparable performance to full fine-tuning with minimal parameters because LayerNorms operate on all token representations within a layer.
- Mechanism: LayerNorms normalize activations across all tokens in a sequence, making them a bottleneck that affects the entire layer's output distribution.
- Core assumption: Normalization layers have broad influence over layer outputs because they operate on all tokens collectively.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "fine-tuning just 0.03% of parameters (37K LayerNorm weights out of 110M total) achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning"
  - [section 2.4] "tuning even only one LayerNorm can yield comparable performance"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related works don't specifically analyze LayerNorm's token-level influence
- Break condition: If token-level tasks don't show LayerNorm effectiveness, this mechanism would be invalidated

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Middle-layer LayerNorms are most transferable because they capture the most task-relevant features during pre-training.
- Mechanism: Middle layers in transformers have been shown to capture more transferable semantic features, and LayerNorms in these layers inherit this property.
- Core assumption: Middle layers contain more transferable features than early or late layers.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.4] "the middle-layer LayerNorms exhibit the best results across all layers, which can be attributed to the high transferability of the middle layers in BERT"
  - [section 2.2] "LayerNorms prove to have a high potential in transferability and adaptability to various downstream tasks"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related works mention layer importance but don't specifically connect to LayerNorm effectiveness
- Break condition: If LayerNorms in other layers show equal or better performance on specific tasks

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: BERT-base architecture consists of 12 transformer blocks, each containing Multi-Head Attention, Feed-Forward, and two LayerNorm layers (one after attention, one after feed-forward). Total parameters ~110M, with LayerNorms comprising ~0.03% (37K weights).

- Critical path: For parameter-efficient fine-tuning, the critical path is identifying which LayerNorm weights to update and at what learning rate. The model can be frozen except for these LayerNorm parameters and the final classifier.

- Design tradeoffs: LayerNorm fine-tuning offers extreme parameter efficiency but may lack the capacity for highly specialized tasks. Full fine-tuning provides maximum adaptability but at high computational cost. Adapter methods offer a middle ground with moderate parameter addition.

- Failure signatures: Poor performance on tasks requiring fine-grained feature extraction, instability when tuning single LayerNorms in shallow layers, degradation when outliers are disabled after training.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune only LayerNorm weights in one transformer block (e.g., layer 6) and compare to full fine-tuning on a simple GLUE task like MRPC
  2. Test LayerNorm fine-tuning on a token-level task (POS tagging) to validate cross-task effectiveness
  3. Implement outlier-based fine-tuning with n=16 parameters per LayerNorm and measure performance impact compared to random weight selection

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the precise mechanism by which LayerNorm weights enable effective knowledge transfer despite minimal parameter updates?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that LayerNorm weights have high magnitudes and that tuning outliers (high-magnitude weights) produces competitive results, but do not provide a mechanistic explanation for why this occurs.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates correlation between high-magnitude LayerNorm weights and effective fine-tuning, it does not explain the underlying reason why these specific weights are so influential for downstream task adaptation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed ablation studies showing how individual LayerNorm parameters affect model behavior across tasks, or theoretical analysis of LayerNorm's role in feature normalization and its interaction with task-specific information.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the effectiveness of LayerNorm fine-tuning extend to other transformer-based architectures beyond BERT (e.g., GPT, RoBERTa, T5)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The study is limited to bert-base-uncased, and the authors acknowledge computational constraints prevented testing on larger models or different architectures.
- Why unresolved: The findings may be specific to BERT's architecture and pre-training process. Other models may have different LayerNorm weight distributions or training dynamics that could affect their transferability when fine-tuned.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic comparison of LayerNorm fine-tuning effectiveness across multiple transformer architectures on the same downstream tasks, controlling for model size and pre-training objectives.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal strategy for selecting which LayerNorms to fine-tune across transformer layers for different task types?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors show that middle-layer LayerNorms are generally most effective, but task-specific variations exist. They also demonstrate that tuning just one LayerNorm can be sufficient.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides evidence that LayerNorms are effective, it does not determine the optimal selection strategy for different task categories (e.g., syntactic vs semantic tasks, token-level vs sequence-level).
- What evidence would resolve it: Task-specific ablation studies systematically varying which LayerNorms are fine-tuned, combined with analysis of task complexity and linguistic properties to identify patterns in optimal LayerNorm selection.

## Limitations
- The study focuses only on BERT-base architecture, limiting generalizability to other transformer variants
- Evidence is limited to English GLUE tasks and standard token-level benchmarks without testing specialized domains
- The outlier-based tuning approach lacks rigorous validation across different task types and model architectures
- The paper doesn't explore interactions between LayerNorm fine-tuning and other parameter-efficient methods

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The empirical finding that LayerNorm fine-tuning achieves comparable performance to full fine-tuning with minimal parameters (0.03% of total weights). This is directly supported by quantitative results across multiple GLUE tasks and token-level datasets.

**Medium Confidence**: The hypothesis that LayerNorm effectiveness stems from high-magnitude weights (outliers). While the authors provide distributional analysis showing bimodal weight distributions in LayerNorms, the causal relationship between outlier magnitude and performance improvement requires further validation.

**Low Confidence**: The generalizability of LayerNorm fine-tuning effectiveness across different transformer architectures and specialized domains. The paper's evidence is limited to BERT-base on English GLUE tasks and standard token-level benchmarks.
