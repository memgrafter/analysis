# Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models as a Defense against Adversarial Attacks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.06871
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06871
- Reference count: 9
- Key outcome: DDPM-based defense achieves 75% robust accuracy on PatchCamelyon dataset, significantly outperforming vanilla ResNet (6%) and NoiseDefense (58%)

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes using Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) as a defense mechanism against adversarial attacks on neural networks. The approach adds noise to adversarial examples and then applies the reverse process of a diffusion model to purify the image, effectively removing both the added noise and adversarial perturbations. The method was evaluated on the PatchCamelyon dataset for histopathologic scans of lymph node sections. The proposed diffusion-based defense achieved 75% robust accuracy against adversarial attacks, significantly outperforming the vanilla ResNet model (6% robust accuracy) and the NoiseDefense baseline (58% robust accuracy). It also showed 88% of the original model's accuracy on standard, non-adversarial examples.

## Method Summary
The defense mechanism works by first adding noise to an adversarial example, then using the reverse process of a pre-trained DDPM to denoise the image. The key insight is that this purification process can remove adversarial perturbations while preserving the underlying image content. The noise level (time step t*) is a critical hyperparameter that must be optimized for each dataset. In this work, t* = 0.04 was chosen for the PatchCamelyon dataset. The approach leverages the fact that diffusion models are trained to reverse the noising process and can reconstruct clean images from noisy inputs, making them well-suited for removing adversarial perturbations.

## Key Results
- Achieved 75% robust accuracy against adversarial attacks on PatchCamelyon dataset
- Outperformed vanilla ResNet (6% robust accuracy) and NoiseDefense baseline (58% robust accuracy)
- Maintained 88% of original model's accuracy on clean, non-adversarial examples
- Successfully defended against L∞ norm ball attacks while preserving classification accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The diffusion defense works by leveraging the inherent properties of DDPMs trained to reverse the noising process. When noise is added to an adversarial example, the DDPM's reverse process can denoise the image back to a clean state. Since adversarial perturbations are often small and subtle, they are effectively removed during this denoising process. The method essentially exploits the fact that diffusion models are robust to noise and can reconstruct the underlying clean image from highly corrupted inputs. By carefully selecting the noise level (t*), the defense can add enough noise to obscure adversarial perturbations while still allowing the DDPM to reconstruct meaningful features for classification.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Input image → Noise addition (t*) → DDPM reverse process → Denoised image → Classifier → Output

**Critical path:** Input → Noise injection → DDPM denoising → Classification

**Design tradeoffs:** The noise level t* must balance between obscuring adversarial perturbations and preserving image features for classification. Higher t* values provide better defense but may degrade clean accuracy. The choice of DDPM architecture affects both defense effectiveness and computational cost.

**Failure signatures:** Poor performance on both clean and adversarial examples indicates the noise level is too high. Low robust accuracy with high clean accuracy suggests insufficient noise injection. Catastrophic forgetting during DDPM training would manifest as inability to denoise effectively.

**First experiments:** 1) Verify DDPM can denoise corrupted versions of clean images back to near-original quality. 2) Test classification accuracy on images denoised from various noise levels (t*). 3) Evaluate robust accuracy against basic adversarial attacks with different t* values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal noise level (t*) for the diffusion defense to achieve the best balance between robustness and inference speed across different datasets and model architectures?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that t* = 0.04 was chosen for the PatchCamelyon dataset, but notes that a wide range of time steps resulted in similar accuracy and that there is room for optimization.
- Why unresolved: The optimal noise level likely depends on the specific dataset characteristics, model architecture, and computational constraints. The paper only explored a limited range of noise levels on one dataset.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying t* across multiple datasets with different properties (e.g., image complexity, class balance) and different model architectures, measuring both robust accuracy and inference speed.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the diffusion model be trained in conjunction with the classifier to improve both standard and robust accuracy simultaneously?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions this as a potential future direction, noting that training the classifier and diffusion model independently may not be optimal.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluated a pipeline where the diffusion model and classifier were trained separately. Joint training could potentially leverage shared information to improve defense effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing separate training versus joint training of the diffusion model and classifier, measuring both standard accuracy and robust accuracy against various attack types.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the diffusion defense perform against more sophisticated adaptive attacks that specifically target the diffusion purification process?
- Basis in paper: The paper used adaptive attacks with full knowledge of the defense but only tested against standard adversarial attacks within the L∞ norm ball.
- Why unresolved: The diffusion defense adds noise and then denoises, which creates opportunities for attacks that exploit this specific pipeline. The paper only tested standard adversarial attacks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with white-box attacks specifically designed to fool the diffusion + classifier pipeline, such as attacks that target the denoising process itself or attacks that exploit the stochastic nature of diffusion models.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the relationship between the amount of noise that can be added before the diffusion model fails to reconstruct relevant features and the inherent difficulty of the classification task?
- Basis in paper: The paper observed that for the PatchCamelyon dataset (which requires detecting small metastatic tissue regions), adding too much noise degraded performance, and discussed how this task's sensitivity to small changes might explain poor performance of other defenses.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explored this on one dataset and did not systematically investigate how task difficulty relates to the maximum effective noise level.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments across datasets with varying difficulty levels (e.g., fine-grained vs. coarse classification tasks), measuring the maximum noise level that preserves classification accuracy and analyzing how this threshold correlates with task difficulty metrics.

## Limitations
- Evaluation was conducted on a single dataset (PatchCamelyon), limiting generalizability to other domains
- Only tested against L∞ norm ball attacks, not exploring broader threat models
- Performance drop from 88% to 75% under attack suggests the defense is not perfect
- The optimal noise level (t*) may vary significantly across different datasets and tasks

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The core methodology of using DDPM reverse process for adversarial example purification is sound and technically described.
- **Medium confidence**: The comparative results against NoiseDefense and vanilla ResNet, though specific to the tested conditions.
- **Low confidence**: Generalizability to other datasets, attack types, and threat models beyond those tested.
