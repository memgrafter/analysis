# PushWorld: A benchmark for manipulation planning with tools and movable obstacles

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.10289
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10289
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: PushWorld benchmark evaluates manipulation planning with tools and movable obstacles, introducing RGD heuristic that achieves 69.1% success on hand-designed puzzles

## Executive Summary
PushWorld is a 2D grid-based benchmark for manipulation planning with tools and movable obstacles. The environment features rigid objects of varying shapes that can be pushed by an agent or by other objects, enabling tool use. Goals require pushing specific objects to target positions, with challenges including path planning, movable obstacles, and simultaneous multi-object manipulation. The paper evaluates classical planning and deep RL baselines, introducing a novel recursive graph distance (RGD) heuristic that improves planning efficiency.

## Method Summary
PushWorld provides a simplified 2D physics environment where objects can be pushed but not rotated, with immediate stopping after pushes. The benchmark includes 223 hand-designed puzzles and 2,000 procedurally generated Level 0 puzzles. Classical planners (FF, FD, LAMA, BFWS, FDSS, Saarplan) and deep RL algorithms (PPO, DQN) are evaluated. The novel RGD heuristic caches minimum-cost paths in domain transition graphs to achieve O(N²) complexity, and when combined with the novelty heuristic, creates the Novelty+RGD planner that outperforms all baselines on the hand-designed puzzles.

## Key Results
- RGD+Novelty solves 69.1% of 223 hand-designed puzzles, outperforming all classical baselines
- RGD+Novelty is 35x faster than FDSS (61.4% success) on Level 1 puzzles
- Deep RL (PPO, DQN) solves <7% of Level 1 puzzles due to sparse rewards and credit assignment challenges
- Human-level performance remains above the best AI algorithms, indicating benchmark difficulty

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Recursive Graph Distance (RGD) heuristic achieves O(N²) time complexity by caching minimum-cost paths in domain transition graphs.
- Mechanism: RGD computes shortest path lengths once between all object positions in domain transition graphs (DTGs), then reuses these cached lengths for subsequent state expansions, avoiding repeated O(N²) searches.
- Core assumption: The DTGs remain static during the search, allowing path length caching to be valid across expanded states.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "This formulation allows hRGD(s) to cache the shortest path lengths Lv(d, d′). Continuing with the example in Fig 3, the minimum-length path to move the agent to the red object requires O(N²) time to compute in the DTG for the agent's position... In a GBF search, expanded states can reuse the minimum-length paths from those computed while expanding preceding states."
  - [abstract]: "RGD improves upon the context-enhanced additive heuristic by caching minimum-cost paths in domain transition graphs, reducing planning time from O(N³) to O(N²) for certain puzzles."
- Break condition: If the state representation changes dynamically (e.g., objects gain new shapes during search), cached paths become invalid and the O(N²) guarantee fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Novelty heuristic promotes exploration of previously unseen substates, improving search efficiency in PushWorld.
- Mechanism: Novelty heuristic assigns higher priority to states containing novel combinations of object positions not seen in prior states, guiding the search toward unexplored regions of the state space.
- Core assumption: Novel substates are more likely to lead to solutions than revisiting previously explored configurations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "The novelty heuristic [Lipovetzky and Geffner, 2017] promotes exploration from states that contain previously unseen substates... We define the Novelty+RGD planner as a GBF search that prioritizes states using a lexicographic combination of heuristics: first prioritize states that minimize w(s), and whenever states have the same novelty, then prioritize states that minimize hRGD(s)."
  - [abstract]: "Combined with the novelty heuristic, RGD+Novelty solves 69.1% of 223 hand-designed puzzles."
- Break condition: In domains where solutions lie in well-trodden state space regions, novelty-driven exploration may waste time exploring unpromising novel states.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: PushWorld's simplified physics enables tractable planning while preserving core manipulation challenges.
- Mechanism: By restricting objects to rigid shapes, discrete positions, no rotation, and immediate stopping after pushes, PushWorld eliminates continuous dynamics complexity while maintaining challenges like movable obstacles and tool use.
- Core assumption: The simplified physics preserves the essential reasoning challenges of manipulation planning without introducing intractable continuous motion planning subproblems.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "PushWorld is a simplified two-dimensional physical environment... Objects have rigid shapes that occupy sets of discrete positions, which cannot intersect with the occupied positions of other objects. Objects can translate but cannot rotate... Objects have no momentum, so they immediately stop moving after the agent pushes them."
  - [abstract]: "PushWorld, an environment with simplistic physics that requires manipulation planning with both movable obstacles and tools."
- Break condition: If real-world manipulation tasks require reasoning about continuous dynamics (e.g., sliding friction, momentum), PushWorld's simplifications may fail to capture necessary planning complexity.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Domain Transition Graphs (DTGs)
  - Why needed here: DTGs represent the possible state transitions for each variable (object position) and are fundamental to computing both CEA and RGD heuristics.
  - Quick check question: What does an edge in a DTG represent, and how is it labeled in the CEA heuristic?

- Concept: Heuristic search with multiple heuristics
  - Why needed here: The Novelty+RGD planner combines two heuristics lexicographically, requiring understanding of how multiple heuristics interact in best-first search.
  - Quick check question: In lexicographic heuristic combination, which heuristic determines the primary ordering, and what happens when two states have equal values for the primary heuristic?

- Concept: Sparse reward reinforcement learning
  - Why needed here: PushWorld presents sparse reward challenges for RL agents, requiring understanding of exploration strategies and credit assignment in delayed-reward environments.
  - Quick check question: Why does PushWorld's reward structure make it more challenging for RL than environments with dense intermediate rewards?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: PushWorld environment -> PDDL generation -> Classical planners (FF, FD, LAMA, BFWS, FDSS, Saarplan) OR RL agents (PPO, DQN) -> Solution extraction
- Critical path: For classical planning: environment → PDDL generation → planner execution → solution extraction. For RL: environment → agent interaction → policy/value function updates → action selection.
- Design tradeoffs: PushWorld trades physical realism for computational tractability, enabling planning research while maintaining core manipulation challenges. The RGD heuristic trades some planning accuracy for significant speed improvements through path caching.
- Failure signatures: Classical planners failing to solve puzzles often indicate either search space explosion or inadequate heuristics. RL agents failing to learn typically indicate sparse reward issues or insufficient exploration.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run a simple Level 1 puzzle through all classical planners to establish baseline performance and identify which planners can solve basic cases.
  2. Implement and test the RGD heuristic on a small puzzle to verify path caching works correctly and provides speed improvements.
  3. Train PPO on a Level 0 puzzle set with dense rewards (modified environment) to establish whether the architecture can learn in easier conditions before tackling sparse reward challenges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do model-based reinforcement learning algorithms like MuZero compare to model-free algorithms on PushWorld?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest exploring model-based RL algorithms as a future direction in the conclusions section, noting they could discover new heuristics.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates model-free algorithms (PPO and DQN) and does not compare them to model-based approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison of MuZero or other model-based algorithms against the current baselines on the PushWorld benchmark.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of hierarchical planning on PushWorld performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention hierarchical planning as a potential future direction, suggesting it may be effective for decomposing PushWorld puzzles into subgoals.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not implement or evaluate any hierarchical planning approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing hierarchical planning algorithms on PushWorld and comparing their performance to flat planning approaches.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do humans solve PushWorld puzzles compared to AI algorithms?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that their best algorithm remains below human-level performance and suggest cognitive science analysis of human problem-solving as a future direction.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not include any human subject studies or cognitive science analysis of human problem-solving strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical study comparing human performance on PushWorld puzzles to AI algorithms, including analysis of human strategies and decision-making processes.

## Limitations

- The RGD heuristic's O(N²) complexity guarantee depends on static domain transition graphs during search
- Human-level performance comparisons are based on informal observations rather than systematic studies
- RL algorithms show limited success (<7%) on Level 1 puzzles, suggesting fundamental challenges with sparse rewards

## Confidence

- RGD heuristic computational improvements: **High**
- Novelty+RGD planner performance claims: **Medium**
- Claims about human-level performance gaps: **Low**
- RL algorithm limitations: **Medium**
