# Scaling Pareto-Efficient Decision Making Via Offline Multi-Objective RL

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.00567
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00567
- Reference count: 20
- This paper introduces D4MORL benchmark and PEDA algorithms for offline multi-objective RL, achieving near-optimal Pareto front approximation.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses offline multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL), where the goal is to learn preference-agnostic policies from fixed datasets of demonstrations by agents with known preferences. The authors introduce D4MORL, a benchmark dataset with 1.8 million trajectories across six MuJoCo environments, each featuring competing objectives. They propose Pareto-Efficient Decision Agents (PEDA), a family of offline MORL algorithms extending Decision Transformers and RvS to handle preference-conditioned, vector-valued returns. PEDA employs preference-and-return conditioning and learns to map preferences to appropriate return scales for stable generalization.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes Pareto-Efficient Decision Agents (PEDA), a family of offline MORL algorithms that extend Decision Transformers and RvS to handle preference-conditioned, vector-valued returns. PEDA uses preference-and-return conditioning by concatenating preference vectors to state, action, and return tokens. The model learns to map preferences to appropriate return scales for stable generalization. A linear regression model is trained to predict appropriate return-to-go values for each preference vector, improving performance and stability. The approach uses multi-dimensional return-to-go (RTG) rather than scalarized RTG, preserving objective-specific information through the network.

## Key Results
- PEDA variants achieve near-optimal hypervolume and sparsity scores on D4MORL, closely matching the behavioral policy's Pareto front.
- In MO-HalfCheetah, PEDA reaches an average hypervolume of 5.77×10^6 (vs. 5.79×10^6 for the expert policy).
- PEDA variants show strong Pareto-efficiency and closely approximate the behavioral policy's Pareto front across multiple environments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Preference-conditioned policies can generalize across the Pareto front using preference-and-return conditioning.
- Mechanism: PEDA concatenates ω to other tokens before any layers in MODT(P), enabling the model to learn mappings from preferences to appropriate return scales.
- Core assumption: Preferences and returns are correlated in the offline dataset such that a learned mapping exists from preferences to appropriate return targets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "PEDA employs preference-and-return conditioning and learns to map preferences to appropriate return scales for stable generalization."
  - [section 5.1] "We propose to concatenate ω to other tokens before any layers in MODT(P)."
- Break condition: If the dataset lacks sufficient coverage across the preference space, the learned mapping may not generalize to unseen preferences.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Multi-dimensional return-to-go (RTG) is more effective than scalarized RTG for MORL.
- Mechanism: PEDA uses vector-valued RTG for each objective rather than scalarizing with preferences, preserving objective-specific information through the network.
- Core assumption: The transformer/MLP architecture can effectively process and utilize multi-dimensional RTG vectors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] "Another unique challenge in the MORL setting concerns the scale of different objectives... we adopt a simple normalization scheme..."
  - [section I] "using multi-dimensional RTG enhances the performance of MODT(P)"
- Break condition: If objectives have vastly different scales or distributions, normalization may not adequately address the scale issue.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training on both expert and amateur datasets improves generalization and robustness.
- Mechanism: The amateur dataset provides diverse, suboptimal trajectories that expose the model to a wider range of behaviors and preferences.
- Core assumption: Suboptimal trajectories contain useful information for learning preference-conditioned policies.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1] "Amateur Dataset: As before, we first find the best reference policy in the policy ensemble. With a fixed probability p, we randomly perturb the actions of the reference policies."
  - [section 6.1] "PEDA variants still approach or even exceed the behavioral policy even when a considerable portion of data is suboptimal."
- Break condition: If the amateur dataset is too noisy or the perturbation rate is too high, it may degrade performance rather than improve it.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Dataset -> Preference-conditioned MODT(P)/MORVS(P) -> Linear regression model for return-to-go prediction -> Hypervolume/sparsity evaluation

- Critical path: 1) Prepare dataset with trajectories containing preferences and vector-valued returns 2) Train preference-conditioned policy using supervised learning loss 3) Use linear regression to map preferences to appropriate return-to-go values 4) Evaluate on test preferences using hypervolume and sparsity metrics

- Design tradeoffs: MODT(P) offers better performance but higher computational cost compared to MORVS(P). Using multi-dimensional RTG preserves more information but increases model complexity. Including amateur data improves robustness but may introduce noise.

- Failure signatures: Poor hypervolume scores indicate failure to approximate the Pareto front. High sparsity suggests dense coverage of the Pareto front but may come at the cost of dominated solutions. Out-of-distribution behavior when conditioning on extreme preferences.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train MODT(P) and MORVS(P) on High-H-Expert dataset and compare hypervolume scores
  2. Ablate the effect of preference conditioning by training baseline models without preferences
  3. Test generalization to unseen preferences by evaluating on test preferences outside the training distribution

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of preference sampling distribution (high, medium, low entropy) impact the Pareto efficiency and sparsity of learned policies in offline MORL?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces three preference sampling distributions with varying entropies (high, medium, low) and notes that the low-entropy distribution is expected to be the hardest due to its restricted coverage.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides results for the high-entropy dataset, leaving the performance of the learned policies under medium and low-entropy distributions unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results showing the hypervolume and sparsity metrics for learned policies under all three preference distributions, with a focus on the low-entropy distribution.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the proposed preference-and-return conditioning in PEDA compare to other methods of incorporating preference information in offline MORL algorithms?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces a novel preference-and-return conditioning mechanism in PEDA and mentions that naively adding preference tokens leads to weak correlation between preferences and predicted actions.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not compare the proposed conditioning mechanism with other potential methods, such as separate preference embeddings or attention mechanisms for preference conditioning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies comparing the performance of PEDA with different preference conditioning mechanisms, measuring hypervolume and sparsity metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of PEDA scale with the number of objectives in the MOMDP?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper introduces a dataset with environments having 2 and 3 objectives, but the results and analysis primarily focus on the 2-objective environments.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of PEDA's performance as the number of objectives increases, particularly for the 3-objective environment.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study of PEDA's performance on environments with varying numbers of objectives, including a detailed analysis of the 3-objective environment, measuring hypervolume and sparsity metrics.

## Limitations
- The proposed preference-and-return conditioning mechanism lacks theoretical grounding and extensive ablation studies.
- The effectiveness of using multi-dimensional RTG versus scalarized RTG is primarily supported by empirical evidence rather than theoretical analysis.
- The amateur dataset generation strategy may introduce noise that degrades performance in certain scenarios.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: PEDA's ability to approximate the Pareto front on D4MORL environments is well-supported by quantitative metrics.
- **Medium confidence**: The claim that preference-and-return conditioning enables stable generalization across preferences requires further validation on more diverse environments.
- **Medium confidence**: The superiority of multi-dimensional RTG over scalarized RTG needs additional ablation studies to confirm.
