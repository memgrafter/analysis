# Higher Order Gauge Equivariant CNNs on Riemannian Manifolds and Applications

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16657
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16657
- Reference count: 40
- Higher-order gauge-equivariant convolutions (GEVNet) achieve better performance with fewer parameters than first-order networks on spherical data and neuroimaging tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a higher-order generalization of gauge-equivariant convolutional networks on Riemannian manifolds, called GEVNets, which can model spatially extended nonlinear interactions while preserving global isometry equivariance. The authors prove theoretical results regarding the equivariance and construction of these higher-order convolutions, and implement them on the sphere for computer vision and neuroimaging applications. Experiments on spherical MNIST, atomic energy prediction, and classification of neurodegenerative disorders (AD, DLB, PD) from diffusion MRI show that GEVNets are more parameter-efficient than first-order gauge-equivariant networks, achieving comparable or better performance with fewer parameters. The compound architecture (CKN + GEVNet) for the neuroimaging task exploits both intra-voxel and inter-voxel information, leading to improved classification accuracy over individual components.

## Method Summary
The paper extends gauge-equivariant convolutional networks to higher orders using a Volterra series expansion on Riemannian manifolds. The key insight is that second-order convolutions can be parameterized efficiently using tensor products of first-order solutions, reducing the number of learnable parameters while maintaining expressivity. The method is implemented on the sphere (S²) using a bandlimit ℓ=1 (ρ₀⊕ρ₁ features) and tested on three tasks: spherical MNIST classification, QM7 atomic energy regression, and classification of neurodegenerative disorders from diffusion MRI data. The compound architecture combines a Cross Kernel Network (CKN) for inter-voxel feature extraction with GEVNet for intra-voxel feature extraction on the sphere.

## Key Results
- GEVNet achieves better performance than first-order GENet on spherical MNIST with fewer parameters, particularly for the NR/NR task.
- On the QM7 dataset, GEVNet achieves comparable or better MAE than GENet with similar or fewer parameters.
- For neuroimaging classification, the compound CKN + GEVNet architecture outperforms individual components and first-order alternatives, with CKN2 + GEVNet achieving the best overall performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Higher-order gauge equivariant convolutions (GEVNet) model spatially extended nonlinear interactions more efficiently than first-order convolutions. By extending the Volterra series to the gauge-equivariant setting, GEVNet captures second-order cross-correlations (pairwise interactions) within the receptive field while preserving global isometry equivariance. The underlying data exhibits spatially extended nonlinear interactions that are not captured by pointwise nonlinearities in first-order networks.

### Mechanism 2
Parameter efficiency is achieved by learning scalar coefficients over a basis of second-order steerable kernels instead of full second-order tensors. Proposition 3.5 shows that second-order SO(2)-steerable kernels can be constructed from first-order solutions via tensor products, reducing the number of learnable parameters while maintaining expressivity. A finite basis of second-order steerable kernels can approximate the full space of second-order interactions needed for the task.

### Mechanism 3
The compound architecture (CKN + GEVNet) exploits both intra-voxel and inter-voxel information, leading to improved classification performance. CKN extracts macro-architectural features across voxels using polynomial kernels, while GEVNet extracts micro-architectural features within each voxel using gauge-equivariant convolutions on the sphere. The neuroimaging task benefits from features at both the voxel level (intra-voxel diffusion patterns) and the whole-brain level (inter-voxel relationships).

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Gauge equivariance on Riemannian manifolds
  - Why needed here: Ensures the network respects the intrinsic geometry of the data and is equivariant to global isometries (e.g., rotations on the sphere)
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between gauge equivariance and global isometry equivariance, and why is this distinction important for spherical CNNs?

- **Concept:** Higher-order Volterra expansions
  - Why needed here: Provides the mathematical foundation for modeling nonlinear interactions beyond first-order convolutions
  - Quick check question: How does a second-order Volterra expansion differ from a first-order convolution in terms of the features it can capture?

- **Concept:** Steerable kernels and representation theory
  - Why needed here: Enables the construction of kernels that transform correctly under gauge transformations, ensuring equivariance
  - Quick check question: What is the steerability constraint for second-order kernels, and how does it relate to the tensor product of first-order kernels?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Diffusion MRI data (96 gradient directions) -> CKN (inter-voxel features) -> Reshape -> GEVNet (intra-voxel features on S²) -> Global pooling -> FC -> Classification

- **Critical path:** Data → CKN (inter-voxel) → Reshape → GEVNet (intra-voxel) → Global pooling → FC → Classification

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Higher-order convolutions vs. parameter count: GEVNet achieves better performance with fewer parameters than first-order alternatives
  - Bandlimit choice (ℓ=1): Balances computational efficiency with expressivity; higher bandlimits may be needed for more complex data
  - Basis size for second-order kernels: Larger bases increase expressivity but also parameter count and computational cost

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Overfitting: Check if test error variance is high (as noted in the paper for GEVNet vs. GENet)
  - Underfitting: If the basis of second-order kernels is too small to capture the required interactions
  - Poor equivariance: If the steerability constraint is violated (should not happen with correct implementation)

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Spherical MNIST ablation:** Compare GEVNet vs. GENet with varying parameter counts on NR/NR task to verify parameter efficiency
  2. **QM7 regression:** Test GEVNet on atomic energy prediction to verify performance on non-classification tasks
  3. **Neuroimaging compound architecture:** Implement CKN2 + GEVNet and compare against ablated versions (CKN2 alone, GEVNet alone, CKN1 + DeepSphere) on AD/DLB classification

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What causes the increased variance in test error observed in higher-order GEVNets compared to first-order networks? The authors note this as a focus for future work but do not investigate the root causes. This could be resolved through controlled experiments isolating different components and theoretical analysis of stability properties.

### Open Question 2
Is there a systematic relationship between the order of convolutions and the underlying data's structure that could guide architectural choices? The authors hypothesize about this but provide no empirical validation. Extensive experiments across datasets with varying degrees of nonlinearity, coupled with analysis of learned feature representations, would establish whether performance plateaus systematically correlate with data structure.

### Open Question 3
How does the parameter efficiency of GEVNets scale with task complexity and dataset size? The paper demonstrates efficiency on specific tasks but does not explore scaling properties. Systematic scaling studies varying dataset size, input dimensionality, and task complexity would reveal generalizability limits.

## Limitations

- The parameterization strategy for second-order steerable kernels lacks direct empirical validation against full tensor parameterization across diverse tasks.
- Performance gains may be partially attributed to neuroimaging-specific data characteristics rather than general superiority of the compound architecture.
- Computational efficiency claims are based on parameter counts but lack direct wall-clock time comparisons with first-order alternatives.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence:** The theoretical framework for higher-order gauge equivariant convolutions and their construction via Volterra series is mathematically sound and well-established in the literature.
- **Medium Confidence:** The empirical performance improvements of GEVNet over first-order alternatives are demonstrated but may be task-specific.
- **Medium Confidence:** The compound architecture's design principle (exploiting both intra-voxel and inter-voxel information) is intuitive and theoretically justified.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study on basis size:** Systematically vary the size of the second-order kernel basis in GEVNet across multiple tasks to empirically validate the relationship between basis cardinality, parameter count, and model performance.

2. **Cross-dataset generalization:** Test the compound CKN + GEVNet architecture on non-neuroimaging datasets with known spatially extended nonlinear interactions (e.g., spherical environmental data, molecular properties) to assess generalizability beyond the current application domain.

3. **Computational efficiency benchmarking:** Compare wall-clock training and inference times for GEVNet versus first-order alternatives across different hardware configurations, accounting for both parameter count and operation complexity.
