# Controllable Lexical Simplification for English

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02900
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02900
- Reference count: 13
- Key outcome: ConLS performs comparably to LSBert (state-of-the-art) using control tokens with T5, showing improved potential and recall at higher K values

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ConLS, the first controllable lexical simplification system that uses fine-tuned T5 with control tokens to guide substitution generation and ranking. The system achieves comparable performance to LSBert (current state-of-the-art) on three evaluation datasets, with improved potential and recall at higher K values, and better MAP@K at K=4,5,10. The control tokens (word length ratio, word rank, and candidate ranking) are embedded in training data to provide explicit relational signals between complex and simple words.

## Method Summary
ConLS fine-tunes T5-Large on the TSAR-EN dataset (386 instances) using control tokens embedded in source sentences. The control tokens include word length ratio (WL), word rank (WR) based on inverse frequency from FastText, and candidate ranking (CR) with fixed values (1.00, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25, 0.00). At inference, beam search (size 15) generates candidates, which are filtered to remove duplicates and complex word matches, yielding ~10 high-quality candidates. Hyperparameter search uses Optuna to optimize token values on a validation set.

## Key Results
- ConLS performs comparably to LSBert on LexMTurk, BenchLS, and NNSeval datasets
- Improved potential and recall at higher K values (K=3,5,10) compared to LSBert
- Better MAP@K at K=4,5,10 compared to LSBert
- Control tokens significantly improve performance over no-token baseline

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Control tokens embedded in training data provide explicit relational signals between complex and simple words
- Mechanism: By prepending word length ratio, word rank, and candidate ranking values to source sentences during training, the model learns to map these numeric relationships to appropriate substitution outputs
- Core assumption: The numeric ratios extracted from training data contain meaningful information about word complexity relationships

- Claim: Fine-tuning T5 with control tokens achieves comparable or better performance than complex pipeline systems
- Mechanism: The T5 model learns to generate simplified candidates conditioned on control tokens while ranking them appropriately, eliminating need for separate generation/filtering components
- Core assumption: T5's sequence-to-sequence architecture can effectively learn both generation and ranking tasks simultaneously when provided with control signals
- Evidence anchors:

  - [section]: "Following recent works... we are inspired to apply a similar approach in lexical simplification task"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input pipeline (sentence + complex word + control tokens) → T5 model → Beam search output → Filtering → Evaluation metrics
- Critical path: Training data preprocessing → T5 fine-tuning → Inference with control tokens → Candidate ranking → Evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Simpler single-model approach vs. complex multi-component pipelines (like LSBert); control token expressiveness vs. model complexity
- Failure signatures: Poor Accuracy@1 indicates control tokens not effectively guiding generation; low Potential@K suggests beam search issues; MAP@K problems indicate ranking failures
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train baseline model without control tokens to establish performance floor
  2. Test with individual control tokens (WL only, WR only, CR only) to measure contribution
  3. Vary beam search size and observe impact on candidate diversity and quality

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would ConLS perform on datasets with different annotation schemes or linguistic characteristics (e.g., non-native speakers, children, or other domains)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that the current evaluation is limited to English datasets annotated by native speakers, and suggests potential difficulties in adapting to non-English languages due to resource limitations.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates ConLS on English datasets with native speaker annotations. There is no empirical evidence of how the model would perform on datasets with different linguistic characteristics or in other languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating ConLS on diverse datasets with different annotation schemes (e.g., non-native speakers, children, or other languages) and comparing the results to LSBert and other state-of-the-art models would provide evidence of the model's generalizability and limitations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would ConLS perform with larger training datasets or higher-quality training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly mentions the limitations of the small training dataset (386 instances) and suggests that training with larger datasets would be recommended to create better models. It also mentions the potential for improving the dataset quality by including substitutions not reported by annotators.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates ConLS on the TSAR-EN dataset, which has a limited number of instances. There is no empirical evidence of how the model would perform with larger or higher-quality training data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training ConLS on larger datasets with more instances and higher-quality annotations, and comparing the results to the current model, would provide evidence of the impact of training data size and quality on the model's performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How would ConLS perform with a custom model to predict the best control token values for each instance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions the plan to build a custom model to predict the best control token values from a given input instance, as humans usually select the best candidate based on context.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates ConLS with fixed control token values determined through a search on the validation set. There is no empirical evidence of how the model would perform with dynamically predicted control token values.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating a custom model to predict the best control token values for each instance, and comparing the results to the current model with fixed control token values, would provide evidence of the impact of dynamic control token prediction on the model's performance.

## Limitations

- Small training dataset (386 instances) limits model generalization and creates overfitting risks
- Limited evaluation scope - only tested on English datasets with native speaker annotations
- Control token effectiveness relies on assumptions about complexity relationships that are not empirically validated

## Confidence

**High confidence** in the basic methodology - the use of T5 fine-tuning with control tokens is technically sound and the evaluation framework is appropriate for the task.

**Medium confidence** in the comparative performance claims - while the paper shows ConLS performs comparably to LSBert, the small dataset size and limited hyperparameter exploration make it difficult to determine if this represents a true performance plateau or could be improved with more training data and optimization.

**Low confidence** in the scalability and robustness claims - the system's behavior on out-of-distribution examples, truly complex vocabulary, or non-native speaker errors is not explored, and the control token approach may not generalize well beyond the training distribution.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study with human evaluation**: Run a human judgment study comparing ConLS outputs with LSBert and the no-token baseline on a held-out test set, focusing on actual simplification quality rather than automated metrics.

2. **Control token sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary individual control token values (WL, WR, CR) during inference on a validation set to determine which tokens contribute most to performance and whether the learned relationships match human intuitions about word complexity.

3. **Cross-dataset generalization test**: Evaluate ConLS on LS datasets from other languages or domains (e.g., biomedical LS) to assess whether the control token approach generalizes beyond the English TSAR-EN training data.
