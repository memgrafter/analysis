# Answering Unanswered Questions through Semantic Reformulations in Spoken QA

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.17393
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17393
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: SURF model improves answer rates by up to 24% on 1M previously unanswered spoken QA questions

## Executive Summary
Spoken QA in voice assistants often fails due to disfluencies, informal syntax, and overly specific questions. This work analyzes millions of failed user queries and identifies key challenges: lexical gaps, complex syntactic structure, polar propositions, and high specificity. SURF, a semantic reformulation model, supports three linguistically-grounded operations—repair, root transformation, and generalization—to rewrite questions for better answerability. The model improves answer rates by up to 24% on 1M previously unanswered questions, with 75% of reformulated queries receiving relevant answers. Live deployment in a major voice assistant demonstrates significant real-world impact.

## Method Summary
The SURF model is a seq2seq Transformer (BART) trained to reformulate spoken QA questions using three operators: repair (REP) fixes lexical and syntactic issues, root transformation (ROO) changes the question type to enable answer retrieval, and generalization (GEN) reduces specificity to broaden matching. The model is pre-trained on weakly supervised pairs from MQR and fine-tuned on manually annotated data. At inference, one operator is applied per question based on its type, and reformulated questions are sent to the QA backend. Evaluation combines intrinsic reformulation accuracy and extrinsic answer rate and relevance metrics.

## Key Results
- SURF improves answer rates by up to 24% on 1M previously unanswered questions
- 75% of reformulated questions receive relevant answers to the original intent
- Live deployment in a major voice assistant shows significant real-world impact
- Model supports three reformulation operators—repair, root transformation, and generalization

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Spoken QA queries often fail due to disfluencies, informal syntax, and overly specific questions that existing systems cannot parse or match. SURF addresses this by reformulating questions semantically before querying the QA backend, using linguistically motivated operations that target the root causes of failure. By rewriting questions to be more standard, less specific, and aligned with answerable types, the model enables better matching and retrieval without retraining the underlying QA system.

## Foundational Learning
- **Seq2seq reformulation**: Rewriting questions to improve answerability by altering syntax or semantics; needed to adapt queries to QA backend capabilities.
- **Question type classification**: Identifying root, polar, open, request, and other types; needed to select the correct reformulation operator.
- **Weak supervision**: Using MQR to generate large-scale training pairs; needed to scale model pre-training.
- **Human annotation for fine-tuning**: Manual labeling of question-reformulation pairs; needed to ensure high-quality operator outputs.
- **Answer relevance scoring**: Judging if answers match original intent (0-2 scale); needed for extrinsic evaluation.
- **Operator selection**: Choosing REP, ROO, or GEN per question type; needed for targeted reformulation.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: User Query → Operator Selection → SURF Reformulation → QA Backend → Answer

**Critical path**: Operator selection → SURF reformulation → QA backend answer retrieval

**Design tradeoffs**: Sequential operator application (latency, cascading errors) vs. joint application (complexity, latency); BART vs. larger LLMs (performance vs. deployment constraints).

**Failure signatures**: Reformulated questions change original intent; cascading errors from sequential operator application; latency increases from complex reformulations.

**First experiments**:
1. Classify a sample of unanswered queries by type and manually apply REP, ROO, or GEN; measure if reformulation would yield an answer.
2. Train a small seq2seq model on a public QA dataset and evaluate reformulation accuracy on a held-out set.
3. Apply reformulations to a public QA backend and measure answer rate and relevance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**: How do the reformulation operations (REP, ROO, GEN) interact when applied sequentially versus jointly? The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and suggests future work on joint application but does not explore it.

**Open Question 2**: How would SURF perform when fine-tuned on large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4 or ChatGPT, and what are the trade-offs? The paper used BART and did not experiment with LLMs due to latency and data constraints.

**Open Question 3**: How can the reformulation model be adapted for multilingual use, and what are the challenges in cross-lingual transfer? The paper only considers English and notes that extending to other languages is an open research area.

## Limitations
- Exact question distribution and operator selection criteria are confidential
- Reformulation may introduce cascading errors when operators are applied sequentially
- Only evaluated on English-language questions; multilingual extension is open research

## Confidence
- Medium: Model efficacy (intrinsic accuracy and extrinsic answer rates) is well supported but depends on proprietary dataset quality
- High: Analysis of reformulation challenges and design of linguistically motivated operators are clearly validated
- Low: Claims about cross-compatibility across QA systems are asserted but not independently validated

## Next Checks
1. Recreate the three reformulation operators on a public spoken QA dataset and evaluate intrinsic accuracy with human judges.
2. Conduct an extrinsic evaluation measuring answer rate and relevance after reformulation on the same public dataset, comparing sequential vs. joint operator application.
3. Run a live A/B test in a different voice assistant platform, applying reformulated questions to multiple backend QA systems to validate cross-system compatibility.
