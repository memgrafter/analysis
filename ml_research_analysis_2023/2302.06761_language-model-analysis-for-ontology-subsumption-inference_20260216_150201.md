# Language Model Analysis for Ontology Subsumption Inference

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.06761
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06761
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: LMs can learn ontology subsumption inference with few training examples, though performance depends heavily on prompt design

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces OntoLAMA, a probing task designed to evaluate whether language models can infer subsumption axioms from OWL ontologies. The authors formulate the problem as a premise-hypothesis inference task, where ontology concepts are verbalized into natural language and mapped to entailment or non-entailment labels. The study examines four ontologies (Schema.org, DOID, FoodOn, GO) and compares ontology subsumption performance against traditional NLI benchmarks, finding that LMs perform worse on ontology tasks in zero-shot settings but improve significantly with minimal training data.

## Method Summary
The OntoLAMA framework transforms OWL subsumption axioms into premise-hypothesis pairs by verbalizing concepts into natural language. Positive samples are extracted directly from entailed axioms, while negative samples are selected using "assumed disjointness" heuristics to avoid logical conflicts. The evaluation supports both atomic and complex concept patterns, with complex patterns representing more challenging inference tasks. Experiments are conducted across multiple ontologies and compared against MNLI, with ablation studies using domain-specific biomedical language models to assess the impact of specialized pretraining.

## Key Results
- LMs perform worse on ontology subsumption than traditional NLI in zero-shot settings
- Performance improves significantly with a small number of training examples
- Complex concept patterns yield harder inference tasks than atomic patterns
- Domain-specific biomedical LMs show improved performance on biomedical ontologies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by leveraging language models' ability to learn semantic relationships through few-shot learning. By framing ontology subsumption as a natural language inference task, the approach exploits the LM's pretraining on vast amounts of text to recognize hierarchical relationships between concepts. The verbalization process bridges formal ontology representations with the distributional patterns LMs have learned during pretraining, allowing them to generalize from limited examples.

## Foundational Learning
- **OWL EL profile**: Why needed - The tractable fragment of OWL that supports efficient reasoning; quick check - Verify the ontologies used conform to EL restrictions
- **Subsumption axioms**: Why needed - Core logical relationship expressing "is-a" hierarchies in ontologies; quick check - Confirm axiom extraction correctly captures intended entailments
- **Natural Language Inference**: Why needed - Framework for testing semantic relationships between premises and hypotheses; quick check - Validate label mapping aligns with logical entailment
- **Few-shot learning**: Why needed - Enables effective learning from limited examples; quick check - Measure performance curves with varying training set sizes
- **Assumed disjointness**: Why needed - Heuristic for selecting logically negative examples; quick check - Assess false positive rate in negative sampling
- **Concept verbalization**: Why needed - Translates formal ontology syntax to natural language; quick check - Evaluate semantic preservation through human validation

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**
OntoLAMA Pipeline: OWL Axioms -> Verbalization Engine -> Premise-Hypothesis Pairs -> LM Inference -> Entailment Classification

**Critical Path:**
Axiom extraction → Verbalization → Pair generation → Model inference → Performance evaluation

**Design Tradeoffs:**
The choice between atomic and complex patterns represents a key tradeoff between task simplicity and expressiveness. Atomic patterns are easier for LMs to handle but may not capture the full complexity of ontology reasoning, while complex patterns better represent real-world ontology structure but increase task difficulty.

**Failure Signatures:**
Poor performance may stem from inadequate verbalization quality, incorrect negative sampling through assumed disjointness, or LMs failing to capture domain-specific terminology. The framework may also struggle with ontologies outside the EL profile or those with non-standard naming conventions.

**First Experiments:**
1. Evaluate zero-shot performance across all four ontologies to establish baseline capabilities
2. Test few-shot learning with varying numbers of training examples to measure learning curves
3. Compare atomic versus complex pattern performance to assess task difficulty gradients

## Limitations
- The evaluation framework relies on heuristic-based negative sampling that may introduce label noise
- Verbalization from formal axioms to natural language may cause semantic drift affecting performance
- The study focuses exclusively on EL profile ontologies, limiting generalizability to more expressive languages
- Benchmark construction depends on existing ontology quality, potentially biasing results toward well-structured ontologies

## Confidence

**High confidence:**
- LMs require few training examples to achieve good performance on subsumption inference
- Complex concept patterns yield harder inference tasks than atomic patterns
- Domain-specific biomedical LMs show improved performance on biomedical ontologies

**Medium confidence:**
- LMs perform worse on ontology subsumption than traditional NLI benchmarks
- Assumed disjointness heuristics are effective for negative sampling

**Low confidence:**
- Generalizability beyond EL ontologies
- Extent to which OntoLAMA captures true semantic understanding versus pattern matching

## Next Checks
1. Validate negative sample quality by conducting manual annotation of a subset of negative examples to assess the accuracy of assumed disjointness heuristics
2. Perform cross-ontology generalization tests by training on one ontology and evaluating on another to assess true semantic understanding versus memorization
3. Compare performance using gold-standard verbalizations (when available) versus automated verbalization to isolate the impact of language generation quality on inference performance
