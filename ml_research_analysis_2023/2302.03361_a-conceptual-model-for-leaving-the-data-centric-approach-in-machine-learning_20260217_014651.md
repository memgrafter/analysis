# A conceptual model for leaving the data-centric approach in machine learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03361
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03361
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: A high-level conceptual framework that unifies diverse approaches to incorporating external knowledge into machine learning models

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a conceptual model that frames machine learning training as a process combining data, a worldview defining how things should be, and constraints derived from that worldview. The model enables unification of approaches like physics-informed ML and fair ML under a common framework despite their different applications and methods. By explicitly separating data, worldview, and constraints as distinct conceptual components, the framework allows practitioners to more intentionally design systems that incorporate external requirements beyond pure data-driven learning.

## Method Summary
The paper develops a conceptual model where machine learning training is viewed as a process involving four key components: a dataset (D), a worldview (W) representing domain-specific knowledge about how things should be, constraints (C) derived from that worldview, and the ML model (M) itself. The model provides a high-level framework for understanding how external knowledge can be incorporated into ML systems, showing that seemingly different domains like physics and fairness can be described using the same conceptual structure.

## Key Results
- The conceptual model provides a unified language for describing external constraints across different ML domains
- Physics-informed ML and fair ML can both be described using the same W-D-C-M framework
- The model enables cross-domain knowledge transfer by revealing shared underlying structures

- Core assumption: Different domains share a common underlying structure where external requirements must be translated into constraints that can be incorporated into ML models, regardless of the specific application area.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Supervised machine learning fundamentals (training data, model architecture, loss functions, optimization)
  - Why needed here: The paper assumes familiarity with basic ML concepts but applies them in a non-standard way by incorporating external constraints. Understanding how standard ML works is essential to grasp how constraint incorporation differs.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between a hard constraint (architectural choice) and a soft constraint (regularization) in ML training?

- Concept: Domain-specific knowledge (physics laws, ethical principles, psychological models)
  - Why needed here: The paper discusses how different domains define their worldviews (W) - physics laws for PIML, ethical principles for fair ML, psychological models for psychology-informed recommender systems. Understanding these domains is crucial for applying the conceptual model.
  - Quick check question: How would you mathematically represent the conservation of energy as a constraint in an ML model?

- Concept: Abstract modeling and conceptual frameworks
  - Why needed here: The paper's main contribution is a high-level conceptual model rather than a technical algorithm. Understanding how to work with abstract models and translate between conceptual frameworks and technical implementations is essential.
  - Quick check question: What are the advantages and disadvantages of working with high-level conceptual models versus domain-specific technical solutions?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data component (𝐷): Raw training data, preprocessing steps
  - Worldview component (𝑊): Domain-specific knowledge about how things should be
  - Constraint translation layer: Converts 𝑊 into 𝑄 specific to the ML model and data
  - ML model component (𝑀): Standard ML model architecture with constraint incorporation
  - Constrained model output (𝑀𝐶): Final model that satisfies both data and worldview requirements

- Critical path:
  1. Define worldview (𝑊) clearly and independently of data
  2. Translate worldview into domain-specific constraints (𝐶)
  3. Incorporate constraints into ML model architecture or training process
  4. Validate that constrained model (𝑀𝐶) satisfies worldview requirements

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Hard vs. soft constraints: Hard constraints guarantee adherence but may be harder to implement; soft constraints are easier but provide no guarantees
  - Model complexity vs. constraint satisfaction: More complex models may better incorporate constraints but risk overfitting
  - Domain generality vs. specificity: More general approaches enable cross-domain transfer but may be less effective for specific problems

- Failure signatures:
  - Model performance degrades significantly when constraints are added (constraint conflicts with data)
  - Constraints are difficult to translate into mathematical form (worldview not quantifiable)
  - Different fairness definitions or physical laws conflict with each other (contradictory constraints)
  - Model cannot learn from data while satisfying constraints (infeasible optimization problem)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a simple physics-informed neural network where conservation laws are added as soft constraints in the loss function, then as hard constraints through architecture design
  2. Apply a fairness constraint (e.g., demographic parity) to a standard classification model and compare performance with and without the constraint
  3. Create a toy problem where two different worldviews (e.g., two different fairness definitions) are both expressed as constraints and test what happens when they conflict

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we systematically map different fairness definitions (𝑊) to appropriate constraint formulations (𝐶) across diverse ML architectures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that different fairness definitions usually contradict each other and that the exact quantitative definition of fairness cannot be independent of the used ML model
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the mapping problem exists but doesn't provide a systematic framework for translating ethical worldviews into model-specific constraints
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive taxonomy showing how specific fairness metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equal opportunity) map to architectural constraints (e.g., regularization terms, architectural modifications) across different ML model types

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the trade-offs between hard constraints (architectural choices) and soft constraints (regularization) when incorporating external knowledge?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions both hard constraints (architectural choices) and soft constraints (regularization) but doesn't analyze their relative effectiveness
- Why unresolved: The paper presents these as different approaches without empirical comparison of their performance, computational efficiency, or applicability to different domains
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies showing performance, computational cost, and generalization differences between hard and soft constraint approaches across multiple domains

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we validate that a constrained ML model (𝑀𝐶) actually satisfies the intended worldview (𝑊) rather than just optimizing the constraints (𝐶)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper describes the process from worldview to constraints to model but doesn't address verification that the final model truly embodies the intended ethical or physical principles
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the conceptual mapping but doesn't discuss validation methodologies to ensure alignment between the abstract worldview and the concrete implementation
- What evidence would resolve it: Validation frameworks that test whether constrained models maintain intended properties under distribution shifts, adversarial attacks, or in previously unseen scenarios

## Limitations
- The framework remains conceptual without specific implementation guidelines or algorithms for translating worldviews into constraints
- No empirical validation across a broader range of domains beyond the two examples provided
- The model does not address how to handle conflicting constraints from different worldviews or how to prioritize among them

## Confidence
- High confidence: The conceptual framework's logical consistency and applicability to physics-informed ML and fair ML domains
- Medium confidence: The model's ability to enable cross-domain knowledge transfer
- Low confidence: The framework's applicability to domains where worldviews are difficult to formalize or where multiple conflicting worldviews exist simultaneously
