# Finding the SWEET Spot: Analysis and Improvement of Adaptive Inference in Low Resource Settings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02307
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02307
- Reference count: 33
- This work analyzes and improves the speed-accuracy trade-off in adaptive inference for NLP models under low-resource settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes the performance trade-offs between Early-Exit and Multi-Model adaptive inference approaches in low-resource NLP settings. The authors identify that Early-Exit models suffer from conflicting gradient updates during training, causing individual classifiers to underperform by 2.3% compared to Multi-Model counterparts. To address this, they propose SWEET, a fine-tuning method that assigns exclusive weights to each classifier, avoiding gradient conflicts while maintaining the computational benefits of Early-Exit.

## Method Summary
The paper compares three adaptive inference approaches: Early-Exit (shared backbone with multiple classifiers), Multi-Model (separate models for each classifier), and SWEET (modified Early-Exit with gradient truncation). All methods use BERT and DeBERTa BASE/LARGE models fine-tuned on seven GLUE tasks with limited training data (6K examples per task). SWEET resolves gradient conflicts by having each classifier update only parameters up to the previous classifier during training, while maintaining computational reuse during inference.

## Key Results
- Early-Exit classifiers perform 2.3% worse than Multi-Model counterparts due to conflicting gradient updates
- Early-Exit maintains better overall speed-accuracy curves than Multi-Model due to sequential overhead
- SWEET individual classifiers outperform Early-Exit ones by 1.1% on average, particularly improving early classifiers
- SWEET achieves better speed-accuracy curves than both baselines at fast inference speeds

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Early-Exit classifiers suffer from conflicting gradient updates because their shared weights receive gradient signals from multiple classifiers simultaneously during training.
- Mechanism: During backpropagation, the loss from each classifier in an Early-Exit model updates not only its own parameters but also shared layers. These overlapping gradient updates can point in different directions, causing optimization instability and reducing the performance of early classifiers.
- Core assumption: The optimization of one classifier's loss is not compatible with the optimization of another classifier's loss when they share model parameters.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Early-Exit suffers from conflicting gradient updates during training, resulting in individual classifiers performing 2.3% worse than Multi-Model counterparts on average."
  - [section 3.1] "We show that this leads to a decrease in performance of individual Early-Exit classifiers compared to corresponding Multi-Model classifiers (2.3% gap on average)."
  - [corpus] Weak correlation with other works on gradient interference in multi-task learning; early-exit specific conflicting gradients are less studied.
- Break condition: If the backbone model becomes shallow enough that the gradient updates from different classifiers naturally align, or if a specialized optimization algorithm mitigates the conflicts.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: SWEET resolves conflicting gradients by assigning each classifier exclusive control over a subset of model weights, preventing overlapping gradient updates.
- Mechanism: Each classifier's loss is truncated at the layer preceding the next classifier, so during backpropagation only that classifier's parameters are updated. This eliminates conflicting gradient signals and allows each classifier to optimize independently.
- Core assumption: Limiting a classifier's influence to a subset of parameters during training will not severely harm its ability to make accurate predictions, especially if early computations are reused during inference.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "SWEET achieves a better speed-accuracy curve than both baselines at fast inference speeds while maintaining comparable performance to Early-Exit at slower speeds."
  - [section 4.1] "In SWEET, each Early-Exit classifier only updates the parameters of layers preceding it up to the previous classifier."
  - [corpus] Minimal direct evidence; related ideas exist in multi-task learning where gradient conflicts are mitigated by layer-wise specialization.
- Break condition: If later classifiers require access to early layer parameters that have been optimized for earlier classifiers, leading to degraded performance at slow speeds.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The speed-accuracy tradeoff favors Early-Exit over Multi-Model at slower speeds because Early-Exit reuses early computations, while Multi-Model incurs sequential overhead.
- Mechanism: In Multi-Model, each instance that reaches a later classifier must run all previous models sequentially, discarding intermediate results. In Early-Exit, once early layers are computed, they are reused by subsequent classifiers, reducing total computation for hard instances.
- Core assumption: The overhead of running multiple independent models sequentially outweighs the benefit of having independently optimized classifiers when many instances require deeper processing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Despite this gap, Early-Exit maintains better overall speed-accuracy curves due to Multi-Model's sequential overhead."
  - [section 3.3] "We find that each method dominates performance in a different region: Multi-Model outperforms Early-Exit at fast inference speeds, while Early-Exit is better at slow speeds."
  - [corpus] Some overlap with works on cascade inference and model cascading, but direct comparison of sequential overhead in adaptive inference is limited.
- Break condition: If computational overhead of sequential model execution is reduced (e.g., through parallel execution or model compression), the advantage may shift to Multi-Model.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Multi-task learning and gradient interference
  - Why needed here: Understanding how shared parameters affect optimization in models with multiple objectives (classifiers) is key to grasping why Early-Exit suffers from conflicting gradients.
  - Quick check question: If two tasks share a common feature extractor, what problem can arise when their gradients conflict during training?

- Concept: Adaptive inference and early exiting strategies
  - Why needed here: Knowing how adaptive inference reduces computational cost by exiting early on easy samples is essential for interpreting the speed-accuracy tradeoff results.
  - Quick check question: In a model with three classifiers, what happens to inference time if 80% of samples exit at the first classifier?

- Concept: Loss truncation and parameter isolation
  - Why needed here: SWEET's core innovation relies on stopping gradient flow beyond a certain layer for each classifier; understanding this requires familiarity with custom backpropagation patterns.
  - Quick check question: How does truncating loss signals at a certain layer affect the gradient updates for deeper layers?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Backbone model (BERT/DeBERTa) with shared layers -> Multiple classification heads inserted after intermediate layers -> Loss functions for each classifier -> Gradient truncation logic (SWEET only) -> Confidence-based or learning-to-exit decision modules

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass through shared layers until exit decision
  2. Loss computation for each classifier that saw the sample
  3. Backward pass with gradient truncation (SWEET) or full pass (standard)
  4. Parameter update with optimizer

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Early-Exit: Memory efficient, reuses computations, suffers from gradient conflicts
  - Multi-Model: Independent optimization, higher inference overhead, easier to extend
  - SWEET: Resolves gradient conflicts, maintains Early-Exit benefits, may limit later classifier capacity

- Failure signatures:
  - Early-Exit: Poor performance on early classifiers, high variance across seeds
  - Multi-Model: Degraded accuracy at slow speeds due to overhead
  - SWEET: Reduced accuracy on later classifiers, especially with large training data

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement Early-Exit with two classifiers and observe conflicting gradients via cosine similarity.
  2. Replace Early-Exit with Multi-Model and compare individual classifier accuracy.
  3. Apply SWEET's gradient truncation and measure performance improvement on early classifiers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do conflicting gradients in Early-Exit models behave as model size increases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note they used relatively small models (≤ 350M parameters) due to computational constraints and defer analysis of larger models to future work.
- Why unresolved: The paper's analysis of conflicting gradients was limited to BERT and DeBERTa BASE and LARGE models, and the authors hypothesize that the negative impact of conflicting gradients may be less prominent with larger amounts of training data, but do not test this.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing the magnitude of conflicting gradients and their impact on classifier performance in models with 1B+ parameters, across different training set sizes.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does SWEET's performance advantage persist when combined with other efficiency techniques like quantization or distillation?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that adaptive inference methods are orthogonal to other efficiency techniques like quantization and distillation, suggesting potential for combination, but do not test this.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates SWEET in isolation against Early-Exit and Multi-Model baselines, without exploring synergistic effects with other efficiency methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the speed-accuracy curves of SWEET combined with quantization/distillation against these techniques applied to Early-Exit and Multi-Model models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does SWEET perform on tasks with different label distributions or class imbalance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge they only tested on seven English text classification tasks and recognize results may vary for other tasks and languages.
- Why unresolved: All experiments were conducted on balanced GLUE benchmark tasks, and the paper does not investigate how SWEET handles skewed label distributions or severe class imbalance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments on tasks with varying degrees of class imbalance, measuring both overall accuracy and per-class performance across SWEET, Early-Exit, and Multi-Model approaches.

## Limitations
- Findings are primarily based on low-resource settings with limited training data (6K examples per task), limiting generalizability to larger datasets
- SWEET's parameter isolation may reduce capacity of later classifiers, though this effect is not thoroughly quantified across different dataset sizes
- Speed-accuracy trade-off conclusions rely on specific BERT/DeBERTa implementations and may vary with different model architectures

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The empirical observation that Early-Exit suffers from conflicting gradient updates is well-supported by the 2.3% performance gap compared to Multi-Model classifiers. The mechanism of gradient interference through shared parameters is theoretically sound and consistent with multi-task learning literature.

- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of SWEET in resolving gradient conflicts for early classifiers is demonstrated, but the long-term implications for later classifier performance and scalability to larger datasets are not fully explored. The 1.1% average improvement claim relies on specific experimental conditions that may not generalize.

- **Low Confidence**: The speed-accuracy trade-off conclusions, particularly regarding Multi-Model's sequential overhead at slow speeds, are based on specific BERT/DeBERTa implementations and may vary significantly with different model architectures or parallel execution capabilities.

## Next Checks

1. **Dataset Scaling Experiment**: Replicate the study with varying dataset sizes (1K, 6K, 12K, 50K examples) to determine how gradient conflicts and SWEET's effectiveness scale with data availability.

2. **Later Classifier Capacity Analysis**: Systematically measure the performance degradation of later classifiers in SWEET across different tasks and backbone model sizes to quantify the capacity limitation trade-off.

3. **Alternative Optimization Comparison**: Implement and compare alternative optimization strategies (such as gradient projection or task-specific learning rates) against SWEET to determine if conflicting gradients can be resolved without parameter isolation.
