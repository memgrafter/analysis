# Rhetorical Role Labeling of Legal Documents using Transformers and Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.04100
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04100
- Reference count: 5
- Key outcome: Context-based LEGAL-BERT achieves 71.02% accuracy on validation set, outperforming previous methods by 5.5 percentage points

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of rhetorical role labeling in Indian court judgements by classifying sentences into 13 semantic categories. The authors propose a context-based LEGAL-BERT approach that incorporates surrounding sentences to improve classification accuracy. By concatenating two preceding and two succeeding sentences with the target sentence, the model captures contextual dependencies that are critical for determining rhetorical roles. The approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on the ILDC dataset, demonstrating the importance of sentence context in legal document understanding.

## Method Summary
The authors experiment with multiple approaches for rhetorical role labeling, including direct LEGAL-BERT classification, graph-based methods using cosine similarity on CLS tokens, and a context-based LEGAL-BERT approach. The context-based method creates 5-sentence input windows by concatenating surrounding sentences with the target sentence, separated by the </s> token. This approach leverages LEGAL-BERT's domain-specific pretraining on legal texts and outperforms both the direct classification baseline and graph-based methods on the validation set.

## Key Results
- Context-based LEGAL-BERT achieves 71.02% accuracy on validation set
- Outperforms previous methods by 5.5 percentage points
- Graph-based approaches (label diffusion and GCN) achieve lower performance at 62.47% and 54.94% respectively

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Context-based LEGAL-BERT improves performance by capturing surrounding sentence information that informs the semantic role of a target sentence.
- Mechanism: The model concatenates two preceding and two succeeding sentences with the target sentence, separated by the </s> token, allowing the transformer to learn contextual dependencies that disambiguate rhetorical roles.
- Core assumption: The semantic function of a sentence is not fully determined by the sentence itself but by its relationship to adjacent sentences in the document structure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the context of each sentence is critically important in determining its label"
  - [section] "we created a 5 sentence input corresponding to any given input by concatenating its two preceeding sentences and its two succeeding sentences"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the related papers focus on different techniques rather than context modeling specifically
- Break condition: If the sentence order or document structure becomes disrupted, or if sentences are too long to fit within the model's token limit, the context window may become ineffective or require truncation that loses critical information.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Graph-based methods using cosine similarity on CLS tokens can model sentence relationships but require high-quality embeddings to be effective.
- Mechanism: Sentences are represented as nodes in a graph where edges connect sentences with high cosine similarity (>0.5) between their CLS token embeddings, enabling label diffusion or GCN-based classification.
- Core assumption: Sentences with similar CLS token representations share similar rhetorical roles and can inform each other's classification through graph propagation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "modeled the data into a graph using cosine similarity on the CLS tokens generated by LEGAL-BERT"
  - [section] "The matrix P is obtained via equation (4), normalizing the adjacency matrix A using the square root inverse of the degree matrix D"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers don't explicitly validate the cosine similarity threshold or graph construction methodology
- Break condition: If the CLS token embeddings don't capture task-relevant features, the cosine similarity will not reflect true semantic similarity, making the graph structure ineffective for classification.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LEGAL-BERT's domain-specific pretraining on legal texts provides better foundation representations than general-purpose BERT for legal document classification.
- Mechanism: The model uses LEGAL-BERT-BASE with 12 layers, 768 hidden units, and 12 attention heads, pretrained on 12 GB of legal texts for 40 epochs, providing legal domain knowledge that general BERT lacks.
- Core assumption: Legal documents have specialized vocabulary, structure, and discourse patterns that require domain-specific pretraining to capture effectively.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The LEGAL-BERT family of models is a modified pretrained model based on the architecture of BERT"
  - [section] "It has a total of 110M parameters and is pretrained for 40 epochs on a corpus of 12 GB worth of legal texts"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - while related papers use LEGAL-BERT, they don't provide ablation studies comparing it with general BERT on the same task
- Break condition: If the legal domain knowledge encoded in LEGAL-BERT doesn't align with the specific rhetorical roles in the dataset, or if the dataset contains significant non-legal content, the domain adaptation may not provide benefits.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would context-based LEGAL-BERT perform if trained on the entire training dataset instead of just the validation set?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that graph-based approaches were limited by computational constraints and could not be trained on the entire training set, suggesting similar constraints may have affected the context-based LEGAL-BERT model.
- Why unresolved: The authors only trained the context-based LEGAL-BERT on the validation set due to computational limitations, preventing assessment of its full potential.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training the context-based LEGAL-BERT on the complete training dataset and comparing its performance to the current model would provide definitive evidence.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of surrounding sentences to include in the context window for the context-based LEGAL-BERT model?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors used a 5-sentence context window (2 preceding, 1 target, 2 succeeding) but suggest this could be extended using a long-former model.
- Why unresolved: The paper only experiments with a fixed 5-sentence context window without exploring different window sizes or their impact on performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experimentation with varying context window sizes (e.g., 3, 5, 7, 9 sentences) and comparison of their classification accuracy would determine the optimal context window size.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would using a different similarity threshold in the graph construction (other than 0.5) improve the performance of graph-based approaches?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors used a cosine similarity threshold of 0.5 for edge creation in their graph-based models, but acknowledge this choice was arbitrary.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore how different similarity thresholds might affect the quality of the graph representation and subsequent classification performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimenting with various similarity thresholds (e.g., 0.3, 0.4, 0.6, 0.7) and evaluating their impact on classification accuracy would reveal the optimal threshold value.

## Limitations

- Graph-based approaches show limited success despite sound methodology, suggesting LEGAL-BERT embeddings may not capture necessary semantic relationships
- Evaluation limited to single dataset (ILDC) with 13 rhetorical roles, restricting generalizability
- Computational constraints prevented training on full dataset and exploring optimal context window sizes

## Confidence

- High confidence: Context-based approach improves accuracy by 5.5 percentage points over previous methods
- Medium confidence: Graph-based methods show limited success due to potential embedding quality issues
- Low confidence: Generalizability to other legal domains or document types remains untested
