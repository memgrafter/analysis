# Using meaning instead of words to track topics
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.00565
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00565
- Reference count: 17
- This paper introduces a semantic-based approach for tracking topics over time using word embeddings, showing comparable performance to traditional lexical methods with potential advantages in handling lexical drift.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel semantic approach to topic tracking that uses word embeddings to capture topic meaning rather than relying on exact word matching. The method represents topics as weighted sums of word vectors and computes semantic distances using cosine similarity. Experiments comparing this semantic approach to traditional Jensen-Shannon divergence on news articles from 2019-2020 show that both methods agree on about 60-65% of topic pairs, with semantic divergence slightly preferred by human annotators (54%) for the remaining pairs. The study suggests that semantic and lexical approaches make different types of errors and could complement each other, with semantic methods potentially being more robust to lexical drift.

## Method Summary
The semantic approach embeds topics as weighted sums of word vectors and computes distances using cosine similarity, contrasting with traditional lexical methods based on Jensen-Shannon divergence. Topics are represented as probability distributions over words in lexical methods, while semantic methods represent them as weighted sums of pre-trained word embeddings. The cosine similarity between these weighted sums provides a measure of semantic distance between topics. The study evaluates both approaches on news articles from 2019-2020, comparing their agreement on topic pair similarity through human annotation.

## Key Results
- Semantic and lexical methods agree on about 60-65% of topic pairs
- Human annotators slightly prefer semantic divergence over lexical divergence (54% vs 46%)
- The remaining 35-40% of pairs differ, suggesting semantic and lexical approaches make different errors
- Semantic approach shows potential robustness to lexical drift, though this requires further validation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The semantic approach works by capturing the underlying meaning of topics through word embeddings rather than exact word matches. When topics evolve over time, they may use different words to express the same concepts (lexical drift). Word embeddings encode semantic relationships between words, so topics using different words with similar meanings will have similar vector representations. By representing topics as weighted sums of word vectors and computing cosine similarity, the method can detect semantic similarity even when lexical overlap is minimal.

## Foundational Learning
- Word embeddings: Dense vector representations of words that capture semantic relationships; needed because they enable semantic similarity measurement beyond exact word matching
- Quick check: Verify that cosine similarity between embeddings of synonyms is higher than between unrelated words
- Cosine similarity: Measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors; needed because it provides a normalized measure of vector similarity regardless of magnitude
- Quick check: Confirm that cosine similarity ranges from -1 to 1 and that identical vectors have similarity of 1
- Jensen-Shannon divergence: A symmetric measure of difference between probability distributions; needed as the baseline lexical method for comparison
- Quick check: Ensure JS divergence is well-defined for the probability distributions used
- Weighted sum of embeddings: Method to represent a topic as a single vector; needed because topics contain multiple words that must be combined
- Quick check: Verify that weighting words by their importance improves topic representation quality

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Topics -> Word Embeddings -> Weighted Sum -> Cosine Similarity
Critical path: Topic representation (weighted sum of embeddings) → Semantic distance computation (cosine similarity) → Comparison with lexical method
Design tradeoffs: Semantic approach trades computational simplicity for potential robustness to lexical drift; lexical approach is more interpretable but brittle to word choice changes
Failure signatures: Semantic method may fail when word embeddings poorly capture topic semantics; lexical method fails when topics use different words for same concepts
First 3 experiments: 1) Test on controlled synthetic data with known lexical drift patterns 2) Evaluate on multiple datasets with varying vocabulary overlap 3) Compare against additional semantic distance measures (e.g., word mover's distance)

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies on human annotation of topic pair similarity rather than direct tracking performance over time
- Small sample size of human annotations (125 topic pairs) limits statistical power
- Study doesn't explicitly test claims about robustness to lexical drift with controlled drift scenarios
- 54% human preference for semantic method is only marginally significant

## Confidence
- Technical implementation: High (follows standard practices for word embedding averaging and cosine similarity)
- Evaluation methodology: Medium (human similarity judgments are indirect measure of tracking performance)
- Claims about drift robustness: Medium (plausible but not directly tested)
- Comparative advantage over lexical methods: Low-Medium (marginal preference in human evaluation)

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a time-series evaluation tracking individual topics across multiple time points to directly measure drift resistance, rather than comparing pairwise similarity judgments
2. Test the semantic method on datasets with known lexical variation (e.g., medical terminology evolving over time, or political discourse around emerging concepts) to validate claims about drift robustness
3. Compare both methods against a ground truth topic tracking dataset where true topic evolution is known, rather than relying solely on human similarity judgments
