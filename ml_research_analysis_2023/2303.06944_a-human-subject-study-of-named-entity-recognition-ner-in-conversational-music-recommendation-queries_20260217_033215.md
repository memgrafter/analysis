# A Human Subject Study of Named Entity Recognition (NER) in Conversational Music Recommendation Queries

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.06944
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06944
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Humans and fine-tuned transformers achieve comparable F1 scores on noisy music NER, with humans excelling in precision and models in recall due to entity exposure

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a human subject study comparing human and transformer model performance on Named Entity Recognition (NER) in conversational music recommendation queries. The researchers created MusicRecoNER, a new dataset of 1800 English queries from Reddit annotated with Artist and Work of Art entities. The study found that humans and fine-tuned transformers achieve comparable overall F1 scores, but exhibit different error patterns - humans have higher precision while models show higher recall, particularly for entities seen during pre-training. The research highlights the importance of entity exposure in model performance and demonstrates that evaluation schema choice significantly impacts reported metrics.

## Method Summary
The study created the MusicRecoNER dataset by collecting 1800 English music recommendation queries from Reddit and annotating them with Artist and Work of Art entities. Three pre-trained transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa, MPNet) were fine-tuned for token classification on the training data. Human annotators served as a baseline, instructed to identify entities based on their knowledge without external resources. Performance was evaluated using multiple schemas (Strict, Exact, Entity, Partial) and analyzed separately for seen versus unseen entities based on pre-training exposure. The methodology controlled for various factors including annotator expertise, evaluation consistency, and entity exposure quantification.

## Key Results
- Humans and fine-tuned transformers achieve comparable F1 scores on music NER tasks
- Humans exhibit higher precision while models demonstrate higher recall, especially for pre-training-exposed entities
- Evaluation schema choice significantly impacts performance metrics, with relaxed schemas yielding higher scores than Strict

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Human and transformer model performances are comparable on noisy music NER due to complementary strengths—humans excel in precision, models in recall.
- Mechanism: Humans rely on prior knowledge and contextual cues to make accurate entity selections, leading to high precision. Models, especially pre-trained transformers, leverage exposure to entity mentions during training to capture more entity mentions (higher recall), even if some are spurious.
- Core assumption: Entity exposure during pre-training and fine-tuning is a strong driver of recall performance, not just contextual generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "humans had higher precision, while the model higher recall because of entity exposure especially during pre-training"
  - [section 5.2] "Table 6 shows that humans and BERT perform differently in terms of precision and recall"
  - [corpus] Weak—no explicit mapping of entity exposure to performance in the corpus description, but inferred from methodology.
- Break condition: If entity exposure is controlled (e.g., all entities unseen to models), recall advantage of models diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Entity type affects error patterns—Artist boundaries are more reliably segmented, while WoA typing is more accurate.
- Mechanism: Artist entities often appear in predictable patterns (e.g., "songs like [artist]") enabling better segmentation. WoA entities, being more varied (album, song, playlist titles), are harder to segment but easier to classify when boundaries are correct.
- Core assumption: Linguistic patterns in music queries differ systematically between entity types.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.2] "we can notice a different behaviour for the two entity types for both BERT and humans"
  - [section 5.3] "boundary errors happen more frequently" for WoA, while "segmentation is more often correct" for Artist
  - [corpus] Weak—corpus description doesn't detail entity type distributions in queries.
- Break condition: If query patterns shift to include more ambiguous or irregular entity types, this advantage erodes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Relaxed evaluation schemas (Exact, Entity) yield higher scores than Strict, revealing that boundary or type errors dominate.
- Mechanism: Strict schema penalizes both boundary and type errors; relaxed schemas allow partial correctness, capturing cases where humans or models get one aspect right.
- Core assumption: Partial correctness is meaningful and achievable in practice.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.2] "the F1 scores under Exact and Entity schemes are larger than under Strict"
  - [section 4.2] "Exact schema classiﬁes a prediction as correct when its boundaries match... Entity schema... when its type matches"
  - [corpus] Weak—no explicit mention of evaluation schema impact in corpus description.
- Break condition: If future work adopts only strict schema, this advantage disappears.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Reddit scraping → cleaning → preprocessing → annotation → fine-tuning → evaluation → analysis
- Critical path: Data collection → annotation → fine-tuning → evaluation → analysis
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Human baseline vs algorithmic baseline: Humans better precision, models better recall
  - Strict vs relaxed schemas: Trade-off between strictness and realism of evaluation
  - Entity exposure control: Balancing between generalization and memorization
- Failure signatures:
  - Low recall: Models miss entities due to insufficient exposure or context ambiguity
  - Low precision: Models over-generate spurious entities or humans miss due to unfamiliarity
  - Schema mismatch: Incorrect interpretation of boundaries or types under Strict schema
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune BERT on 3 folds, test on 4th, compare Strict vs Exact vs Entity scores
  2. Split test set into Seen/Unseen entities, compare recall
  3. Run human annotation on same splits, compare precision/recall patterns

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would human performance on NER tasks change if annotators had access to music streaming platforms during annotation, allowing them to verify unfamiliar entities?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that annotators were instructed not to consult the Internet and to rely on their own knowledge, which resulted in most entities being novel to the annotators.
- Why unresolved: The paper explicitly controlled for entity novelty by prohibiting external resources, but did not explore how performance would differ with access to verification tools.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled study comparing human NER performance with and without access to music databases or search engines, measuring precision, recall, and F1 scores.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the pre-training corpus composition (e.g., Wikipedia vs. BookCorpus) influence the model's ability to recognize named entities in the music domain?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses entity exposure during pre-training and its impact on model performance, noting that BERT was pre-trained on Wikipedia and BookCorpus.
- Why unresolved: While the paper quantifies exposure using Wikipedia2Vec, it does not isolate the specific contribution of different pre-training data sources to entity recognition performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: An ablation study comparing model performance when pre-trained on different combinations of corpora (e.g., Wikipedia-only vs. BookCorpus-only vs. combined) for music NER tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of NER systems vary when dealing with nested named entities, particularly when the outer entity is a well-known band name composed of individual artist names?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses nested named entities and provides an example where the band name "Emerson Lake and Palmer" is annotated as a single Artist entity despite referring to three individual artists.
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions this as an exception to the general rule of favoring innermost entities but does not provide quantitative data on how often this occurs or its impact on performance metrics.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of nested entity recognition performance, including frequency of occurrence, annotation consistency, and impact on precision/recall for different entity types.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How would the introduction of artificial noise (e.g., misspellings, phonetic variations) to named entities affect human and model performance in NER tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that some noise was present in the dataset due to Reddit authors' spelling errors but did not systematically introduce artificial noise to named entities.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the presence of some noise, it does not explore the specific impact of various types of noise on NER performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study introducing controlled amounts of different types of noise (e.g., character substitutions, phonetic variations, transpositions) to named entities and measuring the resulting performance degradation for both humans and models.

## Limitations

- Data representation bias: The MusicRecoNER dataset from Reddit may not generalize to other conversational contexts or user groups
- Annotation schema limitations: Subjectivity in ground truth due to annotator interpretation of "other artists or works of art the user might have in mind"
- Entity exposure calculation: Reliance on Wikipedia entity linking may not capture all relevant exposure contexts

## Confidence

**High Confidence**:
- Humans achieve higher precision than models in NER tasks
- Models achieve higher recall than humans, particularly for entities seen during pre-training
- Performance differences between entity types (Artist vs WoA) are consistent across humans and models

**Medium Confidence**:
- Entity exposure during pre-training is the primary driver of model recall advantage
- The relative performance patterns would hold across different music recommendation contexts
- The annotation guidelines did not introduce systematic bias in the ground truth

**Low Confidence**:
- The specific numerical performance differences would replicate exactly with different annotator pools
- The Reddit-derived dataset is fully representative of conversational music recommendation patterns
- The exposure calculation methodology perfectly captures all relevant pre-training contexts

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-platform validation**: Collect a parallel dataset from a different source (e.g., Twitter, music forums) to assess whether the human-model performance patterns hold across different conversational contexts and user demographics.

2. **Exposure score validation**: Manually verify the Wikipedia entity linking for a random sample of entities in the test set to confirm that exposure scores accurately reflect pre-training exposure. Additionally, test model performance when exposure is explicitly controlled (e.g., training on entities with similar exposure distributions).

3. **Annotation consistency check**: Have a subset of queries re-annotated by a different annotator pool using the same guidelines, then measure inter-annotator agreement and assess whether the original ground truth would change significantly. This would validate the reliability of the human baseline.
