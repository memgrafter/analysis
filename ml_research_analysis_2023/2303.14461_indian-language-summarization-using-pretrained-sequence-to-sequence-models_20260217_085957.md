# Indian Language Summarization using Pretrained Sequence-to-Sequence Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.14461
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14461
- **Reference count**: 30
- **Key outcome**: Ranked 1st across all three ILSUM sub-tasks (English, Hindi, Gujarati) using PEGASUS, MT5, and MBart models respectively

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the ILSUM shared task for text summarization in Hindi, Gujarati, and English, experimenting with various pretrained sequence-to-sequence models including PEGASUS, T5, BART, ProphetNet, MT5, and IndicBART. The authors employ k-fold cross-validation and data filtering to assess dataset quality and model performance. Their approach achieves first place across all three language sub-tasks, with PEGASUS excelling for English, MT5 for Hindi, and MBart for Gujarati. Notably, the models demonstrate strong capability in handling code-mixing, generating high-quality summaries with appropriate English text insertions where relevant.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune multiple pretrained sequence-to-sequence models on the ILSUM dataset using k-fold cross-validation (90% training, 10% validation) and full dataset training approaches. They experiment with models like PEGASUS, T5, BART, ProphetNet, MT5, and IndicBART, applying data filtering to remove low-quality examples including empty instances, duplicates, prefix summaries, and poorly compressed summaries. The methodology includes systematic hyperparameter tuning with batch sizes of 2-4, learning rates of 5e-5, max source lengths of 512, and beam search with size 4 for decoding.

## Key Results
- PEGASUS achieved best performance for English summarization (R-1: 55.83)
- MT5 outperformed other models for Hindi summarization (R-1: 60.72)
- MBart showed superior results for Gujarati summarization (R-1: 26.11)
- Models successfully handled code-mixing, generating appropriate English text in Hindi/Gujarati summaries

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pretrained multilingual models encode cross-lingual knowledge enabling code-mixing handling.
- Mechanism: Models trained on multiple languages learn shared representations that map between languages. When finetuned on mixed Hindi/English data, they can generate summaries containing relevant English tokens for Hindi input.
- Core assumption: Multilingual pretraining creates transferable linguistic features across languages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The models were capable of handling code-mixing in the dataset, generating high-quality summaries with relevant English text where appropriate."
  - [section 4] "The multilingual models have been pretrained on large amounts of data, and they are sufficiently capable of handling the presence of code-mixing in the dataset, which we observe in the outputs as well."
- Break condition: If multilingual pretraining lacks shared vocabulary or orthographic overlap, cross-lingual transfer fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: K-fold cross-validation mitigates overfitting when training data is limited.
- Mechanism: By rotating validation folds, the model sees different data splits, reducing variance and improving generalization compared to training on full data without proper validation.
- Core assumption: Limited dataset contains enough diversity across folds to provide meaningful validation signals.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "This paper also extensively analyzes the impact of k-fold cross-validation while experimenting with limited data size"
  - [section 5] "In order to avoid overfitting, we have performed k-fold cross-validation on the training dataset. We have observed that Hindi k-fold experiments had better scores than the experiments performed with the full version of the released data."
- Break condition: If dataset is too small or homogeneous, k-fold validation provides no benefit and may hurt performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Data filtering improves model performance by removing low-quality examples.
- Mechanism: Removing empty instances, duplicates, prefix summaries, and poorly compressed summaries reduces noise, allowing models to learn better summarization patterns.
- Core assumption: Filtered data contains higher proportion of valid, high-quality summaries than unfiltered data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6] "Filters were applied include checking whether there are: Empty instances, Duplicate pairs and summaries within the dataset, Cases where the first few sentences of the article itself are taken as the summary, Check whether the summary is 'compressed enough'"
  - [section 6.1] "To compare the models' performance on different variations of the training dataset, we have not made any changes in the validation data. As observed in Table 6, the experiments performed with 'original' data produce better scores than the 'filtered' data."
- Break condition: If filtering removes too much data or introduces bias, model performance degrades.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data → Tokenizer → Pretrained model (BART/PEGASUS/T5/MT5/MBart/IndicBART) → Fine-tuning → Evaluation (ROUGE)
- Critical path: Data quality → Model selection → Hyperparameter tuning → Validation strategy → Final evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Larger models (T5-large, PEGASUS) perform better but require more compute; multilingual models handle code-mixing but may underperform monolingual models on pure language tasks
- Failure signatures: Poor ROUGE scores indicate issues with data quality, model choice, or hyperparameters; overfitting shows as large gap between training and validation performance
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Baseline: Fine-tune PEGASUS on English ILSUM data using original dataset
  2. Multilingual: Fine-tune MT5 on Hindi ILSUM data using k-fold cross-validation
  3. Filtered data: Fine-tune IndicBART on Hindi data using filtered dataset only

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the quality and performance of the Indian language summarization models change when trained on larger, high-quality datasets beyond news articles?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the lack of high-quality datasets has hindered progress in Indian language summarization and suggest looking at sources other than news websites.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses the ILSUM dataset scraped from news websites, which is limited in size and potentially contains quality issues.
- What evidence would resolve it: Performance comparisons of the same models trained on larger, curated datasets from diverse sources (e.g., books, social media, academic papers) would provide concrete evidence.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal approach to handle code-mixing in Indian language summarization models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that multilingual models can handle code-mixing but do not explore strategies to explicitly improve this capability.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate techniques like code-switching-aware pretraining, code-specific tokenization, or data augmentation to enhance code-mixing handling.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing different code-mixing handling strategies (e.g., code-switching-aware pretraining vs. standard pretraining) on the same dataset would provide insights.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do lightweight adapters compare to full fine-tuning in terms of performance and efficiency for Indian language summarization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention adapters as a promising approach but only experiment with full fine-tuning of the base models.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide empirical comparisons between adapter-based fine-tuning and full fine-tuning in terms of ROUGE scores, training time, and memory usage.
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparisons of adapter-based and full fine-tuning approaches on the same dataset, reporting ROUGE scores, training time, and memory usage, would provide clear evidence.

## Limitations
- Data filtering approach showed mixed results, with filtered data producing worse performance than original data for Hindi and Gujarati tasks
- Evaluation relies solely on ROUGE metrics, which may not capture semantic quality or factual consistency in summaries
- K-fold cross-validation showed inconsistent benefits across languages, suggesting dataset-specific effects

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Model ranking and overall task performance (ranked 1st across all three sub-tasks) - supported by systematic experimentation and clear metrics
- **Medium Confidence**: Code-mixing handling capability - observed in outputs but not quantitatively measured beyond qualitative description
- **Medium Confidence**: Data filtering effectiveness - implemented but showed negative results for Hindi/Gujarati, contradicting expected benefits
- **Low Confidence**: Cross-lingual transfer mechanisms - described conceptually but lack ablation studies or controlled experiments isolating pretraining effects
