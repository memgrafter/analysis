# Empowering Counterfactual Reasoning over Graph Neural Networks through Inductivity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2306.04835
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04835
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Key outcome:** INDUCE is an inductive counterfactual reasoning algorithm for GNNs that learns to predict minimal perturbations on input graphs to flip GNN predictions for target nodes, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines in fidelity, size, and accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Graph neural networks (GNNs) have become essential for analyzing graph-structured data but lack interpretability in their predictions. This paper introduces INDUCE, an inductive counterfactual reasoning algorithm that generates minimal perturbations to flip GNN predictions for target nodes. The method models counterfactual generation as a Markov decision process where the state captures graph reactions to perturbations and the reward balances prediction change against perturbation size. Through extensive experiments on benchmark datasets, INDUCE demonstrates superior performance compared to existing methods in terms of fidelity, explanation size, and accuracy. The inductive nature enables generalization to unseen nodes, providing significant speedup over transductive approaches.

## Method Summary
INDUCE addresses the interpretability challenge in GNNs by learning to generate counterfactual explanations through minimal graph perturbations. The algorithm frames the problem as a Markov decision process where an agent iteratively perturbs the graph to change a target node's classification. The state representation captures the graph's structure and how it responds to perturbations, while the reward function balances the magnitude of prediction change against the size of the perturbation. INDUCE is trained on perturbed versions of training graphs and can then generate counterfactuals for unseen nodes without retraining, enabling efficient inductive reasoning across graph structures.

## Key Results
- INDUCE outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in fidelity, explanation size, and accuracy on benchmark datasets
- The inductive approach enables generalization to unseen nodes, providing significant computational speedup over transductive methods
- Edge additions lead to better counterfactual results compared to methods that only consider edge deletions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Markov decision process formulation allows INDUCE to learn a policy that effectively navigates the space of possible graph perturbations to find minimal changes that flip predictions. By modeling the graph's reaction to perturbations in the state representation, the algorithm can make informed decisions about which edges to modify. The reward function ensures that explanations are both effective (flip the prediction) and minimal (small perturbation size), aligning with practical needs for interpretable explanations.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:** Input Graph -> MDP Agent -> Perturbation Policy -> Modified Graph -> GNN Prediction

**Critical Path:** Graph → State Representation → Action Selection → Perturbation → Prediction Check → Reward

**Design Tradeoffs:** 
- Edge addition vs deletion only: Additions provide more flexibility but increase search space
- State representation complexity: Richer states capture more information but increase computational cost
- Reward function balance: Must weigh prediction change against perturbation size

**Failure Signatures:**
- Inability to find perturbations that flip predictions (local minima)
- Generating overly complex explanations (imbalanced reward)
- Poor generalization to new graph structures (insufficient training diversity)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does incorporating edge additions consistently improve the fidelity and size of counterfactual explanations across all types of graph datasets, including real-world graphs with different properties?
- Basis in paper: The paper demonstrates that edge additions lead to better counterfactual results compared to methods that only consider edge deletions. It also shows that INDUCE outperforms baselines in terms of fidelity, size, and accuracy on benchmark datasets. However, the experiments are primarily conducted on synthetic datasets with specific motifs.
- Why unresolved: The paper's experiments are limited to benchmark datasets with predefined motifs and one real-world dataset (Amazon-photos). The impact of edge additions on other real-world graphs with different properties, such as citation networks, social networks, or biological networks, is not explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments on a diverse range of real-world graph datasets, including those with different properties (e.g., different average degrees, different types of motifs or structures), and comparing the performance of INDUCE with and without edge additions would provide evidence for the generalizability of the approach.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of heuristic features (degree, entropy, and one-hot encoded labels) impact the performance of INDUCE, and are there other potentially useful features that could be incorporated?
- Basis in paper: The paper presents an ablation study on the impact of different heuristic features on the performance of INDUCE. It shows that using all features together leads to the best overall performance. However, the study is limited to the three mentioned features.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the impact of other potential features that could capture different aspects of the graph structure or node properties. Additionally, the optimal combination of features for different types of graphs or tasks is not investigated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different combinations of features, including other potential features (e.g., centrality measures, clustering coefficients, community information), and analyzing their impact on the performance of INDUCE for different types of graphs and tasks would provide insights into the importance of feature selection.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the inductive approach of INDUCE be extended to other graph-related tasks beyond node classification, such as link prediction or graph classification?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on applying INDUCE to the task of node classification and demonstrates its effectiveness in generating counterfactual explanations for this task. However, the paper does not discuss the applicability of the inductive approach to other graph-related tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper's focus is on node classification, and the experimental evaluation is limited to this task. The inductive approach of INDUCE may not directly translate to other tasks that involve different types of predictions or graph-level explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Investigating the applicability of the inductive approach of INDUCE to other graph-related tasks, such as link prediction or graph classification, and adapting the algorithm to handle the specific requirements of these tasks would provide evidence for its generalizability.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies heavily on synthetic benchmarks with limited validation on real-world datasets where ground truth counterfactuals are unavailable
- The degree of true inductivity/generalization to entirely different graph structures versus unseen nodes within similar distributions is unclear
- The Markov decision process formulation assumes adequate state representation but this assumption is not rigorously validated

## Confidence

- **Superior performance vs baselines:** Medium (based on benchmark results, but limited to specific datasets)
- **True inductivity/generalization capability:** Low-Medium (theoretical claim not fully validated)
- **Markov decision process formulation adequacy:** Low (not empirically validated)
