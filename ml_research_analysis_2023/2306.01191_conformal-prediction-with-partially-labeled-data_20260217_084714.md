# Conformal Prediction with Partially Labeled Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01191
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01191
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 9
- One-line primary result: Three approaches for conformal prediction with partially labeled data, with the average nonconformity score method achieving best results while maintaining coverage guarantees

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of conformal prediction when training data consists of set-valued labels rather than precise labels. The authors propose a generalization of conformal prediction procedures that can handle such partially labeled data by introducing three approaches for computing nonconformity scores: maximum, average, and all-scores methods. These methods are proven to be valid under certain assumptions, and experimental results on benchmark and real-world datasets demonstrate their effectiveness compared to natural baselines.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes three methods for conformal prediction with partially labeled data (PLL): using maximum nonconformity scores (Emax), average nonconformity scores (Emean), and weighted average nonconformity scores (Eall). The approach involves training a probabilistic classifier on set-valued training data using a PLL algorithm (PRODEN), computing nonconformity scores on a calibration set using one of the three methods, and constructing prediction sets using the inductive conformal prediction (ICP) procedure. The methods are validated theoretically and experimentally, showing that they maintain coverage guarantees while achieving competitive efficiency.

## Key Results
- The average nonconformity score method (Emean) achieves the best results in terms of efficiency while maintaining coverage guarantees
- The maximum nonconformity score approach (Emax) provides strong validity guarantees but results in less efficient (larger) prediction sets
- All three approaches compare favorably to natural baselines on benchmark and real-world datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The pessimistic maximum nonconformity score approach (Emax) guarantees marginal coverage for set-valued training data by conservatively bounding the true label's nonconformity.
- Mechanism: For each calibration instance, the maximum nonconformity score across all candidate labels is used. This ensures that if the true label's score is among the smallest ⌈(1+|E1|)(1-ǫ)⌉ scores, it will remain included in the prediction set. The method achieves coverage by ensuring q(Emax, ǫ) ≥ q(E1, ǫ), which holds because 1 - min_{y∈Sj} f(xj)y ≥ 1 - f(xj)yj for all y ∈ Sj.
- Core assumption: The underlying precise data points (O'calib) are exchangeable with the new test point.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We prove the validity of the proposed method and present experimental studies in which it compares favorably to natural baselines."
  - [section 3]: "Theorem 1. If the data points in O'calib ∪ (xnew, ynew) are exchangeable, then the prediction set (11) with underlying score set (10) satisfies P(ynew ∈ T(xnew, fPLL, Emax, ǫ)) ≥ 1 - ǫ."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mentions of conformal prediction with partial labels in the corpus.
- Break condition: If exchangeability assumption fails or the nonconformity function does not properly capture the "strangeness" of data points relative to the model.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The average nonconformity score approach (Emean) can provide efficient and valid prediction sets when each candidate set contains labels with predicted probabilities above 1/|Sj|.
- Mechanism: Instead of using maximum or all scores, this method computes the average nonconformity score for each calibration instance. This creates smaller prediction sets while maintaining coverage when the condition fPLL(xj)yj ≥ 1/|Sj| holds for all calibration instances.
- Core assumption: The induced classifier predicts probability distributions over classes, and for each calibration instance, the true label's predicted probability is at least 1/|Sj|.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3]: "Theorem 3. If the data points in O'calib ∪ (xnew, ynew) are exchangeable and fPLL(xj)yj ≥ 1/|Sj|, ∀j∈I2, then the prediction set T(xnew, fPLL, Emean, ǫ) is valid."
  - [section 3]: "The requirement of this theorem is demanding, especially for the case where most candidate sets consist of only two labels."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mentions of conformal prediction with partial labels in the corpus.
- Break condition: When the condition fPLL(xj)yj ≥ 1/|Sj| is violated for some calibration instances, or when candidate sets are small (size 2), making the requirement difficult to satisfy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The all-scores approach (Eall) provides valid prediction sets by including nonconformity scores for all candidate labels, under certain conditions on the error rate and critical scores.
- Mechanism: This method includes nonconformity scores for all candidate labels in the calibration set. It proves valid under the condition that q(E1, ǫ) ≤ 0.5 and ǫ ≤ min(1/4, (|Ocalib|+|Y|)/(|Y|·(1+|Ocalib|))). The method works by showing that adding scores for false candidates to the right of q(E1, ǫ) maintains or increases the critical score.
- Core assumption: The data points in O'calib ∪ (xnew, ynew) are exchangeable, and q(E1, ǫ) ≤ 0.5.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3]: "Theorem 2. For any ǫ ≤ min(1/4, |Ocalib|+|Y|)/(|Y|·(1+|Ocalib|)), if the points in O'calib ∪ {(xnew, ynew)} are exchangeable and q(E1, ǫ) ≤ 0.5, then the prediction set T(xnew, fPLL, Eall, ǫ) is valid."
  - [section 3]: "Later on, in Section 4, we will see that the validity of prediction sets generated by this method holds even when this condition is violated."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - no direct mentions of conformal prediction with partial labels in the corpus.
- Break condition: When q(E1, ǫ) > 0.5, when ǫ exceeds the specified bound, or when exchangeability assumption fails.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - PLL Algorithm -> Calibration -> Prediction -> Validation

- Critical path: PLL training → Calibration score computation → Prediction set construction → Coverage validation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Emax provides strong validity guarantees but results in inefficient (large) prediction sets
  - Emean offers better efficiency but requires stricter conditions (f(xj)yj ≥ 1/|Sj|) for validity
  - Eall balances between the two but has complex validity conditions (q(E1, ǫ) ≤ 0.5 and bounds on ǫ)

- Failure signatures:
  - Coverage dropping below the specified level indicates violation of exchangeability assumption or incorrect implementation of nonconformity functions
  - Extremely large prediction sets suggest using Emax when efficiency is critical
  - Invalid prediction sets despite satisfying theoretical conditions may indicate numerical precision issues or incorrect handling of edge cases

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement Emax approach on a simple synthetic dataset with random contamination (p=0.1) and verify coverage guarantee

  3. Test Eall approach on a dataset with instance-dependent contamination and verify the conditions q(E1, ǫ) ≤ 0.5 and ǫ ≤ min(1/4, ...) hold in practice

## Limitations

- Validity proofs rely on strong exchangeability assumptions that may not hold in practice, particularly for instance-dependent contamination scenarios
- Efficiency results depend on specific conditions for Emean and Eall methods that may be difficult to satisfy in real-world applications
- Experimental evaluation only considers random contamination and fixed miscalibration rates (ϵ = 0.1), leaving open questions about performance under varying contamination levels and structured noise patterns

## Confidence

- High: Basic validity of Emax approach under exchangeability (Theorem 1)
- Medium: Efficiency comparisons between methods on benchmark datasets
- Low: Validity of Eall approach when q(E1, ǫ) > 0.5 (as claimed in Section 4)
