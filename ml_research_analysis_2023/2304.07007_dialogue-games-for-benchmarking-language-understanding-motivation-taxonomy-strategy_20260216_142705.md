# Dialogue Games for Benchmarking Language Understanding: Motivation, Taxonomy, Strategy

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.07007
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07007
- Reference count: 18
- One-line primary result: Proposes Dialogue Games as a more comprehensive evaluation instrument for Situated Language Understanding (SLU) compared to traditional NLU benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that current Natural Language Understanding (NLU) benchmarks inadequately evaluate Situated Language Understanding (SLU) because they focus on static, abstract tasks rather than interactive, goal-directed activities. To address this, the author proposes "Dialogue Games"—constructed activities that embed language use in practical contexts—as a more comprehensive evaluation instrument. A taxonomy of Dialogue Games is developed, categorizing them based on environment, setting, and game structure, which links to a model of underlying SLU capabilities. The paper also discusses various evaluation modes for Dialogue Games, demonstrating their potential to assess SLU models more effectively than traditional benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a methodology for benchmarking SLU using Dialogue Games, which are constructed activities embedding language use in practical contexts. The method involves defining a taxonomy of Dialogue Game types based on environment (object presence, familiarity, access, reality), setting (communication channel, mutual observability, common ground), and game structure (role division, action space, goal type). This taxonomy links to a model of underlying SLU capabilities, including knowledge domains and anchoring processes. Various evaluation modes are proposed, including rollout tasks, agent/agent play, human/agent interaction, and representation probing, to assess SLU models comprehensively.

## Key Results
- Traditional NLU benchmarks focus on static, abstract tasks that inadequately capture the situational context and interactive nature of real-world language use.
- Dialogue Games embed language use in practical, goal-directed activities, requiring a deeper level of understanding that includes situational awareness, reference resolution, and action coordination.
- The taxonomy of Dialogue Games links game design features to underlying cognitive capabilities, providing construct validity for the evaluation instrument.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Dialogue Games provide a more comprehensive evaluation of artificial language understanding than traditional NLU benchmarks by embedding language use in practical, goal-directed activities.
- Mechanism: Traditional NLU benchmarks focus on static, abstract tasks (like answering questions about text), which lack the situational context and interactive nature of real-world language use. Dialogue Games, by contrast, simulate goal-oriented interactions where language is used to achieve specific objectives, thus requiring a deeper level of understanding that includes not just language comprehension but also situational awareness, reference resolution, and action coordination.
- Core assumption: The paper assumes that true language understanding requires the ability to use language effectively in context, not just to process and respond to isolated language inputs.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "I argue that such tests need to be complemented with tests of language use embedded in a practice, to arrive at a more comprehensive evaluation of 'artificial language understanding'."
  - [section 3]: "Evaluation in NLU centers on the language task, a functional mapping between input and output, where at least one of these involves language... It is this verbal (or intensional) description that enters into an intuitive appeal to face validity—surely, answering questions must require understanding them. However, it can be remarked that the notion of understanding in NLU evaluation typically remains an intuitive one and no further attempt is made at specifying the construct."
- Break condition: If the simulated goal-directed activities in Dialogue Games do not accurately reflect the complexity and nuances of real-world language use, then the evaluation may not provide a truly comprehensive assessment of language understanding.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The taxonomy of Dialogue Games links game design features to underlying cognitive capabilities, providing construct validity for the evaluation instrument.
- Mechanism: By categorizing Dialogue Games based on environment (object presence, familiarity, access, reality), setting (communication channel, mutual observability, common ground), and game structure (role division, action space, goal type), the taxonomy connects specific game features to the cognitive capabilities they challenge. This connection provides a theoretical basis for why a particular game design is effective at evaluating a specific aspect of language understanding, thus establishing construct validity.
- Core assumption: The paper assumes that different aspects of language understanding can be isolated and tested through carefully designed game features, and that these features can be systematically linked to cognitive capabilities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "A taxonomy of Dialogue Games is developed, categorizing them based on environment (object presence, familiarity, access, reality), setting (communication channel, mutual observability, common ground), and game structure (role division, action space, goal type). This taxonomy links to a model of underlying SLU capabilities, including knowledge domains and anchoring processes, providing construct validity."
  - [section 7]: "I distinguish here between three major areas in which decisions must be made when setting up a concrete Dialogue Game: Environment, Setting, Game Proper; with many sub-aspects within. A comprehensive overview is given in Appendix A... This discussion presents these design features and directly links them to the model of the underlying construct(s) from the previous section."
- Break condition: If the link between game design features and cognitive capabilities is not as strong or direct as assumed, then the taxonomy may not provide the intended construct validity.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Dialogue Games can be used in various evaluation modes (rollout tasks, agent/agent play, human/agent interaction, representation probing) to provide a multi-faceted assessment of language understanding models.
- Mechanism: The paper proposes different ways to use Dialogue Games for evaluation, each targeting different aspects of a model's capabilities. Rollout tasks assess the model's ability to predict and execute actions based on partial or complete dialogue history. Agent/agent play evaluates the model's performance in simulated interactions. Human/agent interaction provides insights into the model's interactive experience. Representation probing examines the model's internal representations during dialogue processing. This multi-faceted approach allows for a comprehensive evaluation of different aspects of language understanding.
- Core assumption: The paper assumes that different evaluation modes can provide complementary information about a model's capabilities, and that together they offer a more complete picture than any single mode alone.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The paper also discusses various evaluation modes for Dialogue Games, including rollout tasks, agent/agent play, human/agent interaction, and representation probing, demonstrating their potential to assess SLU models more effectively than traditional benchmarks."
  - [section 8]: "Dialogue games can be used as a means for data generation, simply by collecting game play from people playing the game... In this section, I will highlight some uses that go beyond the train/val/test dataset paradigm typical of NLP, all revolving around the ability of Dialogue Games—at least those with simulated environments—to serve as execution environments."
- Break condition: If the different evaluation modes do not provide the expected complementary information, or if they are not effectively integrated into a cohesive assessment framework, then the multi-faceted approach may not be as effective as intended.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Environment: Defines the objects, their presence, familiarity, access, and reality, as well as the dynamics and action space within the game.
  - Setting: Determines how players interact with each other, including the verbal action channel, mutual observability, and common ground.
  - Game: Specifies the goal of the interaction, the roles of the players, and the scoring mechanism.
  - Cognitive Capabilities: Represents the underlying knowledge domains (language model, world model, situation model, discourse model, agent model) and anchoring processes (incremental processing, conversational grounding, incremental learning, multimodal grounding) that are challenged by the game.

- Critical path:
  1. Define the target cognitive capabilities to be evaluated.
  2. Select appropriate game features from the taxonomy to challenge those capabilities.
  3. Design the Dialogue Game based on the selected features.
  4. Implement the game environment and setting.
  5. Define the game structure, including roles, goals, and scoring.
  6. Evaluate the model using the designed Dialogue Game and chosen evaluation mode(s).

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Static vs. Dynamic Environment: A static environment simplifies the evaluation but may not fully capture the dynamic nature of real-world language use. A dynamic environment is more realistic but also more complex to implement and evaluate.
  - Immediate vs. Mediated Access to Objects: Immediate access allows for more natural interaction but may not be feasible in all scenarios. Mediated access (e.g., through a screen) is more flexible but may limit the types of interactions that can be evaluated.
  - Spoken vs. Written Communication: Spoken communication is more natural but harder to process and generate. Written communication is easier to handle but may not fully reflect the nuances of spoken interaction.

- Failure signatures:
  - Model performs well on traditional NLU benchmarks but poorly on Dialogue Games: This suggests that the model lacks the ability to use language effectively in context, despite having good language processing skills.
  - Model performs well on some types of Dialogue Games but poorly on others: This indicates that the model may have specific strengths and weaknesses in different aspects of language understanding, as targeted by the different game types.
  - Model's performance is highly dependent on the specific design of the Dialogue Game: This suggests that the evaluation may not be robust or generalizable across different contexts.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate a pre-trained language model on a simple reference game with familiar objects in a static environment. This will test the model's basic ability to understand and use referring expressions.
  2. Evaluate the same model on a more complex construction game with unfamiliar objects in a dynamic environment. This will test the model's ability to coordinate actions and learn new concepts in context.
  3. Compare the model's performance on Dialogue Games with its performance on traditional NLU benchmarks. This will highlight the strengths and weaknesses of each evaluation approach and provide insights into the model's overall language understanding capabilities.

## Limitations

- The paper provides theoretical arguments and a comprehensive taxonomy for Dialogue Games but lacks empirical validation demonstrating their superiority over traditional NLU benchmarks.
- The effectiveness of the proposed evaluation modes (rollout tasks, agent/agent play, human/agent interaction, representation probing) in capturing the full spectrum of SLU capabilities remains to be empirically demonstrated.
- The scalability and generalizability of Dialogue Games across different domains and languages are not fully addressed.

## Confidence

- High Confidence: The theoretical framework and taxonomy of Dialogue Games are well-developed and logically consistent. The arguments for why traditional NLU benchmarks are insufficient for evaluating SLU are compelling and supported by relevant literature.
- Medium Confidence: The proposed evaluation modes for Dialogue Games are reasonable and have potential, but their effectiveness in practice needs further validation. The link between game design features and cognitive capabilities, while theoretically sound, may require empirical testing to confirm its strength.
- Low Confidence: The claim that Dialogue Games will significantly improve our ability to evaluate and develop better language understanding models is promising but not yet proven. The paper does not provide concrete examples of how Dialogue Games have been successfully implemented and validated.
