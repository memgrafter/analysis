# Some voices are too common: Building fair speech recognition systems using the Common Voice dataset

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.03773
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03773
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Speaker diversity in training data improves ASR performance more than demographic balancing alone.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates biases in automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems using the French Common Voice dataset and a pre-trained wav2vec 2.0 model. The researchers fine-tuned the model on carefully crafted training sets of fixed size to examine the impact of speaker diversity and demographic biases. They found that models trained on biased train sets performed no better than the reference on the targeted subgroup, and that speaker diversity had a stronger impact on performance than demographic diversity. Models trained on larger numbers of speakers achieved lower word error rates (WER) and reduced biases across demographic subgroups. The study highlights the importance of maximizing speaker diversity in training data to build fairer and more robust ASR systems.

## Method Summary
The study fine-tuned a pre-trained wav2vec 2.0 LB-7K-large model on the French Common Voice 12.0 dataset. Training sets of fixed size (50,000 utterances) were carefully constructed to vary speaker count and demographic composition. The model was fine-tuned for 50 epochs using CTC loss with frozen convolutional layers and SpecAugment for data augmentation. Performance was evaluated on the official test set, with WER analyzed across demographic subgroups including gender, age, and accent.

## Key Results
- Models trained on biased demographic subsets did not improve performance on targeted subgroups compared to reference models
- Increasing speaker count in training data reduced WER and demographic biases more effectively than demographic balancing alone
- Quality labels (upvotes/downvotes) had minimal impact on fine-tuning performance, with "bad quality" samples still yielding reasonable ASR results

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Models trained on biased demographic subsets do not improve performance on targeted demographic subgroups compared to reference models.
- Mechanism: Demographic bias in pre-training data propagates through fine-tuning, making targeted demographic balancing at fine-tuning stage insufficient to overcome pre-existing model biases.
- Core assumption: Pre-training bias is strong enough that fine-tuning on small, demographically focused subset cannot undo it within same domain.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "models trained on biased train sets performed no better than the reference on the targeted subgroup"
  - [section] "We believe that these biases are rooted in the underlying pre-trained model; indeed, LB-7K-large...has been pre-trained on a dataset that contains almost no accented data and is heavily biased toward male speech"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Increasing number of speakers in fine-tuning set improves ASR performance more than increasing demographic diversity alone.
- Mechanism: More speakers introduce greater variability in timbre, recording conditions, and speaking styles, helping model generalize better to unseen speakers and reducing WER across all demographic subgroups.
- Core assumption: Speaker-level variability is stronger driver of model robustness than demographic-level variability when model is already pre-trained.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Models trained on larger numbers of speakers achieved lower word error rates (WER) and reduced biases across demographic subgroups"
  - [section] "We can see that the number of speakers...in the training set has a clear impact on the WER. The higher the number of speakers, the lower the WER"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Quality labels in Common Voice dataset have minimal impact on fine-tuning performance; "bad quality" samples still yield reasonable ASR results.
- Mechanism: Even low-quality labeled data contains sufficient acoustic information for model to learn speech representations, so impact of sample quality on performance is less than expected.
- Core assumption: Fine-tuning process can tolerate some level of label or audio quality noise without severe degradation in WER.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "our two 'high quality' models...score 13.7 and 13.9 respectively" (vs. reference 13.7) and "our 'bad quality' model...scores 16.7, a significant drop in performance"
  - [corpus] "some samples were wrongfully rejected...manual inspections of the data show that some samples were wrongfully rejected"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Pre-trained wav2vec 2.0 LB-7K-large encoder (317M params, frozen conv layers during fine-tuning) -> 3-layer feed-forward network (1024 neurons each) -> CTC loss -> 42-character vocabulary

- Critical path:
  1. Load and preprocess Common Voice French data (16 kHz WAV, cleaned transcriptions)
  2. Sample 50,000 utterances per training set according to experimental design
  3. Fine-tune pre-trained wav2vec 2.0 with frozen conv layers for 50 epochs
  4. Evaluate on official test set, break down WER by demographic attributes

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Freezing conv layers speeds training (~15%) with minimal WER impact
  - Fixed training set size (50k utterances) controls for data quantity while varying speaker count/diversity
  - Using only CTC loss (no language model) keeps evaluation focused on acoustic modeling

- Failure signatures:
  - WER plateaus early or diverges: learning rate too high/low, insufficient data augmentation, or corrupted audio
  - Demographic subgroup WERs diverge widely: pre-training bias too strong, fine-tuning set too small/demographically narrow
  - High variance in repeated runs: random seed sensitivity, small dev set for early stopping

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Fine-tune on reference (random 50k subset) → establish baseline WER ~13.7
  2. Fine-tune on high-diversity demographic set → test if balanced demographics reduce subgroup bias
  3. Fine-tune on large speaker count set (e.g., 9,500 speakers) → verify speaker count drives WER improvement

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the quality of invalidated samples in Common Voice significantly impact ASR model performance, and if so, what threshold of sample quality is optimal for training?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors found models trained on invalidated samples scored significantly worse (WER 16.7) than those trained on validated samples (WER 13.7 ± 0.1), suggesting sample quality does matter.
- Why unresolved: Paper does not investigate relationship between degree of sample quality and model performance, nor explore what constitutes acceptable quality threshold for training.
- What evidence would resolve it: Study varying proportion of invalidated samples in training sets and measuring resulting WER, along with analysis of which specific quality issues most impact performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does fine-tuning step influence demographic biases in wav2vec 2.0 models, and can targeted fine-tuning on specific demographic subgroups reduce these biases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors found models trained on biased training sets (e.g., 100% female speech) did not perform better on targeted demographic subgroup than reference model, suggesting fine-tuning alone may not be sufficient to reduce demographic biases.
- Why unresolved: Paper does not investigate whether pre-training model on more diverse dataset before fine-tuning could reduce demographic biases, nor explore impact of fine-tuning on other demographic attributes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Study pre-training wav2vec 2.0 models on diverse datasets and then fine-tuning them on biased training sets, measuring resulting demographic biases and comparing to models fine-tuned without pre-training.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does speaker diversity have greater impact on ASR performance than demographic diversity, and what is optimal balance between the two?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors found models trained on larger number of speakers achieved lower WERs and reduced biases across demographic subgroups, suggesting speaker diversity is more important than demographic diversity.
- Why unresolved: Paper does not investigate optimal balance between speaker and demographic diversity, nor explore impact of speaker diversity on other aspects of ASR performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Study varying speaker and demographic diversity of training sets and measuring resulting WERs and demographic biases, as well as other performance metrics, to determine optimal balance.

## Limitations
- Findings constrained by specific characteristics of French Common Voice dataset and pre-trained wav2vec 2.0 model
- Conclusions about speaker diversity importance may not generalize to other languages or datasets with different demographic distributions
- Analysis limited to fine-tuning scenarios with fixed training set sizes (50,000 utterances)

## Confidence
- High confidence: Observation that increasing speaker count improves overall WER performance across demographic subgroups
- Medium confidence: Claim that demographic balancing alone cannot overcome pre-existing model biases
- Medium confidence: Assertion that quality labels have minimal impact on fine-tuning performance

## Next Checks
1. Cross-language validation: Repeat experimental protocol using Common Voice datasets in other languages to test whether speaker diversity consistently outweighs demographic diversity across linguistic contexts
2. Pre-training intervention: Fine-tune model on demographically balanced dataset created from scratch (not pre-trained) to isolate effects of pre-training bias versus fine-tuning bias
3. Dataset size scaling: Vary training set size beyond 50,000 utterances while maintaining speaker diversity vs. demographic diversity tradeoff to determine if observed effects scale with data quantity
