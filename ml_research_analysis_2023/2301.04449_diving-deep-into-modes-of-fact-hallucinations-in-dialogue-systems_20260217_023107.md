# Diving Deep into Modes of Fact Hallucinations in Dialogue Systems
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2301.04449
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04449
- **Reference count**: 18
- **Key outcome**: Creates FADE dataset with 90.75% F1 for utterance-level hallucination detection and 75.59% F1 for token-level detection

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of detecting fact hallucinations in knowledge graph-grounded dialogue systems by introducing a comprehensive framework that categorizes eight types of hallucinations and creates a synthetic dataset (FADE) to train detection models. The authors find that extrinsic-soft hallucinations are the most prevalent type and demonstrate that their synthetic dataset generalizes well to established benchmarks like BEGIN and FaithDial. Through extensive experiments, they show that fine-grained perturbation strategies enable strong performance in both utterance-level classification and token-level sequence labeling tasks, achieving up to 90.75% F1 for utterance detection and 75.59% F1 for token detection.

## Method Summary
The authors create FADE (FActual Dialogue Hallucination DEtection Dataset) through synthetic perturbation of knowledge graph triples in the OpenDialKG dataset. They identify eight hallucination categories (extrinsic-soft, extrinsic-hard, extrinsic-grouped, intrinsic-soft, intrinsic-hard, intrinsic-repetitive, and history-corrupted) and apply targeted perturbation strategies to simulate each type. The dataset is constructed by mixing component datasets in specific ratios (60% extrinsic, 35% intrinsic, 5% history-corrupted) to reflect real-world distributions. Pretrained transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa, XLNet) are fine-tuned on FADE for both utterance-level classification and token-level detection tasks. The models are evaluated on both the synthetic test set and established benchmarks (BEGIN and FaithDial) to assess generalization capability.

## Key Results
- FADE achieves up to 90.75% F1 for utterance-level hallucination detection and 75.59% F1 for token-level detection
- Extrinsic-soft hallucinations are identified as the most prevalent type in knowledge graph-grounded dialogue systems
- FADE generalizes well to established benchmarks BEGIN and FaithDial with strong zero-shot inference performance
- Human-verified data shows significant improvements over existing hallucination detection methods
- Models trained on FADE detect extrinsic hallucinations more accurately than intrinsic hallucinations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Synthetic Perturbation-Based Datasets
The paper demonstrates that synthetic datasets created through controlled entity perturbations can effectively simulate real-world hallucinations. By using BM25 and GPT2 embedding similarity metrics to select perturbed entities, the synthetic data captures nuanced hallucination patterns that models encounter during inference. This approach addresses the challenge of expensive human annotation while maintaining detection performance.

### Mechanism 2: Fine-Grained Perturbation Strategies
The eight-category taxonomy enables more precise hallucination detection compared to coarse-grained approaches. By distinguishing between extrinsic-soft, extrinsic-hard, intrinsic-soft, and other subcategories, models can learn to detect hallucinations at the token level with higher precision. This fine-grained approach achieves up to 75.59% F1 for token-level detection.

### Mechanism 3: Mixed Dataset Construction
Combining multiple perturbation strategies in specific ratios (60% extrinsic, 35% intrinsic, 5% history-corrupted) creates a more robust hallucination detection dataset that better reflects real-world distributions. This mixed approach improves generalization to established benchmarks like BEGIN and FaithDial, demonstrating that training on diverse hallucination types prepares models for varied real-world scenarios.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How do the different hallucination types impact user satisfaction in real conversations?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper identifies eight hallucination types and measures their prevalence in generated responses, finding extrinsic-soft hallucinations are most common.
- **Why unresolved**: The study focuses on detection performance rather than downstream effects on conversation quality or user experience.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: User studies comparing conversation satisfaction when different hallucination types are present versus absent.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the relationship between perturbation strategy effectiveness and the underlying language model's knowledge coverage?
- **Basis in paper**: The perturbation strategies rely on BM25 search over knowledge graph triples, suggesting effectiveness depends on how well the KG represents the entity space.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper doesn't analyze how perturbation success varies with KG completeness or entity distribution.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Correlation analysis between KG coverage metrics and perturbation success rates across different entity types.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How do hallucination detection models perform when applied to models with different architectures (e.g., GPT-3 vs. GPT-2)?
- **Basis in paper**: The study uses GPT-2 based models for data generation and tests on benchmark datasets, but doesn't evaluate cross-model generalization.
- **Why unresolved**: The perturbation strategies and detection models are developed using specific model architectures without testing robustness across different model families.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Detection performance comparison when models are trained on data from one model architecture but tested on outputs from another.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data may not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world hallucinations, limiting ecological validity
- Human-verified subset contains only 500 samples, which limits statistical power of validation studies
- Mixing ratios for creating FADE (60% extrinsic, 35% intrinsic, 5% history-corrupted) appear arbitrary without empirical justification

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Categorization of eight hallucination types is methodologically sound and provides valuable taxonomy for the field
- **Medium Confidence**: F1 scores achieved on FADE datasets are reliable for synthetic data but their generalizability to real hallucinations remains uncertain
- **Low Confidence**: Mixing ratios for FADE dataset construction lack empirical justification and the claim about extrinsic-soft prevalence relies entirely on synthetic data patterns

## Next Checks
1. **Ecological Validity Assessment**: Create a small but representative sample of real hallucination cases from deployed dialogue systems and compare detection performance against FADE-trained models
2. **Perturbation Strategy Ablation**: Systematically remove each perturbation type to determine which categories contribute most to generalization performance
3. **Cross-Dataset Consistency**: Train models on each individual perturbation dataset separately and evaluate zero-shot performance on BEGIN and FaithDial to identify which hallucination types generalize better than others
