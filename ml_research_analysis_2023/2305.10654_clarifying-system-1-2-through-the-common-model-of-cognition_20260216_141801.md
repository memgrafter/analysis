# Clarifying System 1 & 2 through the Common Model of Cognition

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10654
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10654
- Reference count: 1
- Primary result: The Common Model of Cognition shows that System-1 and System-2 traits form a spectrum rather than a strict dichotomy.

## Executive Summary
This paper uses the Common Model of Cognition to critically examine dual-system theories of System-1 and System-2. The analysis reveals that traditional distinctions between these systems—such as procedural vs. declarative memory, automatic vs. effortful processing, and independent vs. dependent functioning—do not cleanly align with the Common Model's architecture. Both systems require procedural knowledge and working memory, and effort can occur in both domains. The paper concludes that System-1 and System-2 characteristics exist on a continuum rather than as opposing categories.

## Method Summary
The paper applies computational thinking to map dual-system theories onto the Common Model of Cognition framework. It analyzes how System-1 and System-2 properties interact with the Common Model's five core components: procedural memory (productions), working memory buffers, declarative memory, emotion modules, and metacognition modules. The method involves theoretical analysis of how effort, emotion, and metacognitive control manifest across both systems within this unified architecture.

## Key Results
- System-1 and System-2 characteristics form a spectrum rather than a strict dichotomy
- Both systems require procedural knowledge and working memory buffers
- Effort is not exclusive to System-2 and can occur in System-1 operations
- The traditional alignment assumption of dual systems as opposites is overly simplistic

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: System-1 and System-2 traits form a continuous spectrum rather than a strict dichotomy.
- Mechanism: The Common Model of Cognition integrates procedural and declarative memory systems, both of which require working memory and can exhibit effortful or automatic behaviors depending on task demands.
- Core assumption: The traditional dual-system distinction is too rigid and does not account for shared computational mechanisms.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Results show that characteristics thought to be distinctive of System-1 and System-2 instead form a spectrum of cognitive properties.
  - Between the two extremities lies a spectrum where characteristics are mixed.
  - Corpus signals show multiple papers revisiting dual-system frameworks through computational lenses.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: System-2 is not functionally independent from System-1; it is grounded in procedural knowledge.
- Mechanism: The Common Model's production system (procedural memory) underlies both System-1 automaticity and System-2 rationality.
- Core assumption: Procedural knowledge is necessary for both implicit and explicit cognitive operations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - System-2 cannot operate separately due to architectural constraints of the Common Model.
  - Even if a System-2 process were primarily driven by declarative knowledge, it would still require System-1 procedural knowledge.
  - Corpus shows emerging dual-system computational models that embed both systems within shared architectures.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Effort is not exclusive to System-2; it can also be present in System-1 operations.
- Mechanism: Effort correlates with computational resource allocation and working memory intensity, which can occur in both procedural and declarative processes.
- Core assumption: Effort is a continuum rather than a binary attribute tied to a specific system.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Effort can be directed toward both System-2 rationality and System-1 metacognitive control.
  - The Common Model requires working memory for both System-1 and System-2 type operations.
  - Weak corpus evidence; cited papers focus more on system integration than effort allocation.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Procedural memory (productions) → working memory buffers → declarative memory retrieval; Emotion modules (amygdala, somatic markers) feed into both systems; Metacognition modules monitor and control both systems
- Critical path: Production rules fire based on buffer conditions → actions executed → declarative retrieval if needed → higher-level reasoning via more productions
- Design tradeoffs: Unified architecture simplifies integration but blurs traditional dual-system boundaries; increased computational realism may reduce interpretability for non-technical audiences
- Failure signatures: Misalignment between theoretical dual-system descriptions and computational constraints; over-attribution of effort to System-2 when task demands are high; neglecting the role of working memory in System-1 operations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the Common Model of Cognition definitively support or refute the System-1 and System-2 framework as distinct systems?
- Basis in paper: The paper concludes that the alignment assumption is a false dichotomy and that the framework is far from settled.
- Why unresolved: The Common Model suggests properties form a spectrum, but some cases may still cleanly bifurcate. The fundamental question of whether System-1 and System-2 are valid constructs remains open.
- What evidence would resolve it: Neuroscientific evidence showing distinct neural substrates or computational evidence demonstrating that properties cannot be explained by a single unified cognitive architecture.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can emotion and affect be formally incorporated into the Common Model of Cognition?
- Basis in paper: While functional accounts of emotions have been built in cognitive models, the question of how to model emotion in the Common Model remains unresolved.
- Why unresolved: Despite various approaches (amygdala states, somatic markers, appraisal theories), there is no consensus on formal integration.
- What evidence would resolve it: A computational implementation that successfully integrates emotional processes while maintaining core principles and improving predictive accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Is "effort" a fundamental distinction between System-1 and System-2, or is it better conceptualized as a continuum?
- Basis in paper: Effort is not traditionally well defined, nor is it the sole privilege of System-2, and it can be involved in processes characteristic of both systems.
- Why unresolved: The concept of effort remains vague and context-dependent, suggesting it may be better understood as a continuum.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies measuring cognitive resource allocation across different tasks to demonstrate whether effort follows a continuous distribution or discrete categories.

## Limitations
- The analysis does not fully address empirical neuroscience evidence for distinct neural substrates of System-1 and System-2 processes
- The paper does not resolve how to quantify effort and emotion within the computational framework
- Boundary conditions where System-1 and System-2 might remain functionally distinct under extreme conditions are not explored

## Confidence

- High confidence: Both System-1 and System-2 require procedural knowledge and working memory is well-supported by the Common Model architecture and computational constraints
- Medium confidence: The spectrum model of System-1 and System-2 traits is theoretically coherent but requires empirical validation across diverse cognitive tasks
- Low confidence: The dismissal of functional independence between System-1 and System-2 may overlook specific neural or behavioral evidence for separation
