# Understanding the Effect of Data Augmentation on Knowledge Distillation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12565
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12565
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: KD prefers larger semantic shift degrees (30% sweet spot) and augmented data over synthesized data for moderate-sized datasets

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how data augmentation methods affect knowledge distillation (KD) performance. The authors find that KD prefers augmented data over synthesized data due to lower cost and higher quantity. They also discover that KD benefits from larger semantic shift degrees in data augmentation, with 30% being a sweet spot for moderate-sized datasets. Smaller datasets prefer even larger degrees (up to 50%) until the out-of-distribution problem occurs. The authors provide theoretical analysis and empirical results to support these findings across multiple datasets and model architectures.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct experiments using multiple natural language understanding datasets (MNLI, QNLI, CoLA, MRPC, RTE, QQP, and biomedical datasets). They fine-tune teacher models (BERT-Large, EncT5-Large) on full datasets, then apply knowledge distillation with varying semantic shift degrees using kNN augmentation (k=15) and online augmentation with batch size 64 and 50,000 distillation steps. They compare KD performance across different semantic shift degrees (10%-80%) and evaluate against vanilla KD and fine-tuning baselines using student models like DistilBERT and EncT5-Small.

## Key Results
- KD benefits more from larger semantic shift degrees than fine-tuning, with 30% being optimal for moderate-sized datasets
- Smaller datasets prefer larger semantic shift degrees (up to 50%) until out-of-distribution problems occur
- Augmented data is preferable to synthesized data in KD due to lower cost and higher quantity
- The teacher model's label distributions make KD more tolerant to semantic shifts

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Knowledge distillation benefits more from larger semantic shift degrees than fine-tuning.
- Mechanism: In KD, the teacher model provides label distributions, making the system more tolerant to semantic shifts. This allows for more aggressive data augmentation without losing semantic meaning.
- Core assumption: Teacher models can effectively handle and interpret data with larger semantic shifts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "However, such data augmentation methods are sub-optimal for knowledge distillation since the teacher model could provide label distributions and is more tolerant to semantic shifts."
  - [section 4.1]: "Intuitively, a higher semantic shift degree will produce more diverse data and fully utilize teachers' abilities."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence in corpus about semantic shift tolerance.
- Break condition: If the teacher model cannot handle the increased semantic shift, leading to degraded performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Smaller datasets prefer larger semantic shift degrees until the out-of-distribution problem occurs.
- Mechanism: With less data, the model needs more diverse examples to generalize well. Larger semantic shifts provide this diversity but must be controlled to avoid OOD issues.
- Core assumption: Diversity in training data is crucial for smaller datasets to generalize effectively.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Besides, our findings show that smaller datasets prefer larger degrees until the out-of-distribution problem occurs (e.g., datasets with less than 10k inputs may prefer the 50% degree, and datasets with more than 100k inputs may prefer the 10% degree)."
  - [section 5.1]: "Another conclusion is that KD prefers larger degrees when the dataset is smaller, which aligns with our theory."
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct evidence in corpus about dataset size and semantic shift preferences.
- Break condition: When the semantic shift degree becomes too large, causing the model to encounter out-of-distribution examples.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Augmented data is preferable to synthesized data in KD due to lower cost and higher quantity.
- Mechanism: Synthesized data, while higher quality, is expensive to produce and limits the quantity available. Augmented data is cheaper and can be generated in larger quantities, which is beneficial for KD.
- Core assumption: Quantity of data is more important than quality in KD settings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We first observe that KD prefers as much data as possible, which is different from fine-tuning that too much data will not gain more performance."
  - [section 3]: "We can conclude that (1) More data improves KD performance more."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence in corpus about data augmentation paradigms.
- Break condition: If the cost of generating synthesized data decreases significantly or if quality becomes more critical than quantity.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Teacher Model (BERT-Large, EncT5-Large) -> Provides label distributions and tolerates semantic shifts
  - Student Model (DistilBERT, EncT5-Small) -> Learns from teacher using KD
  - Data Augmentation (kNN, EDA, Random Replacement, back translation) -> Generates more training data
  - Semantic Shift Degree (10%-80% token replacement) -> Controls degree of data modification

- Critical path:
  1. Train teacher model on full dataset
  2. Use data augmentation to generate more training data for student model
  3. Apply KD with different semantic shift degrees to find optimal setting

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Higher semantic shift degrees provide more diversity but risk OOD problems
  - Augmented data is cheaper and more abundant than synthesized data but may be of lower quality

- Failure signatures:
  - Degradation in student model performance with extremely high semantic shift degrees
  - Limited improvement in KD performance with very small datasets

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the effectiveness of different semantic shift degrees vary across different types of natural language understanding tasks (e.g., sentiment analysis, question answering, named entity recognition)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that 30% degree is generally best for moderate-sized datasets, with smaller datasets preferring larger degrees, but only tests on classification tasks.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only cover text classification datasets (MNLI, QNLI, COLA, MRPC, RTE, QQP, and biomedical datasets). The paper acknowledges the need to extend findings to "natural language generation tasks" but doesn't provide empirical evidence.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison of semantic shift degrees across diverse NLP tasks including sequence labeling, generation, and multi-task benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal way to represent and measure semantic shift degrees beyond simple token replacement proportion?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that "We use the proportion of changed tokens to reflect the semantic shift degree. Though intuitive, it is not comprehensive enough" and notes problems with random replacement vs. kNN.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses token replacement proportion as a proxy but identifies it as "not comprehensive enough" and shows that random replacement behaves differently from kNN even with the same proportion.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of semantic shift metrics that account for semantic similarity, syntactic structure preservation, and downstream task relevance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do data augmentation strategies for KD perform when the teacher model is much larger than the student model (e.g., GPT-3 to BERT distillation)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper tests BERT-Large to DistilBERT and EncT5-Large to EncT5-Small, but these are relatively similar in scale. The findings about quantity over quality suggest interesting questions about extreme size differences.
- Why unresolved: All teacher-student pairs tested have teacher models only 4-10x larger than students. The paper's findings about augmented data preference over synthesized data may not hold when the quality gap between teacher and student is extreme.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing augmentation strategies when distilling from very large language models to small models, testing whether synthesized data becomes more valuable when the teacher is significantly more capable.

## Limitations

- Missing implementation details for kNN augmentation method, including neighbor selection and application during training
- Lack of information about back translation method's hyperparameter settings, specifically which eight target languages are used
- Limited testing across diverse NLP task types, focusing primarily on text classification

## Confidence

- High Confidence: KD benefits from larger semantic shift degrees than fine-tuning; augmented data is preferable to synthesized data
- Medium Confidence: Smaller datasets prefer larger semantic shift degrees until OOD problems occur
- Low Confidence: The specific "sweet spot" of 30% semantic shift degree for moderate-sized datasets may be context-dependent
