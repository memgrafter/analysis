# Prioritized offline Goal-swapping Experience Replay

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.07741
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07741
- Reference count: 5
- This paper introduces PGSER, achieving strong performance on challenging dexterous tasks through prioritized goal-swapping experience replay

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the generalizability problem in offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning by introducing Prioritized Goal-swapping Experience Replay (PGSER). The method improves upon standard goal-swapping augmentation by using a pre-trained Q-function to assign higher sampling priority to transitions that are more likely to be reachable, effectively filtering out less useful augmentations. PGSER significantly outperforms baseline methods across a range of benchmark tasks, including previously challenging dexterous in-hand manipulation tasks.

## Method Summary
PGSER operates in two stages: first, a Q-function is pre-trained using TD3BC with random goal-swapping augmentation to learn reachability estimates; second, the main RL agent trains using combined original data and prioritized augmented transitions. The prioritization uses the pre-trained Q-values to estimate the likelihood of reaching goals from state-action pairs, storing augmented transitions in a PER-style buffer where sampling probability is proportional to these Q-value estimates. This approach filters out unreachable goal-swapped transitions while maintaining the benefits of augmented data diversity.

## Key Results
- PGSER significantly outperforms baseline methods including TD3 and TD3BC on Fetch manipulation and dexterous in-hand manipulation tasks
- The method achieves strong performance on previously challenging dexterous tasks like HandBlock-Z and HandEgg, demonstrating improved generalizability
- PGSER shows particular effectiveness in domains where random goal-swapping alone can degrade performance due to negative augmentations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Prioritized goal-swapping improves offline goal-conditioned RL by filtering out unreachable goal-swapped transitions.
- Mechanism: Uses pre-trained Q-function to estimate reachability and assign higher priority to transitions likely to lead to reachable goals.
- Core assumption: Q-function can accurately estimate expected horizon to reach goals from state-action pairs.
- Evidence: Fig. 6 shows Q-value distributions clearly separate reachable from unreachable transitions.
- Break condition: If Q-function cannot distinguish reachable from unreachable transitions, prioritization becomes ineffective.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Goal-swapping augmentation increases effective state-goal space coverage beyond original dataset.
- Mechanism: Randomly swapping goals between trajectories creates new state-goal pairs connected through Q-learning's TD updates.
- Core assumption: All goals are reachable from all states in the same environment.
- Evidence: Method creates "virtually infinite amount of new goal-conditioned trajectories" through goal swapping.
- Break condition: If reachability assumption fails, method prioritizes invalid transitions and performance degrades.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Additional prioritized buffer enables efficient sampling of high-quality augmented transitions.
- Mechanism: Maintains separate PER-style buffer for goal-swapped transitions with sampling probability proportional to pre-trained Q-value estimates.
- Core assumption: Higher Q-values indicate higher likelihood of successful goal achievement.
- Evidence: Agent samples goal-swapped augmented transitions according to their priority values during training.
- Break condition: If pre-trained Q-function is poorly calibrated, prioritization samples low-quality transitions more frequently.

  - Why needed here: Method relies on Q-learning's ability to propagate value estimates across connected state-goal pairs.
  - Quick check question: How does TD-learning enable "chaining" of skills across different trajectories in goal-swapped experience replay?

  - Why needed here: Method extends PER to work with goal-swapped transitions, requiring understanding of how prioritization improves sample efficiency.
  - Quick check question: In standard PER, what determines sampling priority of an experience, and how is this adapted in PGSER?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Original dataset buffer β -> Pre-trained Q-function -> Prioritized goal-swapping buffer βaug -> RL agent (TD3/BC) -> Training updates
- Critical path: Pre-train Q-function with random goal-swapping → Generate prioritized augmented transitions → Store in βaug with priority weights → Sample from both β and βaug during training → Update RL agent
- Design tradeoffs: Trades additional computation (pre-training Q-function, maintaining priority buffer) for improved data efficiency and generalization; reachability assumption simplifies prioritization but may not hold in all environments.
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on tasks where pre-trained Q-function cannot distinguish reachable from unreachable transitions; degraded performance when offline dataset contains mostly unsuccessful trajectories.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement random goal-swapping augmentation and verify it generates valid state-goal pairs by visualizing examples from FetchPush task.
  2. Train pre-trained Q-function using TD3BC with random goal-swapping augmentation and plot Q-value distributions for reachable vs unreachable transitions.
  3. Implement prioritized goal-swapping buffer and run small-scale experiment comparing sampling distributions from priority buffer vs uniform sampling on FetchReach task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does PGSER performance compare to other state-of-the-art offline RL methods like Actionable Models (AM) and Hindsight Experience Replay (HER)?
- How does the choice of pre-trained Q-function impact PGSER performance? Can other value function estimation methods be used instead of TD3BC?
- How does PGSER perform in environments with continuous state and action spaces?
- How does PGSER scale with the size of the offline dataset and the complexity of the tasks?

## Limitations
- The reachability assumption may not hold in environments with constrained state spaces or asymmetric goal relationships
- Performance depends heavily on the quality of the pre-trained Q-function, which may be difficult to obtain in truly offline settings
- The computational overhead of maintaining and sampling from the prioritized buffer could be prohibitive for very large datasets

## Confidence
- Technical validity: High confidence in prioritized goal-swapping mechanism and TD3/BC integration
- Empirical results: Medium confidence due to limited diversity of evaluation environments
- Scalability claims: Medium confidence as method hasn't been tested on larger, more complex benchmarks

## Next Checks
1. Test PGSER on a benchmark with explicit reachability constraints (e.g., maze navigation with dead ends) to verify the prioritization mechanism correctly identifies unreachable transitions
2. Evaluate the method's sensitivity to the quality of the pre-trained Q-function by training with varying amounts of data and measuring performance degradation
3. Compare the computational efficiency against standard goal-swapping methods by measuring wall-clock training time and memory usage on existing benchmarks
