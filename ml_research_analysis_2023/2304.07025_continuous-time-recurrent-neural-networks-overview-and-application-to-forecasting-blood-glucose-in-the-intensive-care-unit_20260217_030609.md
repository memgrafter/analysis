# Continuous time recurrent neural networks: overview and application to forecasting blood glucose in the intensive care unit

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.07025
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07025
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Continuous time models (e.g., ODE-LSTM) generally outperform discrete counterparts in point prediction (RMSE) but are often outperformed by gradient boosted trees (Catboost) on probabilistic metrics like CRPS, ignorance score, and interval score in blood glucose forecasting.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents an overview of continuous time recurrent neural networks (CTRNNs) for modeling irregularly measured time series, with a focus on medical applications. CTRNNs incorporate continuous evolution of hidden states between observations using neural ODEs or neural flows, addressing the challenge of irregular measurements without imputation. The methods are evaluated on forecasting blood glucose in ICU using both simulated and real electronic medical record (EMR) data. Across multiple experiments, continuous time models generally outperform their discrete counterparts in point prediction, but are often outperformed by gradient boosted tree methods on probabilistic metrics.

## Method Summary
The paper evaluates CTRNN models with neural ODEs or flows against discrete RNNs and gradient boosted trees (Catboost) for blood glucose forecasting in ICU settings. The models are trained with combined prediction and jump losses to handle irregularly sampled time series. Experiments use both simulated data (1,000-20,000 trajectories of blood glucose measurements over 24 hours) and real EMR data (772,784 measurements from 44,334 ICU stays). Performance is assessed using probabilistic forecasting metrics including CRPS, ignorance score, and interval score.

## Key Results
- ODE-LSTM models show improved point prediction performance over discrete LSTM models
- Gradient boosted trees (Catboost) achieve comparable or better performance on probabilistic metrics (CRPS: 0.118±0.001 vs 0.118±0.001)
- The study highlights trade-offs between deep learning and tree-based models in handling irregularly sampled clinical time series

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Continuous hidden state evolution between observations allows CTRNNs to model irregularly sampled time series without imputation.
- Mechanism: The hidden state z(t) evolves continuously according to a neural ODE or neural flow between observation times. When a new observation occurs, the hidden state updates via a jump function (e.g., GRU or LSTM update) incorporating the new data, then continues evolving until the next observation.
- Core assumption: The underlying physiological process generating the time series can be approximated by a continuous-time dynamical system with jump discontinuities at observation times.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section], [corpus]
- Break condition: If the physiological process has truly discrete dynamics or the measurement times are too sparse for meaningful continuous interpolation, the continuous evolution assumption breaks down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Incorporating both autoregressive and exogenous input components allows CTRNNs to model both past observations and ongoing interventions.
- Mechanism: The model takes previous observations of the target y(t) and exogenous variables x(t) as input. The hidden state evolution can depend on these inputs, allowing the model to capture how interventions like insulin affect future blood glucose values.
- Core assumption: The relationship between interventions and the target variable can be captured by the neural network mapping inputs to hidden state dynamics.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section], [corpus]
- Break condition: If the intervention effect is too complex or non-stationary to be captured by the neural network, or if key exogenous variables are missing from the input.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Training CTRNNs with a combination of prediction and jump losses enables accurate probabilistic forecasting.
- Mechanism: The loss function has two components: (1) Prediction loss - negative log likelihood of observed data given hidden state before update; (2) Jump loss - negative log likelihood or KL divergence between observed data and distribution implied by hidden state after update. This ensures both the continuous evolution and discrete updates are optimized.
- Core assumption: The assumed distribution P_θ of the target variable matches the true data distribution well enough for the log likelihood/KL divergence to be meaningful.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract], [section], [corpus]
- Break condition: If the assumed distribution is a poor fit to the data, or if the jump loss weight λ is not well-tuned, the combined loss may not lead to good probabilistic forecasts.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input layer -> Neural ODE/flow layer -> Jump function (GRU/LSTM) -> Output layer
- Critical path: Initialize hidden state -> At each observation: update hidden state, evolve continuously until next observation, produce forecast -> Optimize combined prediction and jump losses
- Design tradeoffs: Choice of ODE solver (accuracy vs computational cost), choice of jump function (GRU vs LSTM), choice of output distribution (normal vs others)
- Failure signatures: Poor forecast uncertainty quantification (overdispersion), numerical instability in ODE solver, overfitting to training data
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation study: Compare CTRNN with and without continuous dynamics layer on simulated data with known ground truth.
  2. Sensitivity analysis: Vary ODE solver step size and jump loss weight λ to understand their impact on forecast accuracy.
  3. Benchmark comparison: Compare CTRNN against discrete-time RNN and gradient boosted tree baselines on real-world blood glucose forecasting task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the continuous time model performance improvement over discrete time models persist when measurement error variance is dynamically modeled instead of fixed?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes measurement error is a known issue in ICU blood glucose measurements and varies with value, device, and patient characteristics, but fixed variance was used in experiments.
- Why unresolved: The simulation studies used fixed measurement error variance, not accounting for the dynamic nature of measurement error in real-world settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Re-running the simulation studies with measurement error variance modeled as a function of blood glucose value, measurement device, and patient characteristics, then comparing continuous vs discrete time model performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of continuous time models compare to gradient boosted trees when the data frequency increases from irregularly sparse to moderately frequent measurements?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that results may not generalize to more frequently measured but still irregular data settings, and that in the current study the data was both irregular and temporally sparse.
- Why unresolved: The experiments were conducted on irregularly measured time series with an average of only 7.5 observations over 24 hours, which is quite sparse.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments on datasets with varying measurement frequencies (e.g., hourly, every 2 hours) while maintaining irregularity, and comparing model performance across frequencies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would incorporating model interpretability methods improve the actionability of continuous time models for clinical decision-making in glycaemic control?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion notes that "model actionability" and "production of counterfactual predictions are key goals for future research."
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges this as a future direction but does not explore interpretability methods or their impact on clinical utility.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying interpretability techniques (e.g., SHAP values, feature importance) to continuous time models and assessing whether clinicians find the explanations useful for treatment decisions.

## Limitations

- The empirical evaluation relies on a single medical application domain (blood glucose forecasting), limiting generalizability to other time series forecasting tasks.
- Deep learning approaches do not uniformly outperform traditional machine learning methods on probabilistic forecasting metrics.
- The performance comparison with gradient boosted trees reveals potential limitations in CTRNNs' ability to capture complex relationships in clinical data.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The theoretical framework for CTRNNs and their ability to handle irregularly sampled time series through continuous hidden state evolution is well-supported by the mathematical formulation.
- **Medium Confidence**: The empirical results showing ODE-LSTM's improved performance over discrete counterparts on point prediction metrics are consistent across experiments, though the advantage is modest.
- **Low Confidence**: The claim that CTRNNs significantly outperform discrete RNNs on probabilistic forecasting metrics is not strongly supported, as gradient boosted trees achieve comparable or better performance on CRPS, ignorance score, and interval score.
