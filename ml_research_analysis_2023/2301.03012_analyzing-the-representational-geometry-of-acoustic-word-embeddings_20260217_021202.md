# Analyzing the Representational Geometry of Acoustic Word Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.03012
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03012
- Reference count: 14
- One-line primary result: Learning objectives shape acoustic word embedding geometry more than architectures, with contrastive methods showing highest discriminability but lowest representational consistency

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how different learning objectives and architectures shape the representational geometry of acoustic word embeddings (AWEs) using three analytical perspectives: embedding space uniformity, word discriminability, and network representational consistency. The authors evaluate four learning objectives (autoencoder, correspondence autoencoder, phonologically-guided encoder, and contrastive siamese encoder) with two architectures (recurrent and convolutional) on the LibriSpeech dataset. Their main finding is that learning objectives have a more significant impact on representational geometry than architectural choices, with contrastive objectives creating highly discriminative but less consistent representations compared to decoding-based approaches.

## Method Summary
The study trains AWE models on LibriSpeech using word-aligned speech samples converted to 39-dimensional Mel-frequency spectral coefficients. Four learning objectives are implemented with both recurrent (3-layer GRU with 512 units) and convolutional (3-layer 1D-CNN with increasing filters) architectures. The models are evaluated using three analyses: isotropy scores measuring embedding space uniformity, category discriminability index (CDI) quantifying word discriminability, and linear centered kernel alignment (CKA) measuring representational consistency. The authors systematically compare how each objective-architecture combination affects these geometric properties.

## Key Results
- Contrastive encoders show the largest differences between within-category and cross-category similarities
- Recurrent encoders are more isotropic than convolutional ones across all learning objectives
- Word discriminability correlates more strongly with acoustic distinctiveness than frequency
- Contrastive models exhibit the least representational consistency across different initializations

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Learning objectives shape representational geometry more than architectural choices
- Mechanism: The loss function defines how samples are grouped in embedding space, overriding architectural inductive biases
- Core assumption: Architectural differences between RNN and CNN are secondary to optimization signal
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our main findings highlight the prominent role of the learning objective on shaping the representation profile compared to the model architecture"
  - [section] "our three analyses in this paper have shown that the learning objective shapes the representational geometry of the AWE encoders more than their underlying architectures"
  - [corpus] Weak - neighboring papers focus on specific objectives rather than architecture comparisons
- Break condition: If architectural inductive biases become dominant (e.g., with weak supervision signals)

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Contrastive objectives create distinct representational profiles through explicit separation constraints
- Mechanism: Triplet margin loss forces maximal separation between word categories, creating more anisotropic but discriminable spaces
- Core assumption: Explicit separation signals override other geometric considerations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Contrastive encoders have the largest mean difference–with mean cross-category scores centered at the zero"
  - [section] "Contrastive objectives are less consistent than decoding-based objectives"
  - [corpus] Moderate - neighboring papers explore contrastive approaches but don't directly compare representational geometry
- Break condition: If margin hyperparameters are too small to create meaningful separation

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Isotropy correlates with downstream task performance through better utilization of embedding dimensions
- Mechanism: More isotropic spaces use all dimensions effectively, providing richer representations for discrimination tasks
- Core assumption: Uniform dimension utilization improves task performance
- Evidence anchors:

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the underlying cause of the minimal isotropy observed in AWEs, and why do untrained CNNs exhibit near-1 cosine similarity distributions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates that AWEs are minimally isotropic and notes that untrained CNNs produce near-1 cosine similarities, with an analysis showing this behavior is linked to unbounded activation functions like ReLU in convolutional layers.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies that ReLU contributes to this behavior, it states that pinpointing the exact source requires further investigation with different activation functions and a controlled ablation study.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying activation functions (e.g., ReLU vs. Tanh) and ablation studies on batch normalization in convolutional layers to isolate the exact architectural or mathematical cause.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does word frequency interact with other lexical properties (e.g., phonological neighborhood density, word length) to influence category discriminability in AWEs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that word frequency is a weak predictor of category discriminability (CDI), while acoustic distinctiveness (word length and PIC) has stronger correlations. It suggests future work could use linear mixed effects models to analyze interactions between these properties.
- Why unresolved: The paper only examines correlations between CDI and individual lexical properties, not their interactions or combined effects.
- What evidence would resolve it: Linear mixed effects models or similar statistical analyses incorporating multiple lexical predictors (frequency, phonological neighborhood density, word length) to quantify their interactive effects on CDI.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why do contrastive AWE models exhibit lower representational consistency across different random initializations compared to decoding-based models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes that contrastive models are significantly less consistent (mean CKA ~0.61-0.74) than decoding-based models (mean CKA >0.87), despite similar performance stability on the evaluation task.
- Why unresolved: The paper hypothesizes that the margin-based grouping constraint in contrastive loss forces models to prioritize category separability, but does not test this hypothesis or explore alternative explanations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the margin hyperparameter, comparing different contrastive objectives, or analyzing the geometry of category boundaries in contrastive vs. non-contrastive models.

## Limitations
- The study focuses on word-level embeddings from a single dataset (LibriSpeech), limiting generalizability to subword or sentence-level tasks
- CKA captures linear relationships and may miss non-linear alignment between models
- All models tend toward minimally isotropic spaces, suggesting fundamental architectural constraints
