# Leveraging supplementary text data to kick-start automatic speech recognition system development with limited transcriptions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2302.04975
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04975
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** Using 10 minutes of transcribed speech plus 80k+ tokens of text data reduces WER to ~39% for Gronings and Frisian

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether minimal transcribed speech data (10 minutes) can be effectively leveraged to develop automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems when supplemented with modest amounts of text data. The study tests this approach across five languages (English, Gronings, Frisian, Besemah, and Nasal) using pre-trained transformer models (wav2vec 2.0 and XLS-R) and evaluates the impact of lexicon and language model integration. Results show that lexica alone provide minimal improvement, but combining lexica with language models derived from ~80k tokens of text significantly reduces word error rates to approximately 39% for minority languages, suggesting a viable path for ASR development in low-resource scenarios.

## Method Summary
The study fine-tunes pre-trained transformer models using only 10 minutes of transcribed speech from target languages. For each language, lexica are generated from available text corpora of varying sizes (8k, 80k, and 8M tokens), and language models are trained on these corpora. The ASR systems are evaluated using beam search decoding with and without lexicon constraints and language model integration. Word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER) are measured across all configurations to assess the impact of supplementary text data on ASR performance with minimal transcribed speech.

## Key Results
- Lexicon-only constraints do not significantly reduce WER across any tested language
- Language models derived from 80k+ token corpora reduce WER to ~39% for Gronings and Frisian
- Minimal transcribed speech (10 minutes) combined with sufficient text data can produce human-correctable transcriptions
- Out-of-vocabulary rates increase when using lexica without corresponding language model support

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Lexicon alone doesn't improve ASR performance significantly.
- Mechanism: A lexicon only restricts output to possible words but doesn't provide probabilistic guidance for word sequences, leaving high error rates unchanged.
- Core assumption: The ASR model already has a basic word recognition capability from fine-tuning, so restricting output to possible words doesn't add much value.
- Evidence anchors:
  - For all languages, we found that using only a lexicon did not appreciably reduce the WER.
  - For example, for English, the test set WER remained practically the same with or without a lexicon.
- Break condition: When the out-of-vocabulary rate is extremely high, a lexicon might actually hurt performance by forcing incorrect substitutions.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Language models derived from ~80k tokens significantly reduce WER.
- Mechanism: Language models provide probabilistic guidance for word sequences, biasing the ASR system toward more probable word combinations and reducing substitution errors.
- Core assumption: The pre-trained transformer model has learned good acoustic representations but needs linguistic context to resolve ambiguities in word sequences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - For Gronings and Frisian, we found that lexica and language models derived from 'novel-length' 80k token subcorpora reduced the word error rate (WER) to 39% on average.
- Break condition: If the text corpus is too small (<80k tokens), the language model won't capture enough linguistic patterns to be effective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Pre-trained transformer models can leverage limited transcribed speech when supplemented with sufficient text data.
- Mechanism: The transformer model's pre-training on large speech corpora provides strong acoustic representations that can be fine-tuned with minimal transcribed speech, while text data provides necessary linguistic context.
- Core assumption: The pre-trained model has learned generalizable acoustic features that transfer well to new languages/dialects with minimal fine-tuning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Our findings suggest that where a text corpus in the upper tens of thousands of tokens or more is available, fine-tuning a transformer model with just tens of minutes of transcribed speech holds some promise towards obtaining human-correctable transcriptions.
- Break condition: If the target language/dialect is too different from the pre-training data, the acoustic representations may not transfer well.

  - Why needed here: Understanding how language models bias ASR output toward probable word sequences
  - Quick check question: How does a language model score influence the final ASR output during beam search?

- **Word error rate (WER) calculation and interpretation**
  - Why needed here: Evaluating ASR system performance and understanding what constitutes "human-correctable" transcriptions
  - Quick check question: What WER threshold is typically considered acceptable for human correction?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Pre-trained transformer model -> Fine-tuning pipeline -> Lexicon generation -> Language model training -> Decoding pipeline with lexicon and LM integration

- **Critical path:**
  1. Fine-tune pre-trained model on 10 minutes of transcribed speech
  2. Generate lexicon from available text corpus
  3. Train language model on text corpus
  4. Perform beam search decoding with lexicon and language model
  5. Evaluate WER on test set

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Language model size vs. performance gain
  - Lexicon comprehensiveness vs. decoding speed
  - Out-of-domain vs. in-domain text corpus
  - Multilingual vs. monolingual pre-training

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High WER despite language model integration (>40%)
  - Out-of-vocabulary rate increasing with lexicon use
  - No improvement in WER when increasing text corpus size beyond 80k tokens

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Fine-tune model with 10 minutes of transcribed speech only (no text data)
  2. Fine-tune model with 10 minutes of transcribed speech + 8k token language model
  3. Fine-tune model with 10 minutes of transcribed speech + 80k token language model

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1**
  - Question: What is the optimal text corpus size for ASR performance when fine-tuning with 10 minutes of transcribed speech?
  - Basis in paper: The paper finds that lexica and language models derived from 'novel-length' 80k token subcorpora reduced the WER to 39% on average for Gronings and Frisian, but does not explore corpus sizes between 8k and 80k tokens.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only tests corpus sizes of 8k, 80k, and 8M tokens, leaving a gap in understanding the optimal size.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Additional experiments testing intermediate corpus sizes (e.g., 20k, 40k, 60k tokens) to identify the point of diminishing returns in WER reduction.

- **Open Question 2**
  - Question: How does pre-training on a language similar to the target language affect ASR performance when fine-tuning with limited transcribed speech?
  - Basis in paper: The paper notes that the multilingual XLS-R model was not pre-trained on the target datasets for Gronings and Frisian, unlike the English wav2vec 2.0 model, and suggests domain adaptation techniques could improve performance.
  - Why unresolved: The paper does not experiment with models pre-trained on similar languages (e.g., Dutch for Gronings and Frisian).
  - What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using models pre-trained on linguistically similar languages to assess improvements in WER.

- **Open Question 3**
  - Question: What is the impact of decomposing words into sub-word units on ASR performance with limited transcribed speech and small text corpora?
  - Basis in paper: The paper mentions that decomposing words into sub-word units (e.g., syllables) may be worth investigating for reducing the out-of-vocabulary rate with lexica and LMs derived from small text corpora.
  - Why unresolved: The paper does not test sub-word unit approaches in its experiments.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing word-level and sub-word unit (e.g., syllable) approaches to assess their impact on WER and out-of-vocabulary rates.

## Limitations

- Experiments conducted on only five languages, limiting generalizability across language families and morphological complexities
- Reliance on quality of pre-trained models' acoustic representations, which may not capture phonetic characteristics of all target languages equally well
- Does not explore impact of different text genres or temporal dynamics of language evolution on language model effectiveness

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The finding that lexica alone do not significantly improve ASR performance is well-supported by consistent results across all five languages tested.

**Medium Confidence:** The claim that 80k tokens of text data is sufficient for achieving ~39% WER is supported by results on Gronings and Frisian, but this finding is based on a limited sample of languages and text corpus sizes.

**Low Confidence:** The assertion that fine-tuning with just tens of minutes of transcribed speech can yield human-correctable transcriptions when sufficient supplementary text is available is promising but not conclusively demonstrated.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-linguistic validation:** Replicate the experiments on additional language families, particularly those with different morphological complexity (e.g., polysynthetic languages) and phonetic inventories, to determine whether the 80k token threshold is universal or language-specific.

2. **Text corpus composition analysis:** Systematically vary the genre, register, and temporal origin of the text corpus to measure how these factors influence language model effectiveness and WER reduction, isolating the effects of linguistic diversity versus sheer token count.

3. **Human correction feasibility study:** Conduct a user study where human transcribers attempt to correct ASR output at different WER levels (30%, 39%, 50%) to empirically determine what constitutes "human-correctable" and establish practical WER thresholds for different application domains.
