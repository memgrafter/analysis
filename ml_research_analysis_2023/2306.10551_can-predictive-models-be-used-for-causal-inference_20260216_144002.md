# Can predictive models be used for causal inference?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10551
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10551
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Unregularized deep neural networks can provide near-unbiased causal effect estimates under feature collinearity when constrained by causal feature selection

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether machine learning and deep learning algorithms can provide unbiased causal effect estimates under feature collinearity, a key requirement for causal inference. The authors propose using Pearl's backdoor adjustment criterion to constrain ML/DL learning processes by selecting features based on causal relationships. Through extensive simulations, they find that deep neural networks without regularization can provide near-unbiased effect estimates under collinearity, while other algorithms like random forests and elastic-net regression show varying degrees of bias primarily due to algorithmic regularization mechanisms.

## Method Summary
The authors propose constraining ML/DL learning processes using Pearl's backdoor adjustment criterion for feature selection based on causal relationships. They introduce average conditional effects (ACE) as a model-agnostic explainable AI metric to extract effect estimates from trained models. The method involves: (1) specifying the causal graph between features, (2) selecting features using backdoor criterion, (3) training ML/DL models on selected features, (4) computing ACE to extract effect estimates, (5) tuning hyperparameters separately for inference vs prediction, and (6) testing out-of-distribution generalization. The approach is validated through simulations with varying collinearity structures, sample sizes, and algorithms including RF, BRT, NN, and elastic-net.

## Key Results
- Deep neural networks without regularization provide near-unbiased effect estimates under collinearity
- Random forests and elastic-net show bias due to feature subsampling and L1/L2 penalties respectively
- Optimal hyperparameters for causal inference differ from those for prediction, but the trade-off effect is small compared to causal feature selection
- Causally constrained models show better generalization to out-of-distribution data with altered collinearity structures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: ML/DL models can provide unbiased causal effect estimates under feature collinearity if feature selection is constrained by Pearl's backdoor criterion.
- Mechanism: By selecting only causally relevant features, confounding effects are blocked and the model learns the true causal relationships without spurious correlations.
- Core assumption: The causal graph between features is known and correctly specified.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "it is possible to constrain the learning process of any ML and DL algorithm by selecting features according to Pearl's backdoor adjustment criterion."
  - [section] "if the causal graph is known, research in causal inference has solved the problem of how we should select features such that a statistical regression model (e.g. ordinary least squared (OLS)) would adjust for confounding such that the causal effect of one or several target variables is correctly estimated."
- Break condition: If the assumed causal graph is incorrect or incomplete, backdoor adjustment will fail and bias will remain.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Neural networks without explicit regularization can learn near-unbiased causal effects under collinearity.
- Mechanism: Unregularized NNs do not impose shrinkage or subsampling biases that push causal effects onto collinear features, unlike RF or elastic-net.
- Core assumption: The NN architecture and optimization process do not introduce implicit regularization that mimics sparsity/shrinkage.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "some algorithms, in particular deep neural networks, can provide near unbiased effect estimates under feature collinearity."
  - [section] "Pure (deep) NNs do not explicitly include any of the previously mentioned regularization mechanisms. Nevertheless, it is often reported that trained NNs display a simplicity bias akin to an implicit regularization..."
- Break condition: If the NN architecture or training process introduces implicit regularization (e.g. through architecture choices), bias may reappear.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Optimal hyperparameters for causal inference differ from those for prediction, but the trade-off is small compared to the effect of causal feature selection.
- Mechanism: Hyperparameters that control regularization strength affect bias-variance trade-off differently for inference vs prediction tasks, but causal feature selection has larger impact on bias.
- Core assumption: Hyperparameter optimization can be performed separately for inference and prediction objectives.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Consequently, optimal hyperparameter settings are different when tuned for prediction or inference, confirming the general expectation of a trade-off between prediction and explanation. However, the effect of this trade-off is small compared to the effect of a causally constrained feature selection."
  - [section] "Most importantly, although the bias-variance tradeoffs for inference and prediction often showed similar tendencies for hyperparameters, some hyperparameters had notably different effects for the two goals... This confirms that the bias-variance tradeoff is different for prediction and inference tasks."
- Break condition: If hyperparameter tuning is not performed separately for inference vs prediction, or if causal feature selection is not used, bias may remain large.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Causal inference and confounding adjustment
  - Why needed here: To understand why simply predicting well does not guarantee unbiased causal effect estimates, and why backdoor adjustment is needed.
  - Quick check question: What is a confounder and how does it bias effect estimates if not adjusted for?

- Concept: Feature collinearity and its effects on model estimation
  - Why needed here: To understand why algorithms like RF and elastic-net show bias under collinearity while unregularized NNs do not.
  - Quick check question: How does collinearity between features lead to biased effect estimates in regularized models?

- Concept: Average conditional effects (ACE) as a model-agnostic metric
  - Why needed here: To extract interpretable causal effect estimates from trained ML/DL models.
  - Quick check question: How is ACE computed and why does it correspond to OLS coefficients for linear effects?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Feature selection module -> ML/DL model -> ACE computation module -> Hyperparameter tuning module -> Out-of-distribution prediction module

- Critical path:
  1. Specify causal graph between features
  2. Select features using backdoor criterion
  3. Train ML/DL model on selected features
  4. Compute ACE to extract effect estimates
  5. Tune hyperparameters for inference vs prediction
  6. Test out-of-distribution generalization

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Causal feature selection vs predictive feature selection: Causal selection may reduce in-distribution prediction accuracy but improve out-of-distribution generalization
  - Regularization strength: Higher regularization reduces variance but increases bias in effect estimates
  - Model complexity: More complex models can capture nonlinear effects but may be harder to interpret

- Failure signatures:
  - High bias in ACE estimates: Suggests incorrect causal feature selection or inappropriate regularization
  - Poor out-of-distribution prediction: Suggests model learned spurious correlations not true causal effects
  - High variance in ACE estimates: Suggests insufficient sample size or overly complex model

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Simulate data with known causal structure and collinearity, compare ACE estimates from unregularized NN vs regularized models
  2. Vary sample size and measure bias in ACE estimates, test convergence to OLS
  3. Simulate out-of-distribution data with altered collinearity, compare in-distribution vs out-of-distribution prediction accuracy for causal vs non-causal models

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the exact mechanisms by which different ML algorithms exhibit biased effect estimates under feature collinearity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses various mechanisms like regularization, feature subsampling, and implicit biases, but the exact reasons for biases in specific algorithms remain unclear.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the existence of these mechanisms but does not provide a detailed explanation of how they specifically lead to biased estimates in each algorithm.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed studies analyzing the internal workings of each algorithm during training, specifically focusing on how they handle collinear features and the resulting biases.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do causally constrained ML models perform in real-world scenarios with complex causal structures and unknown confounders?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates the potential of causally constrained models in controlled simulations and a case study, but real-world applications may involve more complex scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on theoretical simulations and a simplified case study, which may not fully capture the complexities of real-world data and causal relationships.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies applying causally constrained ML models to real-world datasets with known causal structures, comparing their performance to traditional methods in terms of both prediction accuracy and causal effect estimation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we develop robust methods for feature selection and causal graph estimation in ML models when the causal relationships between features are unknown?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper assumes the causal graph is known, but in practice, this is often not the case.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not address the challenge of estimating causal relationships from data, which is a crucial step in applying causal inference techniques.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research on algorithms that can simultaneously learn causal relationships and estimate causal effects from observational data, potentially using techniques from causal discovery and reinforcement learning.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What are the optimal hyperparameter settings for ML models when the goal is to balance prediction accuracy and causal effect estimation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that optimal hyperparameters for prediction and inference can differ, but does not provide a clear strategy for finding the best balance.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the existence of a trade-off but does not offer a systematic approach to navigate it.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of hyperparameter optimization techniques that explicitly consider both prediction accuracy and causal effect estimation, potentially using multi-objective optimization or Pareto optimization.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How can we quantify the uncertainty in causal effect estimates obtained from ML models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses ACE to estimate effects but does not discuss methods for quantifying uncertainty.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on point estimates of causal effects but does not address the important issue of uncertainty quantification.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research on methods for constructing confidence intervals or credible intervals for causal effect estimates obtained from ML models, potentially using techniques from bootstrapping, Bayesian inference, or conformal prediction.

## Limitations

- Strong assumption that the causal graph between features is known and correctly specified
- Focus on simulated data with controlled collinearity structures may not capture real-world complexities
- Limited evaluation to tabular data types, with unclear generalizability to other data modalities

## Confidence

- High Confidence: Neural networks without explicit regularization can provide near-unbiased effect estimates under collinearity when causal feature selection is applied
- Medium Confidence: The trade-off between prediction and inference objectives is relatively small compared to the benefits of causal feature selection
- Medium Confidence: Causally constrained models show improved generalization to out-of-distribution data with altered collinearity structures
