# Training Multilayer Perceptrons by Sampling with Quantum Annealers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2303.12352
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12352
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Quantum annealers can train MLPs through equivalence with EBMs, achieving competitive accuracy on small MNIST/Fashion-MNIST subsets with favorable qubit scaling

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel approach to training multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) using quantum annealers by exploiting an equivalence between MLPs and energy-based models (EBMs). The authors demonstrate that under small weight assumptions, the gradients of binary cross-entropy loss for MLPs match the gradients of conditional log-likelihood for EBMs. This allows quantum annealers to sample from the EBM's proposal distribution, enabling quantum-assisted training of MLPs. Experiments on small binary classification tasks from MNIST and Fashion-MNIST datasets using a D-Wave quantum annealer validate the approach, showing test accuracy comparable to classical methods while requiring fewer qubits than previous quantum training approaches.

## Method Summary
The method establishes an equivalence between MLP training with sigmoid activation and EBM training with conditional log-likelihood under small weight assumptions. The MLP's weights are initialized and trained to minimize binary cross-entropy loss, while the EBM's weights are optimized using quantum sampling from the conditional distribution. The energy function of the EBM is reformulated as a Binary Quadratic Model (BQM) and converted to an Ising model for the quantum annealer. Quantum samples are used to estimate gradients, which are then used to update weights via the ADAM optimizer. After training, the optimized EBM weights are transferred to the MLP for evaluation.

## Key Results
- Quantum-assisted MLP training achieves comparable test accuracy to classical methods on small MNIST and Fashion-MNIST binary classification tasks
- The approach requires qubits equal to the number of hidden units plus one, allowing larger networks than previous quantum training methods
- Quantum sampling time remains roughly constant while classical sampling time grows linearly with model size
- Test accuracy is volatile during training but stabilizes to values comparable to classical approaches

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The equivalence between MLP training and EBM conditional log-likelihood gradients allows quantum sampling to train MLPs.
- **Mechanism**: By assuming small weights, the gradient of the binary cross-entropy loss for MLPs is shown to equal the gradient of the conditional log-likelihood for EBMs. This allows quantum annealers to sample from the EBM's proposal distribution, which in turn enables quantum-assisted training of MLPs.
- **Core assumption**: Small weight assumption (|W^(l)_i,j| ≪ 1 for l = 1, 2 and for all (i,j)) is valid during training.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "We exploit an equivalence between MLPs and energy-based models (EBM)... This leads to a strategy to train MLPs with quantum annealers as a sampling engine." [section] "We show that the models are 'dual' up to first-order approximations, in the sense that training one implies training the other..."
- **Break condition**: If weights grow large during training, the first-order Taylor expansion approximations break down, invalidating the equivalence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Quantum annealers can effectively sample from the EBM's proposal distribution for training.
- **Mechanism**: The EBM's energy function is reformulated as a Binary Quadratic Model (BQM), which is then converted to an Ising model for the quantum annealer. The annealer samples from a Boltzmann-like distribution determined by the Ising model parameters.
- **Core assumption**: The effective temperature β_eff can be properly tuned to match the target distribution.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section] "By exerting some level of control over the annealing process, quantum annealers can sample from Boltzmann-like distributions..." [section] "A grid search was conducted and the effective temperature β of 16 was found to be viable for MNIST and Fashion-MNIST."
- **Break condition**: If β_eff cannot be properly calibrated due to hardware noise or other factors, the samples will not represent the true EBM distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The number of qubits required scales favorably compared to previous quantum training methods.
- **Mechanism**: The BQM coefficient matrix Q has size (K + M) × (K + M), which depends only on the number of hidden units and outputs, not the input dimensionality. This allows larger networks to be trained with the same qubit count compared to methods that scale with input dimensions.
- **Core assumption**: The minor embedding overhead doesn't negate the qubit savings.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "Our approach requires the number of qubits equal to the number of hidden units plus one, and allows for non-binary weights and batch training. Compared to [33], our method allows larger networks to be trained given the same qubit count." [section] "Note that the size of Q does not depend on the dimensionality of the input x. This is in stark contrast to Sasdelli and Chin [33]..."
- **Break condition**: If the minor embedding step requires many physical qubits per logical qubit, the theoretical advantage may not translate to practice.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: MLP/EBM definition -> Weight initialization -> QUBO formulation -> Ising model conversion -> Quantum sampling -> Gradient computation -> ADAM optimization

- **Critical path**:
  1. Define MLP/EBM structure and initialize weights
  2. For each training batch:
     a. Compute gradient estimates using quantum sampling
     b. Update weights using ADAM optimizer
  3. Transfer optimized EBM weights to MLP for evaluation

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Small weight assumption vs. model capacity: Enforcing small weights limits the complexity of functions the network can learn.
  - Quantum sampling vs. classical sampling: Quantum sampling offers potential speedup but is limited by current hardware constraints and noise.
  - Input dimensionality independence vs. qubit requirements: The approach scales well with input size but still requires qubits proportional to hidden units.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor test accuracy despite training: Indicates issues with the equivalence assumption or quantum sampling quality.
  - Volatile or constant test accuracy during training: Suggests problems with effective temperature tuning or hardware noise.
  - Excessive training time: May indicate inefficiencies in quantum sampling or data transfer overhead.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Verify the MLP-EBM equivalence on a small binary classification task (e.g., MNIST digits 0 vs 1) by training both models and comparing their outputs and performance.
  2. Test the quantum sampling approach on a small EBM (e.g., 10 hidden units) to ensure proper sampling and gradient estimation before integrating with the full training loop.
  3. Gradually increase the network size (hidden units) while monitoring qubit requirements and performance to identify practical limits of the current quantum hardware.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can the equivalence between MLPs and EBMs be extended to networks with multiple hidden layers and non-sigmoid activation functions?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper proves the equivalence only for MLPs with one hidden layer and sigmoid activation functions, and states "The rest of this section will mathematically prove this equivalence" for these specific cases.
- **Why unresolved**: The proof relies on first-order Taylor expansions and small weight assumptions that may not hold for deeper networks or different activation functions. The paper acknowledges this limitation by stating "The model structures are shown in Fig. 1" which depicts only one hidden layer with sigmoid activation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Mathematical proofs extending the equivalence to multi-layer networks and different activation functions, or experimental validation showing similar equivalence holds in these cases.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the optimal effective temperature (β) for quantum annealing across different datasets and network architectures?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper states "For Quantum-1, a grid search was conducted and the effective temperature β of 16 was found to be viable for MNIST and Fashion-MNIST" but notes this was determined empirically.
- **Why unresolved**: The optimal temperature likely depends on the specific problem instance, network size, and dataset characteristics. The paper only tested one temperature value (β=16) and acknowledges that "QA can be affected by noise from the external environment such as temperature and magnetic fields."
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic studies varying β across different datasets, network architectures, and problem sizes to determine optimal values or develop adaptive temperature selection methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How does quantum-assisted training scale with network size compared to classical methods as quantum hardware improves?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper shows quantum sampling time remains roughly constant while classical methods show linear growth with model size, and notes "The capacity of the Zephyr architecture, which is the latest QPU architecture under development by D-Wave is highlighted as the red dot in Fig. 5. This illustrates great potential for the proposed quantum-assisted training method."
- **Why unresolved**: Current quantum hardware limitations prevent testing larger networks, and the paper acknowledges "Though problem sizes that are feasible on current annealers are limited." The runtime comparison in Fig. 5 only covers small networks (up to ~1000 input nodes).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experimental results on larger quantum processors testing networks of increasing size, and formal scaling analyses comparing quantum and classical training time complexity.

## Limitations
- The small weight assumption required for the MLP-EBM equivalence may not hold during practical training as weights can grow.
- Experiments are conducted on very small datasets (20 images per class), limiting generalizability to real-world scenarios.
- Current quantum hardware limitations restrict the size of networks that can be trained, with limited qubit connectivity and noise affecting sampling quality.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The theoretical equivalence between MLP and EBM gradients under small weight assumptions is mathematically sound.
- **Medium Confidence**: Quantum sampling effectively trains MLPs as demonstrated on small binary classification tasks.
- **Low Confidence**: The approach scales to larger networks or complex datasets beyond the simple MNIST/Fashion-MNIST binary classification tasks.
