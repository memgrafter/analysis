# Retraining A Graph-based Recommender with Interests Disentanglement

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.03624
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03624
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: 44% Recall@20 improvement over baseline incremental learning methods on three benchmark datasets

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of retraining graph-based recommender systems to handle new user interactions while preserving long-term user preferences. The authors propose a Disentangled Incremental Learning (DIL) framework that extracts historical preferences from existing model parameters and blends them with new preferences learned from newly observed interactions. By using an Information Extraction Module to dynamically extract historical information and a Disentanglement Module to decorrelate historical and new preferences, DIL effectively prevents catastrophic forgetting while capturing emerging trends. Experiments on three benchmark datasets demonstrate DIL's effectiveness when applied to LightGCN and NGCF base models, with Recall@20 improvements of up to 44% compared to baseline incremental learning methods.

## Method Summary
The DIL framework extracts historical preferences from previous model parameters through an Information Extraction Module (IEM) and decorrelates them from new preferences using a Disentanglement Module (DM). The IEM dynamically extracts historical information, treating different neighbor hops separately to capture long-lasting user attributes. The DM ensures the base recommendation model learns additional information on top of historical knowledge by decorrelating the historical and new embeddings. The framework redesigns the embedding layer in GCN-based recommenders to combine both historical and learnable new information, which are then processed through standard GCN layers. The model is trained using a combination of BPR ranking loss, disentanglement regularization, and historical supervision.

## Key Results
- DIL achieves up to 44% improvement in Recall@20 compared to baseline incremental learning methods
- The framework effectively handles the trade-off between capturing new trends and preserving long-term user preferences
- DIL demonstrates consistent performance improvements across three benchmark datasets (Amazon books, Amazon electronic, and Yelp) when applied to both LightGCN and NGCF base models

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: IEM (extracts historical info) -> DM (decorrelates embeddings) -> Base GCN (processes embeddings) -> Prediction
- Critical path: New interactions → IEM extracts historical info → DM decorrelates → Base GCN processes → Prediction
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Flexibility vs. performance: Two IEM designs offer different approaches to extracting historical information
  - Complexity vs. effectiveness: Disentanglement adds computational overhead but improves accuracy
  - Memory vs. scalability: Storing previous model parameters enables better historical information extraction
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance on historical interactions indicates ineffective IEM
  - Inability to capture new trends suggests insufficient disentanglement
  - Degraded overall performance may indicate problems with the redesigned embedding layer
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement DIL with only IEM (no disentanglement) to verify historical information extraction works
  2. Add disentanglement without historical supervision to test decorrelation effectiveness
  3. Full DIL implementation with all components to evaluate overall performance against baselines

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the continuity of historical information be directly modeled across different periods in the Information Extraction Module, given that historical embeddings from different periods appear to be in different latent spaces?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge this limitation in the Discussion section, noting that DIL ignores historical embeddings learned in periods before the immediate previous period (e.g., ignoring embeddings from period n-2 when updating in period n), and that directly modeling continuity is non-trivial because historical embeddings across periods may exist in different latent spaces.
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions this as a limitation but does not provide a solution, leaving it as future work. The challenge of modeling continuity across embeddings in different latent spaces remains an open problem.
- What evidence would resolve it: A proposed method for aligning or mapping historical embeddings across different periods into a common latent space, along with experimental results demonstrating improved recommendation accuracy when using such a continuity-aware approach compared to DIL.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal frequency for model retraining in incremental learning scenarios, and how does this frequency interact with the recommendation accuracy and computational cost?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that retraining can be scheduled periodically with fixed frequency or based on the number of newly observed interactions, but do not investigate the impact of different retraining frequencies on performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper assumes a fixed retraining schedule for fair comparison but does not explore how varying the frequency affects recommendation accuracy, computational efficiency, or the trade-off between them.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study varying retraining frequency across different datasets and recommendation scenarios, measuring both accuracy metrics (Recall@N, NDCG@N) and computational costs (training time, resource usage) to identify optimal scheduling strategies.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed disentanglement approach perform when dealing with multi-domain or multi-interest recommendation scenarios where users have diverse and potentially conflicting preferences?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] While the paper demonstrates disentanglement of historical and new preferences, it does not explore scenarios where users have multiple distinct interest domains that may require different handling strategies.
- Why unresolved: The current framework treats disentanglement as a binary separation between historical and new information, but does not address scenarios where users might have multiple simultaneous interest domains (e.g., a user who shops for both electronics and books) that could benefit from more sophisticated disentanglement strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on multi-domain datasets where users interact with items from different categories, comparing the standard disentanglement approach against multi-domain disentanglement strategies that can handle diverse user interests simultaneously.

## Limitations

- The framework has not been tested on very large-scale recommendation systems with millions of users and items
- The computational overhead introduced by the disentanglement module is not fully characterized
- The scalability of the IEM module for massive graphs remains an open question

## Confidence

- High confidence: The core mechanism of extracting historical preferences and decorrelating them from new preferences is well-supported by the experimental results
- Medium confidence: The specific implementation details of the Information Extraction Module, particularly the choice between Design 1 and Design 2, may affect reproducibility
- Medium confidence: The claim of up to 44% improvement over baselines is supported by experiments but may vary with different dataset characteristics or hyperparameter settings
