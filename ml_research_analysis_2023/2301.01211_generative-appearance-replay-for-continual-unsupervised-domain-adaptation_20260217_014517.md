# Generative appearance replay for continual unsupervised domain adaptation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.01211
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.01211
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: GarDA achieves 82.6% overall Dice for optic disc segmentation and 75.3% for cardiac segmentation, outperforming previous state-of-the-art by 3.9 percentage points

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses continual unsupervised domain adaptation for medical image segmentation, where a model must adapt to a sequence of unlabeled target domains without retaining any previously seen data. The proposed Generative Appearance Replay for continual Domain Adaptation (GarDA) employs a conditional GAN to synthesize images from previously seen domains, enabling effective knowledge preservation while adapting to new domains. GarDA achieves state-of-the-art performance on both optic disc segmentation and cardiac segmentation tasks, demonstrating robust performance across multiple domains while maintaining high segmentation accuracy in challenging continual adaptation scenarios.

## Method Summary
GarDA uses a conditional GAN to generate synthetic images that preserve the content of source images while adopting the appearance characteristics of target domains. The method employs appearance transfer via AdaIN to enable content-consistent style transfer between domains. During continual adaptation, GarDA prevents catastrophic forgetting through knowledge distillation, where the segmentation model is trained to predict the same segmentation map for a target-like image as the previous model predicted for the corresponding source-like image. The approach is evaluated on two medical imaging tasks: optic disc segmentation across three retinal image datasets and cardiac segmentation across three MRI scanner types.

## Key Results
- Achieves 82.6% overall Dice score for optic disc segmentation, surpassing previous state-of-the-art by 3.9 percentage points
- Achieves 75.3% overall Dice score for cardiac segmentation with similar performance improvement
- Demonstrates consistent performance gains across all individual domains compared to competing methods
- Shows robustness to different random initializations with the smallest standard deviations among evaluated methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Generative replay using a conditional GAN can effectively substitute inaccessible data from previous domains
- Mechanism: The GAN learns to generate synthetic images that preserve the content of source images while adopting the appearance characteristics of target domains. This allows the segmentation model to be trained on representative samples from all previously seen domains without storing real data
- Core assumption: The generator can capture and reproduce the full distribution of appearance characteristics from each domain
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Generative Appearance Replay for continual Domain Adaptation (GarDA) employs generative replay using a conditional GAN to synthesize images from previously seen domains"
  - [section]: "We substitute the missing D0:t-1 with synthetic data produced by a GAN and replay this data to M to prevent forgetting"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - only mentions generative replay in similar contexts but no specific GAN-based substitution mechanism
- Break condition: If the generator fails to capture the full distribution of appearance characteristics, synthetic samples will be insufficiently representative, leading to catastrophic forgetting

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Appearance transfer via AdaIN enables content-consistent style transfer between domains
- Mechanism: By re-normalizing instance-level statistics (mean and variance) of feature maps, the method can transfer appearance from target images to source images while preserving content, enabling effective domain adaptation
- Core assumption: Instance-level statistics in feature space adequately capture appearance information
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Inspired by [69], [17], we leverage the idea behind adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) that the instance-level statistics (e.g., mean and variance) of a feature map represent the appearance of the corresponding image"
  - [section]: "Using AdaIN, we can re-normalize the instance-level statistics of f p 0|z to those of ft and obtain a new feature map ˜f p t|z that encodes the content of xp 0|z and the appearance of xt"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence found for AdaIN-based appearance transfer in continual domain adaptation context
- Break condition: If instance-level statistics don't fully capture appearance, the transferred images will lose domain-specific characteristics

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Knowledge distillation prevents catastrophic forgetting while adapting to new domains
- Mechanism: The segmentation model is trained to predict the same segmentation map for a target-like image as the previous model predicted for the corresponding source-like image, preserving knowledge from all previously seen domains
- Core assumption: The previous model's predictions on source images are more accurate than on target images
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "the current model M is trained to make the same predictions as the previous model Mp. Since labeled data is only available during the source training, as described in Section 3.1, we assume that the predictions of Mp on the source images are the most accurate compared to images from other target domains"
  - [abstract]: "GarDA employs generative replay using a conditional GAN to synthesize images from previously seen domains, enabling effective knowledge preservation and adaptation"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - mentions knowledge distillation but not specifically in continual domain adaptation with synthetic data
- Break condition: If the previous model's source predictions are not sufficiently accurate, the distillation will propagate errors

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Segmentation model (U-Net) -> Generator (conditional GAN) -> Discriminator (feature-level) -> Feature extractor (frozen VGG19) -> EMA-averaged generator

- Critical path:
  1. Source training: Train segmentation model and GAN on source domain
  2. GAN adaptation: Adapt GAN to new target domain using appearance transfer
  3. GAN distillation: Prevent forgetting of previous domains via distillation losses
  4. Segmentation training: Train segmentation model using synthetic data and knowledge distillation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - GAN quality vs. training time: Higher-quality generators require longer training but provide better synthetic samples
  - Feature extractor choice: VGG19 provides good features but adds computational overhead
  - Distillation vs. direct adaptation: Distillation prevents forgetting but may slow adaptation to new domains

- Failure signatures:
  - Generator produces unrealistic images → Segmentation performance degrades on all domains
  - Discriminator becomes too strong → Generator fails to adapt to new domains
  - Knowledge distillation too strong → Segmentation model fails to adapt to new domains
  - EMA averaging insufficient → Generator exhibits mode collapse or instability

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test generator quality: Generate images from all domains and visually inspect for realism and domain-specific characteristics
  2. Test adaptation capability: Measure segmentation performance on each domain after adaptation to verify knowledge retention
  3. Test distillation effectiveness: Compare performance with and without distillation losses to quantify forgetting prevention

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the proposed GarDA method perform when adapting to more than three domains in sequence?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that "the trends observed in our experiments indicate GarDA to potentially yield better results for increasingly longer domain sequences" and suggest that "when new datasets from different domains become available, longer continual sequences with more domains shall be studied" in the future.
- Why unresolved: The current evaluation only considers sequences of up to three domains (D0→D1→D2). The scalability and performance of GarDA for longer sequences remains untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with sequences containing four or more domains would demonstrate how GarDA's performance scales with sequence length and whether the observed trends continue.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of different initialization strategies on the segmentation model's performance in continual UDA?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors report that "GarDA has by far the smallest standard-deviations for adaptation, which shows the robustness of our method to different initializations." They also mention using random training initializations in their experiments.
- Why unresolved: While the authors demonstrate that GarDA is robust to initialization, they do not explore how different initialization strategies (e.g., different pre-trained weights, different random seeds) affect the performance of both GarDA and competing methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematically comparing the performance of GarDA and other methods under various initialization strategies would reveal the importance of initialization and potentially lead to improved methods.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the quality of the generated images by the GAN impact the segmentation performance in continual UDA?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors discuss the importance of the GAN in generating realistic images from previously seen domains and compare the quality of images generated by their method to those of ACE. They also mention that "the performance gain from our method GarDA also comes at a lower budget" in terms of model parameters.
- Why unresolved: While the authors demonstrate that their GAN generates higher-quality images than ACE, they do not quantify the relationship between image quality and segmentation performance. It remains unclear how much the quality of generated images contributes to the overall performance gain.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments where the GAN's image generation quality is deliberately degraded (e.g., by reducing the number of training iterations) and measuring the impact on segmentation performance would establish a quantitative relationship between image quality and segmentation accuracy.

## Limitations
- The approach's scalability to scenarios with many domains remains untested, as experiments only cover sequences of up to three domains
- Reliance on high-quality synthetic data generation assumes the GAN can capture the full diversity of target domain distributions, which may not hold for domains with high appearance variability
- The method requires careful hyperparameter tuning for the trade-off between knowledge distillation and adaptation to new domains

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core mechanism of using generative replay for continual domain adaptation, the experimental methodology, and the reported performance improvements over baseline methods
- **Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of AdaIN-based appearance transfer for content-consistent style transfer and the sufficiency of knowledge distillation to prevent catastrophic forgetting
- **Low Confidence**: The generalizability of the approach to domains with higher appearance variability and the scalability of the method to scenarios with many domains

## Next Checks
1. **Generator Distribution Coverage**: Conduct quantitative analysis of the synthetic data distribution compared to real target domain data using metrics like Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) or Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) to verify the generator captures sufficient domain diversity

2. **Ablation of Adaptation Components**: Perform systematic ablation studies removing knowledge distillation, appearance transfer, and EMA averaging to quantify the contribution of each component to overall performance

3. **Cross-Domain Generalization**: Test the method on non-medical domains with higher appearance variability (e.g., natural scene segmentation across seasons/weather conditions) to evaluate generalizability beyond the medical imaging context
