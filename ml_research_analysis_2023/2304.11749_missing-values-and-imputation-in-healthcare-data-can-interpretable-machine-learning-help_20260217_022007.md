# Missing Values and Imputation in Healthcare Data: Can Interpretable Machine Learning Help?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.11749
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11749
- Reference count: 21
- This paper shows how interpretable machine learning, specifically Explainable Boosting Machines (EBMs), can help understand and handle missing values in healthcare data.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores how interpretable machine learning models, particularly Explainable Boosting Machines (EBMs), can provide insights into missing data patterns and imputation risks in healthcare datasets. The authors propose methods to detect missingness mechanisms, identify problematic imputation methods, and visualize the effects of imputation on model behavior. Through case studies on real healthcare datasets, they demonstrate that EBMs can reveal hidden issues with common imputation approaches that are difficult to detect with traditional methods.

## Method Summary
The authors train EBMs on healthcare datasets with missing values encoded as unique values (e.g., -1). They use EBM shape functions to visualize feature contributions, apply Wald tests to detect MCAR (Missing Completely at Random) patterns, predict missingness using other features as inputs, and employ Isolation Forest to detect spikes in shape functions that indicate problematic imputation. The method involves training separate EBMs for target prediction and missingness prediction, then analyzing the resulting shape functions for anomalies and patterns.

## Key Results
- EBM-based test for MCAR outperforms Little's test in detecting missingness mechanisms
- EBM shape functions reveal when missing values are systematically different from observed values
- EBMs achieve superior accuracy in predicting missingness compared to other methods
- Visualizations show how mean, MissForest, and KNN imputation can introduce risks not apparent in the data

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** EBMs can test whether missing data is MCAR by checking if the shape function score for the missing value bin equals zero.
- **Mechanism:** EBM splits feature values into bins with associated scores. For MCAR, the missing-value bin's score should match the population mean (zero in EBM). The Wald test on these bin coefficients provides a statistical MCAR test.
- **Core assumption:** Shape function scores are mean-centered, so zero represents population average contribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "an EBM-based test for Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) that outperforms Little's test"
  - [section] "if the missingness is MCAR... the expected score for bins representing the missing value should be the same as the entire population, which is 0"
- **Break condition:** If the data has complex interactions between features that affect the shape function in ways not captured by simple bin score differences, the MCAR test may give false positives.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** EBM shape functions reveal when missing values are systematically different from observed values, indicating problems with imputation.
- **Mechanism:** When values are missing because they're assumed normal, EBMs learn risk contributions based on imputed values rather than true distribution. Spikes or anomalies at the mean of the observed data indicate imputation issues.
- **Core assumption:** The shape function captures the relationship between feature values and target, so systematic differences between missing and observed groups appear as shape anomalies.
  - [section] "shape functions can help identify the case when feature values are missing because they were assumed to be normal"
  - [section] "anomalies in the EBM shape functions can be used to automatically identify potentially harmful imputation with the mean or median"
- **Break condition:** If the observed data distribution is very narrow or has multiple modes, spikes may occur naturally and not indicate imputation problems.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** EBMs can predict missingness from other features, revealing patterns in why data is missing.
- **Mechanism:** Treat the missingness indicator (0/1) as a target and train EBM to predict it from other features. High AUC and interpretable shape functions show which features drive missingness.
- **Core assumption:** Missingness patterns are predictable from observed features, even if the true cause is MNAR.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "EBM's superior accuracy in predicting missingness compared to other methods"
  - [section] "the test AUC for predicting missingness of 'Na (Sodium)' and 'Urea' are 98% and 99%, which suggests their missingness can be almost fully explained by other observed variables"
- **Break condition:** If missingness is truly random with respect to all observed features (including the label), prediction accuracy will be near chance level and no interpretable patterns will emerge.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) and their shape functions
  - **Why needed here:** EBMs are GAMs with boosted trees for shape functions, and understanding GAMs is essential to interpret EBM behavior
  - **Quick check question:** What does it mean that EBM shape functions are "mean-centered" and how does this enable the MCAR test?

- **Concept:** Missing data mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR)
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's methods depend on understanding these mechanisms to interpret results and choose appropriate techniques
  - **Quick check question:** How does the proposed EBM MCAR test differ from Little's test in terms of what it reveals about the data?

- **Concept:** Imputation methods and their assumptions
  - **Why needed here:** The paper critiques different imputation methods (mean, MissForest, KNN) and their potential risks, requiring understanding of how each works
  - **Quick check question:** Why might MissForest imputation systematically bias predictions for patients with low P/F ratios?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** EBM model → shape function visualization → statistical tests (Wald test for MCAR) → missingness prediction models → anomaly detection for imputation quality
- **Critical path:** 1) Train EBM on data with encoded missing values 2) Extract shape functions 3) Apply statistical tests or visualize for anomalies 4) Interpret results for data quality assessment
- **Design tradeoffs:** EBMs provide interpretability but may sacrifice some predictive accuracy compared to black-box models; the trade-off is worthwhile for understanding missingness patterns
- **Failure signatures:** 1) Shape functions show no clear patterns when predicting missingness (prediction accuracy near chance) 2) Wald test p-values are uniformly high (cannot reject MCAR) 3) No spikes detected at mean values (mean imputation may be safe)
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Train EBM on MIMIC-II with missing values encoded as -1, extract shape functions, and apply MCAR test to each feature
  2. Predict missingness of each feature using all other features, evaluate AUC, and visualize top shape functions
  3. Apply mean imputation to continuous features, train EBM, detect spikes at mean values using the proposed anomaly detection method

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the proposed MCAR test be extended to handle missing not at random (MNAR) data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows the EBM-based MCAR test outperforms Little's test on MCAR and MAR datasets, but does not evaluate its performance on MNAR data.
- Why unresolved: The test relies on assumptions that may not hold for MNAR data, but it is unclear how well it would perform in practice.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the MCAR test on datasets with known MNAR mechanisms and comparing its performance to existing methods.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the interpretable missing value methods be applied to other glass-box models beyond EBMs?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on using EBMs for interpretability, but does not explore whether similar methods could be used with other interpretable models like decision trees or sparse linear models.
- Why unresolved: Different interpretable models have different strengths and weaknesses, and it is unclear how well the proposed methods would generalize.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing the missing value analysis methods with other interpretable models and comparing their performance and interpretability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the automatic detection of harmful mean imputation be improved to reduce false positives?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes using an outlier detection algorithm to identify potentially harmful mean imputations based on spikes in the EBM shape function, but does not explore ways to refine the detection.
- Why unresolved: The current method may flag some harmless imputations as potentially harmful, leading to unnecessary concern or model edits.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimenting with different outlier detection algorithms, thresholds, or additional features to improve the accuracy of the harmful imputation detection.

## Limitations
- The methods rely heavily on EBM-specific properties that may not generalize to other interpretable models
- Spike detection effectiveness depends on Isolation Forest parameters that are not fully specified
- Performance claims are based on specific datasets and may not generalize to all healthcare data scenarios

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: EBM shape functions can reveal systematic differences between observed and imputed values; EBM's predictive accuracy for missingness
- **Medium Confidence**: The Wald test approach for MCAR detection is more effective than Little's test; visualization of imputation effects is practically useful
- **Low Confidence**: Spike detection reliably identifies harmful imputation across all datasets and imputation methods
