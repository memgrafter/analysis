# Cross-Domain Image Captioning with Discriminative Finetuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.01662
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01662
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: Discriminatively fine-tuned image captions outperform human captions for cross-domain image retrieval

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a self-supervised method for improving cross-domain image captioning by fine-tuning pre-trained captioners with a discriminative objective. The approach trains captioners to generate captions that help a frozen text-based image retriever identify the target image among distractors. Experiments with ClipCap and BLIP models show that discriminatively fine-tuned captions significantly outperform both non-finetuned and human captions on out-of-domain retrieval tasks, particularly on Flickr, nocaps, and Concadia datasets. Qualitative analysis reveals that fine-tuned captions are more visually descriptive and precise compared to the abstract style of human captions.

## Method Summary
The method fine-tunes pre-trained image captioners using REINFORCE optimization with a reward based on the log probability of the target image under a frozen CLIP-based retriever. During training, the captioner generates captions for a target image alongside distractor images, and the reward is computed based on how well the generated caption helps identify the target. The approach uses a constant learning rate of 1e-7, batch size of 100, and greedy decoding during training with beam search (beam=5) at test time. Hard distractors can be mined based on CLIP visual similarity or sampled randomly.

## Key Results
- Discriminatively fine-tuned captions achieve 62.0 P@1 on Flickr compared to 53.3 for non-finetuned captions
- On nocaps, discriminatively fine-tuned captions achieve 34.0 P@1 versus 31.1 for human captions
- Human evaluators more accurately identify target images using discriminatively fine-tuned captions than human captions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Discriminative finetuning improves caption discriminability by optimizing for a downstream retrieval reward rather than language model likelihood.
- Mechanism: The captioner is trained with REINFORCE to maximize the log-probability of the target image under a frozen CLIP-based retriever, making captions more distinctive for image identification.
- Core assumption: CLIP-based retrieval rewards correlate with human judgment of caption informativeness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "fine-tuning an out-of-the-box neural captioner with a self-supervised discriminative communication objective helps to recover a plain, visually descriptive language"
  - [section 4.2.4]: Human annotators more accurately identify targets using discriminatively finetuned captions than human or non-finetuned ones.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors include "Pragmatic Inference with a CLIP Listener for Contrastive Captioning" and "IG Captioner: Information Gain Captioners are Strong Zero-shot Classifiers," both exploring discriminative caption objectives.
- Break condition: If the retriever is too noisy or biased, the reward signal becomes unreliable and degrades caption quality.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Finetuning on noisy caption datasets (like Conceptual Captions) reduces overfitting to abstract caption styles.
- Mechanism: By optimizing for retrieval rather than caption likelihood, the model shifts away from reproducing dataset-specific idiosyncrasies (e.g., generic hypernyms) toward more visually specific language.
- Core assumption: Web alt-text captions often contain non-visual metadata that harms generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5]: Qualitative analysis shows ClipCap outputs abstract terms like "tourist attraction," while DiscriTune outputs more concrete descriptors.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors include "ImageCaptioner$^2$: Image Captioner for Image Captioning Bias Amplification Assessment," implying concern about caption dataset biases.
- Break condition: If the dataset is already highly descriptive, the shift may not improve generalization and could harm in-domain performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: CLIP-based encoders used for both captioning and retrieval create a shared embedding space that amplifies finetuning benefits.
- Mechanism: The captioner's visual encoder and the retriever share the same CLIP model, so finetuning the captioner's language part aligns caption embeddings with image embeddings in the same space.
- Core assumption: Shared embeddings improve the coherence of caption-image similarity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2.3]: BLIP experiments show benefits even without CLIP-based captioning, suggesting the retriever alignment is not the sole driver.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors include "Revisiting Image Captioning Training Paradigm via Direct CLIP-based Optimization," indicating active research on CLIP-based captioning methods.
- Break condition: If the retriever encoder changes, the finetuned captioner may lose alignment and degrade in performance.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: Caption generation is discrete and non-differentiable, requiring policy gradient methods.

- Concept: Cross-modal embeddings and similarity
  - Why needed here: Image and caption representations must be comparable for retrieval-based training.
  - Quick check question: How does CLIP's contrastive loss structure the joint image-text embedding space?

- Concept: Domain adaptation and generalization
  - Why needed here: The method aims to improve performance on out-of-domain datasets, a key challenge in captioning.
  - Quick check question: What metrics best capture generalization from in-domain to out-of-domain performance?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Pre-trained captioner -> CLIP visual encoder -> Mapper (ClipCap only) -> Text generation model -> CLIP text encoder (retriever) -> Reward computation -> REINFORCE update

- Critical path:
  1. Input image → CLIP visual encoder → mapped embeddings → text generation
  2. Generated caption → CLIP text encoder → similarity scores with candidates
  3. Reward (log probability of target image) → REINFORCE gradient update

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Pros: No need for human annotations, leverages existing models, improves cross-domain performance
  - Cons: Requires large candidate sets for stable gradients, relies on quality of frozen retriever, may slightly reduce in-domain caption fidelity

- Failure signatures:
  - Captions become repetitive or degenerate → check REINFORCE variance and baseline
  - No improvement on retrieval → check candidate sampling and reward scaling
  - Significant in-domain performance drop → check learning rate and reward function balance

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train ClipCap on COCO with random distractors, evaluate retrieval P@1 on COCO validation
  2. Repeat with hard negative mining (k closest neighbors), compare NLG metrics vs random distractors
  3. Swap CLIP retriever with BLIP-based retriever, measure impact on caption quality and retrieval

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the number and type of distractors used during discriminative finetuning affect the quality and generalizability of the generated captions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions an experiment with hard distractors mined based on similarity, showing mixed results in terms of NLG metrics and retrieval accuracy.
- Why unresolved: The paper only briefly explores this with a limited number of hard distractors. It suggests that harder setups might lead to overfitting the quirks of the frozen retriever, potentially leading to poorer generalization.
- What evidence would resolve it: A more comprehensive study varying the number and type of distractors (e.g., semantic similarity, visual similarity, random) during finetuning, followed by extensive evaluation on multiple out-of-domain datasets, would clarify the optimal distractor strategy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of retriever model impact the performance of the discriminatively finetuned captioner?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses CLIP as the retriever for all experiments. It mentions that other pre-trained text-based image retrievers are available, but does not explore this.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the impact of using different retriever architectures (e.g., BLIP, Flamingo) on the finetuning process and the resulting caption quality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing the performance of the discriminatively finetuned captioner when using different retriever models as the "discriminator" during finetuning, followed by evaluation on both retrieval and caption quality metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the style and content of the dataset used to pre-train the captioner influence the effectiveness of discriminative finetuning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper observes a significant discrepancy between the abstract style of alt-text captions in Conceptual Captions and the more descriptive captions generated by the discriminatively finetuned model. It suggests this might be due to unlearning the idiosyncrasies of the pre-training dataset.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores this with Conceptual Captions and COCO. It does not investigate how different pre-training datasets (e.g., Flickr, SBU) with varying caption styles would impact the effectiveness of discriminative finetuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments training the base captioner on different datasets with varying caption styles, followed by discriminative finetuning and evaluation on both in-domain and out-of-domain datasets, would reveal the influence of pre-training data characteristics.

## Limitations

- The method's effectiveness depends heavily on the quality of distractor sampling, with poor distractor selection potentially leading to misleading reward signals
- Significant in-domain performance drops (particularly for BLEU@4 and CIDEr on COCO) suggest a tradeoff between domain generalization and in-domain fidelity
- The reliance on CLIP-based retrieval as both training signal and evaluation metric creates potential circularity in the reported improvements

## Confidence

- High confidence: The core claim that discriminative fine-tuning improves cross-domain image retrieval performance is well-supported by the experimental results
- Medium confidence: The claim that fine-tuning shifts captions from abstract to visually descriptive language is supported by qualitative examples and human evaluation
- Low confidence: The mechanism claim that CLIP-based encoder alignment is not the primary driver (based on BLIP experiments) is weakly supported

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study with different retriever architectures**: Replace the CLIP retriever with a non-CLIP model (e.g., BLIP-based retriever) while keeping the CLIP-based captioner to test whether improvements persist independently of the shared embedding space.

2. **Human evaluation of caption quality**: Conduct a human study where annotators rate captions on descriptiveness, informativeness, and naturalness (rather than just retrieval accuracy).

3. **Distractor quality analysis**: Systematically vary distractor sampling strategies (random, hard negatives, curriculum learning approaches) and measure their impact on training stability, convergence speed, and final performance.
