# Echoes of Biases: How Stigmatizing Language Affects AI Performance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2305.10201
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10201
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Removing stigmatizing language (SL) from clinician notes improves AI mortality prediction accuracy and reduces racial disparities, with SL from central clinicians having the strongest impact.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how stigmatizing language (SL) in electronic health records affects AI model performance, particularly in mortality prediction. Using a Transformer-based deep learning model and explainable AI techniques, the research finds that SL written by clinicians adversely affects AI performance, especially for black patients, highlighting SL as a source of racial disparity in AI model development. The study identifies central clinicians in collaborative networks as having a stronger impact on racial disparity in AI models. Removing SL written by these central clinicians proves to be a more efficient bias reduction strategy than eliminating all SL in the entire dataset. These findings provide actionable insights for responsible AI development and contribute to understanding clinician behavior and EHR note writing in healthcare.

## Method Summary
The study uses MIMIC-III dataset clinical notes to train a Transformer-based deep learning model for mortality prediction. The method involves identifying stigmatizing language keywords, training the model on original and SL-removed datasets, applying explainable AI techniques (leave-one-out, input reduction, adversarial perturbations), and analyzing clinician collaboration networks to identify central clinicians. The research compares AI performance and fairness metrics before and after SL removal, particularly focusing on racial disparities between Black and White patients.

## Key Results
- SL written by clinicians adversely affects AI performance, particularly for black patients
- Removing SL from central clinicians improves model performance more efficiently than removing all SL
- SL serves as a source of racial disparity in AI model development

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Removing SL from medical notes can reduce the performance of AI models for mortality prediction.
- Mechanism: If SL reflects systematic racial discrimination in clinical practice, it contains informative patterns about the medical consequences of such bias (e.g., higher mortality risk). Transformer models integrate all tokens, so removing SL eliminates these predictive signals.
- Core assumption: SL is a consistent and informative indicator of bias-driven health outcomes, not random noise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our findings demonstrate that SL written by clinicians adversely affects AI performance, particularly so for black patients"
  - [section] "clinicians may discriminate against certain racial groups during treatment, ultimately resulting in higher odds of mortality than other racial groups"
- Break condition: If SL patterns are inconsistent across racial groups or unrelated to health outcomes, the model loses no valuable information.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Removing the SL from medical notes can improve the performance of AI models on mortality prediction.
- Mechanism: If SL is non-informative noise or reflects clinicians' subjective feelings rather than objective medical facts, its presence dilutes the model's focus on meaningful features. Removing it reduces noise and improves prediction accuracy.
- Core assumption: SL is primarily noise that disrupts the model's ability to learn true medical signals.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "removing SL written by central clinicians is a more efficient bias reduction strategy"
  - [section] "SL could be about expressing clinicians' feelings (either positive or negative), which are not informative and may reflect biases"
- Break condition: If SL contains systematic medical information unrelated to bias, removing it would harm model performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Eliminating SL written by central clinicians improves AI performance and fairness in mortality prediction more than eliminating all SL.
- Mechanism: Central clinicians (high degree centrality in the collaborative network) write more SL, and this SL is more disruptive to model performance. Removing their SL targets the most harmful noise while preserving potentially informative SL from non-central clinicians.
- Core assumption: SL from central clinicians is more biased, less systematic, and more disruptive than SL from non-central clinicians.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "removing SL written by central clinicians is a more efficient bias reduction strategy"
  - [section] "central clinicians are more likely to write SL in EHR notes, and removing the SL in their notes can further improve the model performance"
- Break condition: If non-central clinicians write equally disruptive SL, or if all SL is equally informative, the strategy offers no advantage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the specific mechanisms by which clinicians' centrality in collaborative networks leads to increased stigmatizing language use?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies central clinicians as having a stronger impact on racial disparity but does not fully explain why.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests familiarity and social influence but does not empirically test these mechanisms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental or observational studies showing direct relationships between centrality, peer influence, and stigmatizing language use.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do different types of stigmatizing language (e.g., substance-related vs. non-substance-related) differentially impact AI model performance and fairness?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on general stigmatizing language but doesn't analyze subtypes or their varying impacts.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a broad categorization of stigmatizing language without examining specific categories.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of AI performance and fairness metrics when different categories of stigmatizing language are removed.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the long-term impact of removing stigmatizing language from AI training data on clinical decision-making and patient outcomes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper demonstrates short-term improvements in AI performance but doesn't examine long-term effects.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses on immediate model performance improvements rather than downstream clinical impacts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking patient outcomes and clinical decisions after implementing AI models trained on non-stigmatizing language data.

## Limitations

- Limited generalizability to healthcare systems beyond MIMIC-III
- Cannot definitively distinguish between SL acting as bias signals versus disruptive noise
- Assumes degree centrality accurately captures meaningful collaborative patterns

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The empirical finding that SL removal affects model performance differently across racial groups is well-supported by the experimental design and results.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that removing SL from central clinicians is more efficient than removing all SL is supported by comparative analysis, though the magnitude of improvement and its generalizability remain uncertain.

**Low confidence**: The assertion that this finding provides "actionable insights for responsible AI development" extends beyond the immediate empirical results and requires further validation in real-world deployment scenarios.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-dataset validation**: Replicate the analysis using multiple EHR datasets from different healthcare systems and time periods to assess generalizability of the SL-performance relationship and the central clinician strategy.

2. **Mechanism isolation experiment**: Conduct controlled experiments where SL is systematically varied while holding other variables constant to definitively determine whether SL acts primarily as bias signal or noise.

3. **Longitudinal impact study**: Track model performance and fairness metrics over time as SL removal strategies are implemented in clinical practice to validate whether short-term experimental findings translate to sustained improvements.
