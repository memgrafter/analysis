# CALaMo: a Constructionist Assessment of Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03589
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03589

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces CALaMo, a constructionist framework for evaluating neural language models' linguistic abilities. Unlike traditional nativist approaches that treat language as abstract grammar, CALaMo treats language as a structured inventory of form-meaning pairs (constructions) that emerge from input through distributional analysis. The framework tracks how grammatical knowledge evolves during training by representing constructions as distributional vectors and measuring their semantic shifts over time. Two use cases are demonstrated: analyzing abstraction patterns during individual acquisition and modeling variation across a population of speakers. Experiments on CHILDES corpus show that constructions with intermediate input similarities exhibit the largest distributional shifts during learning, supporting constructionist hypotheses about grammatical generalization. The approach enables evaluation of both syntactic and semantic aspects of learned language, providing a more cognitively plausible alternative to standard perplexity-based metrics.

## Method Summary
CALaMo evaluates neural language models using a constructionist framework that treats language as structured inventory of form-meaning pairs (constructions) that emerge from input through distributional analysis. The method extracts constructions from CHILDES corpus data, represents them as distributional vectors, and tracks their semantic shifts during LSTM training across 7 acquisition steps. Distributional similarities between constructions are measured using cosine distances, with the framework specifically examining how intermediate similarity constructions show largest shifts during learning. The approach models both individual development through sequential training steps and population variation through speaker-specific construction frequencies.

## Key Results
- Constructions with intermediate input similarities exhibit the largest distributional shifts during training, supporting constructionist hypotheses about grammatical generalization
- The framework successfully tracks both syntactic and semantic aspects of language learning through construction representations
- Core constructions (used by 50% of speakers) show different distributional patterns compared to peripheral constructions (used by 1-5 speakers), with intermediate frequency constructions showing the largest shifts

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do distributional similarities in input constructions influence the acquisition of more abstract grammatical constructions during training?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly tests whether input distributional similarities affect learnability of abstract constructions, showing that intermediate similarity pairs exhibit the largest shifts during acquisition.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates this relationship, the exact mechanisms by which distributional similarity drives abstraction remain unclear. The paper doesn't explore whether this is a universal pattern across different languages or model architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying input similarity distributions across multiple languages and model architectures, measuring abstraction patterns and generalization abilities.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we meaningfully compare semantic meaning spaces associated with the same construction across different speakers in a population model?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces the concept of comparing portions of λσi and λσj meaning spaces activated by the same construction but doesn't provide methods for actual comparison.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges that different speakers may associate different meanings to the same construction but doesn't specify how to measure or compare these semantic spaces, particularly when they may not be directly comparable.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of robust metrics for comparing distributional semantic spaces across different speakers, validated through human experiments and applied to NLM populations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the relationship between frequency of constructions in input and their status as core versus peripheral constructions in a population of speakers?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows significant differences in input frequency between core and peripheral constructions but notes these differences are not as striking as expected, suggesting the relationship is more complex than simple frequency.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates that core constructions are not necessarily the most frequent ones, but doesn't explain what other factors determine core status or how this relates to actual language use and communication success.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking construction frequency and usage patterns across multiple speakers, combined with communication success metrics, to identify which factors predict core construction status.

## Limitations
- Construction extraction methodology relies heavily on catena approximation which may not capture all relevant form-meaning pairs
- CHILDES corpus may not generalize to adult language patterns or other domains
- 5-epoch acquisition steps represent an artificial constraint that may not reflect natural learning dynamics

## Confidence
- High confidence: The constructionist framework and distributional vector methodology are theoretically sound and well-grounded in usage-based linguistics
- Medium confidence: The experimental results showing intermediate similarity constructions exhibiting largest shifts are compelling but limited to a single corpus and model architecture
- Medium confidence: The claim that CALaMo provides a more cognitively plausible evaluation than perplexity metrics, though this requires broader comparative studies
