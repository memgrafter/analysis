# Automatic Glossary of Clinical Terminology: a Large-Scale Dictionary of Biomedical Definitions Generated from Ontional Knowledge

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00665
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00665
- Reference count: 4
- Generated over 400,000 biomedical definitions with average quality scores above 4.5/5

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Automatic Glossary of Clinical Terminology (AGCT), a large-scale biomedical dictionary created by generating human-readable definitions for SNOMED CT ontology concepts using GPT-3.5. The approach verbalizes ontological relationships and provides them as prompts to the language model, producing definitions evaluated on factuality, insight, and fluency. The generated definitions achieved high average scores (>4.5/5) across all quality axes, with over 80% meeting the standard required for patient-facing applications. The resource provides valuable support for biomedical tasks requiring human-readable definitions and can serve as a bronze standard for evaluating large language models' biomedical knowledge.

## Method Summary
The method involves prompting GPT-3.5 Turbo with verbalized SNOMED CT relationships for each biomedical concept, along with specific instructions for definition generation. The prompt includes four times more tokens of ontological relationships than generated output tokens to ensure information saturation. Generated definitions are evaluated by NLP researchers with biomedical expertise on three independent axes: factuality, insight, and fluency using 5-point scales. A quality classification system processes the outputs, and the definitions serve as a resource for biomedical applications and LLM evaluation.

## Key Results
- Generated human-readable definitions for over 400,000 biomedical concepts from SNOMED CT
- Achieved average quality scores above 4.5/5 on factuality, insight, and fluency axes
- Over 80% of definitions met the quality standard required for patient-facing applications
- The remaining definitions still provide utility for machine learning tasks like training retrieval models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GPT-3.5 can generate factually accurate biomedical definitions when provided with comprehensive ontological relationship data.
- Mechanism: The model receives a saturated prompt containing both verbalized SNOMED CT relationships and precise generation instructions, leveraging its inherent biomedical knowledge to produce accurate definitions.
- Core assumption: GPT-3.5's biomedical knowledge base is sufficiently comprehensive to support factual definition generation without hallucinations.
- Evidence anchors: Human evaluation showed 83% of definitions contained no incorrect information; average factuality scores exceeded 4.5/5.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The combination of ontological data saturation and model knowledge prevents hallucination.
- Mechanism: By providing four times more tokens of ontological relationships than generated output tokens, the model has sufficient grounding information to stay on topic and avoid fabricating content.
- Core assumption: The information density in the prompt is high enough that the model cannot easily invent content without contradicting provided facts.
- Evidence anchors: The saturation approach was hypothesized to ensure well-informed definitions; evaluation results showed high factuality scores supporting this approach.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Human evaluation along three independent axes provides a robust quality assessment framework.
- Mechanism: By rating definitions on factuality, insight, and fluency separately, the system captures different failure modes and prevents conflating distinct quality issues.
- Core assumption: The three axes capture the most critical dimensions of definition quality and are sufficiently independent to provide orthogonal information.
- Evidence anchors: The study found different definitions scored differently across the three axes, demonstrating their independence; a 6-level quality scale was developed based on these axes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: SNOMED CT ontology -> Prompt Generation -> GPT-3.5 -> Definition Output -> Human Evaluation -> Quality Classification
- Critical path: SNOMED CT → Prompt Generation → GPT-3.5 → Definition Output → Human Evaluation → Quality Classification
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Information saturation vs. computational cost
  - Prompt complexity vs. model performance
  - Human evaluation coverage vs. scalability
  - Quality thresholds vs. dataset completeness
- Failure signatures:
  - Low factuality scores indicate insufficient ontological information or model knowledge gaps
  - Low insight scores suggest the prompt template needs refinement
  - Low fluency scores may indicate prompt information overload or poor template design
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Generate definitions for 100 randomly selected SNOMED CT concepts with varying prompt information density to find the optimal saturation ratio
  2. Test different prompt templates focusing on factuality vs. insight to understand their independent effects
  3. Run ablation studies removing different types of SNOMED CT relationships to identify which provide the most valuable information for definition quality

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the impact of the imperfect quality of generated definitions on downstream applications like patient-facing systems or critical reasoning tasks?
- Basis in paper: The paper states that the generated definitions do not meet standards for patient-facing scenarios or reasoning-required scenarios due to imperfect quality.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how the imperfections in definitions would affect specific downstream applications or what level of quality is needed for each use case.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of the generated definitions in specific downstream applications, measuring the impact of definition quality on task performance and identifying the minimum quality thresholds required for each application.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the bias introduced by using SnomedCT as the knowledge source affect the coverage and representation of biomedical concepts in the generated definitions?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that SnomedCT does not cover all possible relationships between concepts, and biasing the output towards SnomedCT relationships might perpetuate existing biases in the data.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not analyze the extent of the bias introduced by the choice of SnomedCT or its impact on the diversity and completeness of the generated definitions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of the coverage and representation of biomedical concepts in the generated definitions versus other knowledge sources or expert-curated definitions, identifying gaps and biases introduced by the SnomedCT-centric approach.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal length of definitions for biomedical concepts, and how does the current average length of 49 words affect the balance between comprehensiveness and conciseness?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that the average length of generated definitions is 49 words and that the rating of acceptable insight was biased towards what could be condensed in short definitions.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a systematic analysis of the trade-off between definition length and the ability to capture essential information, nor does it explore whether longer definitions would improve the quality of the generated definitions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying the length of generated definitions and measuring the impact on the quality of the definitions across different biomedical domains and concept types, identifying the optimal length that balances comprehensiveness and conciseness.

## Limitations
- The human evaluation covered only 130 definitions out of over 400,000 generated, representing approximately 0.03% of the dataset
- The approach is tightly coupled to SNOMED CT's relationship structure and may not generalize well to other biomedical ontologies
- The study didn't validate whether high evaluation scores translate to actual utility in downstream applications

## Confidence
**High Confidence**: The core claim that GPT-3.5 can generate factually accurate biomedical definitions from ontological data is well-supported by human evaluation results showing 83% of definitions containing no incorrect information.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim about preventing hallucinations through information saturation is plausible given the results, but lacks direct mechanistic evidence. The paper shows correlation between information density and quality but doesn't establish causation through controlled experiments.

**Medium Confidence**: The assertion that AGCT provides a valuable resource for biomedical tasks is reasonable, but the actual utility in real-world applications remains to be demonstrated beyond the theoretical framework presented.

## Next Checks
1. **Downstream Task Validation**: Test AGCT definitions in actual biomedical applications (e.g., patient-facing medical explainers, clinical decision support systems) to verify that high evaluation scores translate to practical utility.

2. **Generalization Testing**: Apply the same methodology to alternative biomedical ontologies (e.g., MeSH, UMLS) with different relationship structures to assess robustness and identify potential failure modes.

3. **Long-term Stability Analysis**: Generate definitions for the same concepts at different time points to assess consistency and identify whether the model's performance degrades or improves with repeated exposure to similar ontological patterns.
