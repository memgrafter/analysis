# Pre-training for Speech Translation: CTC Meets Optimal Transport

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.11716
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11716
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 30
- Key outcome: CTC pre-training reduces modality gap in speech-to-text translation and outperforms cross-entropy, with optimal transport between speech and text encoders providing additional gains when combined with positional encoding.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the modality gap in speech-to-text translation (ST) by improving pre-training methods for speech and text encoders. The authors propose using Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss instead of cross-entropy for ASR pre-training, which preserves alignment information that benefits the subsequent ST task. They further introduce a Siamese-like architecture where speech and text encoders are trained to produce representations close in Wasserstein space using optimal transport, enhanced with positional encoding to capture monotonicity. Extensive experiments on CoVoST-2 and MuST-C datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art performance under no-external-data settings, outperforming strong multi-task learning baselines.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a two-stage approach to reduce the modality gap in speech translation. First, they replace standard cross-entropy pre-training with CTC loss for ASR, which trains the encoder to directly align speech to text without requiring a decoder for alignment. Second, they introduce Siamese-PT, a Siamese-like architecture that jointly trains speech and text encoders with CTC and optimal transport (OT) losses to produce representations close in Wasserstein space. The OT formulation incorporates positional encoding to account for the monotonic alignment between speech frames and text tokens. The method can be applied as pre-training before ST fine-tuning or potentially integrated into multi-task learning frameworks.

## Key Results
- CTC pre-training consistently achieves better ST accuracy than CE pre-training across multiple language pairs and datasets
- Siamese-PT with CTC and OT losses outperforms CTC alone, demonstrating the benefit of cross-modal alignment during pre-training
- Positional encoding in OT further improves performance by incorporating monotonicity constraints
- The method achieves state-of-the-art results on CoVoST-2 and MuST-C under no-external-data settings, improving over strong multi-task learning systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: CTC pre-training reduces modality gap because the encoder learns to align speech directly to text without needing a decoder for alignment.
- Mechanism: In standard CE pre-training, the ASR decoder aligns encoder representations to text, but this alignment information is lost when the decoder is discarded for ST. CTC trains the encoder to produce output-aligned representations directly, preserving this alignment when used in ST.
- Core assumption: The ST decoder benefits from encoder representations that already encode alignment information.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "pre-training with CTC consistently achieves better final ST accuracy"
  - [section 4.2] "the encoder trained with CTC already learns to align speech input to text output without a decoder"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related work focuses on zero-shot ST and OT, not specifically on CTC's alignment preservation mechanism
- Break condition: If the ST architecture doesn't rely on encoder-decoder alignment (e.g., purely decoder-based models), this benefit disappears.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Optimal transport (OT) between speech and text encoder representations further reduces modality gap by learning cross-modal alignment during pre-training.
- Mechanism: A Siamese-like architecture trains speech and text encoders to produce representations close in Wasserstein space, explicitly learning cross-modal alignment that benefits ST fine-tuning.
- Core assumption: Speech and text representations of the same content should be close in some meaningful metric space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "our method pre-trains a Siamese-like model composed of two encoders...such that they produce representations that are close to each other in the Wasserstein space"
  - [section 5.2] "we feed them to the corresponding encoders and train the model to produce features that are close to each other in terms of Wasserstein distance"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - related work on CMOT uses OT for cross-modal alignment in ST
- Break condition: If speech and text representations are fundamentally incompatible (e.g., different feature spaces), OT alignment won't help.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Positional encoding in OT loss improves performance by incorporating monotonicity constraints that exist in speech-text alignment.
- Mechanism: By modifying the OT cost matrix to penalize position mismatches, the model learns alignment that respects the monotonic nature of speech-to-text correspondence.
- Core assumption: Speech frames and text tokens maintain monotonic correspondence (first audio frames correspond to first text tokens).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we introduce positional encoding to the given optimal transport formulation in order to take into account the monotonicity of the inputs"
  - [section 5.2] "the inputs to our encoders are monotonically aligned...the first audio frames should represent the same part of the sentence as the first text tokens do"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no related work explicitly addresses monotonicity in OT for ST
- Break condition: If monotonicity assumption doesn't hold (e.g., heavily edited transcripts), positional encoding may hurt performance.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC)
  - Why needed here: CTC loss is central to both mechanisms - it reduces modality gap by preserving alignment and serves as the base loss in the Siamese architecture.
  - Quick check question: What problem does CTC solve in sequence modeling that standard cross-entropy cannot address?

- Concept: Optimal Transport (Wasserstein distance)
  - Why needed here: OT provides a principled way to compare sequences of different lengths (speech vs text features) and learn cross-modal alignment.
  - Quick check question: How does Wasserstein distance differ from Euclidean distance when comparing sequences of different lengths?

- Concept: Siamese networks
  - Why needed here: The Siamese architecture enables joint training of speech and text encoders with a shared objective (minimizing OT distance).
  - Quick check question: What are the key architectural requirements for a Siamese network to effectively learn cross-modal alignment?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Speech features → Speech encoder → CTC loss (alignment) + OT loss (cross-modal alignment) → Text encoder → OT loss → Siamese training → ST fine-tuning
- Critical path: Speech features → Speech encoder → CTC loss (alignment) + OT loss (cross-modal alignment) → Text encoder → OT loss → Siamese training → ST fine-tuning
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Weight sharing vs. separate encoders: Sharing may help with low-resource languages but separate encoders give more flexibility
  - Positional encoding in OT: Improves monotonic alignment but adds complexity and hyperparameters
  - CTC vs. CE: CTC preserves alignment but may have worse ASR performance; CE may be better for ASR but hurts ST alignment
- Failure signatures:
  - BLEU scores drop significantly on dev set after switching from CE to CTC pre-training (expected if alignment is important)
  - OT training becomes unstable or produces NaNs (check regularization λ and cost function scaling)
  - No improvement over baseline despite correct implementation (may need more training data or different hyperparameters)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement CTC pre-training baseline: Replace CE with CTC in standard ASR pre-training, evaluate ST performance vs. CE baseline
  2. Add Siamese OT without positional encoding: Jointly train speech and text encoders with CTC and OT loss, compare to CTC alone
  3. Add positional encoding to OT: Modify OT cost matrix with position penalties, evaluate impact on both alignment quality and ST performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the proposed Siamese-PT method perform when applied to extremely low-resource languages beyond those in CoVoST-2 (X→En directions)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that results for very low-resource languages in CoVoST-2 were poor (0.13–4.56 BLEU points) and excluded from comparison due to high variance.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation was limited to a specific set of low-resource languages, and the variance in performance suggests that scalability and effectiveness in truly resource-constrained settings remain unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on a broader set of extremely low-resource languages, possibly with additional data augmentation or transfer learning techniques, would clarify the method's robustness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the Siamese-PT approach be effectively integrated into multi-task learning frameworks as an additional loss term, rather than as a pre-training stage?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions this as a potential direction: "Applying OT directly to the latter (instead of pre-training) could be an interesting direction to explore."
- Why unresolved: The current work only evaluates Siamese-PT as a pre-training method, leaving open the question of whether it could be adapted for joint training with other tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing Siamese-PT as a pre-training stage versus as an additional loss in multi-task learning would provide clarity.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is the Siamese-PT method to the choice of optimal transport parameters, such as the regularization weight λ and the positional encoding weight γ?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses default OT parameters (λ = 1, γ = 1.0) but acknowledges that tuning these could further boost performance.
- Why unresolved: While default settings are provided, the paper does not explore the sensitivity or optimal ranges for these hyperparameters across different datasets or language pairs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies varying λ and γ across diverse language pairs and datasets would reveal their impact and optimal configurations.

## Limitations

- The method's effectiveness on extremely low-resource languages remains unclear, with poor performance observed on the most resource-constrained language pairs in CoVoST-2.
- The positional encoding mechanism in OT lacks strong theoretical justification and related work support, making it difficult to assess whether it's truly necessary.
- Experiments are limited to speech-to-text translation, leaving unclear whether the benefits transfer to other cross-modal tasks or architectures.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: CTC pre-training outperforms CE pre-training for ST accuracy. This claim is directly supported by quantitative results (Table 1) showing consistent BLEU improvements across multiple language pairs and datasets.

**Medium Confidence**: Optimal transport between speech and text encoders further reduces modality gap and improves ST performance. While the results (Table 1) show improvements with Siamese-PT, the mechanism is more complex and the gains are smaller than CTC alone, suggesting potential confounding factors.

**Low Confidence**: Positional encoding in OT significantly improves performance by incorporating monotonicity constraints. The paper provides minimal ablation studies on positional encoding's impact, and the related work section doesn't support this specific claim.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study on positional encoding**: Remove positional encoding from the OT loss and measure the impact on ST performance across multiple language pairs. This would directly test whether monotonicity constraints are necessary for the claimed benefits.

2. **Architecture generalization test**: Apply the CTC+OT pre-training approach to a decoder-only ST model (rather than encoder-decoder) to verify whether the alignment preservation mechanism is architecture-dependent or more general.

3. **Cross-modal alignment visualization**: Generate t-SNE or UMAP visualizations of speech and text encoder representations before and after Siamese-PT training to qualitatively assess whether representations actually become more aligned in practice.
