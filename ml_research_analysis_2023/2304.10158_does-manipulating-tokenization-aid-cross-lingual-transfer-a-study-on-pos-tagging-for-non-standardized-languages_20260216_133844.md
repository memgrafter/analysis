# Does Manipulating Tokenization Aid Cross-Lingual Transfer? A Study on POS Tagging for Non-Standardized Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.10158
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10158
- Reference count: 29
- Key outcome: Split word ratio difference is the strongest predictor of cross-lingual POS tagging performance; character-level noise injection can improve transfer but optimal noise level varies by language pair and model

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates zero-shot cross-lingual transfer for part-of-speech tagging from standardized languages to closely related non-standardized varieties. The authors find that the similarity in subword tokenization patterns between source and target data (measured as split word ratio difference) is the strongest predictor of model performance. Character-level noise injection during fine-tuning can improve transfer by adjusting source data tokenization to better match target data, though the optimal noise level depends on the specific language pair and model used. The study challenges the assumption that subword token overlap alone predicts transfer success, showing that this metric becomes less predictive when noise injection is used.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune multilingual PLMs (mBERT, XLM-R, and monolingual models) on seven source languages (German, Dutch, Norwegian, French, Spanish, MSA, Finnish) with character-level noise injection at varying rates (0% to 95%). They evaluate zero-shot transfer performance on 18 target dialect datasets from three language families, measuring POS tagging accuracy and analyzing subword tokenization differences including split word ratio, seen subwords/words, and TTR ratio. The experiments systematically vary noise injection levels to identify optimal operating points for each language pair.

## Key Results
- Split word ratio difference (similarity in subword tokenization between source and target) is the strongest predictor of cross-lingual POS tagging accuracy
- Character-level noise injection can improve transfer by adjusting source tokenization patterns, but optimal noise level varies significantly across language pairs
- Seen subword ratio becomes a poor predictor of performance when noise injection is used, suggesting token overlap alone is insufficient for transfer success

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Split word ratio difference predicts transfer performance
- Mechanism: Similar subword tokenization between languages preserves linguistic information boundaries, enabling better representation transfer
- Core assumption: Subword tokenization quality directly impacts POS tagging accuracy
- Evidence anchors: Abstract and section claims; weak corpus-level evidence
- Break condition: When linguistic differences are too large for tokenization similarity to compensate

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Noise injection adjusts tokenization patterns
- Mechanism: Random character modifications increase source data subword splits to match target patterns
- Core assumption: Mimicking target tokenization through noise improves transfer
- Evidence anchors: Abstract and section claims; weak corpus-level evidence
- Break condition: When noise level exceeds target split ratio or destroys linguistic patterns

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Token overlap is a poor predictor under noise injection
- Mechanism: Noise creates artificial overlaps that don't reflect true linguistic similarity
- Core assumption: Overlap alone is insufficient when morphological systems differ
- Evidence anchors: Section claims; weak corpus-level evidence
- Break condition: When noise injection is not used or languages are extremely similar

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Does optimal noise level generalize across different NLP tasks beyond POS tagging?
- How does split word ratio difference compare to other tokenization divergence metrics?
- What is the mechanism by which noise injection improves transfer across different morphological structures?
- How does character-level noise injection compare to BPE dropout?
- Does optimal noise level vary systematically with linguistic relatedness?

## Limitations

- Findings may not generalize beyond POS tagging to deeper linguistic tasks
- Experimental scope limited to 7 source languages and 18 target dialects
- Correlation between split word ratio difference and performance not causally proven
- Unclear whether noise injection preserves POS-relevant morphological information
- Optimal noise levels vary significantly across language pairs with no universal solution

## Confidence

**High confidence**: Split word ratio difference as predictor of transfer performance - consistently observed across language pairs and models
**Medium confidence**: Effectiveness of character-level noise injection - improvements shown but optimal levels vary and mechanism unclear
**Low confidence**: Seen subword ratio as poor predictor under noise injection - negative correlations observed but underlying reasons unexplored

## Next Checks

3. **Cross-task generalization experiment**: Apply tokenization manipulation to dependency parsing or semantic role labeling to test broader applicability
