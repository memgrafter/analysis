# Domain-adapted large language models for classifying nuclear medicine reports

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.01258
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01258
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Domain adaptation improved all transformer models for Deauville score prediction, with domain-adapted RoBERTa achieving 77.4% accuracy

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated the impact of domain adaptation on large language models for interpreting nuclear medicine reports, specifically for predicting Deauville scores from FDG PET/CT lymphoma reports. The researchers adapted multiple transformer-based models (BERT, RoBERTa, bioClinicalBERT, RadBERT) using masked language modeling on 4,542 nuclear medicine reports, then fine-tuned them for five-class classification. Domain adaptation consistently improved performance across all models, with domain-adapted RoBERTa achieving 77.4% accuracy - significantly outperforming both human physicians (66%) and vision-based models (48.1%). The results demonstrate that domain adaptation is crucial for optimizing language models in specialized medical domains.

## Method Summary
The study used 4,542 text reports and 1,664 images from FDG PET/CT lymphoma exams (2008-2018) to evaluate domain adaptation of transformer models. Reports were preprocessed by removing Deauville scores, dates, and punctuation, with only impression and findings sections retained due to token limits. Four transformer architectures (BERT, RoBERTa, bioClinicalBERT, RadBERT) were domain-adapted using masked language modeling on the full corpus for 3 epochs with a learning rate of 1e-6. Both original and domain-adapted models were then fine-tuned using 7-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation (80% train, 10% validation, 10% test) with cross-entropy loss and ADAM optimizer. Performance was evaluated using five-class classification accuracy and weighted Cohen's κ, compared against human expert interpretation and vision-based models using 2D MIP projections.

## Key Results
- Domain-adapted RoBERTa achieved 77.4% five-class accuracy, outperforming physicians (66%) and vision models (48.1%)
- All four transformer models showed consistent performance improvements after domain adaptation
- Domain-adapted bioClinicalBERT performed similarly to domain-adapted BERT (77.2% vs 77.4% accuracy)
- Vision models using 2D MIP projections achieved only 48.1% accuracy despite using both images and text

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Domain adaptation works by further pretraining general-purpose language models on domain-specific text corpora, allowing the models to learn specialized vocabulary, terminology, and contextual relationships unique to nuclear medicine. The masked language modeling objective forces the model to predict masked tokens based on surrounding context, effectively teaching it the statistical patterns and semantic relationships specific to PET/CT reports. This specialized pretraining creates a more informative feature representation for the downstream classification task, as evidenced by the consistent performance gains across all model architectures when adapted to the nuclear medicine domain.

## Foundational Learning
- Masked language modeling: Why needed - learns contextual representations of domain-specific terminology; Quick check - monitor validation loss during pretraining
- Transformer architecture: Why needed - captures long-range dependencies in clinical narratives; Quick check - verify attention patterns focus on clinically relevant terms
- Domain adaptation vs. fine-tuning: Why needed - distinguishes between task-specific learning and domain-specific knowledge acquisition; Quick check - compare performance of adapted vs. non-adapted models
- Five-class classification: Why needed - matches clinical Deauville scoring system for lymphoma assessment; Quick check - ensure balanced class distribution in training data
- Cross-validation strategy: Why needed - provides robust performance estimates and model selection; Quick check - verify consistent performance across folds

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Reports -> Preprocessing -> Domain Adaptation -> Fine-tuning -> Evaluation -> Accuracy/κ metrics
**Critical Path:** Domain adaptation → Fine-tuning → Evaluation (this sequence is essential for performance gains)
**Design Tradeoffs:** Using only impression/findings sections improves computational efficiency but may lose context; synonym replacement increases homogeneity but may obscure physician-specific patterns
**Failure Signatures:** Overfitting during domain adaptation (validation loss increases); poor tokenization causing information loss; class imbalance affecting classification performance
**First Experiments:** 1) Compare domain-adapted vs. original models on validation set; 2) Analyze attention weights to identify clinically relevant tokens; 3) Test different learning rates for domain adaptation to optimize convergence

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How well do the findings generalize to institutions with different reporting styles and practices?
- How does the performance of domain-adapted language models compare to other specialized medical domain adaptations beyond nuclear medicine?
- Can domain adaptation improve the performance of language models on other nuclear medicine interpretation tasks beyond Deauville score prediction?
- Would multimodal models show improved performance if trained with higher-dimensional image data rather than 2D MIP projections?

## Limitations
- Single-institution dataset limits generalizability to different reporting practices and vocabularies
- Token limit restrictions forced exclusion of complete reports, potentially omitting relevant clinical information
- 2D MIP projections used for vision models may not capture full prognostic value of 3D PET data
- Synonym replacement procedure lacks specific details, affecting reproducibility

## Confidence
High: Main findings about domain adaptation improving performance are well-supported by consistent results across multiple model architectures
Medium: Exact implementation details for preprocessing and synonym replacement are insufficiently specified for perfect reproducibility

## Next Checks
1. Verify the impact of different tokenization strategies on downstream performance by comparing results when including additional report sections versus the restricted impression/findings approach

3. Test model generalization by evaluating performance on reports from different institutions or time periods to assess whether improvements generalize beyond the training distribution
