# Accelerating exploration and representation learning with offline pre-training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.00046
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00046
- **Reference count**: 26
- **Primary result**: Offline pre-training with contrastive learning and progress rewards improves sample efficiency on NetHack tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method for improving sample efficiency in reinforcement learning by leveraging offline pre-training of state representations and progress reward models. The approach combines contrastive learning to pre-train a ResNet encoder on expert trajectories with a learned exploration bonus (ELE) that encourages expert-like progress. When used with the Muesli RL algorithm, this method significantly outperforms standard imitation learning baselines and the prior state-of-the-art Explore-Like-Experts method across a range of dense and sparse NetHack tasks.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two phases: offline pre-training and online fine-tuning. In the offline phase, a ResNet encoder is pre-trained using contrastive learning on expert trajectories from the NAO dataset, learning to capture temporal state dependencies. Simultaneously, a progress reward model is trained to estimate expert-like progress between states. In the online phase, the frozen pre-trained encoder initializes the Muesli RL agent, which learns to exploit the learned representation while using the progress reward to guide exploration. The approach demonstrates that freezing the pre-trained encoder during fine-tuning does not degrade performance, and that ResNet encoders are more effective than vision transformers for this domain.

## Key Results
- Offline pre-training with contrastive learning significantly improves sample efficiency on NetHack tasks
- Combining pre-trained representations with progress rewards provides orthogonal benefits for exploration and exploitation
- Freezing the pre-trained encoder during online fine-tuning does not degrade performance
- ResNet-based encoders outperform vision transformers for this domain

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Pre-training state representations via contrastive learning improves sample efficiency by capturing temporal state dependencies and perceptual invariances. Contrastive pre-training minimizes a loss that encourages the encoder to distinguish future states from negative samples, implicitly learning to approximate the future state visitation probability under the expert policy. This learned representation captures state connectivity structure, action effects, and sequential dependencies. Core assumption: The contrastive loss with negative sampling provides a robust learning signal that generalizes to states not seen in the offline data and remains useful when the agent explores new regions of the state space. Evidence anchors: abstract states contrastive learning can significantly improve sample efficiency; section claims contrastive learning is optimal for value function representation; corpus provides weak support from neighbors discussing offline-to-online RL.

### Mechanism 2
Combining the pre-trained representation with a learned progress reward model yields orthogonal benefits for exploration and exploitation. The progress model (ELE) provides an intrinsic reward that encourages the agent to follow expert-like trajectories by estimating temporal progress. When combined with the pre-trained representation, the agent can exploit the learned state space more effectively while being guided to explore promising states. Core assumption: The progress reward aligns well with the optimal value function in the MDP, providing a useful heuristic for exploration without conflicting with the representational advantages of the pre-trained encoder. Evidence anchors: abstract states representations improve downstream exploitation while the reward aids exploration; section discusses dataset heuristic alignment with optimal value function; corpus provides weak support from neighbors discussing offline policy improvement.

### Mechanism 3
Freezing the pre-trained encoder during online fine-tuning preserves the learned inductive biases while allowing the policy network to adapt. By keeping the representation fixed, the agent benefits from the pre-trained features without risking catastrophic forgetting or drift, while the LSTM and MLP on top learn to map these features to actions and values in the online environment. Core assumption: The frozen representation is sufficiently expressive to support learning in the new online task, and the policy network can adapt to the fixed features without needing to fine-tune them. Evidence anchors: section states the pre-trained encoder is kept frozen so agent does not drift away from pre-trained representation; section observes no significant difference on most tasks with or without freezing representations; corpus provides weak support as neighbors do not discuss freezing representations in offline-to-online RL context.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Contrastive learning and noise-contrastive estimation**
  - Why needed here: Contrastive learning is used to pre-train the state representation by distinguishing future states from negative samples, capturing temporal dependencies and state similarities.
  - Quick check question: How does the contrastive loss ensure that the learned representation captures meaningful state similarities rather than trivial or collapsed features?

- **Concept: Intrinsic motivation and exploration bonuses**
  - Why needed here: The progress reward model provides an intrinsic signal that guides the agent to explore states that lead to expert-like progress, addressing the challenge of sparse rewards in long-horizon tasks.
  - Quick check question: Why might a progress-based reward be more effective than count-based exploration in tasks with sparse rewards?

- **Concept: Representation learning for RL and value function approximation**
  - Why needed here: The pre-trained representation is used to initialize the encoder in the RL agent, improving sample efficiency by providing a good starting point for learning value functions and policies.
  - Quick check question: How does using a pre-trained representation simplify the task of learning value functions compared to learning from raw observations?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Offline pre-training (contrastive encoder + progress model) -> Online fine-tuning (Muesli agent with frozen encoder + ELE reward)
- **Critical path**: Pre-train contrastive encoder → Pre-train progress model → Initialize Muesli with frozen encoder → Add ELE reward → Train online
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Freezing vs. fine-tuning encoder: Freezing preserves pre-trained features but may limit adaptation; fine-tuning risks overfitting or forgetting.
  - ResNet vs. ViT encoder: ResNet is more sample-efficient and better suited to the TTY observation space; ViT requires more data and may not capture local invariances as well.
  - Progress reward vs. no reward: Progress reward helps in sparse tasks but may not improve dense tasks; representation learning alone may not suffice for exploration.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor performance on sparse tasks: May indicate the progress reward is not well aligned or the representation is not expressive enough.
  - No improvement from pre-training: May suggest the offline data is unrepresentative or the contrastive loss is not capturing useful features.
  - Degradation when freezing encoder: May indicate the online task differs significantly from offline data.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Run Muesli with and without pre-trained encoder on a dense task (e.g., Score) to verify sample efficiency gains.
  2. Run ELE with and without pre-trained encoder on a sparse task (e.g., Depth-2) to verify exploration benefits.
  3. Compare freezing vs. fine-tuning the encoder on a representative task to confirm the best approach.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do pre-trained representations from contrastive learning and progress rewards from ELE interact at a mechanistic level? Are there scenarios where they might conflict or reinforce each other? The paper states that pre-training and progress rewards provide "orthogonal benefits" but does not explore their interaction dynamics in detail. The paper focuses on empirical results showing combined performance but lacks theoretical analysis of the interplay between representation learning and exploration rewards. Experiments isolating the effects of pre-trained representations and progress rewards on learning dynamics, or theoretical analysis of their combined impact on exploration and exploitation, would resolve this.

### Open Question 2
What are the limitations of using contrastive learning for pre-training in environments with different characteristics than NetHack (e.g., continuous state spaces, different types of dynamics)? The paper uses contrastive learning on discrete TTY observations in NetHack and compares ResNet to ViT, but does not explore other architectures or continuous domains. The experiments are limited to one domain and architecture, leaving open questions about generalizability. Experiments on diverse environments (e.g., continuous control tasks, Atari games) and comparisons of different pre-training objectives would resolve this.

### Open Question 3
How does the choice of encoder architecture (e.g., ResNet vs. ViT) impact the quality of pre-trained representations and their effectiveness in downstream RL tasks? The paper includes an ablation showing ResNet outperforms ViT on NetHack, but does not explain why or explore other architectures. The paper identifies a performance difference but does not investigate the underlying reasons or explore the design space of encoder architectures. Analysis of representation quality (e.g., through probing tasks or visualization), or experiments with other architectures (e.g., MLP-Mixer, Swin Transformer), would resolve this.

## Limitations
- The reported benefits are demonstrated primarily on NetHack tasks and may not generalize to other domains or observation spaces
- The analysis of freezing vs. fine-tuning is limited to a narrow set of conditions without exploring scenarios where domain shift might necessitate fine-tuning
- The comparison between ResNet and ViT is confined to NetHack, leaving open questions about which architectures work best for different types of visual observations

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the orthogonal benefits claim: The ablation studies and combined results provide strong evidence that pre-training representations and learning progress rewards offer complementary advantages for exploitation and exploration respectively.
- **Medium confidence** in the freezing mechanism: While results show no significant difference, the limited scope of tested scenarios and absence of analysis on domain shift scenarios prevents stronger claims.
- **Medium confidence** in ViT vs ResNet findings: The conclusion that ResNet is more effective is based on comparisons within NetHack, but the analysis does not establish whether this holds across different types of visual observations or task complexities.

## Next Checks

1. Test representation pre-training benefits with alternative offline datasets (e.g., different games or environments) to assess generalization beyond NetHack.
2. Evaluate the impact of freezing vs. fine-tuning under domain shift conditions where online states differ substantially from offline data.
3. Conduct ablation studies comparing different progress reward formulations (e.g., count-based vs. ELE) to isolate the specific contribution of the proposed progress model to exploration performance.
