# Adversarial robustness of amortized Bayesian inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.14984
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14984
- Reference count: 40
- The paper proposes a computationally efficient regularization scheme based on penalizing the Fisher information matrix to improve adversarial robustness of amortized Bayesian inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of adversarial robustness in amortized Bayesian inference, where small, targeted perturbations to observed data can drastically change the predicted posterior distributions. The authors propose a computationally efficient regularization scheme based on penalizing the Fisher information of the conditional density estimator. This approach is shown to improve the adversarial robustness of amortized Bayesian inference across several benchmark tasks and a real-world example from neuroscience.

## Method Summary
The authors propose regularizing amortized Bayesian inference with the trace of the Fisher information matrix (FIM) to improve adversarial robustness. During training, they estimate the FIM trace using Monte Carlo sampling and update it with an exponential moving average to reduce computational cost. The FIM regularization term is added to the standard negative log-likelihood loss, with a hyperparameter β controlling the trade-off between accuracy on clean data and robustness to adversarial perturbations. The method is evaluated on six benchmark tasks and a real-world neuroscience example, comparing it to other defense methods such as adversarial training and TRADES.

## Key Results
- FIM regularization significantly reduces the Kullback-Leibler divergence between posterior estimates given clean and perturbed data
- The method leads to more reliable posterior estimates with realistic predictive samples
- FIM regularization is shown to be effective and computationally efficient compared to other defense methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Regularizing the negative log-likelihood with the trace of the Fisher information matrix (FIM) reduces the sensitivity of amortized Bayesian inference to adversarial perturbations. Adversarial perturbations are small, targeted changes in input data that exploit high-curvature directions in the model's output space. The trace of the FIM captures the average curvature of the conditional density estimator with respect to the input, and penalizing it smooths the model along directions where small changes in input cause large changes in output. Core assumption: The curvature of the conditional density estimator with respect to the input correlates with its vulnerability to adversarial perturbations.

### Mechanism 2
Using a moving average estimator for the trace of the FIM reduces computational cost while maintaining robustness. The trace of the FIM needs to be estimated for every data point at every training iteration, which is expensive. By using an exponential moving average, the estimator smooths across iterations, reducing the number of Monte Carlo samples needed and avoiding explicit computation of the full FIM matrix. Core assumption: The FIM changes slowly across training iterations, so a moving average is a good approximation.

### Mechanism 3
The FIM regularization induces a trade-off between accuracy on clean data and robustness to adversarial perturbations. Regularizing with the FIM trace biases the conditional density estimator to be smoother, which reduces its sensitivity to small input changes (improving robustness) but can also make the posterior estimates overly broad or incorrect on unperturbed data (reducing accuracy). Core assumption: There is an inherent trade-off between model smoothness (robustness) and accuracy (expressivity) that can be controlled by the regularization strength β.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input data -> Conditional density estimator (MAF/NSF) -> Posterior distribution -> FIM regularization term -> Total loss -> Backpropagation
- Critical path: During training, for each batch: (1) compute the standard NPE loss, (2) estimate the trace of the FIM for each data point via MC sampling, (3) update the moving average of the FIM trace, (4) add the FIM regularization term to the loss, (5) backpropagate and update the model parameters.
- Design tradeoffs: The FIM regularization improves robustness but can make posterior estimates overly broad; the moving average estimator reduces computational cost but may lag behind rapid changes in the model; using the trace instead of the largest eigenvalue simplifies computation but is a looser bound on adversarial sensitivity.
- Failure signatures: If the model becomes too smooth, posterior estimates will be overly broad and uninformative; if the moving average is too slow, the regularization may not adapt to changes in the model; if the MC samples are too few, the FIM estimate will be noisy and regularization ineffective.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a simple conditional density estimator (e.g., diagonal Gaussian) on a toy 1D regression task with and without FIM regularization, and visualize how the posterior changes under adversarial perturbations.
  2. Implement the moving average estimator for the FIM trace and verify that it tracks the true trace over training iterations on a small dataset.
  3. Compare the adversarial robustness of a model trained with FIM regularization versus adversarial training on a simple benchmark (e.g., Lotka-Volterra) by measuring the KL divergence between posteriors on clean vs. perturbed data.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does the FIM regularization approach scale effectively to high-dimensional data beyond the examples tested in the paper? The paper demonstrates FIM regularization on a real-world neuroscience example with 31 parameters but does not extensively test scalability to much higher dimensions. Testing FIM regularization on a task with significantly higher dimensionality (e.g., hundreds or thousands of parameters) and comparing its performance to other methods in terms of both robustness and computational efficiency would help resolve this question.

### Open Question 2
How does the choice of divergence measure (e.g., MMD vs. KL) impact the effectiveness of adversarial attacks and defenses? While the paper briefly compares attacks using KL divergence and MMD, it does not systematically investigate how different divergence measures affect the strength of adversarial attacks or the performance of defenses. A comprehensive comparison of adversarial attacks and defenses using various divergence measures (e.g., KL, MMD, Wasserstein distance) on multiple benchmark tasks, analyzing the impact on both attack strength and defense effectiveness, would help resolve this question.

### Open Question 3
Can the FIM regularization approach be extended to other amortized inference methods beyond NPE, such as Neural Ratio Estimation (NRE) or Neural Likelihood Estimation (NLE)? The paper focuses on NPE but mentions that other simulation-based inference methods can be categorized as amortized as well. Extending the FIM regularization approach to NRE and NLE, evaluating its effectiveness in improving adversarial robustness for these methods, and comparing the results to those obtained with NPE would help resolve this question.

## Limitations

- The effectiveness of the moving average estimator for FIM trace may degrade when training involves rapid parameter changes or non-stationary dynamics
- The choice of regularization strength β requires careful tuning and may need problem-specific calibration
- The approach has not been tested on high-dimensional, real-world datasets with complex posterior geometries

## Confidence

- FIM regularization mechanism: High (supported by theory and experiments)
- Computational efficiency claims: Medium (depends on implementation details and dataset size)
- Real-world applicability: Medium (validated on one neuroscience example, needs broader testing)
