# Discourse Structure Extraction from Pre-Trained and Fine-Tuned Language Models in Dialogues

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05895
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05895
- Reference count: 29
- One-line primary result: Unsupervised and semi-supervised methods achieve 57.2% and 59.3% F1 scores respectively on STAC corpus for dialogue discourse structure extraction

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of extracting discourse structures from dialogue data, which is typically sparse. The authors propose an unsupervised and semi-supervised method to leverage attention matrices from pre-trained language models (PLMs) to build discourse structures for dialogues. They introduce a novel Sentence Ordering fine-tuning task tailored to dialogues and propose a Dependency Attention Support (DAS) metric to select the most informative attention heads. The proposed methods achieve encouraging results on the STAC corpus, with F1 scores of 57.2% and 59.3% for unsupervised and semi-supervised methods, respectively. When restricted to projective trees, the scores improve to 63.3% and 68.1%.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a novel approach to extract discourse structures from dialogues using attention matrices from pre-trained language models. The method involves fine-tuning a BART model on a sentence ordering task with dialogue-specific shuffling strategies, then extracting dependency trees from the attention matrices. A Dependency Attention Support (DAS) metric is introduced to select the most informative attention heads for discourse structure extraction. The approach is evaluated on the STAC corpus using Micro-F1 and Unlabeled Attachment Score (UAS) metrics.

## Key Results
- Unsupervised DAS-selected method achieves 57.2% F1 score on STAC corpus
- Semi-supervised method with validation set selection achieves 59.3% F1 score
- Projective tree restriction improves scores to 63.3% (unsupervised) and 68.1% (semi-supervised)
- Sentence ordering fine-tuning outperforms other fine-tuning tasks like summarization and QA

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Discourse information is encoded in specific attention heads within PLMs.
- Mechanism: Attention heads in deeper layers capture discourse-level dependencies by aggregating token-level relationships into EDU-level structures.
- Core assumption: Discourse structure can be inferred from token-level attention patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we explore approaches to build discourse structures for dialogues, based on attention matrices from Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs)."
  - [section 3.3] "Given an attention matrix At∈ Rk×k where k is the number of tokens in the input dialogue, we derive the matrix Aedu∈ Rn×n... by computing Aedu(i, j) as the average of the submatrix of At corresponding to all the tokens of EDUs ei and ej."
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus evidence is indirect, relies on observed F1 improvements.
- Break condition: If attention patterns do not correlate with EDU dependencies, this mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning on dialogue-tailored sentence ordering improves discourse extraction.
- Mechanism: Shuffling strategies (partial, minimal-pair, block, speaker-turn) expose the model to discourse relationships specific to dialogues.
- Core assumption: Dialogue discourse differs structurally from monologue discourse, requiring tailored training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose a new Sentence Ordering (SO) fine-tuning task tailored to dialogues."
  - [section 3.2] "Since fully random shuffling showed very limited improvements, we considered additional strategies to support a more gradual training tailored to dialogues."
  - [corpus] STAC and DailyDialog corpora provide dialogue-specific examples, but corpus quality assessment reveals Molweni has issues.
- Break condition: If discourse relationships are not better captured through SO fine-tuning compared to other tasks, this mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Dependency Attention Support (DAS) metric effectively identifies discourse-rich attention heads.
- Mechanism: DAS measures the degree of support for the maximum spanning tree from the attention matrix, enabling selection of the most informative heads.
- Core assumption: Discourse information is not uniformly distributed across attention heads.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose an unsupervised and a semi-supervised method. Our proposals achieve encouraging results on the STAC corpus..."
  - [section 3.4.1] "We propose a DAS metric measuring the degree of support for the maximum spanning (dependency) tree (MST) from the attention matrix."
  - [corpus] Direct - F1 scores (57.2% unsupervised, 59.3% semi-supervised) provide evidence.
- Break condition: If DAS does not correlate with discourse information, or if discourse is uniformly distributed across heads, this mechanism fails.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input: Dialogue text (EDUs) -> PLM: BART (encoder attention matrices) -> Fine-tuning: Sentence ordering task with various shuffling strategies -> Selection: DAS metric (unsupervised) or validation set (semi-supervised) -> Output: Discourse dependency tree

- Critical path:
  1. Preprocess dialogue into EDUs
  2. Obtain attention matrices from PLM
  3. Aggregate to EDU-level attention
  4. Generate MST using Eisner algorithm
  5. Select best head using DAS or validation set

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Unsupervised vs semi-supervised: Requires labeled data vs. more generalizable
  - Head selection: Global vs local support strategy
  - Fine-tuning: Intra-domain vs inter-domain training data

- Failure signatures:
  - Low DAS scores across all heads
  - MST generation fails (disconnected graph)
  - F1 scores close to LAST baseline

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare unsupervised DAS-selected head vs random head on STAC
  2. Evaluate impact of different shuffling strategies in SO fine-tuning
  3. Test layer-wise vs head-wise attention matrix aggregation

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of sentence ordering (SO) shuffling strategies impact the performance of the fine-tuned language models in capturing discourse information?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces several novel shuffling strategies for the SO task tailored to dialogues and demonstrates their effectiveness over other fine-tuning tasks.
- Why unresolved: While the paper shows that the SO task outperforms other fine-tuning tasks, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how different shuffling strategies within the SO task impact the performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments evaluating the performance of language models fine-tuned with different SO shuffling strategies on discourse structure extraction tasks would provide insights into the impact of shuffling strategies on capturing discourse information.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the Dependency Attention Support (DAS) metric be adapted to select discourse-rich attention heads in pre-trained language models for longer documents or dialogues?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper proposes the DAS metric to measure the degree of support for dependency trees generated by attention heads and mentions the computational efficiency concern for longer documents.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the application of the DAS metric to select discourse-rich heads in pre-trained models for longer documents or dialogues, and the computational efficiency concern for longer documents is mentioned but not addressed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments demonstrating the effectiveness of the DAS metric in selecting discourse-rich attention heads in pre-trained models for longer documents or dialogues, along with an analysis of computational efficiency, would provide evidence for the adaptability of the DAS metric.

## Limitations
- The unsupervised DAS selection mechanism's effectiveness remains uncertain for domains beyond STAC and DailyDialog
- Sentence ordering fine-tuning approach shows diminishing returns for long dialogues (>23 EDUs)
- The EDU segmentation model (DisCoDisCo) used for experiments with predicted EDUs is not fully specified

## Confidence
- High confidence: The attention matrix extraction and EDU aggregation mechanism (Section 3.3) is well-specified and reproducible
- Medium confidence: The DAS metric's effectiveness in identifying discourse-rich attention heads is supported by experimental results
- Low confidence: The claim that discourse information is uniformly distributed across attention heads is contradicted by the need for DAS selection

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain robustness test**: Apply the unsupervised DAS-selected method to a different dialogue corpus (e.g., Switchboard or MRDA) to assess whether the 57.2% F1 score on STAC generalizes to other domains and whether DAS remains effective without domain-specific fine-tuning.

2. **Long-range dependency analysis**: Conduct ablation studies on document length effects by systematically varying dialogue length (10, 15, 20, 25 EDUs) and measuring precision/recall specifically for indirect arcs (>6 EDUs apart) to understand the performance degradation patterns.

3. **Attention head distribution study**: Perform statistical analysis of DAS scores across all attention heads (12 layers × 16 heads = 192 heads) to verify whether discourse information is indeed concentrated in specific heads or whether the current selection mechanism is capturing random variation in attention patterns.
