# Faster Federated Learning with Decaying Number of Local SGD Steps

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.09628
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09628
- Reference count: 39
- Key outcome: Decaying local SGD steps per round improves federated learning convergence and efficiency

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of client drift in federated learning when using multiple local SGD steps per round. The authors propose decaying the number of local steps (K) as training progresses, showing that this approach can match or improve upon fixed-K baselines in terms of convergence speed, generalization, and total computational cost. The method is theoretically motivated and validated across four benchmark federated learning datasets.

## Method Summary
The method involves modifying Federated Averaging by decaying the number of local SGD steps per round according to either a fixed schedule (Kr-rounds) or an adaptive schedule based on training error plateaus (Kr-error). The decay schedule is designed to reduce client drift in later training stages when the model is closer to convergence, while maintaining fast initial convergence through larger K values early in training.

## Key Results
- Decaying K can match or improve convergence speed compared to fixed-K baselines
- The approach reduces total computational cost by decreasing expensive local training in later rounds
- Error-based decay schedules automatically balance convergence speed and final accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Decaying K reduces client-drift in later training stages when the model is closer to convergence
- Mechanism: As training progresses and the global model error decreases, the benefit of more local SGD steps diminishes because the models are already close to the global minimum. Reducing K decreases the divergence between client models, allowing better alignment during averaging.
- Core assumption: Client-drift scales with both the heterogeneity level (Γ) and the number of local steps (K²) as shown in Theorem 1.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "client data in real-world FL is highly heterogeneous, which has been extensively shown to slow model convergence and harm final performance when K > 1 steps of SGD are performed on clients per round"
  - [section] "The extent of client-drift has been shown theoretically to be proportional to the level of heterogeneity between client data, the client learning rate (η), and K"
- Break condition: If heterogeneity is very low (Γ ≈ 0), decaying K provides minimal benefit and may slow convergence.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Decaying K reduces total computational cost by decreasing expensive local training in later rounds.
- Mechanism: The runtime model shows that total computation scales with ∑R Kr. By decreasing K over time, the paper achieves the same or better convergence with fewer total SGD steps.
- Core assumption: Communication time dominates computation time (|x|/D + |x|/U >> βK), making round time less sensitive to K in later stages.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Using K > 1 local steps improves the per-round convergence rate compared to dSGD (hence saving on communication), and FedAvg only requires a fraction of all clients to participate in each round"
  - [section] "Therefore by decaying Kr during training, a balance between fast convergence and higher round-completion rate can be achieved"
- Break condition: If client computation is extremely slow relative to communication, the benefit of reducing K may be offset by increased communication overhead.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Adaptive K scheduling based on training progress (error-based decay) automatically balances convergence speed and final accuracy.
- Mechanism: By monitoring training loss and decreasing K when convergence plateaus, the algorithm allocates computational resources efficiently - using many steps early for fast progress, then fewer steps later for precision.
- Core assumption: Training loss is a reliable proxy for proximity to the global minimum and can guide when to reduce K.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "If we make the further assumption that F* = 0, substituting these two sets of values into (9) and dividing gives us Kr in terms of K0: Kr = ⌈∛(F(x̄xr)/F(x̄x0) K0⌉"
  - [section] "We can therefore use a similar strategy for FedAvg: once the validation error plateaus we decay either K or η"
- Break condition: If validation error plateaus are not clearly detectable due to noise, the decay schedule may trigger prematurely or too late.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Strongly convex optimization theory and its convergence bounds
  - Why needed here: The convergence analysis (Theorem 1) relies on L-smoothness and µ-strong convexity assumptions to derive bounds on gradient norms
  - Quick check question: Why does the analysis assume strongly convex objectives when most FL applications use neural networks?

- Concept: Variance reduction in distributed SGD
  - Why needed here: The paper shows that K > 1 reduces variance introduced by client stochastic gradients, but this benefit diminishes as training progresses
  - Quick check question: How does client heterogeneity (Γ) affect the variance reduction benefit of larger K?

- Concept: Communication-computation tradeoff in federated learning
  - Why needed here: The runtime model balances download/upload time against local computation, which is critical for understanding when to decay K
  - Quick check question: Why does the optimal K decrease with O(1/∛W) according to Theorem 2?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Server-side: Model averaging, K and η scheduling, validation monitoring
  - Client-side: Local SGD computation, loss reporting, model upload
  - Communication: Download/upload bandwidth constraints, participation sampling
  - Monitoring: Training loss tracking, validation error plateau detection

- Critical path:
  1. Server sends global model to selected clients
  2. Clients perform Kr local SGD steps and compute training loss
  3. Clients upload updated models and training losses
  4. Server averages models, updates schedules, checks validation performance
  5. Server selects next round's participants

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Aggressive K decay (Kr-rounds) vs. conservative K decay (Kr-error)
  - Fixed η vs. adaptive η scheduling
  - Training loss vs. validation loss for decay triggering
  - Communication frequency vs. local computation intensity

- Failure signatures:
  - Training loss increases after K decay: decay too aggressive or triggered too early
  - Validation accuracy plateaus: insufficient K or η decay schedule
  - Communication bottleneck dominates: K decay provides minimal benefit
  - Client dropout increases: K decay makes rounds too computationally demanding

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run FedAvg with Kη-fixed as baseline, record convergence curves and total SGD steps
  2. Implement Kr-rounds schedule, compare training error and validation accuracy at fixed wall-clock time
  3. Add Kr-error schedule with rolling average loss estimation, validate improved resource efficiency

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Open Question 1: How does decaying K in Federated Averaging impact convergence and generalization performance for non-convex objectives, such as deep neural networks?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors analyze convergence for strongly-convex objectives and note that this assumption is commonly used in the literature despite FL typically being used for DNNs with non-convex objectives. They state this as a limitation and suggest deriving optimal K schedules for non-convex objectives as future work.
  - Why unresolved: The analysis relies on strong convexity, which does not hold for most deep learning problems. Non-convex optimization has different convergence properties, and the relationship between K and client drift may differ significantly.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing decaying K schedules on non-convex benchmarks, along with theoretical analysis extending the current results to non-convex settings.

- Open Question 2: What is the optimal trade-off between the number of local steps K and the number of clients participating per round N to minimize wall-clock time and communication costs in realistic federated learning deployments?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors discuss how larger K reduces variance but increases client drift, while more participating clients reduce variance. They note that real-world participation rates vary and pose the trade-off between K and N as an interesting direction for future research.
  - Why unresolved: The analysis assumes constant participation rates, but real-world FL systems have highly variable participation. The optimal balance between K and N depends on many factors including client compute capabilities, network conditions, and data heterogeneity.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Large-scale simulations and real-world experiments varying both K and N across diverse client populations and network conditions.

- Open Question 3: How do the proposed decaying K schedules interact with and improve upon other federated learning techniques designed to address client drift, such as FedProx, SCAFFOLD, or adaptive optimization methods?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that their decaying K approach could in principle be combined with any FedAvg variant, and suggest this as a potential avenue for future research. They also survey several such techniques in the related work section.
  - Why unresolved: The analysis and experiments focus on FedAvg in isolation. Combining decaying K with other techniques could lead to multiplicative benefits, but the interactions are not well understood.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies combining decaying K with various client drift mitigation techniques on diverse FL benchmarks, measuring convergence speed, generalization, and resource efficiency.

## Limitations
- The theoretical convergence analysis assumes strongly convex objectives, which may not hold for deep neural networks commonly used in FL
- The effectiveness of decaying K depends heavily on the heterogeneity level Γ, which varies across datasets and tasks
- The computational cost reduction claims assume the runtime model accurately reflects real FL systems

## Confidence
- Theoretical convergence guarantees: Medium (limited to strongly convex case)
- Experimental results on benchmark datasets: High (empirical evidence provided)
- Runtime cost reduction claims: Medium (model-based rather than measured)

## Next Checks
