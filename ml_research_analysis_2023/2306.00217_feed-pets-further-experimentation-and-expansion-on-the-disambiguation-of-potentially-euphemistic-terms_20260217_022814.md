# FEED PETs: Further Experimentation and Expansion on the Disambiguation of Potentially Euphemistic Terms

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00217
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00217
- Reference count: 7
- Key outcome: Transformers perform better on vague euphemistic terms and multilingual models achieve preliminary success in euphemism disambiguation across English, Yoruba, Spanish, and Mandarin Chinese

## Executive Summary
This study expands on English euphemism disambiguation by annotating potentially euphemistic terms (PETs) for vagueness and creating novel euphemism corpora in three additional languages: Yoruba, Spanish, and Mandarin Chinese. The researchers find that transformers are generally better at classifying vague PETs (VETs) than non-vague ones, suggesting linguistic differences in the data impact performance. They also demonstrate that multilingual transformer models like mBERT and XLM-RoBERTa can perform euphemism disambiguation across languages, establishing preliminary results for future work in multilingual euphemism detection.

## Method Summary
The study involves annotating English PETs for vagueness using annotator paraphrase similarity, then creating new euphemism corpora in Yoruba, Spanish, and Mandarin Chinese. The researchers fine-tune multilingual transformer models (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa-base, XLM-RoBERTa-large) on these datasets using 80-20 train-test splits with 10 epochs, learning rate 1e-5, and batch size 16. Evaluation uses 5-fold cross-validation to report macro-F1, precision, and recall scores across all four languages.

## Key Results
- Transformers achieve higher performance on vague euphemistic terms (VETs) compared to non-vague PETs
- Multilingual transformer models successfully disambiguate euphemisms in Yoruba, Spanish, and Mandarin Chinese
- Performance varies across languages, with some languages showing more challenges than others

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Transformers capture pragmatic context cues for euphemism disambiguation.
- Mechanism: The transformer architecture models long-range dependencies and contextual embeddings, enabling it to infer whether a term is being used euphemistically based on surrounding words and syntactic patterns.
- Core assumption: Contextual word representations learned during pretraining contain implicit knowledge about figurative language usage.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "transformers perform well on many major NLP benchmarks" and are applied to "euphemism disambiguation task"
  - [section] Performance is higher for vague euphemistic terms (VETs), indicating transformers leverage context to resolve ambiguity
- Break condition: If the dataset lacks sufficient context-rich examples, or if the euphemisms are too culturally specific for the pretraining corpus, performance will degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Vagueness annotations help reveal differences in classification difficulty.
- Mechanism: Annotating PETs for vagueness (VETs vs non-VETs) creates subgroups with different linguistic properties, exposing that transformers handle some cases better than others.
- Core assumption: Vagueness is a meaningful, measurable linguistic property that affects model performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "performance is generally higher for VETs" and analysis suggests non-vague PETs co-occur with more sensitive words, confusing the classifier
  - [corpus] Annotation method uses cosine similarity of annotator paraphrases as proxy for vagueness
- Break condition: If vagueness is not a stable property across contexts or annotators disagree significantly, the labels will not be meaningful.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multilingual transformers generalize euphemism disambiguation across languages.
- Mechanism: Pretrained multilingual models (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) transfer knowledge of euphemism-like patterns from English to other languages, achieving reasonable performance without language-specific training.
- Core assumption: There are cross-linguistic commonalities in how euphemisms are constructed and used.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] Experiments are conducted in Yoruba, Spanish, and Mandarin Chinese using "multilingual transformer models"
  - [section] All languages achieve "at least decently" performance, suggesting transformers pick up on shared features
- Break condition: If euphemisms are too culturally bound or structurally different across languages, transfer will fail and performance will be poor.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the underlying linguistic differences that make transformers better at classifying vague euphemistic terms (VETs) compared to non-vague PETs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that transformers perform better on vague PETs but doesn't provide a clear explanation for this discrepancy.
- Why unresolved: The study attempted to investigate this through error analysis but didn't reach a definitive conclusion. They speculated about BERT associating sensitive words with euphemisms, but this was not conclusively proven.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further experiments with model interpretability techniques like attention analysis could reveal whether transformers are indeed focusing on sensitive words when classifying VETs. Additionally, testing on datasets with controlled linguistic features could isolate the specific properties that affect classification performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we develop a more reliable and standardized method for annotating vagueness in euphemistic terms across different languages and contexts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that their current method for annotating vagueness (using annotator paraphrases and cosine similarity) is experimental and makes strong assumptions about context-independence of vagueness.
- Why unresolved: The lack of a clear, universally accepted definition of vagueness for this task led to the indirect annotation approach. The assumption that all PETs are vague or not regardless of context may not hold true in practice.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and validating a more direct annotation scheme for vagueness, potentially through expert linguistic consultation, would provide more reliable labels. Testing this scheme across multiple languages and contexts would establish its generalizability and robustness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the cross-lingual features that contribute to euphemism disambiguation, and how can we leverage them to improve multilingual BERT models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper presents initial results on multilingual euphemism disambiguation but notes that the datasets differ in ways that prevent direct comparison, and suggests that cross-lingual features could be investigated.
- Why unresolved: The study establishes preliminary baselines but doesn't conduct detailed error analyses or systematic comparisons across languages to identify common features that aid disambiguation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting systematic error analyses on each language dataset to identify common misclassification patterns, and then testing whether incorporating these features (e.g., sensitivity scores, pragmatic markers) improves disambiguation across all languages would reveal cross-lingual patterns. Additionally, testing zero-shot and few-shot learning approaches could indicate whether certain features are universal or language-specific.

## Limitations
- Small euphemism corpora (961-1952 examples) may not represent full diversity of euphemistic usage
- Vagueness annotation method using cosine similarity of paraphrases may introduce noise and lacks clear definition
- Cross-lingual experiments lack language-specific adaptation, leading to variable performance across languages
