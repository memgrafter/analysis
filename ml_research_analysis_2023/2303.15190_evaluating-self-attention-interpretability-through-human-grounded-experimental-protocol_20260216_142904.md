# Evaluating self-attention interpretability through human-grounded experimental protocol

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15190
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15190
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CLS-A method achieves comparable human performance to LIME/SHAP while being computationally cheaper

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes CLS-A, a novel interpretability method for Transformer-based text classifiers that leverages attention weights from the classification token. The method computes importance scores for each word by aggregating attention coefficients from the last layer across all heads. Through human-grounded experiments where participants classify texts with words colored by importance scores, CLS-A demonstrates performance comparable to established methods like LIME and SHAP in terms of reaction time and accuracy, while offering lower computational cost since it uses attention weights already computed during the forward pass.

## Method Summary
CLS-A is a model-specific interpretability method that extracts feature importance scores from Transformer attention weights. The approach focuses on the classification token's attention distribution in the last layer of the Transformer, aggregating attention weights across all heads to produce a score for each input word. These scores are then normalized and used to color words in experimental displays. The method was evaluated through human-grounded experiments where participants performed text classification tasks (sentiment analysis and movie genre classification) while viewing texts with words colored according to CLS-A, LIME, SHAP, or random baseline importance scores. Performance was measured through reaction time and classification accuracy.

## Key Results
- CLS-A achieves comparable reaction times and accuracy to LIME and SHAP in human-grounded experiments
- The method demonstrates lower computational cost by using attention weights already computed during the forward pass
- CLS-A performs well across two different classification tasks: sentiment analysis and movie genre classification
- The method's availability within the classifier (no additional model evaluations needed) makes it particularly practical for real-time applications

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: CLS-A leverages the distribution of attention coefficients from the classification token to compute local feature importance for each word.
- Mechanism: For each input word, CLS-A aggregates the attention weights from all heads in the last layer of the Transformer, where intermediate representations become more abstract. These weights represent how much the classification token attends to each word, so averaging them produces a score proportional to the word's influence on the final prediction.
- Core assumption: The attention distribution in the last layer encodes the most abstract and decision-relevant information, and averaging across heads preserves useful signal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "CLS-A computes an interpretability score for each word based on the attention coefficient distribution related to the part specific to the classification task within the Transformer architecture."
  - [section] "Motivated by the fact that intermediate representations of deep neural networks become more abstract with network depth [29], we focus on the last layer of the BERT architecture."
- Break condition: If the classification token does not pass through the CLS token, or if the last-layer attention is noisy or non-informative, the averaged scores will not reflect true feature importance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: CLS-A's attention-based scores provide semantic congruence with human judgment, improving both annotation speed and accuracy.
- Mechanism: In the human-grounded experiment, participants see texts with words colored according to CLS-A importance. Words highlighted by CLS-A tend to align with those humans would naturally focus on for the classification task, reducing cognitive load and enabling faster, more accurate decisions.
- Core assumption: Human semantic processing relies on similar cues as the Transformer's attention when performing classification, so aligning explanations with attention improves task performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "CLS-A performs comparably to usual interpretability methods regarding average participant reaction time and accuracy."
  - [section] "An explanation will then be the object of a semantic congruence between the label to be predicted and the words highlighted."
- Break condition: If CLS-A's attention weights do not correlate with human salience for the task, or if the coloring distracts rather than aids, the semantic congruence and performance gains will disappear.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: CLS-A is computationally cheaper than post-hoc methods like LIME and SHAP because it uses attention weights already computed during the forward pass.
- Mechanism: Since attention matrices are produced internally by the Transformer for every forward pass, extracting them incurs no additional model evaluations or perturbations, unlike LIME's neighborhood sampling or SHAP's permutation-based estimates.
- Core assumption: The attention matrices from the last layer are available at no extra cost beyond the standard classification forward pass.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The lower computational cost of CLS-A compared to other interpretability methods and its availability by design within the classifier make it particularly interesting."
  - [section] "Since the CLS token plays a central role in the computation of CLS-A, it is recommended that the BERT forward pass passes through the CLS token in order to perform its prediction."
- Break condition: If extracting attention weights requires additional computation (e.g., from intermediate layers or across all heads individually), the cost advantage diminishes or vanishes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input text → Tokenizer → DistilBERT encoder stack (6 layers) → CLS token embedding → Dense classifier → Prediction
  - Attention extraction: Last-layer attention matrices (h heads) → Average across heads → CLS-A scores per token
  - Coloring: Map scores to blue intensity for display in experiment

- Critical path:
  1. Tokenize input
  2. Run forward pass through DistilBERT
  3. Extract last-layer attention matrices
  4. Average across heads to get CLS-A scores
  5. Truncate/scale scores for display
  6. (In experiment) Show colored text to user and log response

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Speed vs. fidelity: Averaging across heads is fast but may lose head-specific patterns; focusing on the last layer captures abstract info but may miss lower-layer nuances.
  - Simplicity vs. flexibility: CLS-A is model-specific to Transformers using CLS tokens, so it can't be applied to models without such a token without modification.

- Failure signatures:
  - CLS-A scores are uniform or random → likely attention matrices are not being extracted correctly or the model does not use CLS token for classification.
  - CLS-A performance matches or is worse than random baseline → semantic congruence is broken; attention may not encode task-relevant cues.
  - CLS-A is slower than LIME/SHAP → implementation inefficiency in extracting attention or unnecessary recomputation.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Verify attention extraction**: Run a forward pass on a known text, print last-layer attention matrices, confirm non-trivial values per head.
  2. **Check CLS-A score distribution**: Compute CLS-A scores on a validation set, plot histogram, ensure they vary meaningfully across words.
  3. **Benchmark runtime**: Time CLS-A extraction vs. LIME/SHAP on same inputs to confirm claimed computational advantage.

## Limitations

- The method's effectiveness may not generalize to multi-class classification tasks, as experiments were only conducted on binary classifiers
- The semantic congruence between attention weights and human judgment may not extend to domains with different linguistic patterns or more complex reasoning requirements
- The averaging of attention weights across all heads may mask important head-specific patterns that could provide more nuanced explanations

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Computational efficiency claim for CLS-A compared to LIME and SHAP is well-supported
- **Medium Confidence**: Comparative performance results showing CLS-A matches LIME and SHAP in reaction time and accuracy are credible
- **Low Confidence**: Broader claims about attention weights inherently encoding task-relevant feature importance extend beyond experimental evidence

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-Domain Validation**: Test CLS-A on classification tasks from different domains (e.g., medical text classification, legal document categorization) to assess whether the semantic congruence between attention weights and human judgment holds across diverse semantic spaces.

2. **Attention Head Ablation Study**: Conduct experiments removing or masking specific attention heads before averaging to determine whether all heads contribute equally to CLS-A's performance, or whether certain heads are more informative than others.

3. **Comparative Ablation with Random Attention**: Generate random attention matrices with similar statistical properties to actual attention weights and compare CLS-A's performance against this baseline to quantify how much of the method's effectiveness stems from genuine signal versus artifacts in the attention mechanism.
