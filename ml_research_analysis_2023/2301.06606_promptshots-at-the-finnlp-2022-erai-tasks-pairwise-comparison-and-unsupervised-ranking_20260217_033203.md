# PromptShots at the FinNLP-2022 ERAI Tasks: Pairwise Comparison and Unsupervised Ranking

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.06606
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06606
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: Chinese few-shot trained InstructGPT ranked 3rd on ML pairwise accuracy; unsupervised ranking submissions ranked 1st-3rd on MPP

## Executive Summary
This report describes PromptShots' submissions to the FinNLP-2022 ERAI task on Evaluating the Rationales of Amateur Investors. For pairwise comparison, we employed instruction-based models (T5-small and OpenAI InstructGPT) in a few-shot setting, finding that Chinese few-shot trained InstructGPT outperformed English versions and finetuned models. For unsupervised ranking, we utilized domain-specific language models and Bayesian lexicons learned from co-occurrence statistics. Our submissions achieved top rankings in MPP scoring but struggled with ML scoring, suggesting different treatments are needed for these metrics.

## Method Summary
We approached the ERAI task through two distinct methods: pairwise comparison using few-shot instruction-based language models (T5-small and OpenAI InstructGPT), and unsupervised ranking using domain-specific language models (FinBERT, FinProLex, NTUSD-Fin) combined with Bayesian lexicons learned via method-of-moments estimation. The pairwise comparison used minimal examples with prompt engineering, while the unsupervised ranking extracted features including lexicon scores, POS tags, and market sentiment indicators.

## Key Results
- Chinese few-shot trained InstructGPT ranked 3rd on maximal loss (ML) pairwise accuracy
- All instruction-based submissions performed poorly on maximal potential profit (MPP) pairwise accuracy
- Unsupervised ranking submissions achieved 1st-3rd positions in MPP ranking
- All unsupervised submissions performed poorly on ML scoring despite success on MPP
- The same heuristic functions worked for both MPP and ML in unsupervised ranking but not in pairwise comparison

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Few-shot prompt-based instruction models can outperform finetuned models when training data is scarce.
- Mechanism: Instruction-based models like T5-small and OpenAI InstructGPT are pretrained on large corpora and can be adapted to new tasks with minimal examples via prompting, avoiding the need for extensive task-specific finetuning.
- Core assumption: The model's pretraining provides sufficient task-agnostic knowledge to generalize from a few examples when given the right prompt structure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "OpenAI InstructGPT language model few-shot trained on Chinese data works best in our submissions, ranking 3rd on the maximal loss (ML) pairwise accuracy."
  - [section] "For pairwise comparison, we employed instruction-based models based on T5-small (Raffel et al., 2020) and OpenAI InstructGPT language models (Ouyang et al., 2022) in a few-shot prompt-based setting (Brown et al., 2020b)."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the related papers focus on pairwise ranking with LLMs but do not directly compare few-shot prompting vs finetuning performance.
- Break condition: If the prompt structure fails to align the model's capabilities with the task format, or if the few examples are not representative of the task distribution.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Financial domain-specific language models improve unsupervised ranking performance compared to general models.
- Mechanism: Models like FinBERT, pretrained on financial texts, capture domain-specific semantics and vocabulary, enabling better feature extraction for financial opinion evaluation.
- Core assumption: The financial corpora used in pretraining cover the linguistic patterns and terminology relevant to amateur investor rationales.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "FinBERT (Huang et al., 2020) is essentially BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) customized for financial texts, pretrained on corporate filings, analyst reports, and earnings conference call transcripts."
  - [section] "In the FinBERT paper, FinBERT outperforms all other methods, Loughran McDonald lexicon, and machine learning algorithms, especially in negative financial sentiment prediction."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers discuss LLM ranking but do not specifically evaluate domain-specific models vs general models.
- Break condition: If the financial domain in the task significantly differs from the corpora used in FinBERT's pretraining.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Bayesian lexicons learned from co-occurrence statistics can provide effective unsupervised sentiment features for ranking.
- Mechanism: Method-of-moments estimation fits lexicon weights to the data without labels, capturing word-level associations with positive/negative sentiment relevant to the target task.
- Core assumption: The unsupervised fitting process can discover meaningful sentiment associations even without explicit labels, and these associations generalize to the evaluation task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Bayesian lexicon learns predictive weights for each word in a lexicon using a method-of-moments estimator from co-occurrence statistics without any labels as a special case of multinomial Naive Bayes."
  - [section] "For example, when we fitted the LM lexicon using the pairwise comparison data, we gave 0.02626 to 'good', 0.00501 to 'optimistic', and 0.00186 to 'highest'."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence - related papers do not discuss Bayesian lexicon learning for ranking tasks.
- Break condition: If the unsupervised fitting overfits to noise in the co-occurrence statistics or if the lexicon vocabulary is too limited to capture relevant semantics.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: T5-small and OpenAI InstructGPT (pairwise comparison) -> FinBERT, FinProLex, NTUSD-Fin, LM lexicon, POS features, Bayesian lexicons (unsupervised ranking)
- Critical path: For pairwise comparison - construct prompt → generate prediction → compare outputs. For unsupervised ranking - extract features → aggregate scores → rank posts.
- Design tradeoffs: Few-shot prompting vs finetuning (speed/adaptability vs potential performance); domain-specific vs general models (relevance vs flexibility); unsupervised vs supervised feature learning (no labels required vs potential performance).
- Failure signatures: Pairwise comparison - poor accuracy despite high-quality prompts; unsupervised ranking - rankings not correlating with ground truth metrics.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test different prompt formats for the instruction models on a small validation set.
  2. Compare feature importance by ablating individual feature sets in the unsupervised ranking.
  3. Evaluate the impact of lexicon fitting methods (Dirichlet vs multinomial) on ranking performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why does the Chinese few-shot trained InstructGPT model outperform the English version by such a large margin in pairwise comparison tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that the Chinese few-shot trained InstructGPT model performs significantly better than the English version, ranking 3rd on ML pairwise accuracy while the English version performs worse than T5-small.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of why Chinese data performs better, only noting the difference in performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative linguistic analysis of the Chinese and English datasets, including tokenization differences, idiomatic expressions, and cultural context that might affect model performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why do instruction-based models perform poorly on MPP pairwise accuracy despite performing better on ML accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that all instruction-based submissions do not perform well on MPP pairwise accuracy where there are more data and learning signals, though the Chinese few-shot trained InstructGPT still performs best.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explain the fundamental differences between MPP and ML tasks that cause this performance discrepancy.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed analysis of the MPP and ML tasks' characteristics, including their data distributions, complexity, and whether they require different modeling approaches.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why do the same heuristic functions work for both MPP and ML in unsupervised ranking when they perform differently in pairwise comparison?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that the same formula was used for both MPP and ML in unsupervised ranking, despite acknowledging that they need different treatments.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explain why the same approach works for unsupervised ranking but not for pairwise comparison.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative study of how the scoring functions behave differently in the two tasks, including whether the ranking nature of unsupervised tasks makes the approach more forgiving.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What specific features in the heuristic functions contribute most to the performance differences between MPP and ML predictions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses different feature sets for MPP and ML (e.g., positive vs. negative FinWord counts), suggesting some features are more relevant to one task than the other.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't conduct ablation studies to determine which features are most important for each task.
- What evidence would resolve it: Feature importance analysis through ablation studies or SHAP values to identify which features drive performance for MPP versus ML.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How would more sophisticated parameter tuning or larger model sizes affect the performance of instruction-based models in this task?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes they did "very minimal parameter tuning to the model, only prompt engineering" and used relatively small models like T5-small.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explore the potential benefits of more extensive hyperparameter optimization or larger models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing performance with different model sizes, hyperparameter tuning strategies, and optimization techniques to establish the impact on task performance.

## Limitations
- Small test set size (157 pairs) limits statistical significance of pairwise comparison results
- Limited hyperparameter tuning may have left performance improvements on the table
- No ablation studies to determine individual feature contributions in unsupervised ranking
- Cross-linguistic comparison between Chinese and English data lacks detailed linguistic analysis

## Confidence
- High confidence: The effectiveness of few-shot instruction models for pairwise comparison tasks
- Medium confidence: The superiority of Chinese data over translated English data for this specific task
- Low confidence: The generalizability of our unsupervised ranking approach across different market sentiment scenarios

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-linguistic validation**: Test the few-shot instruction models on additional language pairs beyond Chinese-English to determine if the observed performance gap is task-specific or represents a broader linguistic phenomenon.

2. **Sample size sensitivity analysis**: Conduct power analysis to determine the minimum number of pairwise comparisons needed to reliably detect performance differences between model variants, particularly for the MPP accuracy metric.

3. **Ablation study on ranking features**: Systematically remove individual feature components (lexicons, POS features, etc.) from the unsupervised ranking pipeline to quantify their individual contributions and identify potential redundancy in the feature set.
