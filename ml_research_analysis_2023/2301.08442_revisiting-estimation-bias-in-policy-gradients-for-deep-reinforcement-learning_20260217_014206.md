# Revisiting Estimation Bias in Policy Gradients for Deep Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.08442
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08442
- Reference count: 18
- This paper studies bias from using undiscounted state distributions in policy gradient estimation and shows three techniques that mitigate this bias in deep RL.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates a fundamental bias issue in policy gradient methods when using undiscounted state distributions instead of the theoretically correct discounted ones. The authors demonstrate that this bias can lead to suboptimal policies through a phenomenon they call "state aliasing" in deep RL with neural network parameterizations. Through theoretical analysis and empirical experiments on continuous control tasks, they show that this bias can be effectively reduced using three techniques: small learning rates, adaptive learning rate optimizers (RMSProp/Adam), and KL regularization. The results provide important insights into practical issues in deep RL optimization and suggest concrete directions for improving policy gradient methods.

## Method Summary
The paper analyzes policy gradient bias arising from using undiscounted state distributions in deep RL. The theoretical framework considers episodic MDPs with discount factor γ, where the true policy gradient should use discounted state distribution dγπ(s) but implementations often use undiscounted d1π(s). The authors introduce the state alias phenomenon where neural network feature representations cause different states with different optimal actions to map to similar representations, amplifying the bias effect. They propose three mitigation techniques: small learning rates that prevent large, harmful parameter updates; adaptive optimizers like RMSProp and Adam that estimate empirical Fisher Information Matrices; and KL regularization that creates a "potential well" preventing large policy updates in suboptimal regions. Experiments are conducted on Mujoco continuous control tasks using simple 2-hidden-layer networks with Monte Carlo value estimation.

## Key Results
- Using undiscounted state distributions introduces significant bias in policy gradient estimation
- Small learning rates, adaptive optimizers (RMSProp/Adam), and KL regularization all effectively reduce this bias
- Experimental results show these techniques can rectify performance degradation caused by the bias in continuous control tasks
- Analysis demonstrates the proposed techniques reduce bias influence during training

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Small learning rates reduce the influence of bias in policy gradient estimation.
- Mechanism: When the learning rate is small, parameter updates are incremental, which prevents the policy from making large, potentially harmful steps that could be steered by the biased gradient estimates. This creates a more stable optimization trajectory that is less susceptible to the state alias phenomenon.
- Core assumption: The state alias phenomenon causes biased gradients to point in directions that would lead to suboptimal policies, but small updates allow the policy to gradually correct course.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that, despite such state distribution shift, the policy gradient estimation bias can be reduced in the following three ways: 1) a small learning rate; 2) an adaptive-learning-rate-based optimizer; and 3) KL regularization."
  - [section] "We further find a relatively small learning rate is also beneficial for bias rectiﬁcation. However, a smaller learning rate directly leads to a much slower convergence process..."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct mention of learning rate effects on bias in related papers.
- Break condition: If the learning rate is too small, convergence becomes impractically slow, making the approach infeasible for real-world applications.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adaptive learning rate optimizers like RMSProp and Adam reduce bias by estimating an empirical Fisher Information Matrix.
- Mechanism: These optimizers adapt the learning rate per parameter based on the second moment of gradients. This can be interpreted as estimating an empirical Fisher Information Matrix, which provides curvature information about the loss surface. By scaling gradients inversely proportional to their variance, these optimizers dampen the effect of noisy, biased gradient estimates.
- Core assumption: The Fisher Information Matrix captures the curvature of the loss surface in a way that naturally mitigates the harmful effects of biased gradient estimates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that, despite such state distribution shift, the policy gradient estimation bias can be reduced in the following three ways: 1) a small learning rate; 2) an adaptive-learning-rate-based optimizer; and 3) KL regularization."
  - [section] "RMSProp estimates the second moment of the gradients by statistics, and divides the parameter gradient by the square root of the second moment... RMSProp can be seen as estimating an empirical Fisher information matrix (FIM), which is a measure of the curvature of the loss surface."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers don't discuss Fisher Information Matrix connections to bias mitigation.
- Break condition: If the gradient estimates are consistently biased in a specific direction, the adaptive scaling might not fully compensate for the systematic error.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: KL regularization reduces bias by creating a "potential well" that penalizes large policy updates in suboptimal regions.
- Mechanism: KL regularization adds a term to the objective that penalizes divergence between the current policy and the previous policy. This creates a loss surface where the region around the current policy is relatively flat, but the loss increases sharply as you move away. This "potential well" structure means that even if biased gradients suggest large updates, the regularization term creates a steep penalty that discourages such moves.
- Core assumption: The biased gradient estimates often suggest large policy updates that would move the policy into regions of high KL divergence, which the regularization term naturally penalizes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We further draw connections between optimizers and the optimization regularization to show that both the KL and the reverse KL regularization can significantly rectify this bias."
  - [section] "The regularized objective function gives a large penalty on the suboptimal areas... the loss surface rapidly becomes harsh in the larger neighborhood in the regularized case. This 'potential well' mechanism maintains the performance, mitigating large disturbances caused by biased gradient estimations."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers mention KL regularization but don't discuss its role in bias mitigation.
- Break condition: If the regularization coefficient is too large, it might overly constrain exploration and prevent the policy from escaping local optima.

## Limitations

- The empirical evaluation is limited to specific continuous control tasks with relatively simple network architectures (2 hidden layers, 16 units each), which may not capture the full complexity of real-world deep RL applications.
- The theoretical analysis relies on assumptions about state aliasing that, while plausible, are not rigorously proven for general neural network parameterizations.
- The connection between the Fisher Information Matrix interpretation of adaptive optimizers and bias mitigation, while intuitively appealing, lacks formal theoretical grounding.
