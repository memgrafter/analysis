# Gradient Descent in Neural Networks as Sequential Learning in RKBS

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2302.00205
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00205
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Neural network training via gradient descent is exactly equivalent to sequential regularized risk minimization in reproducing kernel Banach spaces

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a rigorous theoretical framework that represents neural network training as sequential learning in reproducing kernel Banach spaces (RKBS). The authors construct an exact feature map representation of neural networks that enables training analysis beyond the over-parametrized regime. By representing the network as an inner product in RKBS, they prove that gradient descent weight updates are precisely equivalent to regularized risk minimization, with the learning rate and iteration count serving as implicit regularizers. The framework provides novel uniform convergence bounds where iteration count and learning rate play central roles, and derives Rademacher complexity bounds that scale inversely with dataset size and network width, supporting early stopping as a regularization mechanism.

## Method Summary
The paper develops a power series representation of fully-connected feedforward neural networks in a finite neighborhood of initial weights. This representation enables the construction of feature maps from data and weight-step space to feature space in RKBS. The authors prove that gradient descent updates on network weights are exactly equivalent to regularized risk minimization in this space. The method involves computing shadow weights and scale factors that ensure the feature maps converge properly, with theoretical bounds on weight changes that keep the training within the modelable range. The framework connects to existing neural tangent kernel theory while extending analysis to the non-asymptotic regime where network width and dataset size are finite.

## Key Results
- Gradient descent weight steps are exactly equivalent to regularized risk minimization in RKBS
- Rademacher complexity bound scales as O(1/√m) with dataset size m and network width
- Learning rate η and iteration count T serve as implicit regularizers with trade-off coefficient λ = 1/(ην)
- Theoretical bounds ensure weight changes stay within the modelable neighborhood of initial weights

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework works by constructing a power series representation of the neural network that converges in a neighborhood around initial weights. This representation allows the network to be expressed as an inner product in RKBS, where gradient descent becomes equivalent to sequential regularized risk minimization. The key insight is that the learning rate and iteration count in gradient descent naturally correspond to regularization strength in the RKBS formulation. The shadow weights and scale factors ensure the feature maps converge properly, while the bounded activation functions and weight changes keep the representation valid throughout training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reproducing Kernel Banach Spaces (RKBS)**: Function spaces with kernel representations that generalize RKHS theory - needed for the feature map construction, check by verifying kernel properties
- **Power series representation**: Local approximation of network functions around initial weights - needed for the RKBS embedding, check by validating convergence radius
- **Rademacher complexity**: Measure of function class complexity for generalization bounds - needed for uniform convergence analysis, check by computing empirical bounds
- **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**: Limit kernel for infinite-width networks - needed as comparison point, check by verifying the NNK reduces to NTK in the infinite-width limit
- **Shadow weights**: Auxiliary weights in the feature map construction - needed for convergence, check by verifying theoretical bounds on their magnitude
- **Scale factors**: Normalization constants in the feature map - needed for proper scaling, check by ensuring they satisfy the convergence conditions

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Input data -> Activation functions -> Power series expansion -> Feature maps (data & weight-step space) -> RKBS inner product -> Regularized risk minimization

**Critical Path**: The critical path is the computation of feature maps from data and weight-step space, which enables the RKBS representation. This requires computing shadow weights, scale factors, and ensuring the power series converges within the specified neighborhood.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The framework trades off between the radius of convergence for the power series (larger radius allows more weight change but requires more terms) and the complexity of computing the feature maps (more terms increase computational cost but provide better accuracy).

**Failure Signatures**: 
- Weight steps exceeding the modelable range (violating theorem 1 bounds)
- Feature map divergence (scale factors not satisfying theorem conditions)
- Rademacher complexity bound becoming vacuous (indicating poor generalization)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What is the precise mathematical form of the neural neighborhood kernels (NNKs) for networks of arbitrary depth and width?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The authors mention that the NNK is the un-approximated analog of the NTK but do not provide its explicit form, stating "the interested reader can find them in appendix C" and later noting "A Taylor approximation the NNKs is readily obtained, but it is diﬃcult to say how accurate this may be without further analysis."
- **Why unresolved**: The explicit derivation and closed-form representation of NNKs is not provided in the main text or supplementary material, leaving their exact mathematical structure unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A complete derivation showing the exact matrix-valued kernel expressions for arbitrary network architectures, along with numerical validation of their accuracy compared to NTK approximations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How do the shadow weights (ω[j] and ˜ω[j]) scale with network depth and width in practice?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The authors derive theoretical bounds on shadow weights in Theorem 8 but do not provide empirical evidence or practical guidelines for their selection in real networks.
- **Why unresolved**: While the paper establishes theoretical conditions for shadow weights to ensure convergence of feature maps, it does not demonstrate how these weights behave in practical implementations or how they scale with network parameters.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical studies showing shadow weight values across different network architectures, depth levels, and width configurations, along with analysis of their relationship to other network parameters.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the exact relationship between learning rate and the degree of regularization required for the canonical scaling equivalence?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state "The trade-oﬀ coeﬃcient (degree of regularisation) required by this canonical scaling is: λ = 1/(ην) = B[D−1]2/(4η)" and show this in Figure 3, but do not explore the practical implications of this relationship.
- **Why unresolved**: While the paper derives the theoretical relationship between learning rate and regularization, it does not investigate how this relationship manifests in actual training dynamics or provide practical guidance for hyperparameter tuning.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical studies showing the effect of different learning rates on the required regularization strength across various network architectures, and practical guidelines for setting these hyperparameters based on the theoretical framework.

## Limitations
- The framework requires activation functions with power-series representations having specific radii of convergence, which may be restrictive for common activations like ReLU
- The exact construction of neural neighborhood kernels (NNKs) is not fully specified in the main text
- The scale factors μ[j]_ij+1 and shadow weights require careful selection to ensure convergence, with limited practical guidance provided
