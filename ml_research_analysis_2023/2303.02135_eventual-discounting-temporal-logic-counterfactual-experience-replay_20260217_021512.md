# Eventual Discounting Temporal Logic Counterfactual Experience Replay

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.02135
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02135
- Reference count: 24
- This paper introduces eventual discounting and LTL-guided counterfactual experience replay for reinforcement learning with LTL specifications

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of reinforcement learning with Linear Temporal Logic specifications, where standard discounting can lead to catastrophic myopic behavior. The authors introduce eventual discounting, a technique that avoids discriminating between return times between good states in the LTL satisfaction automaton. They also develop LTL-guided Counterfactual Experience Replay (LCER), which exploits the known structure of the LTL specification to generate off-policy data from on-policy rollouts, addressing reward sparsity issues.

## Method Summary
The method combines two key innovations: eventual discounting and counterfactual experience replay. Eventual discounting modifies the standard discount factor by setting it to 1 for states not in the accepting set SB*, preventing myopic behavior in LTL tasks. LCER generates counterfactual trajectories by exploiting the LDBA structure, creating multiple off-policy trajectories from single on-policy rollouts by changing initial LDBA states or adding jump transitions. The approach works with both Q-learning and policy gradient methods, providing theoretical guarantees on satisfaction probability through discretization bounds.

## Key Results
- Eventual discounting prevents myopic behavior by ensuring equal reward for visiting accepting states regardless of inter-visit time
- LCER significantly improves learning performance by generating counterfactual experiences from on-policy trajectories
- The approach provides non-asymptotic guarantees on satisfaction probability through discretization bounds

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Eventual discounting prevents catastrophic myopic behavior by avoiding discrimination between return times between good states in the LTL satisfaction automaton.
- Mechanism: The discount factor γ is set to 1 for states not in the accepting set SB*, but remains unchanged (typically < 1) for states in SB*. This ensures that the agent is rewarded equally for visiting accepting states regardless of how long it takes between visits.
- Core assumption: The probability of satisfying the LTL specification is maximized by policies that visit the accepting states of the LDBA infinitely often, not by those that minimize time between visits.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "First, we develop a new value-function based proxy, using a technique we call eventual discounting, under which one can find policies that satisfy the LTL specification with highest achievable probability."
  - [section] "To avoid this myopic behavior, we must avoid discriminating between return times between good states."
  - [corpus] "The core idea is to reformulate the value function to account for the fact that optimally satisfying an LTL specification may not depend on the length of time it takes to satisfy it."
- Break condition: If the LTL specification inherently requires minimizing time between certain events (e.g., stability requirements), eventual discounting may not be optimal.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LCER generates counterfactual trajectories by exploiting the known structure of the LTL specification automaton, addressing reward sparsity.
- Mechanism: For each on-policy trajectory, LCER generates multiple off-policy trajectories by changing the initial state of the LDBA or adding jump transitions. This creates many trajectories that are consistent with the LTL specification but start from different points in the automaton.
- Core assumption: The LDBA transition function PB is completely known and does not depend on the MDP dynamics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Second, we develop a new experience replay method for generating off-policy data from on-policy rollouts via counterfactual reasoning on different ways of satisfying the LTL specification."
  - [section] "By knowing PB, we can generate multiple off-policy trajectories from a single on-policy trajectory by modifying which states in the LDBA we start in."
  - [corpus] "We combat reward sparsity by exploiting the LDBA: PB is completely known."
- Break condition: If the LDBA is too large or complex, generating all counterfactual trajectories may become computationally intractable.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The eventual discounting approach provides non-asymptotic guarantees on the probability of satisfying the LTL specification.
- Mechanism: By bounding the difference between the value function under eventual discounting and the true probability of satisfaction, the paper shows that policies optimized under eventual discounting approach optimal probability satisfaction as the discount factor approaches 1.
- Core assumption: The number of policies in the policy class Π is finite, or the MDP and LDBA are discretized.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The eventual discounting approach provides non-asymptotic guarantees on the probability of satisfying the LTL specification."
  - [section] "Corollary 4.4 reveals that discretization suffices to generate probability optimal policies, with suboptimality shrinking at a rate of log(1/γ)."
  - [corpus] "We prove in Section 4 that the optimal policy under eventual discounting maximizes the probability of satisfying the LTL specification."
- Break condition: If the MDP has continuous state-action spaces without discretization, the guarantees may not hold.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  MDP environment with labeled states -> LDBA for LTL specification tracking -> Policy (Q-network or policy network) -> LCER module for generating counterfactual experiences -> Training loop with eventual discounting

- Critical path:
  1. Execute policy in MDP environment
  2. Collect trajectory with state labels and LDBA states
  3. Generate counterfactual experiences using LCER
  4. Update policy using eventual discounted rewards
  5. Repeat until convergence

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Eventual discounting vs. standard discounting: Eventual discounting avoids myopic behavior but may not be optimal for all LTL specifications.
  - LCER vs. no experience replay: LCER addresses reward sparsity but increases computational cost.
  - Q-learning vs. policy gradient: Q-learning is simpler but may not scale to continuous action spaces; policy gradient is more flexible but requires more careful handling of the LDBA structure.

- Failure signatures:
  - If the policy fails to satisfy the LTL specification, check if the LDBA is correctly constructed and if the eventual discounting is properly implemented.
  - If learning is slow or unstable, verify that the LCER is generating diverse counterfactual experiences and that the discount factor is appropriately chosen.
  - If the policy exhibits myopic behavior, ensure that the eventual discounting is correctly setting γ=1 for non-accepting states.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test eventual discounting on a simple LTL specification (e.g., eventually reach a goal) in a gridworld environment. Verify that it outperforms standard discounting.
  2. Implement LCER on the same task and compare learning speed with and without LCER. Check that LCER generates the expected counterfactual experiences.
  3. Scale up to a more complex LTL specification (e.g., oscillate between two regions while avoiding a third) and a continuous control environment. Verify that the policy learns to satisfy the specification and that LCER provides a significant performance boost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the sufficient conditions on the MDP transition function P and policy class Π to ensure supπ∈ΠOπ is finite for the eventual discounting bound to be meaningful?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses this as the "bad news" section, noting that conditions for supπ∈ΠOπ being finite are nontrivial and dependent on the landscape of P and Π.
- Why unresolved: The paper states that determining these conditions is left for future work and provides only the discrete case as an example where the bound is finite.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal proof showing that compactness of P and continuity of P are sufficient conditions for supπ∈ΠOπ to be finite in continuous state-action spaces.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of LCER scale with the size of the LDBA state space SB?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the LCER algorithm generates multiple counterfactual trajectories by modifying LDBA states, but doesn't analyze how this scales with |SB|.
- Why unresolved: The experiments only use relatively small LDBAs, and the paper doesn't provide theoretical analysis of the computational complexity of LCER as a function of |SB|.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results showing LCER performance degradation as |SB| increases, or theoretical bounds on the computational complexity of LCER in terms of |SB|.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the LCER approach be extended to other temporal logics beyond LTL, such as Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) or Signal Temporal Logic (STL)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses exclusively on LTL and LDBA constructions, with no discussion of other temporal logics.
- Why unresolved: The paper establishes LCER specifically for LTL/LDBA but doesn't explore whether the core idea of counterfactual experience replay could work with other temporal logics that have different automata representations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation and experimental results of LCER using automata representations for MTL or STL, showing whether the approach transfers to these richer temporal logics.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on discretization assumptions that may not hold in truly continuous spaces
- Computational cost of LCER grows with LDBA size, potentially limiting scalability
- Limited validation to relatively simple LTL specifications and environments

## Confidence

- Medium confidence: LCER approach's effectiveness in more complex scenarios
