# Adversarial Clean Label Backdoor Attacks and Defenses on Text Classification Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19607
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19607
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 24

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces an adversarial clean-label attack for NLP backdoor attacks that leverages adversarial examples to poison training data with significantly reduced poisoning budgets. The method achieves high attack success rates (ASRs >99%) across sentiment classification, natural language inference, and spam detection tasks while maintaining task accuracy. The authors also benchmark several defense methods, including adaptations of computer vision defenses and novel text-specific approaches. Their proposed Soft-DPA (S-DPA) defense performs best, offering improved ASR reduction and computational efficiency over existing methods.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes an Adversarial Clean Label (A-CL) attack that generates poisoned samples by augmenting target class training points using adversarial example generation approaches, specifically BertAttack. The attack requires only 20% of the poisoning budget compared to baseline clean-label attacks. For defense, the authors explore several methods including ONION, Random perturbation, DPA, S-DPA, k-NN, and Paraphrasing. The proposed S-DPA defense is an extension of Deep Partition Aggregation (DPA) that trains a single classification model using soft labels from k DPA models, achieving superior performance in ASR reduction and computational efficiency.

## Key Results
- A-CL attacks achieve >99% ASR across three NLP tasks with only 20% of the poisoning budget compared to baseline methods
- S-DPA defense outperforms other defense methods, including DPA, with better ASR reduction and computational efficiency
- Paraphrasing defenses show surprisingly strong performance despite their simplicity

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adversarial Clean Label (A-CL) attacks reduce poisoning budget by generating adversarial examples that simulate misclassified instances while preserving clean labels.
- Mechanism: The attack uses adversarial example generation to create samples that the model perceives as belonging to the non-target class, but retain their true target class labels. This simulates label-flipping without actually mislabeling.
- Core assumption: Adversarial examples generated from the target class can be perceived by the model as belonging to the non-target class while maintaining their true labels.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We introduce an Adversarial Clean Label attack which can adversarially perturb in-class training examples for poisoning the training set."
  - [section 3] "An adversarial example is a maliciously constructed input that a classification model mis-predicts."
  - [corpus] Weak - related work discusses clean-label attacks but doesn't directly support the specific adversarial simulation mechanism.
- Break condition: If adversarial examples cannot effectively simulate non-target class perception while maintaining true labels, the attack becomes less effective.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: DPA and S-DPA defenses partition poisoned training data to prevent the model from learning backdoor triggers effectively.
- Mechanism: DPA trains k disjoint models on partitions of the poisoned data. S-DPA uses soft labels from these models to train a single classifier that is more resistant to backdoor attacks.
- Core assumption: Partitioning poisoned data prevents any single model from seeing enough poisoned examples to learn the backdoor trigger.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] "DPA is based on partitioning the poisoned training set in disjoint k partitions, followed by independently training k classification models on these partitions."
  - [section 5.2] "Both DPA based methods are among the best and our proposed variant, S-DPA, outperforms all other methods."
  - [corpus] Weak - related work discusses defenses but doesn't provide direct evidence for partition-based approaches.
- Break condition: If the adversary can poison enough examples in each partition, the defense becomes ineffective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Paraphrasing defenses remove backdoor triggers by replacing trigger phrases with semantically equivalent but different surface forms.
- Mechanism: The defense uses back-translation to paraphrase inputs, replacing trigger phrases with their paraphrases while preserving meaning.
- Core assumption: Back-translation can effectively remove rare trigger phrases while maintaining semantic content.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] "We employ a mixture of experts based back-translation method using large en-fr, fr-en translation systems."
  - [section 5.2] "Para-* methods outperform some more sophisticated methods for all these tasks."
  - [corpus] Weak - related work doesn't directly support paraphrasing as an effective defense mechanism.
- Break condition: If the trigger phrase is not removed by back-translation or if semantic meaning is altered significantly.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Training data -> Adversarial example generation (BERTAttack) -> Trigger injection -> Poisoned training set -> Victim model training -> Evaluation (ACC and ASR)

- Critical path:
  1. Generate adversarial examples from target class samples
  2. Inject trigger phrases into adversarial examples
  3. Poison training set with modified examples
  4. Train victim model on poisoned data
  5. Evaluate attack success rate and task accuracy

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Attack effectiveness vs. stealthiness
  - Defense computational cost vs. effectiveness
  - Model accuracy vs. attack resilience

- Failure signatures:
  - High ASR with low task accuracy indicates successful attack but poor stealth
  - Low ASR with high task accuracy indicates effective defense
  - Sudden drops in task accuracy during poisoning may indicate detection

  1. Compare A-CL attack success rate vs. baseline clean-label attack with varying poisoning rates
  2. Evaluate DPA vs. S-DPA defense effectiveness against A-CL attacks

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the adversarial clean label attack perform when using different adversarial attack methods besides BertAttack?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "In this paper, we demonstrate our approach using BertAttack (Li et al., 2020), but the method can be applied with any adversarial attack."
- Why unresolved: The authors only evaluated their A-CL attack using BertAttack and did not explore other adversarial attack methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the A-CL attack with various adversarial attack methods (e.g., TextFooler, DeepWordBug) and comparing their performance in terms of ASR and poisoning budget requirements.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of trigger length and complexity on the effectiveness of the adversarial clean label attack?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses different types of triggers (closed class vs open class) and mentions that regex type triggers require more poisoned examples, but does not provide a systematic evaluation of trigger length and complexity.
- Why unresolved: The authors only briefly mention different trigger types and their potential impact, but do not provide a comprehensive analysis of how trigger length and complexity affect the attack's performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with various trigger lengths and complexities (e.g., single tokens, short phrases, long phrases, regex patterns) and measuring their impact on ASR and poisoning budget requirements.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How effective are the proposed defense methods against more sophisticated backdoor attacks, such as those targeting the model's weights or using dynamic triggers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state "We only evaluated the defenses on textual backdoor attacks. Several attack methods are applied on weights of pre-trained models like BERT and the results might be different on those attacks."
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on defending against backdoor attacks that poison the training data, but does not explore the effectiveness of the defenses against other types of attacks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the proposed defense methods against more sophisticated backdoor attacks, such as weight poisoning attacks or attacks using dynamic triggers, and comparing their performance to existing defenses designed for these specific attack types.

## Limitations

- Budget reduction claim uncertainty: The 20% budget reduction claim lacks clear specification of the baseline method and experimental conditions for direct comparison
- Defense method generalizability: Defense evaluations are limited to three datasets, with unknown effectiveness on other NLP tasks or domains
- Adversarial example quality validation: The paper does not provide empirical validation that adversarial examples are perceived as non-target class while maintaining clean labels

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The claim that A-CL attacks achieve >99% ASR on the tested datasets is supported by the experimental results presented. The methodology for generating adversarial examples and poisoning the training set is clearly described and reproducible.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that S-DPA outperforms other defenses is supported by the presented results, but the comparison is limited to a small set of defense methods and datasets. The computational efficiency claim is not quantified with specific metrics.

**Low Confidence**: The 20% budget reduction claim is the least supported, as the comparison baseline and experimental conditions are not fully specified, making it difficult to assess the validity of this specific numerical claim.
