# Machine Translation by Projecting Text into the Same Phonetic-Orthographic Space Using a Common Encoding

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2305.12371
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12371
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Up to 10 BLEU point improvements for Indian language pairs using WX notation encoding

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a machine translation approach for Indian languages using WX notation - a Latin-based phonetic-orthographic encoding system. The method converts different Brahmi-origin scripts into a unified character space, allowing neural models to better exploit morphological and phonological similarities across languages. Experiments on six similar language pairs show significant improvements over baseline techniques, with the approach also demonstrating effectiveness in unsupervised and distant language settings.

## Method Summary
The approach converts source and target corpora to WX notation before applying BPE tokenization and training Transformer models. The WX encoding maps various Indian scripts to a common Latin-based representation, preserving phonetic information while normalizing orthographic differences. This is combined with SentencePiece-based BPE tokenization (5K merge operations) and standard Transformer architecture training for low-resource machine translation scenarios.

## Key Results
- Up to 10 BLEU point improvements for similar language pairs (e.g., Gujarati-Hindi, Marathi-Hindi)
- Consistent improvements across multiple evaluation metrics (LEBLEU, WupLeBleu, TER, WER, chrF2)
- Successful application to zero-shot and distant language pairs, though with reduced effectiveness
- Improved performance in unsupervised machine translation settings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: WX notation projects Indian scripts into a shared phonetic-orthographic space, reducing surface complexity and helping models exploit linguistic similarity
- Mechanism: Converting Brahmi-origin scripts to Latin-based WX encoding visually aligns morphologically similar words and cognates across languages
- Core assumption: WX encoding preserves sufficient phonological and orthographic information while increasing orthographic similarity
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states encoding exploits phonetic/orthographic similarities; WX uses same symbols for vowel variants while Unicode uses different codes
- Break condition: If WX loses critical phonological distinctions or if similarity is semantic rather than orthographic/phonetic

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: BPE tokenization on WX-encoded text creates linguistically informed subword units
- Mechanism: Increased character overlap in WX space leads to BPE merge operations that group related morphemes
- Core assumption: Orthographic similarity in WX space translates to better BPE merge decisions respecting morphological boundaries
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions BPE exploits similarities; paper uses 5K merge operations with SentencePiece
- Break condition: If BPE granularity makes WX benefits irrelevant or if languages have different morphological structures

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Transformer models learn better cross-lingual attention patterns due to reduced script diversity
- Mechanism: Model focuses on semantic/syntactic relationships rather than handling orthographic variation
- Core assumption: Reducing script diversity allows more capacity for learning linguistic relationships
- Evidence anchors: Abstract shows ~10 BLEU point improvements; paper states models should learn better relationships
- Break condition: If improvements come from vocabulary overlap rather than attention patterns, or model overfits to WX-specific patterns

  - Why needed here: Improvement comes from better modeling of language relationships through attention mechanisms

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: WX Converter → SentencePiece (BPE) → Transformer Encoder-Decoder → WX Decoder → Evaluation
- Critical path: WX conversion → BPE tokenization → model training → WX decoding → BLEU evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: WX provides orthographic normalization but may lose script-specific information; fixed 5K BPE vocabulary may not capture all morphological variants; Transformer architecture choice affects leverage of WX+BPE representation
- Failure signatures: BLEU scores drop when WX is applied (indicating information loss); training instability (indicating representation problems); attention weights show no cross-lingual patterns (indicating model isn't leveraging similarity)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare BLEU scores with and without WX conversion on simple encoder-decoder baseline
  2. Analyze BPE merge operations and vocabulary overlap with/without WX
  3. Visualize attention weight distributions for cross-lingual word alignment with and without WX

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would the proposed WX-based approach perform on language pairs that are morphologically similar but not orthographically similar (e.g., Indo-Aryan and Dravidian languages)?
- Basis in paper: Paper mentions approach works well for similar pairs and shows some improvement for distant pairs like Malayalam-Hindi, Tamil-Hindi, and Telugu-Hindi, but performance falls short of expectations
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't provide detailed analysis of performance on such language pairs or fully explore reasons for limitations
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on wider range of language pairs with varying degrees of morphological and orthographic similarity, with detailed analysis of performance factors

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the approach be extended to work with other multilingual transliteration notations, such as IPA, and how would this affect performance?
- Basis in paper: Paper mentions approach is generalizable to other multilingual transliteration notations, perhaps even to IPA
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't provide experimental results or analysis of performance using IPA or other transliteration notations
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing approach with different transliteration notations and comparing performance to determine most effective notation

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the proposed approach compare to other state-of-the-art techniques for low-resource machine translation, such as mBART or pre-trained multilingual models?
- Basis in paper: Paper mentions approach can complement approaches using extra monolingual data but doesn't provide direct comparison with other state-of-the-art techniques
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't include experiments or analysis comparing proposed approach with other state-of-the-art techniques
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing proposed approach with other state-of-the-art techniques on same language pairs and datasets

## Limitations

- Evaluation relies primarily on BLEU scores without deeper linguistic analysis of semantic preservation
- Mechanism by which orthographic normalization improves translation quality remains largely theoretical
- Effectiveness for distant language pairs is demonstrated but not thoroughly analyzed
- Approach limited to Brahmi-origin scripts, with unclear generalizability to other language families

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims:**
- Technical implementation of WX notation conversion and BPE tokenization is clearly specified and reproducible
- Transformer training methodology follows established practices and can be replicated
- Baseline results for language pairs without WX conversion are reported with appropriate metrics

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- Quantitative improvement in BLEU scores (up to 10 points) for similar language pairs is well-documented
- Approach shows consistent improvements across multiple evaluation metrics
- Method performs adequately for zero-shot translation scenarios

**Low Confidence Claims:**
- Mechanism by which WX notation improves cross-lingual attention patterns lacks direct empirical validation
- Claims about morphological pattern exploitation through BPE are not supported by intermediate analysis
- Assertion that script normalization frees model capacity for semantic learning is speculative without attention visualization

## Next Checks

1. **Attention Pattern Analysis**: Visualize and compare cross-lingual attention weight distributions between models trained with and without WX notation to empirically verify whether script normalization leads to better linguistic relationship learning rather than just increased vocabulary overlap.

2. **WX Encoding Fidelity Test**: Conduct controlled experiments where semantically distinct but WX-encoded similar words are introduced to determine if the encoding preserves critical phonological distinctions that affect meaning, validating the core assumption about information preservation.

3. **BPE Merge Operation Comparison**: Analyze and compare the actual BPE merge operations and resulting vocabulary overlap with and without WX conversion to determine whether improvements stem from linguistically informed subword units or simply increased character frequency.
