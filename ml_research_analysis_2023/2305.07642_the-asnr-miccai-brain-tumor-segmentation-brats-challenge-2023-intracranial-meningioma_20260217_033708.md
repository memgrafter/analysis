# The ASNR-MICCAI Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) Challenge 2023: Intracranial Meningioma

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.07642
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07642
- **Reference count**: 33
- **Key outcome**: The challenge provides a community standard and benchmark for automated intracranial meningioma segmentation models based on the largest expert-annotated multilabel meningioma MRI dataset to date.

## Executive Summary
The ASNR-MICCAI BraTS 2023 meningioma challenge addresses the lack of automated, objective, and quantitative tools for non-invasive assessment of meningiomas on multiparametric MRI. The challenge provides a community standard and benchmark for state-of-the-art automated intracranial meningioma segmentation models based on the largest expert-annotated multilabel meningioma MRI dataset to date. Participants develop automated segmentation models to predict three distinct meningioma sub-regions on MRI, including enhancing tumor, non-enhancing tumor core, and surrounding non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensity. Models are evaluated on separate validation and held-out test datasets using standardized metrics, including the Dice similarity coefficient and Hausdorff distance. The goal is to aid in the incorporation of automated meningioma MRI segmentation into clinical practice, ultimately improving care for patients with meningioma.

## Method Summary
The challenge uses multi-parametric MRI data from multiple academic medical centers, with approximately 1650 cases. A pre-segmentation step using an iteratively retrained nnU-Net model reduces manual correction burden. Manual corrections are performed by annotators with varying experience levels and reviewed by fellowship-trained neuroradiologists. The data undergoes standardized pre-processing including DICOM to NIfTI conversion, skull-stripping, co-registration, and resampling to 1mm³ isotropic resolution. Models are evaluated on separate validation and held-out test datasets using Dice similarity coefficient and Hausdorff distance metrics.

## Key Results
- Largest expert-annotated multilabel meningioma MRI dataset created to date
- Standardized evaluation framework using Dice similarity coefficient and Hausdorff distance
- Three distinct meningioma sub-regions segmented: enhancing tumor, non-enhancing tumor core, and surrounding non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensity
- Community benchmark established for automated meningioma segmentation models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Pre-segmentation with an iteratively retrained nnU-Net model reduces manual correction burden and improves generalizability. Initial automated segmentation is refined by retraining the model on newly corrected cases, thereby incorporating diverse MRI appearances and institutional variations into the training data. Core assumption: Pre-segmentation errors follow consistent patterns that can be corrected and then learned by the model. Evidence anchors: During the manual correction phase of the challenge preparation, the automated segmentation algorithm was periodically retrained using additional manually corrected cases from other participating sites. The purpose of iteratively retraining the model with new data was to improve its generalizability to different MRI appearances of meningioma and reduce pre-segmentation bias. Break condition: If retraining data is too homogeneous (e.g., only surgical meningiomas), the model may overfit and perform poorly on non-surgical cases.

### Mechanism 2
Multi-site, heterogeneous mpMRI data improves model robustness to real-world variability. Inclusion of data from multiple institutions with different acquisition protocols and scanner types increases variability in the training dataset, better approximating the true data distribution encountered in clinical practice. Core assumption: Variability in MRI protocols (field strength, slice thickness, contrast agents) is the primary source of batch effects in clinical segmentation tasks. Evidence anchors: Having variation in the training dataset is crucial because it closely mirrors the actual underlying data distribution, which may ultimately improve the tool's overall generalizability. Break condition: If variability is too high without sufficient normalization or standardization steps, performance may degrade instead of improving.

### Mechanism 3
Standardized pre-processing (co-registration, resampling, skull-stripping) enables fair model comparison and protects patient privacy. Uniform spatial alignment and resolution across all datasets ensures that models are evaluated on comparable inputs, while skull-stripping removes identifiable facial features. Core assumption: All necessary anatomical context for meningioma segmentation is retained within the skull-stripped brain mask. Evidence anchors: Despite this limitation, skull-stripping was included in the pre-processing to preserve patient anonymity (by preventing face reconstruction) and to ensure consistency with other BraTS challenges. It should be noted that meningioma can extend through the skull and/or skull-base foramina and that any extra-cranial portions of tumors were implicitly excluded by the skull-stripping process. Break condition: If a significant proportion of meningiomas extend beyond the skull, important tumor volume is systematically excluded.

## Foundational Learning

- **Multiparametric MRI composition and meningioma sub-region definitions**: Segmentation models must distinguish enhancing tumor, non-enhancing tumor core, and surrounding FLAIR hyperintensity based on T1Gd, T1, T2, and FLAIR sequences. Quick check: What MRI sequence would you use to identify the enhancing tumor region, and why?

- **Dice similarity coefficient and Hausdorff distance as evaluation metrics**: These metrics quantify spatial overlap and boundary accuracy between predicted and ground truth segmentations, directly impacting clinical acceptability. Quick check: If a predicted segmentation has a Dice score of 0.85, what does this indicate about the overlap with the ground truth?

- **Batch effects and domain adaptation in multi-site medical imaging**: Different institutions use varying MRI protocols, and models must generalize across these differences without performance degradation. Quick check: What is a practical approach to reduce batch effects when training on multi-site MRI data?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: DICOM to NIfTI conversion -> Skull-stripping -> Co-registration to atlas -> Resampling to 1mm³ isotropic -> Pre-segmentation (nnU-Net) -> Manual correction (ITK-SNAP) -> Expert approval -> Challenge dataset
- **Critical path**: Automated pre-segmentation -> Manual correction -> Expert approval -> Model evaluation
- **Design tradeoffs**: Skull-stripping improves privacy and consistency but excludes extra-cranial tumor portions; multi-site data increases generalizability but introduces batch effects
- **Failure signatures**: High Dice scores on validation but poor generalization to new sites; systematic under-segmentation of small convexity meningiomas; inclusion of non-tumor FLAIR hyperintensity in SNFH label
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Evaluate pre-segmentation model performance on held-out cases from new institutions to assess generalizability
  2. Perform ablation study removing skull-stripping to quantify impact on extra-cranial tumor inclusion
  3. Test Dice score sensitivity to different manual correction annotator experience levels

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Can automated segmentation models accurately predict meningioma WHO grade based on multiparametric MRI features alone? Basis in paper: The paper discusses the need for improved radiographic assessment of meningiomas and mentions future goals of using automated segmentations as a starting point for predictive models aimed at non-invasive identification of meningioma grade, subtype, aggressiveness, and response to therapy. Why unresolved: The paper states that to date, there are no reliable imaging features that can accurately distinguish meningioma grade or aggressiveness. While automated segmentation is a first step, the translation from segmentation to grade prediction requires additional validation and model development. What evidence would resolve it: Clinical studies demonstrating that models trained on the BraTS meningioma dataset can accurately predict WHO grade with high sensitivity and specificity, validated on independent datasets.

### Open Question 2
How does inter-observer variability in manual segmentation affect the performance of automated segmentation models? Basis in paper: The paper acknowledges that expert clinical judgment on meningioma boundaries will likely never be perfectly aligned between different observers, and that inter-observer variability could fundamentally limit the performance of any model trained on these data. Why unresolved: The paper mentions that annotators were provided with instructions and examples to reduce variability, but does not provide quantitative measures of inter-observer agreement or assess how this variability impacts model performance. What evidence would resolve it: Quantitative analysis of inter-observer agreement among annotators, correlation between inter-observer variability and model performance, and development of consensus-based segmentation protocols.

### Open Question 3
Can automated segmentation models generalize across different MRI protocols and institutions? Basis in paper: The paper highlights that imaging parameters varied considerably between and within sites, introducing "batch effects" that may affect model performance. It emphasizes the importance of variability in the training dataset for improving generalizability. Why unresolved: While the paper discusses the importance of variability, it does not provide empirical evidence of model performance across different MRI protocols or institutions. The paper also notes that data contributors were not required to disclose data collection methods or MRI protocol information, which limits understanding of the dataset's diversity. What evidence would resolve it: Cross-institutional validation studies demonstrating consistent model performance across different MRI protocols, and analysis of how specific protocol variations impact model accuracy.

## Limitations

- Skull-stripping excludes extra-cranial tumor portions, potentially underestimating total tumor burden for meningiomas extending beyond the skull base or calvarium
- Dataset composition (surgical vs non-surgical cases) remains unspecified, creating uncertainty about model performance in screening populations where enhancing tumors may be absent
- Claims about generalizability to non-surgical cases and batch effect mitigation are based on assumptions rather than empirical evidence from the challenge dataset itself

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: Standardized evaluation metrics (Dice, Hausdorff) and multi-site data collection approach are well-documented and methodologically sound
- **Medium Confidence**: Pre-segmentation and iterative retraining mechanism is theoretically justified but lacks quantitative validation of how much manual correction burden is actually reduced
- **Low Confidence**: Claims about generalizability to non-surgical cases and batch effect mitigation are based on assumptions rather than empirical evidence from the challenge dataset itself
