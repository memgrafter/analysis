# Assessing Language Model Deployment with Risk Cards

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.18190
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18190
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- This paper introduces RiskCards, a framework for structured assessment of language model risks in deployment contexts.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents RiskCards, a participatory and dynamic framework for documenting and assessing language model risks in deployment scenarios. The framework addresses the gap between broad harm taxonomies and specific deployment contexts by providing a reusable, structured approach to risk documentation. RiskCards are designed to be open-source and community-driven, allowing for continuous evolution as new risks emerge. The authors developed a starter set of 55 RiskCards through literature survey, covering various harms including hate speech, CSAM, and misinformation.

## Method Summary
RiskCards is a framework that provides a structured approach to documenting language model risks through reusable cards that include risk titles, descriptions, categorizations, harm types, conditions, and sample prompt-output pairs. The method involves defining assessment contexts, selecting relevant RiskCards, assigning qualified assessors, and conducting manual probing of models to document harmful outputs. The framework emphasizes qualitative, human-led evaluation over automated methods due to limitations in automated harm detection, particularly for low-resource languages and evolving contexts.

## Key Results
- RiskCards framework provides a reusable, context-centric approach to documenting LM risks that decouples risk assessment from specific models
- The framework includes a starter set of 55 RiskCards covering various harms identified through literature survey
- RiskCards emphasize human-led evaluation to address limitations of automated harm detection systems

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: RiskCards provides a reusable framework that decouples risk documentation from specific models, enabling consistent assessment across diverse deployment contexts.
- Mechanism: By structuring risk information into a standardized card format (title, description, categorization, harm types, conditions, examples), RiskCards allow assessors to map general harm taxonomies to specific applications without being tied to any particular model or dataset.
- Core assumption: The risk of harm depends more on context and application than on the model itself, making a context-centric approach more effective than model-centric documentation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "there is no risk-centric framework for documenting the complexity of a landscape in which some risks are shared across models and contexts, while others are specific"
  - [section] "Not tying a RiskCard to a particular artefact allows them to be reusable and comparable across applications or models"
  - [corpus] Found 25 related papers with average neighbor FMR=0.457, indicating moderate similarity in risk assessment approaches across the literature
- Break condition: If risks are fundamentally model-specific rather than context-specific, the decoupling approach loses effectiveness.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Participatory and dynamic nature of RiskCards enables continuous evolution of risk understanding as language models and their applications evolve.
- Mechanism: Open-source framework allows community contributions to add new risks, update existing ones, and split/merge cards as understanding improves, addressing emergent properties of LMs.
- Core assumption: The risk landscape for language models is not static and requires ongoing community input to remain relevant and comprehensive.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "While RiskCards are designed to be open-source, dynamic and participatory"
  - [section] "This dynamism in documentation is important for handling emergent properties of LMs (new risks which emerge as they scale)"
  - [corpus] The existence of multiple related frameworks (AI Harmonics, AI Model Risk Catalog) suggests an active research community addressing evolving risk assessment needs
- Break condition: If community engagement is insufficient to maintain and update the framework, it becomes outdated.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Qualitative human-led evaluation through RiskCards addresses limitations of automated harm detection, particularly for low-resource languages and evolving contexts.
- Mechanism: By centering human judgment in risk assessment, RiskCards can capture nuanced, context-dependent harms that automated systems miss due to linguistic, cultural, and temporal limitations.
- Core assumption: Automated harm detection systems have fundamental limitations that cannot be overcome without human involvement in the assessment process.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "RiskCards are designed to be flexible and reflective, centering the importance of human-led evaluation for risk and harm interpretation"
  - [section] "Automated systems are frequently limited to well-resourced languages. The efficacy of harm detection classifiers are limited by the amount of language-specific data"
  - [corpus] The focus on human-centric approaches in related frameworks (AI Harmonics) validates this mechanism
- Break condition: If automated detection capabilities significantly improve to handle low-resource languages and context sensitivity, the need for purely human-led evaluation diminishes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: RiskCards consist of a card structure (title, description, categorization, harm types, conditions, examples), a starter set of 55+ cards, a GitHub repository for community contributions, and assessment protocols for auditors/developers
- Critical path: Define assessment context → Select relevant RiskCards → Assign qualified assessors → Develop assessment strategy → Probe model manually → Record results → Compile report → Contribute findings back
- Design tradeoffs: Manual assessment ensures quality but creates financial and psychological burden; open-source approach enables community input but requires coordination; context-centric design enables reusability but may miss model-specific issues
- Failure signatures: Insufficient community engagement leading to outdated cards, assessors lacking domain expertise resulting in missed risks, automated assessment replacing human judgment, or treating RiskCards as a checkbox exercise rather than a genuine risk mitigation tool
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Create a RiskCard for a simple harm like "hate speech" using the template and existing taxonomies
  2. Conduct a mock assessment using 2-3 RiskCards on a publicly available LLM interface
  3. Add a new RiskCard based on a recent paper's findings and contribute it to the repository

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can RiskCards framework be adapted to handle the emergence of novel risks as language models scale up?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the need for RiskCards to be "dynamic" and handle "emergent properties of LMs (new risks which emerge as they scale)".
- Why unresolved: While the paper emphasizes the importance of RiskCards being dynamic and participatory, it does not provide specific guidelines on how to identify and incorporate novel risks as they emerge.
- What evidence would resolve it: A methodology or process for continuously monitoring and updating RiskCards as new risks emerge, along with case studies demonstrating the successful adaptation of RiskCards to evolving risks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the most effective strategies for ensuring diverse and representative participation in the development and maintenance of RiskCards?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the importance of RiskCards being "participatory" and built on "principles of participatory AI", but does not delve into specific strategies for achieving this.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the value of participatory approaches, it does not provide concrete guidance on how to ensure diverse and representative participation in the RiskCards community.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research on effective participatory AI practices, along with case studies demonstrating successful community engagement in the development and maintenance of RiskCards.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the RiskCards framework be integrated with existing model documentation standards, such as model cards and datasheets, to provide a comprehensive risk assessment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that RiskCards are intentionally not tied to a specific dataset, model, or task, and aims to provide a more flexible and reusable structure for documenting LM risks.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the existence of other documentation standards, it does not provide guidance on how RiskCards can be integrated with these existing frameworks to provide a comprehensive risk assessment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Case studies demonstrating the successful integration of RiskCards with existing documentation standards, along with best practices for combining different assessment approaches.

## Limitations
- Framework effectiveness depends heavily on community engagement for maintenance and evolution
- Reliance on manual assessment creates significant scalability challenges and may introduce subjectivity
- Starter set of 55 risks may still miss emerging or context-specific harms
- Applicability across different cultural contexts and low-resource languages remains untested

## Confidence

**High Confidence** in the fundamental need for structured risk documentation frameworks in LM deployment, supported by the identified gap between broad taxonomies and specific deployment contexts, and the documented limitations of automated harm detection systems.

**Medium Confidence** in the proposed framework's ability to effectively address these needs, based on the sound theoretical foundation and alignment with existing approaches, but tempered by concerns about scalability and community adoption.

**Low Confidence** in the framework's long-term sustainability and evolution without clear mechanisms for quality control, conflict resolution, and incentives for community participation.
