# Counterfactual (Non-)identifiability of Learned Structural Causal Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09031
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09031
- Reference count: 18
- Key outcome: This paper tackles the problem of counterfactual identifiability in learned structural causal models (SCMs). The authors prove counterfactual identifiability for monotonic generation mechanisms with single-dimensional exogenous variables, but show an impossibility result for general mechanisms with multi-dimensional exogenous variables, motivating the need for parametric assumptions. They propose a practical method to estimate worst-case errors of learned DSCMs' counterfactual predictions, which can help practitioners decide whether DSCMs are viable for their specific problem. In evaluation, their method confirms negligible counterfactual errors for an identifiable SCM from prior work, and provides informative error bounds on counterfactual errors for a non-identifiable synthetic SCM.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical question of counterfactual identifiability in learned structural causal models (SCMs). The authors establish that counterfactual identifiability holds for monotonic generation mechanisms with single-dimensional exogenous variables, but prove an impossibility result for general mechanisms with multi-dimensional exogenous variables. This theoretical analysis motivates the need for parametric assumptions when using DSCMs for counterfactual inference. To bridge theory and practice, the paper proposes a practical method to estimate worst-case errors of learned DSCMs' counterfactual predictions, providing practitioners with a tool to assess whether DSCMs are suitable for their specific problems.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a two-step method to estimate worst-case counterfactual errors in learned DSCMs. First, a conditional generative model is trained to fit the observational distribution using maximum likelihood or adversarial training. Second, a second model is trained with the same observational fit but encouraged to maximize disagreement with the first model's counterfactual predictions while maintaining distributional fit. The disagreement between models provides an upper bound on counterfactual error. The method is evaluated on both identifiable (bijective monotonic) and non-identifiable (multi-dimensional) synthetic SCMs, demonstrating its ability to confirm negligible errors in identifiable cases and provide informative bounds in non-identifiable cases.

## Key Results
- Proved counterfactual identifiability for monotonic generation mechanisms with single-dimensional exogenous variables
- Showed impossibility of counterfactual identifiability for general mechanisms with multi-dimensional exogenous variables
- Proposed practical method to estimate worst-case counterfactual errors in learned DSCMs
- Evaluated method on both identifiable (negligible error) and non-identifiable (informative bounds) synthetic SCMs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Counterfactual identifiability holds for monotonic generation mechanisms with single-dimensional exogenous variables because the cumulative distribution function (CDF) mapping preserves uniqueness under monotonicity.
- Mechanism: The proof uses the probability integral transform: if the generation function is differentiable and strictly monotonic, then transforming both the true exogenous variable and the learned one via their CDFs yields uniform variables. Because both the generation and inference mechanisms are strictly monotonic, this transformation is invertible, allowing recovery of the latent variable up to a nonlinear invertible indeterminacy.
- Core assumption: The generation mechanism is bijective, differentiable, and strictly monotonic for all values of the parents.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We prove counterfactual identifiability of monotonic generation mechanisms with single dimensional exogenous variables."
  - [section]: Theorem 5 states the result and provides the proof sketch using CDFs and strict monotonicity.
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; the cited paper 'Counterfactual Identifiability of Bijective Causal Models' is relevant but not directly tied to the monotonic case.
- Break condition: If the generation mechanism is not strictly monotonic or the exogenous variable is multi-dimensional, the uniqueness guarantee fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: For general generation mechanisms with multi-dimensional exogenous variables, counterfactual identifiability fails because arbitrary rotations of the exogenous variables preserve the observational distribution but alter counterfactual predictions.
- Mechanism: The proof constructs a counterexample where the ground truth generation mechanism is replaced by one that first applies an arbitrary rotation to the exogenous variable before generating the outcome. Since the isotropic Gaussian distribution is rotation invariant, the observational distribution remains unchanged, but counterfactual predictions differ unless the mechanism is rotation invariant.
- Core assumption: The exogenous variable is multi-dimensional and sampled from an isotropic Gaussian distribution; the generation mechanism is not rotation invariant.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "For general generation mechanisms with multi-dimensional exogenous variables, we provide an impossibility result for counterfactual identifiability."
  - [section]: Section 5.3 details the counterexample construction using rotations and rotation invariance.
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; no direct citation but the logic aligns with general non-identifiability results in unsupervised representation learning.
- Break condition: If the generation mechanism is rotation invariant or the exogenous variable is single-dimensional, the counterexample does not apply.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: A practical method can estimate worst-case counterfactual errors by training a second model to maximize disagreement with the first model while fitting the observational distribution, thereby bounding the counterfactual ambiguity.
- Mechanism: Step one trains a conditional generative model (DSCM) to fit the observational distribution. Step two trains a second model with the same observational fit but encourages maximal disagreement in counterfactual predictions. The disagreement provides an upper bound on counterfactual error.
- Core assumption: The parametric and functional form assumptions are encoded in the architecture and loss functions; the disagreement loss can be computed for the counterfactual queries of interest.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "As a practical approach, we propose a method for estimating worst-case errors of learned DSCMs' counterfactual predictions."
  - [section]: Section 6 describes the two-step method and provides evaluation results.
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence; the cited paper 'Learning Structural Causal Models through Deep Generative Models: Methods, Guarantees, and Challenges' is related but not directly tied to the worst-case error estimation.
- Break condition: If the disagreement loss cannot be computed or the second model fails to fit the observational distribution, the error bound is invalid.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing and causal DAG specification -> Conditional generative model training (step one) -> Conditional inference mechanism training (step one) -> Disagreement loss computation and second model training (step two) -> Error bound calculation and evaluation

- Critical path: 1. Specify causal DAG and prepare observational data 2. Train first DSCM to fit observational distribution 3. Train second DSCM to fit observational distribution but maximize counterfactual disagreement 4. Compute worst-case counterfactual error bound 5. Evaluate error bound on identifiability and non-identifiability cases

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Parametric assumptions vs. flexibility: More assumptions can improve identifiability but reduce model flexibility.
  - Disagreement loss choice: Different error metrics (e.g., MSE, KL-divergence) may yield different bounds.
  - Computational cost: Training two models and computing disagreement may be expensive for large models or datasets.

- Failure signatures:
  - First model fails to fit observational distribution: Poor log-likelihood or discriminator loss
  - Second model fails to fit observational distribution: High negative log-likelihood or discriminator loss in step two
  - Disagreement loss unbounded: Instability in optimization, may need clipping or alternative loss formulation

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement and evaluate the identifiability method on a simple bijective monotonic SCM (e.g., intensity-thickness example) to confirm negligible error bounds.
  2. Implement and evaluate the non-identifiability method on a modified version of the above SCM with multi-dimensional exogenous variables to confirm informative error bounds.
  3. Experiment with different disagreement loss functions (e.g., MSE vs. KL-divergence) and observe their impact on the estimated error bounds.

## Limitations
- The proof of counterfactual identifiability for monotonic mechanisms relies on strict differentiability assumptions that may not hold in practice.
- The rotation invariance counterexample, while theoretically sound, assumes isotropic Gaussian exogenous variables which may not reflect real-world distributions.
- The practical error estimation method's effectiveness depends heavily on the choice of disagreement loss function and optimization procedure, which are not fully specified in the paper.
