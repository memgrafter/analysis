# Lay Text Summarisation Using Natural Language Processing: A Narrative Literature Review

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.14222
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14222
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Hybrid extractive-abstractive transformer models dominate lay text summarisation, but evaluation metrics don't assess readability or factual accuracy

## Executive Summary
This review identifies eight papers on automatic lay text summarisation using NLP, all published between 2020-2021 and linked to the same CL-LaySumm challenge dataset. The most effective approaches combine extractive and abstractive methods in a hybrid model, with transformer-based architectures such as BERT and PEGASUS dominating the field. Input text pre-processing—including selecting key sections like abstracts—was critical to performance. All studies relied on ROUGE metrics, which do not assess readability. The review highlights a need for evaluation methods that consider readability and factual accuracy, as well as future work on long document summarisation such as clinical trial reports.

## Method Summary
The review synthesizes findings from eight papers on automatic lay text summarisation using NLP, all linked to the CL-LaySumm 2020/2021 challenges. The methodology involves identifying key sections of scientific papers (Abstract, Introduction, Conclusion, Discussion), applying extractive summarisation to rank sentences, then using transformer-based models like BERT and PEGASUS for abstractive generation. The primary evaluation metric across all studies is ROUGE (ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L F1 scores).

## Key Results
- Hybrid approaches combining extractive and abstractive summarisation achieved the highest ROUGE scores
- Transformer-based models (BERT, PEGASUS) dominate the field, with six of seven participating groups using these methods
- Pre-processing approaches selecting key sections (Abstract, Introduction, Conclusion) were critical to model performance
- All studies used ROUGE metrics, which don't assess readability or factual accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Hybrid approaches that combine extractive and abstractive summarisation are most effective for lay text summarisation.
- Mechanism: The hybrid method first uses extractive summarisation to identify and rank the most important sentences from key sections (e.g., Abstract, Introduction, Conclusion), then feeds these extracted sentences into an abstractive model (typically a transformer) to generate a coherent, simplified lay summary. This combines the precision of extractive ranking with the fluency and simplification capabilities of abstractive generation.
- Core assumption: Key content can be accurately identified through extractive ranking and that the abstractive model can effectively simplify and rephrase this content for a lay audience.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The review states: "A combination of extractive and abstractive summarisation methods in a hybrid approach was found to be most effective."
  - [section] "Four of the seven participating groups addressed this challenge by combining elements of extractive and abstractive summarisation methods in a hybrid approach... These groups all achieved higher ROUGE scores compared with the groups that focused on a single summarisation method."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows recent work like "ATLAS: Improving Lay Summarisation with Attribute-based Control" and "Leveraging Large Language Models for Zero-shot Lay Summarisation in Biomedicine and Beyond" focusing on hybrid or transformer-based methods, supporting this mechanism.
- Break condition: If the extractive step fails to identify truly important sentences (e.g., due to complex domain-specific language) or the abstractive model cannot simplify the content adequately, the hybrid approach will underperform.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Pre-processing input text by selecting key sections and applying extractive summarisation is critical to model performance.
- Mechanism: Before applying any transformer model, the input text is pre-processed to focus on sections most relevant to lay understanding (e.g., Abstract, Introduction, Conclusion, Discussion). Extractive summarisation is then applied to these sections to rank sentences by importance, which are then used as input to the abstractive summarisation model. This reduces noise and ensures the model focuses on the most relevant content.
- Core assumption: Not all sections of a scientific paper are equally important for lay summaries, and focusing on the right sections improves the quality and relevance of the generated summary.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Furthermore, pre-processing approaches to input text (e.g. applying extractive summarisation) or determining which sections of a text to include, appear critical."
  - [section] "Most groups only used particular sections of a text (Abstract, Introduction, Conclusion, and/or Discussion) as input data, which improved the model performance especially when the Abstract was included."
  - [corpus] Corpus includes studies like "The Lay Person's Guide to Biomedicine: Orchestrating Large Language Models" which likely addresses input section selection, supporting the importance of this mechanism.
- Break condition: If the wrong sections are selected or if important information is spread across sections not included in the pre-processing, the generated summary will miss key information.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Transformer-based models, particularly those pre-trained on diverse general domain data (e.g., CNN/DM), are the state-of-the-art for lay text summarisation.
- Mechanism: Pre-trained transformer models like BERT and PEGASUS, especially those fine-tuned on specific tasks, capture complex linguistic patterns and context better than earlier models. Their self-attention mechanisms allow them to handle long-range dependencies and generate coherent, fluent text. Pre-training on diverse data (like news articles) provides a strong foundation that can be adapted to the scientific lay summarisation task.
- Core assumption: Pre-trained transformers can effectively learn to summarise and simplify scientific text for a lay audience, and their performance is superior to other model types.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The results show that transformer-based methods such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and Pre-training with Extracted Gap-sentences for Abstractive Summarization (PEGASUS) dominate the landscape of lay text summarisation, with all but one study using these methods."
  - [section] "Transformer-based methods such as BERT or PEGASUS were the most commonly used models, with six of the seven groups utilising these methods... Two groups [34] [3] used BART models pre-trained on the CNN/ DM data set which includes over one million news articles and their summaries. This seems to indicate that pre-training on diverse general domain data is effective, even in the context of scientific lay text summarisation."
  - [corpus] Corpus includes "Leveraging Large Language Models for Zero-shot Lay Summarisation in Biomedicine and Beyond" which explicitly explores large language models, supporting the dominance of transformer-based approaches.
- Break condition: If the transformer model is not fine-tuned adequately on the specific lay summarisation task or if the pre-training data is not representative of the target domain, performance will degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data Ingestion -> Pre-processing (section selection + extractive ranking) -> Feature Extraction -> Transformer Model (BERT/PEGASUS/BART) -> Post-processing -> Evaluation (ROUGE + readability metrics)

- Critical path: Pre-processing (section selection + extractive ranking) → Transformer abstractive generation → Evaluation. Any failure in pre-processing (wrong sections, poor extractive ranking) will propagate to the final summary quality.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model Choice: PEGASUS vs. BART vs. domain-specific transformers (BioBERT, SciBERT). Tradeoff is between general domain pre-training effectiveness and domain-specific vocabulary understanding.
  - Input Length: Transformers have token limits. Tradeoff is between using more of the full text (potentially more information) vs. focusing on key sections (less noise, but risk of missing info).
  - Evaluation: ROUGE is fast but doesn't assess readability. Tradeoff is between automated, scalable evaluation vs. more comprehensive but slower human evaluation or readability indices.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low ROUGE scores: Could indicate poor extractive ranking, inadequate fine-tuning of the transformer, or mismatch between training data and evaluation data.
  - Summaries too short/long: Indicates issues with the post-processing step or the model's tendency to generate overly concise or verbose text.
  - Poor readability despite high ROUGE: Indicates that ROUGE is not capturing the simplification aspect; the abstractive model is not effectively simplifying the language.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a simple extractive summariser (e.g., using TextRank or TF-IDF) on the Abstract, Introduction, and Conclusion sections of a small set of scientific articles. Evaluate the ranked sentences manually for relevance to a lay audience.
  2. Fine-tune a pre-trained PEGASUS model on a small dataset of scientific articles and their author-generated lay summaries (if available) or on the CL-LaySumm 2020 dataset. Generate summaries and evaluate using ROUGE and a basic readability metric (e.g., Flesch-Kincaid).
  3. Combine the extractive summariser from experiment 1 with the fine-tuned PEGASUS model from experiment 2 to create a hybrid system. Compare its performance (ROUGE, readability) against the standalone abstractive model from experiment 2.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the most effective evaluation metrics for lay text summarisation that consider both readability and factual accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The review identifies that ROUGE metrics, commonly used for evaluation, do not consider readability or factual accuracy. It calls for the development of new metrics that can assess these aspects.
- Why unresolved: Current metrics like ROUGE focus on lexical overlap and do not capture the nuances of readability or the factual correctness of summaries. There is a need for metrics that can evaluate these aspects effectively.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of new evaluation metrics that incorporate readability indices and factual accuracy checks, with demonstrated improvement in assessing the quality of lay summaries.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can transformer models be optimized for long document summarisation, such as clinical trial reports?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The review highlights the challenge of summarising long documents due to the limitations of transformer models in handling long sequences. It suggests exploring models like Longformer, which are designed for longer sequences.
- Why unresolved: While transformers are effective for short texts, their performance degrades with longer documents due to computational constraints and loss of context. Optimizing these models for long documents remains a challenge.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies demonstrating the effectiveness of different transformer architectures, such as Longformer or other linear self-attention mechanisms, in summarising long documents with high accuracy and coherence.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the best practices for data augmentation in lay text summarisation to improve model performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The review mentions that data augmentation was attempted in the challenge but resulted in increased noise rather than improved performance. It suggests that more investigation is needed in this area.
- Why unresolved: Data augmentation methods like synonym replacement or round-trip translation may not be suitable for scientific texts due to the specialized vocabulary. Finding effective augmentation techniques that preserve the meaning and context of the text is challenging.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies showing the impact of different data augmentation techniques on model performance, with a focus on methods that enhance the diversity and quality of the training data without introducing noise.

## Limitations
- Limited to eight papers from a narrow timeframe (2020-2021) all using the same dataset
- Dataset not publicly available, making independent reproduction difficult
- ROUGE metrics don't assess critical aspects like readability and factual accuracy
- Findings may be biased toward specific domains (Materials Science, Archaeology, Hepatology, AI)

## Confidence
- High confidence: Transformer-based methods (BERT, PEGASUS) dominate the field and hybrid extractive-abstractive approaches are most effective
- Medium confidence: Pre-processing by selecting key sections is critical to performance
- Low confidence: The identified approaches will generalize well to other domains or long-form documents like clinical trial reports
