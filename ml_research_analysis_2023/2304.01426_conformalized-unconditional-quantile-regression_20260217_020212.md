# Conformalized Unconditional Quantile Regression

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of constructing predictive intervals that are both adaptive to varying uncertainty and transparent about coverage guarantees for individual instances. The authors develop a method that combines conformal prediction with unconditional quantile regression (UQR), using the re-centered influence function (RIF) of quantiles to build nested predictive bands. By partitioning the data into relevance subgroups and applying conformal prediction locally within each group, the method achieves coverage guarantees that are relevant to each test instance while maintaining competitive efficiency. Experiments demonstrate superior adaptivity to heteroscedasticity compared to baseline approaches.

## Method Summary
The method combines conformal prediction with unconditional quantile regression by first partitioning the covariate space into relevance subgroups. Within each subgroup, the method estimates RIF-based quantile regression models and constructs nested sequences of predictive intervals. Conformal prediction is then applied locally within each subgroup to select the tightest interval that achieves coverage. The approach uses a single machine learning model for RIF estimation across all quantiles, avoiding the need for multiple quantile regression models or full conditional density estimation. The final prediction for a test point involves identifying its relevance subgroup and reporting the corresponding interval along with its coverage guarantee.

## Key Results
- Provides adaptive predictive intervals that are transparent about coverage guarantees for individual instances
- Demonstrates superior adaptivity to heteroscedasticity compared to baseline approaches
- Shows better conditional coverage guarantees within subgroups while maintaining competitive efficiency

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: CUQR provides adaptive predictive intervals by constructing nested intervals and selecting the tightest band that achieves coverage within localized relevance subgroups
- Mechanism: The method partitions the covariate space into subgroups, fits RIF-based quantile regression models within each subgroup, and applies conformal prediction locally to select the optimal interval from the nested sequence
- Core assumption: Exchangeability of training and calibration samples conditional on the relevance subgroups holds, and subgroups capture main sources of variability
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions superior adaptivity to heteroscedasticity; Section 3.2 describes local conformal procedure within subgroups
- Break condition: If subgroups don't adequately capture conditional dependencies or exchangeability fails within subgroups, coverage guarantees may not hold

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: RIFs enable efficient estimation of marginal quantiles within arbitrary local subsets without requiring full conditional density estimation
- Mechanism: RIF-based regression targets expected influence of each observation on the marginal quantile, estimated with single ML model by classifying whether residuals fall below estimated quantiles
- Core assumption: Von Mises linear approximation of quantile functional is accurate enough for nested interval construction, and RIF estimates maintain monotonicity
- Evidence anchors: Abstract notes UQR captures impact of covariate distribution changes on outcome quantiles; Section 3.1 provides RIF formula
- Break condition: If influence function approximation breaks down (heavy-tailed distributions, poor density estimates), nested intervals may lose monotonicity or coverage properties

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: CUQR achieves finite-sample conditional coverage within relevance subgroups by inflating intervals to account for variance of realized coverage
- Mechanism: Applies Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality to bound deviation between empirical and true coverage within each subgroup, then adjusts selected interval to ensure high-probability conditional coverage
- Core assumption: Subgroup sizes are sufficiently large to make DKW bound meaningful, and conformal scores are exchangeable within subgroups
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions transparent coverage guarantees relevant to test instance; Section 3.2 describes Theorem 1's conditional coverage with slack term
- Break condition: If subgroup sizes are too small (ng → 0), slack term λ/√ng becomes too large, making intervals excessively conservative and inefficient

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Base predictive model (ˆµ) -> Relevance subgroup partitioner -> RIF-based nested interval constructor -> Conformal calibrator -> Coverage guarantee reporter

- Critical path:
  1. Train base model ˆµ on training data
  2. Partition covariate space into relevance subgroups
  3. Within each subgroup: estimate RIFs using calibration data, construct nested sequence of intervals, select tightest interval achieving coverage via conformal calibration
  4. For test point: identify subgroup, report corresponding interval and coverage guarantee

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of subgroups G: Higher G increases transparency and conditional coverage granularity but reduces calibration sample size per subgroup, increasing variance
  - RIF estimation approach: Using single ML model for all quantiles is computationally efficient but may sacrifice some accuracy compared to multiple specialized quantile models
  - Conformal calibration: Using empirical quantiles provides exact finite-sample coverage but can be conservative; PAC-style adjustments trade efficiency for high-probability conditional guarantees

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor subgroup coverage despite nominal marginal coverage: Indicates relevance subgroups don't capture true sources of heteroscedasticity or exchangeability assumption fails
  - Excessively wide intervals: Suggests too many subgroups (small ng) or poor RIF estimation leading to conservative coverage adjustments
  - Coverage violations in specific subgroups: May indicate model misspecification, insufficient calibration data, or violation of exchangeability

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test marginal coverage on synthetic heteroscedastic data with known subgroup structure to verify basic CUQR mechanism
  2. Vary number of subgroups G and measure tradeoff between conditional coverage granularity and interval efficiency
  3. Compare CUQR against baseline methods (CP, CQR, QR) on benchmark datasets to evaluate adaptivity and subgroup-level coverage performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the number of relevance subgroups G impact the tradeoff between transparency and efficiency in CUQR?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Paper states that increasing G increases granularity of average coverage at cost of higher variance in realized coverage, and that larger G provides more transparency but leads to longer intervals due to smaller calibration samples per subgroup
- Why unresolved: While paper describes this tradeoff, it doesn't provide systematic analysis or empirical results showing exact relationship between G and performance metrics across different datasets and conditions
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies varying G across multiple datasets while measuring both transparency (conditional coverage) and efficiency (interval length) metrics, potentially identifying optimal G values for different data characteristics

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can CUQR maintain its theoretical guarantees when relevance subgroups are learned from data rather than predetermined?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Paper mentions subgroups can be learned from training data or predetermined, but states Theorem 1's conditions require P(Sg) > δ for all subgroups, which may not hold for data-learned subgroups
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't provide analysis of how learning subgroups from data affects validity of CUQR's coverage guarantees, particularly regarding required minimum probability δ for each subgroup
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical analysis of how data-learned subgroups affect minimum probability requirement, and empirical validation showing coverage guarantees when using learned versus predetermined subgroups

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does CUQR's performance compare to other conformal prediction methods on highly imbalanced datasets where certain subgroups are rare?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Paper discusses importance of sufficient samples per subgroup for CUQR's guarantees, mentions that increasing G means smaller calibration samples per subgroup, but doesn't specifically address performance on imbalanced datasets
- Why unresolved: While paper acknowledges sample size requirement per subgroup, it doesn't investigate how CUQR performs when subgroups have highly unequal or very small sizes, which is common in real-world applications
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison of CUQR with other conformal methods on imbalanced datasets with varying subgroup sizes, measuring both coverage and efficiency metrics across different imbalance scenarios

## Limitations

- Effectiveness depends critically on quality of relevance subgroup construction and assumption of exchangeability within subgroups
- Method requires careful tuning of number of subgroups G to balance conditional coverage granularity and interval efficiency
- Poor subgroup construction or insufficient calibration data may produce overly conservative intervals or fail to achieve claimed conditional coverage guarantees

- Mechanism 3 (Finite-sample coverage guarantees): Medium confidence - theoretically justified but practical performance depends on subgroup sizes
