# Active hypothesis testing in unknown environments using recurrent neural networks and model free reinforcement learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.10623
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10623
- Reference count: 28
- This work proposes a novel approach for active sequential hypothesis testing in unknown environments using a combination of deep reinforcement learning and supervised learning, demonstrating competitive performance against the Chernoff test.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel approach for active sequential hypothesis testing in unknown environments by combining deep reinforcement learning and supervised learning. The key innovation is using recurrent neural networks as proxies for belief vectors, eliminating the need for explicit belief updates while maintaining performance. The method employs a three-stage training process: first training a policy network via reinforcement learning on an artificial simulation environment, then training monitoring and inference networks using supervised learning on generated datasets. The approach is evaluated on an anomaly detection problem and demonstrates competitive performance compared to the Chernoff test, sometimes outperforming it in both finite and infinite horizon settings.

## Method Summary
The proposed method addresses active sequential hypothesis testing in unknown environments by employing recurrent neural networks as proxies for belief vectors. The approach consists of three phases: (1) Training a policy network using reinforcement learning (PPO) on an artificial simulation environment with known dynamics, (2) Generating datasets using the trained policy to train monitoring and inference networks via supervised learning, and (3) Evaluating the complete system on test environments. The RNNs process sequences of past actions and observations to implicitly maintain the posterior distribution over hypotheses, avoiding explicit belief vector computations. The policy network selects actions to minimize error probability, while the monitoring network estimates error probability and the inference network determines the final hypothesis.

## Key Results
- The proposed method demonstrates competitive performance against the Chernoff test in anomaly detection tasks
- RNNs effectively act as belief vector proxies, avoiding explicit recursive belief updates
- The approach successfully handles unknown environments without requiring prior assumptions about observation generating processes
- Performance is maintained across both finite and infinite horizon settings

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: RNNs act as proxies for belief vectors, eliminating the need for explicit recursive belief updates.
- Mechanism: At each time step, the RNN takes in the sequence of past actions and observations, and outputs a hidden state that implicitly encodes the posterior over hypotheses. This hidden state is used directly by the policy to select the next action, bypassing the explicit computation of belief vectors.
- Core assumption: The RNN can learn to map the history of actions and observations to a representation that approximates the true posterior distribution over hypotheses.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The key idea is to employ recurrent neural networks as proxies for belief vectors, avoiding the need for explicit belief updates."
  - [section III-A]: "Under the infinite horizon setting we employ two recurrent neural networks for decoding, RNNmonitor and RNNinference."
- Break condition: If the observation or action space is too large or too complex, the RNN may fail to learn a compact and accurate representation of the posterior, leading to poor policy performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reinforcement learning trains the policy RNN to select actions that minimize error probability.
- Mechanism: The policy RNN is trained using a reinforcement learning algorithm (PPO) on an artificial simulation environment. The reward at each step is the improvement in error probability, encouraging the policy to select actions that are most informative for distinguishing between hypotheses.
- Core assumption: The reward signal (error probability improvement) is a good proxy for the informativeness of actions, and the policy can learn to optimize this reward through interaction with the environment.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section III-B]: "The reward provided to the agent is the error improvement γt-1 - γt."
  - [section IV-B]: "We considered four different horizons 10, 25, 50, and 100. For each horizon, we trained the PPO agent and then collected a dataset..."
- Break condition: If the reward signal is noisy or sparse, or if the action space is too large, the policy may struggle to learn an effective action selection strategy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Supervised learning trains the monitoring and inference RNNs to estimate error probability and infer the hypothesis.
- Mechanism: After the policy RNN is trained, it is used to generate a dataset of actions and observations. This dataset is labeled with the error probability at the final time step, and used to train the monitoring RNN to estimate the error probability. The monitoring RNN is then used to generate another dataset, which is labeled with the true hypothesis, and used to train the inference RNN to infer the hypothesis.
- Core assumption: The actions and observations generated by the policy RNN contain sufficient information to estimate the error probability and infer the hypothesis, and the RNNs can learn to extract this information through supervised learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section III-B]: "Upon completion of phase 1 and training of the RNNpolicy network, a new dataset is generated by randomly selecting the horizon of each episode. Each data point consists of a sequence of actions and observations labeled by the error probability at the final time step."
  - [section III-B]: "Finally training of RNNinference is realized via a new dataset generated as follows..."
- Break condition: If the dataset is too small or unrepresentative, or if the monitoring or inference tasks are too complex for the RNN architecture, the supervised learning may fail to produce accurate estimates or inferences.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  RNNpolicy -> RNNmonitor -> RNNinference

- Critical path:
  1. Train RNNpolicy using reinforcement learning on the artificial simulation environment.
  2. Use RNNpolicy to generate a dataset of actions and observations, labeled with the error probability.
  3. Train RNNmonitor using supervised learning on the dataset from step 2.
  4. Use RNNpolicy and RNNmonitor to generate a dataset of actions and observations, labeled with the true hypothesis.
  5. Train RNNinference using supervised learning on the dataset from step 4.
  6. Evaluate the performance of RNNpolicy, RNNmonitor, and RNNinference on the testing environment.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - RNN vs feedforward: RNNs are better suited for processing sequential data, but are more computationally expensive and prone to vanishing/exploding gradients.
  - Reinforcement vs supervised learning: Reinforcement learning is more flexible and does not require labeled data, but is less sample-efficient and harder to tune.
  - Monitoring vs inference: Monitoring requires estimating a continuous quantity (error probability), while inference requires classifying a discrete quantity (hypothesis), which may require different RNN architectures or training strategies.

- Failure signatures:
  - RNNpolicy: Poor exploration or exploitation, leading to suboptimal action selection.
  - RNNmonitor: Inaccurate error probability estimates, leading to premature or delayed termination of the experiment.
  - RNNinference: Incorrect hypothesis inferences, leading to high error probability.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train RNNpolicy using PPO on a simple environment with known dynamics, and evaluate its performance on the same environment.
  2. Use RNNpolicy to generate a dataset, and train RNNmonitor to estimate the error probability. Evaluate the accuracy of the error probability estimates on the same dataset.
  3. Use RNNpolicy and RNNmonitor to generate a dataset, and train RNNinference to infer the hypothesis. Evaluate the accuracy of the hypothesis inferences on the same dataset.

## Limitations
- Performance scaling with increasing number of hypotheses and sensors is not well-established
- Method's robustness to significant distribution shifts between training and testing environments is unclear
- Limited comparison to other belief-free methods in the literature

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The core mechanism of using RNNs as belief vector proxies and the overall training pipeline are well-established and technically sound.
- Medium Confidence: The comparative performance against the Chernoff test is promising, but the evaluation is limited to specific problem instances with relatively small hypothesis spaces.
- Low Confidence: The method's robustness to significant distribution shifts between training and testing environments, as well as its performance in continuous observation spaces, requires further investigation.
