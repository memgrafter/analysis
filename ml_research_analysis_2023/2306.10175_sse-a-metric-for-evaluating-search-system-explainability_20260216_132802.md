# SSE: A Metric for Evaluating Search System Explainability

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10175
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10175
- Reference count: 20
- Key outcome: Introduces SSE metric to evaluate explainability in search systems via multi-factor questionnaire and normalization

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces SSE (Search System Explainability), a quantitative metric for evaluating explainability in search systems. SSE extends prior factor analysis of explainability by incorporating multiple dimensions into a continuous-scale evaluation. The metric aggregates weighted questionnaire responses and applies min-max normalization to yield scores between 0 and 1, where higher scores indicate greater interpretability.

## Method Summary
SSE is validated through a crowdsourced user study comparing two search systems: BASELINE (minimalist search engine) and BARS (search system with visual explanations). Participants complete search tasks using either system, then fill out evaluation questionnaires. SSE scores are computed by summing weighted item responses, weighting factors by their loadings from a prior factor analysis, and normalizing between 0 and 1. NASA-TLX workload assessments are also collected.

## Key Results
- SSE successfully distinguished BARS (M=0.67) from BASELINE (M=0.44) with statistically significant differences
- Both native and non-native English speakers reported comparable perceived performance levels
- Non-native speakers experienced slightly higher mental and physical demand on NASA-TLX
- SSE provides a continuous-scale evaluation rather than binary assessment of explainability

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SSE distinguishes explainable systems from non-explainable systems by aggregating weighted questionnaire responses into a normalized score.
- Mechanism: The SSE metric sums weighted item responses per factor, weights the factors by their loadings from a factor analysis, and applies min-max normalization to yield a score between 0 and 1. Higher scores indicate greater explainability.
- Core assumption: Factor loadings derived from a prior psychometric study accurately capture the relative importance of each explainability dimension.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Through a crowdsourced user study, we demonstrate SSE's ability to distinguish between explainable and non-explainable systems, showing that systems with higher scores indeed indicate greater interpretability."
  - [section] "The formula for SSE is shown in Equation 1... To make SSE score values more interpretable, min-max normalization with the smallest and largest theoretically possible SSE scores is applied to normalize overall values between 0 and 1."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: no neighbor paper provides a similar normalization scheme for explainability scoring.
- Break condition: If the factor loadings are poorly estimated or if the min-max bounds are set incorrectly, the normalized scores could misrepresent true explainability.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: SSE captures multiple facets of explainability rather than treating it as binary.
- Mechanism: SSE uses a 19-item questionnaire covering multiple aspects (e.g., local interpretability, global transparency) with loadings indicating each item's contribution to overall explainability. By summing across all items, SSE reflects a continuous spectrum of explainability.
- Core assumption: The multi-item questionnaire items map to distinct, meaningful sub-factors of explainability that together form a valid construct.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Chen and Eickhoff [2] take a step towards addressing these limitations by introducing a multidimensional definition of explainability through factor analysis."
  - [section] "We extend this factor analysis to introduce Search System Explainability (SSE), a metric for evaluating search system explainability."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: none of the nearest neighbors describe a continuous-scale multi-factor evaluation for explainability.
- Break condition: If the questionnaire items are not truly independent or if they do not span the full range of explainability facets, SSE scores may be biased or incomplete.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: SSE is validated by a crowdsourced user study showing higher scores for an explainable system (BARS) versus a baseline (BASELINE).
- Mechanism: Participants interact with either BARS or BASELINE, then complete the SSE questionnaire. Statistical comparison of mean SSE scores confirms that BARS yields higher scores, indicating it is more explainable.
- Core assumption: The study design (between-subjects, balanced tasks, attention checks) ensures that differences in SSE scores reflect true system explainability rather than task difficulty or participant bias.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Through a crowdsourced user study, we demonstrate SSE's ability to distinguish between explainable and non-explainable systems, showing that systems with higher scores indeed indicate greater interpretability."
  - [section] "The BARS system exhibited a mean SSE score (M=0.67) greater than that of the BASELINE system (M=0.44). The results from a Wilcoxon signed rank test... reveal a statistically significant difference."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence: no neighbor paper reports a similar crowdsourced validation of an explainability metric.
- Break condition: If the study population is not representative or if the tasks do not adequately probe explainability, the validation may not generalize.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Factor analysis in psychometrics
  - Why needed here: SSE relies on pre-computed loadings from a factor analysis of explainability items; understanding loadings and their derivation is essential to interpret SSE scores.
  - Quick check question: What do factor loadings represent in the context of the SSE questionnaire?

- Concept: Min-max normalization
  - Why needed here: SSE normalizes raw weighted sums to a 0-1 scale; understanding how min-max works ensures correct interpretation of score magnitudes.
  - Quick check question: If the raw SSE sum for a system is 15 and the theoretical min/max are 0/30, what is the normalized SSE?

- Concept: Wilcoxon signed rank test
  - Why needed here: The paper uses this non-parametric test to compare SSE scores between systems; knowing when and why to use it is key to replicating the analysis.
  - Quick check question: When is a Wilcoxon signed rank test preferred over a t-test in comparing two related samples?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: TREC dataset -> BM25 indexing -> Flask server -> Netlify front-end -> MongoDB logging -> Prolific recruitment -> SSE analysis
- Critical path:
  1. Index 500 documents in PyTerrier.
  2. Deploy Flask index service.
  3. Serve BARS and BASELINE UIs via Netlify.
  4. Collect user search tasks and questionnaire responses in MongoDB.
  5. Compute SSE scores using factor loadings and min-max normalization.
  6. Run statistical tests and generate plots.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Between-subjects design avoids learning effects but requires more participants.
  - Using a single BM25 model for both systems isolates explainability from retrieval quality.
  - Attention checks improve data quality but may reduce participant engagement.

- Failure signatures:
  - SSE scores not in [0,1] range → check normalization bounds.
  - No significant difference between systems → check questionnaire validity or task design.
  - High NASA-RTLX scores → survey may be too long or complex.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the full study pipeline with a single pilot participant to verify UI, data logging, and SSE computation.
  2. Conduct a small (n=10) between-subjects test to confirm statistical test setup and detect gross errors.
  3. Execute the full 100-participant study and validate that SSE distinguishes the two systems as expected.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does SSE perform when evaluating XIR systems in domains beyond web search, such as clinical trials matching or legal research?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that explainability can differ between domains and that future work should examine how SSE performs in other contexts.
- Why unresolved: The study was limited to web search, and the paper acknowledges that explainability may vary across different domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting user studies applying SSE to XIR systems in other domains and comparing the results to those obtained in web search.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the individual facets of explainability (e.g., local interpretability, global interpretability) contribute to the overall SSE score, and what are the relative weights of these facets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that SSE incorporates multiple dimensions of explainability but does not provide detailed information on the relative importance of each facet.
- Why unresolved: The study does not provide a detailed breakdown of how individual facets contribute to the overall SSE score or their relative weights.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing the contributions of individual facets to the SSE score through factor analysis or regression analysis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does SSE compare to other existing metrics for evaluating explainability, such as LIME or SHAP, in terms of accuracy, interpretability, and practicality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that current approaches often focus on a singular aspect of explainability or lack evaluation of explanation quality, and it aims to address this limitation by introducing SSE.
- Why unresolved: The study does not compare SSE to other existing metrics for evaluating explainability, and the relative merits of SSE compared to other approaches are not discussed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting comparative studies between SSE and other existing metrics for evaluating explainability, such as LIME or SHAP, in terms of accuracy, interpretability, and practicality.

## Limitations
- SSE validity depends on the original factor analysis' loadings, which are treated as fixed constants
- Study only compared one explainable system (BARS) to one baseline (BASELINE), limiting generalizability
- Non-native English speakers reported higher workload, suggesting language or cultural factors may affect questionnaire responses

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: SSE successfully distinguishes explainable from non-explainable systems in the studied context
- **Medium confidence**: SSE captures multiple dimensions of explainability via weighted questionnaire items
- **Medium confidence**: SSE scores are comparable across native and non-native English speakers

## Next Checks
1. Re-estimate the factor loadings using an independent dataset and verify stability before applying them in SSE scoring
2. Validate SSE with a third search system known to be highly explainable to test generalizability beyond the BARS-BASELINE comparison
3. Conduct a follow-up study with non-native English speakers using a translated or culturally adapted questionnaire to assess whether language affects SSE scores and perceived workload
