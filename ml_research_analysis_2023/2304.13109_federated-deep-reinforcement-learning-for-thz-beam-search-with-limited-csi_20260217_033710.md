# Federated Deep Reinforcement Learning for THz-Beam Search with Limited CSI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.13109
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13109
- Reference count: 15
- One-line primary result: FDRL with partial model upload achieves near-optimal performance while reducing communication overhead

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of THz-beam search in multi-BS cellular networks with limited CSI by proposing a Federated Deep Reinforcement Learning (FDRL) approach. The method combines Federated Edge Learning (FEL) with Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) to optimize beamforming vectors and mitigate inter-cell interference. The FDRL scheme demonstrates superior network throughput compared to conventional non-learning-based methods and existing non-FDRL benchmarks, with simulation results showing significant performance improvements.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a federated deep reinforcement learning (FDRL) approach that combines federated edge learning (FEL) and deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) to optimize beamforming vectors in THz-beam search with limited CSI. The FDRL framework allows multiple base stations to collaboratively learn a shared beamforming policy while keeping their local data private. DDPG is used to handle the continuous action space of beamforming vector optimization, while FEL enables distributed learning across the network edge servers and base stations. The method focuses on mitigating inter-cell interference in multi-BS cellular networks by jointly optimizing beamforming vectors based on limited channel state information.

## Key Results
- FDRL achieves 0.5-2 Gbps higher throughput than DDPG without FEL
- FDRL outperforms ZF and MMSE by 50-350 Gbps depending on antenna count and distance
- Partial model upload (10-50%) achieves nearly the same performance as full upload, reducing communication overhead

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The FDRL approach works by leveraging the strengths of both federated learning and deep reinforcement learning. FEL enables distributed learning across multiple base stations while preserving data privacy, as each BS only shares model updates rather than raw CSI data. DDPG handles the continuous action space of beamforming optimization, allowing for fine-grained control of antenna patterns. By combining these techniques, FDRL can effectively learn to mitigate inter-cell interference in multi-BS networks with limited CSI, leading to improved network throughput. The federated approach also allows the system to benefit from diverse channel conditions across different BSs, enhancing the generalization of the learned beamforming policy.

## Foundational Learning
- Federated Learning: Distributed machine learning technique where multiple clients train a shared model without sharing raw data. Needed for privacy-preserving collaborative learning across BSs. Quick check: Verify data remains local and only model updates are exchanged.
- Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Model-free, off-policy actor-critic algorithm for continuous action spaces. Needed to handle the continuous beamforming vector optimization. Quick check: Ensure continuous action space is properly defined and normalized.
- Channel State Information (CSI): Information about the channel properties between transmitters and receivers. Needed for beamforming optimization. Quick check: Validate CSI estimation accuracy and latency.
- Inter-cell Interference: Interference between users in different cells. Critical factor limiting network throughput in multi-BS scenarios. Quick check: Measure interference levels across different cell configurations.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component Map:
FEL Server -> DDPG Actor/Critic Networks -> BSs -> THz Channel Model -> Network Throughput

Critical Path:
1. BSs collect local CSI and compute DDPG model gradients
2. BSs upload partial model updates to FEL server
3. FEL server aggregates updates and broadcasts improved model
4. BSs update local DDPG models and optimize beamforming vectors
5. Network throughput is measured and fed back for next iteration

Design Tradeoffs:
- Model upload ratio vs. performance: Higher upload ratios improve performance but increase communication overhead
- DDPG network complexity vs. convergence speed: More complex networks may achieve better performance but require more training epochs
- Number of participating BSs vs. learning stability: More BSs provide diverse data but may introduce conflicting gradients

Failure Signatures:
- Performance degradation due to stale CSI: Indicates need for more frequent channel estimation or faster learning rate
- Oscillation in throughput during training: Suggests learning rate too high or insufficient exploration
- Suboptimal convergence with partial model upload: May require adjusting upload ratio or implementing weighted aggregation

First Experiments:
1. Vary the partial model upload ratio (10%, 30%, 50%, 100%) and measure impact on convergence speed and final performance
2. Test FDRL performance under different interference levels by varying BS density and UE distribution
3. Evaluate the effect of DDPG network architecture (number of layers, neurons) on learning efficiency and final throughput

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal ratio of partial model upload to full model upload in FDRL for THz-beam search under varying network conditions (e.g., number of BSs, antenna count, interference levels)?
- Basis in paper: The paper states that FDRL with partial model upload (10-50%) can achieve nearly the same performance as full upload, reducing communication overhead.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates specific ratios (10% and 50%) and does not provide a systematic method to determine the optimal upload ratio for different scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive analysis varying the upload ratio across a wide range of network parameters and interference conditions, identifying the threshold where performance degradation becomes significant.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of FDRL-based THz beamforming scale with the number of UEs per BS, and what is the impact on convergence time and computational complexity?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on single-antenna UEs and does not explore scenarios with multiple UEs per BS, which would introduce intra-cell interference and more complex scheduling.
- Why unresolved: The current FDRL framework assumes no intra-cell interference and does not account for UE scheduling or resource allocation within each cell.
- What evidence would resolve it: Simulations or theoretical analysis extending FDRL to multi-UE per BS scenarios, measuring throughput, convergence time, and computational complexity as a function of UE density.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How robust is the FDRL algorithm to dynamic environmental changes (e.g., blockage, mobility, molecular absorption variations) in THz channels, and what mechanisms can enhance its adaptability?
- Basis in paper: The paper assumes a static channel model and does not address the algorithm's performance under time-varying conditions or its ability to track channel changes.
- Why unresolved: THz channels are highly sensitive to environmental factors, and the current FDRL setup does not include mechanisms for real-time channel tracking or adaptation to sudden changes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments or analysis under dynamic channel conditions, testing the algorithm's ability to maintain performance with moving UEs, blockages, or changing molecular absorption, and evaluating potential enhancements like online learning or channel prediction.

## Limitations
- Limited scalability analysis: The paper does not discuss how the FDRL approach scales with larger network sizes or varying user densities
- Lack of dynamic environment handling: The current FDRL framework does not address performance under time-varying channel conditions or environmental changes
- Missing privacy risk assessment: Beyond federated learning, the paper does not explore potential privacy concerns such as inference attacks on the trained models
