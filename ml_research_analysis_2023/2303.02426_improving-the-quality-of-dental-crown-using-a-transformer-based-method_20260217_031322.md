# Improving the quality of dental crown using a Transformer-based method

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.02426
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02426
- Reference count: 13
- Mean chamfer distance improves from 56.39 to 8.04 × 10^-3 when margin line information is included

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a fully automatic method for dental crown generation using a transformer-based network that addresses the challenge of generating crowns that precisely fit prepared teeth at the margin line. The method treats crown generation as a point cloud completion problem, incorporating margin line information into the network input as high-resolution spatial cues. Experimental results demonstrate significant improvement in crown quality compared to previous methods, with chamfer distance improving by more than an order of magnitude when margin line information is included.

## Method Summary
The method generates dental crowns by treating it as a point cloud completion problem using a geometry-aware transformer architecture. It takes as input a partial point cloud of the prepared tooth along with extracted margin line points (sampled into 1000 points from the ground truth crown), neighboring teeth, and opposing jaw teeth. The transformer encoder processes this context, generates point proxies, and passes them to a decoder that produces the crown shell through multi-scale folding layers. The key innovation is incorporating margin line information to provide dense spatial cues exactly where the crown meets the prepared tooth, improving fitting accuracy at the critical boundary.

## Key Results
- Mean chamfer distance improves from 56.39 × 10^-3 to 8.04 × 10^-3 when margin line information is included
- The generated crowns respect spatial constraints and do not overlap existing teeth
- The method achieves high-resolution detail at the margin line where it matters most for proper crown adhesion

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Margin line points provide dense spatial cues that improve fitting accuracy at the tooth boundary
- Mechanism: By sampling the ground truth margin line into 1000 points and feeding them as part of the input context, the network receives high-resolution guidance exactly where the crown meets the prepared tooth. This localized point density biases the transformer's attention to preserve margin shape fidelity.
- Core assumption: Margin line points are reliable anchors that can be extracted from ground truth crowns and correctly mapped back to the generated shell
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We also add the margin line points to our ground truth crown to increase the resolution at the margin line"
  - [section 2.1] "We extract the margin line from a die... sample the spline into 1000 points"
  - [corpus] Weak—no direct comparisons in neighbors; assumption relies on domain geometry rather than external studies
- Break condition: If margin line extraction fails due to poor prep scan quality, the high-resolution cues degrade into noise and hurt chamfer distance

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Including neighboring and opposing teeth constrains the shell geometry to respect occlusion and spacing rules
- Mechanism: The context set (two neighbors + three opposing teeth) provides spatial constraints so that the generated shell does not overlap adjacent teeth and maintains proper occlusal relationships, reducing post-hoc corrections
- Core assumption: The surrounding teeth geometry is accurately segmented and consistently aligned across training samples
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1] "We feed the obtained margin line along with two neighbor teeth of the prepared tooth and three closest teeth in the opposing jaw"
  - [abstract] "The designed crown should not overlap existing teeth, and it must respect a space occlusion relationship with the opposing teeth"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; relies on stated functional requirements
- Break condition: Incorrect segmentation or alignment of context teeth introduces false constraints, leading to unrealistic shell shapes

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Geometry-aware transformer blocks incorporate 3D inductive biases that improve shape completion over vanilla transformers
- Mechanism: The geometry-aware module models geometric relations (distances, normals) between point proxies, allowing attention to be weighted by spatial coherence rather than raw coordinates, which helps preserve thin-shell topology
- Core assumption: Point proxies generated by the encoder capture sufficient structural information for the geometry-aware block to enhance
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.2] "geometry-aware transformer block to facilitate transformers to better leverage the inductive bias about 3D geometric structures"
  - [section 2.2] "a geometry-aware block that models the geometric relations which can be a plug-and-play module"
  - [corpus] No direct citations to ablation studies; inference based on cited [11] paper's design
- Break condition: If proxy quality is poor, geometry-aware module amplifies errors instead of correcting them

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input point set (context + margin line) → Transformer encoder (geometry-aware) → Proxy generation → Transformer decoder (query generator) → Multi-scale folding decoder → Output crown shell
- Critical path: Context → Encoder → Proxies → Decoder → Folding layers → Final shell; margin line is injected at the input level and preserved through attention
- Design tradeoffs: Margin line resolution (1000 points) vs. input memory; geometry-aware blocks vs. plain transformer (accuracy vs. speed); multi-scale folding vs. single-scale (detail vs. compute)
- Failure signatures: High chamfer distance spikes when margin line is mis-extracted; shell overlaps with neighbors if context segmentation fails; missing thin regions if geometry-aware blocks are disabled
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train baseline without margin line; measure mean chamfer distance
  2. Add margin line points; compare improvement on chamfer distance
  3. Remove geometry-aware blocks; evaluate if performance regresses, isolating their contribution

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of this transformer-based method compare to other deep learning approaches like PointNet++ or Voxel-based methods for dental crown generation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions related work including SA-IFN (voxel-based) and mentions folding-based methods, but does not provide direct comparative experiments with these approaches
- Why unresolved: The paper only compares against a baseline method and doesn't benchmark against other state-of-the-art point cloud completion methods
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct experimental comparison showing chamfer distance and other metrics between the transformer method and PointNet++, SA-IFN, and other competing approaches on the same dataset

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of margin line sampling density (currently 1000 points) on crown generation quality and margin line accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states they sample margin lines into 1000 points but doesn't explore how this choice affects results
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a fixed sampling density without investigating the sensitivity of results to this parameter
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying margin line sampling density (e.g., 500, 1000, 2000 points) and measuring the effect on chamfer distance and margin line accuracy

### Open Question 3
- Question: How well does the model generalize to unseen tooth types or pathological cases not present in the training data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions training on all tooth types (incisors, canines, molars, premolars) but doesn't discuss performance on rare or pathological cases
- Why unresolved: Clinical applications may require handling unusual anatomical variations not well-represented in the dataset
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the model on dental scans containing rare anatomical variations, congenital defects, or severe wear patterns and measuring performance degradation

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the computational complexity and inference time of the method in clinical settings?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper provides implementation details but doesn't discuss real-world deployment considerations
- Why unresolved: While the method shows improved accuracy, practical clinical adoption requires understanding of processing speed and resource requirements
- What evidence would resolve it: Measurements of inference time on standard clinical hardware and memory/computational requirements for different batch sizes

## Limitations
- The dataset size is relatively small (90 training cases), which may limit generalizability and raises questions about overfitting to specific tooth geometries
- Margin line extraction relies on ground truth crowns being available, creating a circular dependency that may not hold in clinical practice where only prepared teeth are scanned
- The geometry-aware transformer's contribution is inferred from architectural description rather than ablation studies, making it difficult to quantify its specific impact

## Confidence
- High confidence in the general approach of incorporating margin line information for improved crown fitting
- Medium confidence in the specific quantitative improvements (chamfer distance reduction) due to limited dataset size and lack of external validation
- Low confidence in the claimed superiority of geometry-aware transformer blocks without direct ablation evidence
