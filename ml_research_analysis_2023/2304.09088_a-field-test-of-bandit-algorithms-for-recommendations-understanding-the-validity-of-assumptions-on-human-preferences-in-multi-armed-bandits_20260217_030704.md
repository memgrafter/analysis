# A Field Test of Bandit Algorithms for Recommendations: Understanding the Validity of Assumptions on Human Preferences in Multi-armed Bandits

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.09088
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09088
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Mean reward distributions differ significantly between fixed recommendation sequences (CYCLE vs REPEAT) for three comic genres, with differences up to 0.57 on a 9-point Likert scale (95% CI = [0.30, 0.84], p-value < 0.001).

## Executive Summary
This study challenges a core assumption in multi-armed bandit (MAB) algorithms used for recommendations: that user preferences (reward distributions) remain fixed over time. Through a controlled experiment with 360 crowdworkers reading comics and providing enjoyment ratings, the research compares two fixed recommendation sequences (CYCLE and REPEAT) and finds significant differences in mean reward distributions for three comic genres. The study reveals that the fixed-reward-distribution assumption is violated in real-world recommendation settings, with preference dynamics differing between heavy and light comic readers. Additionally, the research shows that cumulative reward performance does not necessarily align with user satisfaction or attentiveness, highlighting important limitations in using reward-based metrics as proxies for user experience.

## Method Summary
The study recruited 360 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers to participate in a comics recommendation experiment. Participants were randomly assigned to one of seven experimental conditions, including different MAB algorithms (UCB, Thompson Sampling, ETC, ε-Greedy) and two fixed recommendation sequences (CYCLE and REPEAT). Each participant read 50 comics across five genres (family, political conservative, political liberal, office, gag), providing enjoyment ratings on a 9-point Likert scale. The study used two-sample permutation tests with Holm's Sequential Bonferroni correction to compare mean rewards between the CYCLE and REPEAT sequences, controlling for family-wise error rate. Additional analyses examined differences between heavy and light comic readers and explored the relationship between cumulative rewards and user satisfaction.

## Key Results
- Mean reward distributions differ significantly between CYCLE and REPEAT sequences for three comic genres (family, political conservative, political liberal), with differences up to 0.57 on a 9-point Likert scale.
- Heavy comic readers prefer REPEAT recommendations while light comic readers prefer CYCLE recommendations, indicating different preference dynamics based on consumption frequency.
- Cumulative reward performance does not necessarily align with user satisfaction or attentiveness, with the highest-reward algorithm not showing the highest satisfaction ratings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Temporal stability of user preferences is a core assumption in multi-armed bandit algorithms, but this assumption is violated in real-world recommendation settings.
- Mechanism: The study tests this assumption by comparing two fixed recommendation sequences (CYCLE and REPEAT) and using permutation tests to analyze whether user preferences remain constant over time. The results show that for three comic genres (family, political conservative, and political liberal), the mean reward distributions differ significantly between sequences, with differences up to 0.57 on a 9-point Likert scale.
- Core assumption: The reward distribution of each arm is fixed over time.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "These preference assumptions are seldom tested using human subject studies... In this work, we conduct a study with crowdworkers in a comics recommendation MABs setting... We check the validity of core MABs assumptions—that human preferences (reward distributions) are fixed over time—and find that they do not hold."
  - [section]: "Results show that for three comic genres (family, political conservative, and political liberal), the mean reward distributions differ significantly between sequences, with differences up to 0.57 on a 9-point Likert scale (95% CI = [0.30, 0.84], p-value < 0.001)."
- Break condition: If user preferences were truly fixed over time, the mean reward distributions for each arm would not differ significantly between the CYCLE and REPEAT sequences.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Heavy and light comic readers show different preference dynamics in a multi-armed bandit recommendation setting.
- Mechanism: The study divides participants into heavy comic readers (who read comics daily) and light comic readers (who read comics at a lower frequency). The analysis reveals that for each genre, heavy readers tend to prefer recommendations from the REPEAT sequence over the CYCLE sequence, while light readers prefer recommendations from the CYCLE sequence over the REPEAT sequence.
- Core assumption: User preferences evolve differently based on their consumption frequency.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Interestingly, we find that for each genre, the heavy readers tend to prefer the recommendations from the REPEAT sequence over the CYCLE sequence. On the contrary, for each genre, light readers prefer recommendations from the CYCLE sequence over the REPEAT sequence."
  - [section]: "This distinction between heavy and light users may be due to various reasons. For example, light readers may prefer variety in their recommendations because they are exploring their interests or the light readers and heavy readers share different satiation rates."
- Break condition: If user preferences evolved uniformly regardless of consumption frequency, heavy and light readers would show similar preference dynamics in the recommendation setting.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Cumulative reward performance does not necessarily align with user satisfaction or attentiveness in multi-armed bandit algorithms for recommendations.
- Mechanism: The study compares different MAB algorithms (Self-selected, UCB, TS, ETC, ε-Greedy, CYCLE, and REPEAT) in terms of cumulative rewards, hindsight satisfaction, and preference towards autonomy. The results show that the algorithm with the highest cumulative reward (UCB) does not have the highest percentage of participants who believe the comics they read captured their preferences well in hindsight.
- Core assumption: Cumulative reward is a reliable indicator of user satisfaction and attentiveness.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Though Self-selected does not have the highest mean cumulative reward, it has the highest percent of participants who believe that the comics they read have captured their preference well in hindsight."
  - [section]: "This misalignment between mean cumulative reward and hindsight satisfaction also shows in other algorithms (i.e., higher mean cumulative reward may not indicate higher hindsight satisfaction), including ε-Greedy and ETC."
- Break condition: If cumulative reward were a reliable indicator of user satisfaction and attentiveness, the algorithm with the highest cumulative reward would also have the highest hindsight satisfaction and preference towards autonomy.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Permutation test
  - Why needed here: To quantify the significance of the difference between the mean reward distributions under the CYCLE and REPEAT sequences, accounting for the different numbers of participants in each group.
  - Quick check question: How does a permutation test help determine whether the observed difference in mean reward distributions is statistically significant?

- Concept: Confidence interval
  - Why needed here: To report the range of plausible values for the difference between the mean reward distributions under the CYCLE and REPEAT sequences, providing a measure of uncertainty around the point estimate.
  - Quick check question: What does a 95% confidence interval represent in the context of comparing mean reward distributions?

- Concept: Bonferroni correction
  - Why needed here: To control the family-wise error rate when conducting multiple hypothesis tests (one for each arm) simultaneously, ensuring that the probability of falsely rejecting any null hypothesis is at most the specified significance level.
  - Quick check question: Why is it necessary to adjust the significance level when performing multiple hypothesis tests, and how does the Bonferroni correction achieve this?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Web interface -> Server -> MongoDB database -> MAB algorithms
- Critical path:
  1. Participant completes background survey and registers on the platform using their completion code.
  2. Participant is assigned an algorithm and begins the comic rating portion of the study.
  3. Participant reads comics, provides enjoyment scores, and answers attention check questions.
  4. Participant completes the post-study survey about their reading experience.
  5. Server stores and processes the collected data for analysis.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using a 9-point Likert scale for enjoyment scores balances the need for distinguishable options with the potential for subjective interpretation and high variance.
  - Implementing MAB algorithms on the server ensures consistent execution and parameter updates but may introduce latency in the participant's experience.
  - Hosting the server on Amazon EC2 provides scalability and reliability but incurs additional costs.

- Failure signatures:
  - High variance in enjoyment scores may indicate issues with the subjective nature of the rating scale or the quality of the comics used in the study.
  - Low attention check correctness percentages may suggest that participants are not engaging with the comics as intended or that the attention checks are not effective.
  - Significant differences in mean reward distributions between heavy and light readers may indicate that the study design does not account for the potential impact of consumption frequency on user preferences.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement and test the web interface with a small group of participants to ensure the functionality of comic rating, attention checks, and post-study survey.
  2. Deploy the server and MAB algorithms, verifying that data is correctly stored and processed, and that the algorithms are executing as expected.
  3. Conduct a pilot study with a larger group of participants to identify any issues with the study design, data collection, or analysis methods before scaling up to the full study.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do preference dynamics differ between heavy and light comic readers in the context of the CYCLE and REPEAT recommendation sequences?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that heavy and light comic readers show different preference dynamics under the CYCLE and REPEAT sequences, with heavy readers preferring REPEAT and light readers preferring CYCLE.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies these differences, it does not provide a definitive explanation for the underlying causes of this phenomenon. It suggests that factors like satiation rates or personality traits might be involved, but does not test these hypotheses.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further experiments could be conducted to measure satiation rates and personality traits of participants, and then correlate these factors with their preference dynamics under different recommendation sequences.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of different MAB algorithms (UCB, TS, ETC, ε-Greedy) compare in terms of user satisfaction and attentiveness in a recommendation setting?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper presents exploratory analyses comparing the performance of different MAB algorithms in terms of cumulative rewards, hindsight satisfaction, and rating memory.
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges that these analyses are exploratory and should not be interpreted as definitive answers. The results suggest that there is no clear winner in terms of user satisfaction and attentiveness, and that the relationship between cumulative rewards and user satisfaction is not straightforward.
- What evidence would resolve it: More rigorous experiments could be conducted with larger sample sizes and more diverse recommendation domains to obtain more conclusive results on the performance of different MAB algorithms in terms of user satisfaction and attentiveness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can MAB algorithms be adapted to account for evolving user preferences in a recommendation setting?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates that user preferences are not fixed over time in a recommendation setting, which challenges the assumption underlying many traditional MAB algorithms. It suggests that future MAB algorithms should account for such preference dynamics.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not propose specific methods for adapting MAB algorithms to account for evolving preferences. It only provides an experimental framework and toolkit for testing such algorithms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Research could be conducted to develop and test new MAB algorithms that explicitly model and adapt to evolving user preferences. These algorithms could be evaluated using the experimental framework and toolkit provided in the paper.

## Limitations
- The controlled experiment setting may not fully capture the complexity of real-world recommendation systems where user preferences could be influenced by additional contextual factors.
- The fixed recommendation sequences (CYCLE and REPEAT) represent a simplified comparison that may not reflect the full spectrum of possible recommendation patterns.
- The study focuses on comic recommendations, which may have different preference dynamics compared to other domains like music or news.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The finding that mean reward distributions differ significantly between CYCLE and REPEAT sequences (up to 0.57 on 9-point scale) is supported by rigorous permutation testing and multiple hypothesis correction.

**Medium Confidence**: The observation that heavy and light readers show different preference dynamics is based on post-hoc analysis and may require further validation with larger sample sizes.

**Medium Confidence**: The misalignment between cumulative reward and user satisfaction is an important finding but relies on self-reported measures that may be subject to bias.

## Next Checks

1. **Replication with alternative recommendation patterns**: Test additional recommendation sequences beyond CYCLE and REPEAT to understand the full spectrum of preference dynamics and validate whether the observed differences persist.

2. **Cross-domain validation**: Conduct similar experiments in different recommendation domains (e.g., music, news, or product recommendations) to assess whether the violation of fixed reward distribution assumptions generalizes across contexts.

3. **Real-time preference tracking**: Implement methods to track preference changes within individual participants over time, rather than comparing aggregate distributions, to better understand the temporal dynamics of preference evolution.
