# The Geometry of Multilingual Language Models: An Equality Lens

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07839
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07839
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Low-resource languages show lower cross-lingual similarity and higher separability in multilingual models compared to high-resource languages

## Executive Summary
This study analyzes the geometric representations of different languages in three multilingual language models (mBERT, MiniLM, XLMR) using PCA visualizations, Cross-Lingual Similarity Index (Γ), and Language Separability Index (Φ). The results show that languages from the same linguistic families tend to have lower separability (Φ) and higher similarity (Γ), but languages are generally well-separated across families. The analysis reveals that low-resource languages have lower cross-lingual similarity and higher separability compared to high-resource languages in all models, indicating less optimal representations. The high separability values suggest that language subspaces are relatively isolated, which may impact cross-lingual transfer performance.

## Method Summary
The study uses the XNLI-15way dataset with 300 parallel sentences across 15 languages to analyze multilingual model representations. Three pre-trained models (mBERT, MiniLM, XLMR) are evaluated using two geometric metrics: Cross-Lingual Similarity Index (Γ) measuring average cosine similarity between language embeddings, and Language Separability Index (Φ) measuring geometric separability between language clusters. PCA visualizations complement the quantitative analysis. The metrics are specifically designed to account for model anisotropy, which can make standard cosine similarity unreliable in models like XLMR.

## Key Results
- Languages from the same linguistic families show lower separability (Φ) and higher similarity (Γ)
- Languages are generally well-separated across different families
- Low-resource languages exhibit lower cross-lingual similarity and higher separability compared to high-resource languages
- High anisotropy in models like XLMR necessitates the proposed Γ metric for reliable similarity measurement

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Geometric separation (Φ) and similarity (Γ) metrics capture meaningful differences in multilingual model representations across language families.
- Mechanism: The study introduces two geometric metrics to quantify how languages are represented in the embedding space. Φ measures separability by treating each language as a cluster and calculating pairwise geometric separability. Γ measures cross-lingual similarity by computing average cosine similarity between parallel sentences, normalized by model anisotropy. These metrics reveal that languages from the same family have lower separability and higher similarity, while languages are generally well-separated across families.
- Core assumption: The geometric structure of embedding space meaningfully reflects cross-lingual relationships and transfer potential.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using a geometric separability index we find that although languages tend to be closer according to their linguistic family, they are almost separable with languages from other families."
  - [section] "We study the separation of different languages in embedding space by treating all the points belonging to a language as a single cluster and calculating the pairwise Geometric Separability Index Thornton (2008) between two languages."
  - [corpus] "Found 25 related papers (using 8). Average neighbor FMR=0.579" - Weak corpus support for geometric analysis approach
- Break condition: If language representations become highly isotropic (uniformly distributed), geometric separability metrics may lose discriminative power.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Low-resource languages have systematically worse representations in multilingual models compared to high-resource languages.
- Mechanism: The analysis reveals that low-resource languages exhibit lower cross-lingual similarity (Γ) and higher separability (Φ) compared to high-resource languages across all models studied. This indicates that low-resource languages are less optimally represented in the shared embedding space, potentially due to insufficient training data.
- Core assumption: Representation quality directly correlates with resource availability and training data volume.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our findings indicate that the low-resource languages are not represented as good as high resource languages in any of the models"
  - [section] "We observe that the Language Model starts by representing a language in relative isolation, and the more text it sees, the more it contextualizes that language in terms of the other languages, leading to better Γ for high-resource languages"
  - [corpus] "average neighbor citations=0.0" - Limited external validation of low-resource representation findings
- Break condition: If training procedures or architectural changes specifically optimize for low-resource language representation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: High anisotropy in models like XLMR makes cosine similarity less reliable, necessitating the proposed Γ metric.
- Mechanism: The paper notes that models like XLMR have extremely high anisotropy (average cosine similarity of random words), making standard cosine similarity metrics unreliable for cross-lingual comparison. The Γ metric normalizes similarity by model anisotropy to provide more interpretable values.
- Core assumption: Anisotropy significantly impacts the interpretability of similarity measures in multilingual models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "due to the extremely high anisotropy(average cosine similarity of any two randomly sampled words in the dataset) of models like XLMR, it is difficult to make conclusions using cosine similarity"
  - [section] "For a modelM, Γ ranges from−1/Anisotropy(M) to 1/Anisotropy(M). Low model anisotropy and high average cosine similarity is ideal, therefore higher Γ is ideal."
  - [corpus] "average neighbor FMR=0.579" - Moderate corpus support for anisotropy-related concerns
- Break condition: If future models achieve lower anisotropy through architectural improvements or training modifications.

  - Why needed here: The study's findings about language family clustering and low-resource language representation issues are directly relevant to understanding cross-lingual transfer capabilities and potential biases in multilingual models.
  - Quick check question: Why might languages from the same linguistic family show higher cross-lingual similarity and lower separability in embedding space?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data pipeline: XNLI-15 dataset with 300 parallel sentences per language across 15 languages
  - Model implementations: mBERT, MiniLM, XLMR from HuggingFace
  - Metric computation: PCA visualization, Γ (Cross-Lingual Similarity Index), Φ (Language Separability Index)
  - Analysis framework: Comparative analysis across models and language families

- Critical path:
  1. Load parallel sentences and extract embeddings from target models
  2. Compute anisotropy for each model
  3. Calculate Γ values for all language pairs
  4. Calculate Φ values for all language pairs
  5. Generate PCA visualizations for selected language groups
  6. Analyze patterns across models and language resources

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using 300 sentences per language provides sufficient data for statistical analysis but may miss longer-range dependencies
  - Focusing on 15 languages balances coverage with computational feasibility
  - The proposed Γ metric addresses anisotropy issues but introduces normalization complexity

- Failure signatures:
  - Unusually uniform Γ or Φ values across all language pairs may indicate issues with the metric computation
  - Extreme anisotropy values (>0.9) may render similarity measures unreliable
  - Missing or corrupted embeddings for certain languages may skew family-level analysis

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify metric computations by testing on synthetic data with known geometric properties
  2. Compare results across different sample sizes (100, 300, 500 sentences) to assess robustness
  3. Analyze individual sentence-level embeddings to identify outliers or anomalies in language representations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the geometric representation of languages in multilingual models impact their cross-lingual transfer performance, and what is the optimal balance between language separability and similarity for maximizing transfer?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that their observation on high Φ values relates to Philippy et al.'s work showing that language distance in embedding space correlates with cross-lingual performance, and they conclude that languages need some degree of intersection for better cross-lingual transfer while maintaining some separation for individual masked modeling.
- Why unresolved: While the paper establishes that both high separability and cross-lingual similarity affect performance, it does not determine the optimal balance between these competing factors or how this balance varies across different tasks and language pairs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments measuring cross-lingual transfer performance across different Φ and Γ ranges, potentially using a multi-objective optimization approach to find the ideal geometric configuration for various downstream tasks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the specific factors that cause low-resource languages to have lower cross-lingual similarity and higher separability compared to high-resource languages in multilingual models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that low-resource languages have lower average Γ values and higher Φ values compared to high-resource languages across all models, indicating less optimal representations.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not investigate the underlying causes, such as whether this is due to insufficient training data, differences in linguistic structure, or specific aspects of the pretraining procedure.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of training data statistics, linguistic features, and model behavior during pretraining to identify which factors most strongly influence the geometric representation quality of different languages.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different pretraining objectives and procedures (e.g., MLM vs. NSP vs. cross-lingual pretraining) influence the geometric properties of language representations in multilingual models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that XLMR's high Γ for English may be due to its cross-lingual pretraining procedure, and they compare models with different pretraining objectives (mBERT with MLM+NSP, RoBERTa without NSP, and MiniLM with distillation).
- Why unresolved: While the paper observes differences between models, it does not systematically investigate how specific pretraining components affect geometric properties like anisotropy, language separability, and cross-lingual similarity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying individual pretraining components while keeping others constant, or ablations that measure how geometric properties change when specific objectives are added or removed during training.

## Limitations
- Small sample size of 300 parallel sentences per language may not capture full language complexity
- Limited coverage of only 15 languages, missing significant global language diversity
- No direct validation of geometric patterns against downstream task performance

## Confidence

- **Geometric Metric Validity (High confidence)**: The mathematical formulation of Γ and Φ is well-defined, and the results align with intuitive expectations about language family relationships. The consistency across three different models strengthens confidence in the findings.
- **Low-Resource Language Findings (Medium confidence)**: While the results clearly show differences between high and low-resource languages, the causal relationship between resource availability and geometric properties requires further investigation. Other factors like linguistic distance or script differences may contribute.
- **Cross-Lingual Transfer Implications (Low confidence)**: The study identifies geometric patterns but does not directly validate whether these patterns predict cross-lingual transfer performance. The connection between separability and transfer capability remains theoretical.

## Next Checks
1. **Downstream Task Correlation**: Evaluate whether Γ and Φ values correlate with actual cross-lingual transfer performance on tasks like XNLI or parallel text classification across the same language pairs.
2. **Sample Size Sensitivity**: Repeat the analysis with varying numbers of parallel sentences (50, 100, 500, 1000) per language to determine the stability of geometric metrics and identify minimum sample requirements.
3. **Out-of-Domain Robustness**: Test whether the observed geometric patterns persist when using non-parallel texts or different domains (news, Wikipedia, social media) for the same languages.
