# Textual Augmentation Techniques Applied to Low Resource Machine Translation: Case of Swahili

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07414
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07414
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: MLM-based augmentation shows improvements in both English-Swahili translation directions

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates three textual data augmentation techniques—synonym replacement using Word2Vec, random insertion using TF-IDF, and contextual augmentation using Masked Language Models (MLM)—applied to English-Swahili low-resource machine translation. The study finds that while baseline Transformer models generally perform well, MLM-based augmentation shows promise in improving translation quality in both directions. However, significant performance degradation occurs with other augmentation methods, likely due to loss of sentence structure. The results suggest that contextual augmentation techniques warrant further exploration for low-resource NMT tasks.

## Method Summary
The study applies three augmentation methods to source-language sentences before training Transformer NMT models. Word2Vec-based augmentation performs synonym replacement using cosine similarity thresholds. TF-IDF-based augmentation inserts words with low TF-IDF scores at random positions. MLM-based augmentation uses RoBERTa to predict masked tokens, generating semantically consistent paraphrases. All augmented data is combined with original parallel corpora (JW300 and Tanzil) and used to train Transformer models with 512 hidden units, 4 layers, and 8 attention heads. Models are evaluated using BLEU, ChrF, and Meteor scores on in-domain test sets.

## Key Results
- Baseline Transformer models generally outperform augmented models across test domains
- MLM-based augmentation shows improvements in both English→Swahili and Swahili→English directions
- Word2Vec + TF-IDF augmentations do not lead to significant improvements and sometimes degrade performance
- Augmented data generation can produce up to double the original parallel sentences

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Contextual data augmentation using Masked Language Models (MLM) improves translation quality by generating semantically consistent paraphrases.
- Mechanism: MLM-based augmentation masks 15% of input tokens, predicts them using a pre-trained transformer (RoBERTa), and creates augmented sentence pairs that preserve contextual meaning while increasing data diversity.
- Core assumption: The MLM can generate high-quality, context-preserving augmentations that maintain semantic equivalence to the original sentence.

- Claim: Word2Vec-based synonym replacement increases lexical diversity without altering sentence semantics.
- Mechanism: The algorithm selects random words in the source sentence, finds similar words using cosine similarity in Word2Vec space, and replaces them while maintaining a cosine similarity threshold of 0.85 to preserve meaning.
- Core assumption: Synonym replacement in Word2Vec space preserves semantic equivalence while increasing vocabulary coverage.

- Claim: TF-IDF-based insertion adds informative content words without disrupting sentence semantics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal augmentation strategy for Swahili-English NMT - source-side only augmentation, target-side augmentation, or both?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state they only augmented the source language for Word2Vec-based augmentation and only the English sentences for MLM-based augmentation, suggesting this as future work.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explored source-side augmentation and found mixed results, with performance degradation in some cases possibly due to loss of sentence structure. They did not test target-side or both-side augmentation strategies.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing source-only, target-only, and both-side augmentation strategies on the same datasets, measuring impact on BLEU, ChrF, and Meteor scores.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the performance of MLM-based augmentation generalize to out-of-domain datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note they used in-domain test sets and mention future work will explore using out-of-domain datasets to test generalization.
- Why unresolved: All experiments used in-domain test sets (JW300 and Tanzil). The authors observed that performance varied between domains (better on JW300 than Tanzil), suggesting domain-specific effects.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying the MLM-based augmentation approach to out-of-domain test sets and measuring performance degradation or improvement compared to baseline models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the impact of Word2Vec-based augmentation without Tf-idf word replacement on NMT performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention this as future work, noting that Tf-idf word replacement "adds more noise to the sentences."
- Why unresolved: The paper combined Word2Vec and Tf-idf augmentations, finding they did not lead to significant improvements over baseline. They hypothesize the combination may add noise.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments isolating Word2Vec-based augmentation (without Tf-idf) and comparing its performance to baseline and combined approaches using the same datasets and evaluation metrics.

## Limitations
- Small test set size (around 1,000 sentence pairs) may not represent full translation complexity
- Inconsistent performance across test domains suggests potential overfitting to specific linguistic patterns
- Word2Vec-based augmentation consistently degrades performance without clear analysis of underlying causes
- Limited exploration of augmentation parameters, particularly for MLM-based methods

## Confidence
**High confidence**: The observation that baseline models generally outperform augmented models in most test scenarios is well-supported by the experimental results across multiple evaluation metrics (BLEU, ChrF, Meteor). The finding that contextual augmentation shows improvements in both translation directions has moderate support from the presented data.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that MLM-based augmentation has potential for NMT tasks is supported but requires more extensive validation. The performance improvements are modest and not consistently observed across all test domains, suggesting the results may be sensitive to dataset characteristics or evaluation conditions.

**Low confidence**: The assertion that augmentation techniques will benefit low-resource translation more broadly is not well-supported, as the study only examines English-Swahili pairs and does not test on other low-resource language pairs or different data sizes. The hypothesis about specific mechanisms (semantic preservation in Word2Vec, syntactic integrity in TF-IDF insertion) lacks direct experimental validation.
