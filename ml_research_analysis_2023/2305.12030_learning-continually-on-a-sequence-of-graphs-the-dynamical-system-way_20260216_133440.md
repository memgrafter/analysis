# Learning Continually on a Sequence of Graphs -- The Dynamical System Way

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12030
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12030
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Introduces the first theoretical framework for continual graph learning using a two-player game between generalization and forgetting, achieving 44% improvement in forgetting metric (FM) over state-of-the-art methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a novel theoretical framework for continual learning on graph data by modeling the problem as a two-player sequential game between generalization and forgetting. The authors introduce a vertex-edge random graph (VERG) formalism to capture the non-stationary nature of graph data and derive a partial differential equation that characterizes how the optimal continual learning solution evolves with incoming tasks. The method demonstrates significant improvements in forgetting metric (FM) while maintaining competitive performance mean (PM) across multiple benchmark datasets.

## Method Summary
The method formulates continual graph learning as a two-player min-max game where one player maximizes generalization by simulating worst-case graph perturbations while the other minimizes forgetting by updating model weights. The VERG formalism models vertices and edges as measurable stochastic processes, enabling tractable gradient computation through Taylor expansion and Lipschitz continuity assumptions. The algorithm uses stochastic gradient ascent-descent to find a Stackelberg equilibrium, with experience replay to prevent forgetting of previous tasks. The training procedure involves alternating updates to perturbation parameters and model weights, with convergence guaranteed under certain conditions on the loss function and task distribution.

## Key Results
- Achieves 44% improvement in forgetting metric (FM) compared to state-of-the-art methods on vertex classification tasks
- Maintains competitive performance mean (PM) across Cora, CiteSeer, and Reddit datasets
- Demonstrates 21% improvement over naive experience replay through ablation studies
- Shows robustness to hyperparameters across large-scale experiments on graph classification datasets (MUTAG, Proteins)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The vertex-edge random graph (VERG) formalism captures dynamic non-Euclidean data while enabling tractable gradient computation.
- Mechanism: VERG models vertices and edges as measurable stochastic processes, allowing changes in topology and features to be expressed as derivatives in the GCL ODE.
- Core assumption: Vertex and edge stochastic processes are sufficiently smooth for Taylor expansion and Lipschitz continuity to hold.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "explicit modelling of nonstationary behavior of vertices and edges"
  - [section 2.1] Definition 2.1 formally constructs VERG with x(v,t) and φ(i,j,t)
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; the claim is theoretical.
- Break condition: If vertex/edge distributions are non-differentiable or have discontinuities, the Taylor expansion and Lipschitz assumptions fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The two-player min-max game enforces balance between forgetting and generalization.
- Mechanism: One player maximizes generalization by simulating worst-case graph/task perturbations; the other minimizes forgetting by updating policy weights via gradient descent. The Stackelberg equilibrium yields the optimal balance.
- Core assumption: The game is sequential with differentiable Hk; convergence is guaranteed by Theorem 4.6.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "two-player sequential game between the act of learning new tasks (generalization) and remembering previously learned tasks (forgetting)"
  - [section 3] Equations (3.7)-(3.8) formalize the game and gradient updates
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; the claim is theoretical.
- Break condition: If Hk is non-convex or gradients vanish, the equilibrium may not be reachable or may be suboptimal.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The ODE dynamics model how value function L* changes with each new task.
- Mechanism: Using Bellman's principle of optimality, the ODE expresses dL*/dt in terms of task loss and perturbations in vertices, edges, and weights. This enables continual updates without full recomputation.
- Core assumption: The higher-order remainder Rt is negligible for small task intervals.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "derive a partial differential equation that characterizes how the optimal continual learning solution evolves with incoming tasks"
  - [section 2.5] Proposition 2.3 and Lemma 2.4 derive the ODE and verify it reduces to prior work
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; the claim is theoretical.
- Break condition: If task intervals are large or Rt is significant, the ODE approximation breaks down.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - VERG layer -> GNN backbone -> Game module -> Replay buffer -> ODE solver

- Critical path:
  1. Receive new task → update VERG
  2. Sample from replay + new data
  3. Simulate perturbations (u updates)
  4. Update weights (w updates)
  5. Store new data in replay

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger ζ/ρ → better convergence but higher compute cost
  - Smaller batch size b → noisier gradients but more frequent updates
  - High β → stronger regularization but risk underfitting new tasks

- Failure signatures:
  - Exploding gradients in u or w updates
  - Vanishing gradients (no learning after initial tasks)
  - High FM but stable PM (forgetting dominates)
  - Low FM but low PM (undergeneralization)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run on Cora with fixed ζ=10, ρ=1000; verify FM/PM improve vs ER-GAT baseline
  2. Vary β across {0.1, 1, 10}; observe effect on forgetting/generalization balance
  3. Test with batch size b=32 vs b=256; measure convergence speed and final FM/PM

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the Stackelberg equilibrium between generalization and forgetting evolve as the number of tasks increases indefinitely?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proves existence of a Stackelberg equilibrium but doesn't analyze its behavior in the asymptotic regime with infinite tasks.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical analysis focuses on convergence to a single equilibrium point rather than tracking its trajectory over many tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies tracking the equilibrium parameters over hundreds of tasks, or theoretical analysis showing whether the equilibrium converges to a fixed point or continues evolving.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the vertex-edge random graph (VERG) formalism perform when applied to temporal graphs with highly dynamic connectivity patterns?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The VERG formalism is introduced as a key theoretical contribution but the experiments use relatively static graph datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates VERG on standard benchmark datasets but doesn't test its robustness to extreme non-stationarity in graph structure.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on streaming graphs with rapidly changing topology, or theoretical analysis of VERG's approximation quality under various non-stationarity assumptions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal balance between the number of gradient ascent updates (ζ) for the delta parameters and gradient descent updates (ρ) for the model weights?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions these hyperparameters but doesn't provide theoretical guidance on their optimal ratio.
- Why unresolved: While the convergence analysis shows asymptotic convergence, it doesn't specify how to tune these parameters for practical performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies varying the ζ/ρ ratio, or theoretical analysis of their impact on convergence speed and solution quality.

## Limitations
- Limited empirical validation on highly dynamic graph streams where VERG's assumptions might break down
- No theoretical analysis of hyperparameter sensitivity (ζ/ρ ratio) for practical deployment
- Missing ablation studies isolating VERG-specific contributions from standard continual learning techniques

## Confidence
- Theoretical guarantees: **High** confidence in VERG framework and ODE formulation
- Practical convergence: **Medium** confidence due to potential non-convexity in real-world graph data
- Empirical evaluation: **Low** confidence stemming from limited ablation studies on VERG-specific components
