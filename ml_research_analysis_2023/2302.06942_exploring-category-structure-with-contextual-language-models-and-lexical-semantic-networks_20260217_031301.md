# Exploring Category Structure with Contextual Language Models and Lexical Semantic Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.06942
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06942
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: BERT-based methods with disambiguation outperform previous static embedding approaches in predicting human typicality judgments

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether contextual language models like BERT and lexical semantic networks like WordNet can predict human typicality judgments for categories and exemplars. The authors develop a suite of unsupervised probing methods to extract typicality scores from BERT and evaluate WordNet-based similarity measures. A key finding is that polysemy significantly impacts performance, with disambiguation mechanisms substantially improving results. The best-performing approach combines BERT-based and WordNet-based methods, achieving Spearman correlations above 0.5 with human rankings. This work demonstrates that both contextual language models and lexical semantic networks capture complementary aspects of category structure relevant to human semantic memory.

## Method Summary
The study employs multiple unsupervised probing methods to predict typicality scores. For BERT-based methods, the authors use masked language modeling (BERT-MLM), sentence embedding (BERT-SentEmb), static word embeddings from average contexts (BERT-Avg), and multi-prototype embeddings with k-means clustering (BERT-MPro). For WordNet-based methods, they evaluate shortest path similarity and Lin similarity with information content (WNIC). The methods compute typicality scores by estimating how likely exemplar words are to appear in category contexts, with disambiguation mechanisms to handle polysemy. Wikipedia sentences serve as context for BERT methods, while WordNet synsets provide structured semantic relationships. Performance is evaluated using Spearman rank correlation against three human typicality datasets.

## Key Results
- BERT-based methods with disambiguation (especially BERT-MPro) outperform previous static embedding approaches
- WordNet-based methods (especially WNIC) achieve competitive performance with BERT-based methods
- Polysemy significantly impacts performance; disambiguation mechanisms improve results substantially
- Combining BERT-MPro and WNIC through ensemble achieves highest correlation (Spearman > 0.5) with human rankings
- BERT and WordNet capture complementary information about category structure

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
BERT-based methods can outperform previous static embedding approaches when using masked language modeling or sentence embedding approaches to capture category internal structure. The more central an exemplar is to a category, the more likely it is to be used in the category context(s). Performance degrades if context selection for category words doesn't align with the exemplar's sense.

### Mechanism 2
Polysemy is crucial for typicality prediction, with disambiguation mechanisms significantly improving performance. Methods like BERT-MPro and WNIC use disambiguation to select the most category-compatible sense of both exemplar and category words. Humans consider the senses of category and exemplar words that are most compatible with one another when judging typicality, performing joint disambiguation. Performance decreases if disambiguation fails or words have too many senses.

### Mechanism 3
WordNet-based methods achieve competitive performance when endowed with disambiguation and information content. WordNet similarity measures combined with information content values capture hierarchical concept organization and frequency information relevant to typicality. The more closely linked two synsets are in the WordNet graph, the more typical the exemplar is of the category. Performance suffers if WordNet structure doesn't align with human judgments or IC values are inaccurate.

- **Critical path**: Map concepts to words → Compute typicality scores using chosen method → Evaluate scores against human judgments using Spearman correlation
- **Design tradeoffs**: BERT-based methods offer rich contextual information but require large text corpora; WordNet-based methods provide structured sense-oriented knowledge but depend on lexical database quality. Disambiguation adds complexity but improves performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What is the optimal disambiguation method for extracting typicality scores from contextual language models and lexical semantic networks? The paper shows disambiguation mechanisms substantially improve performance but only explores limited approaches. A comprehensive comparison of different disambiguation methods on various typicality datasets would resolve this.

### Open Question 2
How do contextual language models and lexical semantic networks capture complementary information for typicality prediction? The paper finds BERT and WordNet capture complementary information but only provides preliminary analysis. A detailed study of different linguistic and semantic features captured by each, plus exploration of sophisticated ensemble methods, would resolve this.

### Open Question 3
How do geographical and cultural differences affect typicality judgments, and can models account for these variations? The paper acknowledges limitations of English-language data from UK/US participants. Experiments with diverse language/culture datasets and evaluation of cross-cultural generalization would resolve this.

## Limitations

- Study focuses on English language data, limiting generalizability to other languages and cultural contexts
- Performance on categories with fewer exemplars remains untested (study uses only categories with ≥20 exemplars)
- Impact of different Wikipedia sampling strategies on BERT-based methods' performance not fully characterized
- Ensemble method combines only top two performers, leaving potential gains from broader model combination unexplored

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: BERT-based methods with disambiguation (BERT-MPro) outperforming static embeddings; polysemy's importance for typicality prediction
- **Medium confidence**: WordNet-based methods matching BERT performance; complementary information captured by different approaches
- **Medium confidence**: Ensemble method achieving highest correlation (Spearman > 0.5) with human rankings
