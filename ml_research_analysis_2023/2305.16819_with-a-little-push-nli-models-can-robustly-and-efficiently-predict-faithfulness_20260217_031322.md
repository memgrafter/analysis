# With a Little Push, NLI Models can Robustly and Efficiently Predict Faithfulness

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.16819
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16819
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 17
- Key outcome: Faithfulness metrics for conditional language models suffer from inaccuracies when relying solely on NLI models, often requiring complex additional machinery or large models. This work demonstrates that augmenting NLI training data with dialogue-specific statements, using both entailment and contradiction probabilities, and applying Monte-Carlo dropout during inference significantly improves the performance of a relatively small NLI model. Applied to the TRUE benchmark, the proposed approach outperforms previous methods on average, achieving an AUC of 83.2, while being more computationally efficient. The modifications address task-specific incompatibilities and improve robustness, particularly in dialogue domains, by reducing reliance on personal statements and leveraging contradiction scores for better unfaithfulness detection.

## Executive Summary
Faithfulness metrics for conditional language models face challenges when relying solely on NLI models, particularly in dialogue domains where personal statements and hedging phrases can cause false unfaithfulness detections. This paper presents a series of targeted modifications to NLI models that significantly improve their performance on faithfulness prediction without requiring complex additional machinery or large models. By augmenting training data with dialogue-specific phrases, incorporating both entailment and contradiction probabilities, and using Monte-Carlo dropout during inference, the approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on the TRUE benchmark with an average AUC of 83.2.

## Method Summary
The proposed method involves three key modifications to standard NLI models for faithfulness prediction. First, the training data is augmented with manually curated dialogue phrases (e.g., hedging, sentiment, introductory statements) that are added to the hypothesis part of NLI training instances while preserving original entailment labels. Second, instead of relying solely on entailment probability, the model uses e-c (entailment minus contradiction probability) to better distinguish between neutral and contradictory hypotheses. Third, Monte-Carlo dropout is applied during inference by sampling multiple dropout masks (k=15) and averaging predictions, creating an ensemble effect that handles domain shift from human-written to machine-generated text. These modifications are implemented on a DeBERTa-large model and evaluated on the TRUE benchmark across summarization, knowledge-grounded dialogue, and paraphrasing tasks.

## Key Results
- The proposed approach achieves an average AUC of 83.2 on the TRUE benchmark, outperforming previous faithfulness metrics
- Incorporating contradiction scores (e-c) significantly improves discrimination between neutral and contradictory hypotheses, with nearly all contradictory instances correctly identified as unfaithful
- Monte-Carlo dropout during inference provides consistent performance improvements across all datasets while reducing sensitivity to domain shifts
- The method is particularly effective in dialogue domains, where it reduces false unfaithfulness detections caused by personal statements and hedging phrases

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: NLI models can be adapted to faithfulness prediction by augmenting training data with dialogue-specific phrases that should not influence faithfulness scoring.
- **Mechanism**: The augmentation process involves adding manually curated dialogue phrases (e.g., hedging, sentiment, introductory statements) to the hypothesis part of NLI training instances while preserving the original entailment labels. This teaches the model to recognize and appropriately handle these phrases during inference.
- **Core assumption**: Certain dialogue phrases are irrelevant for faithfulness evaluation but would otherwise cause NLI models to incorrectly flag them as unfaithful.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Augmenting NLI training data to adapt NL inferences to the specificities of faithfulness prediction in dialogue"
  - [section 2.1]: "We augment NLI data from the ANLI corpus (Nie et al., 2020) by adding a randomly chosen phrase from this set to each instance, while preserving the label"
  - [corpus]: Limited direct evidence; the paper shows performance improvements on dialogue datasets but doesn't provide specific examples of how individual phrases affected model behavior.
- **Break condition**: If the augmented phrases don't generalize well to new dialogue contexts or if the model overfits to the specific phrases used during training.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Integrating contradiction scores (e-c) into the NLI model's decision-making process improves faithfulness detection by better distinguishing between neutral and contradictory hypotheses.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of relying solely on entailment probability, the model uses e-c (entailment minus contradiction probability) to differentiate cases where the hypothesis is neutral versus contradictory with respect to the premise. This is particularly effective because high contradiction probability is a more reliable indicator of unfaithfulness than low entailment probability.
- **Core assumption**: The contradiction score provides additional discriminative power that the entailment score alone lacks for faithfulness detection.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Making use of both entailment and contradiction probabilities in NLI"
  - [section 5.2]: "We observe that almost all low-scoring faithful generations are classified as neutral, whereas nearly all instances that are classified as contradictory are indeed unfaithful"
  - [corpus]: The paper demonstrates improved performance across multiple datasets, particularly showing that e-c helps differentiate between neutral and contradictory instances.
- **Break condition**: If the contradiction score becomes unreliable due to distribution shifts or if the relationship between contradiction and unfaithfulness doesn't hold in new domains.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Monte-Carlo dropout during inference creates a robust ensemble effect that improves model performance under domain shift from human-written to machine-generated text.
- **Mechanism**: By sampling multiple dropout masks (k=15) during inference and averaging the predictions, the model creates an implicit ensemble that handles noisy labels and domain shift better than deterministic inference.
- **Core assumption**: The domain shift from training data (largely human-written) to inference data (automatically generated text) introduces noise that can be mitigated through ensemble-like behavior.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Using Monte-Carlo dropout during inference"
  - [section 2.2]: "To compute scores under Monte-Carlo dropout, we randomly sample k dropout masks and compute the average of the model predictions"
  - [corpus]: The paper shows consistent score improvements across multiple datasets when using MC dropout, though specific quantitative evidence for handling domain shift is limited.
- **Break condition**: If the computational overhead of multiple forward passes becomes prohibitive or if the dropout masks don't provide sufficient diversity for ensemble benefits.

- **Critical path**: Training pipeline → NLI model fine-tuning with augmented data → Integration of e-c scoring → Inference with MC dropout → Faithfulness evaluation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Does the proposed approach maintain its effectiveness when applied to larger NLI models such as T5-11B, given that the current experiments were conducted on a relatively small DeBERTa-large model?
- **Open Question 2**: How does the proposed approach perform on non-English languages, given that the availability of generation and annotated faithfulness data limits the experiments to English?
- **Open Question 3**: Can the proposed approach be further improved by incorporating additional task-specific augmentations or modifications for other domains beyond dialogue, such as summarization or paraphrasing?

## Limitations
- The performance gains are most pronounced in dialogue domains, with less dramatic improvements on summarization tasks where faithful generations already score highly
- The method's reliance on manually curated dialogue phrases introduces potential bias toward specific discourse phenomena that may not generalize to all conditional generation tasks
- While Monte-Carlo dropout improves robustness, it increases inference time by a factor of k (15 in experiments), which may limit practical deployment in latency-sensitive applications

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The mechanism of using contradiction scores (e-c) to improve faithfulness detection is well-supported by empirical results showing that nearly all instances classified as contradictory are indeed unfaithful
- **Medium confidence**: The data augmentation approach for dialogue adaptation shows consistent improvements across datasets, but the manual curation of exempt phrases introduces subjectivity
- **Medium confidence**: The Monte-Carlo dropout inference mechanism provides robustness benefits, but evidence for handling domain shift is indirect

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain generalization test**: Evaluate the model's performance on non-dialogue conditional generation tasks (e.g., machine translation or code generation) to assess whether the dialogue-specific adaptations generalize beyond their intended domain
2. **Ablation study on augmentation phrases**: Systematically remove individual categories of exempt phrases (hedging, sentiment, introductory statements) to identify which types contribute most to performance improvements
3. **Efficiency-robustness tradeoff analysis**: Compare the performance of different k values in Monte-Carlo dropout (e.g., k=1, 5, 15, 30) to quantify the relationship between inference time, ensemble diversity, and faithfulness prediction accuracy
