# Understanding the Spectral Bias of Coordinate Based MLPs Via Training Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.05816
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05816
- Reference count: 4
- Coordinate-based MLPs underfit high-frequency components due to high gradient confusion in dense low-dimensional inputs

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes spectral bias in ReLU networks by connecting activation regions to gradient descent convergence dynamics. The authors find that dense low-dimensional inputs restrict networks to fewer activation regions, causing higher gradient confusion and slower convergence for high-frequency components. Positional encoding mitigates this by dispersing inputs across more regions, reducing confusion and improving learning of high-frequency patterns. The study provides both theoretical analysis and experimental validation of these mechanisms.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces a framework for analyzing spectral bias through activation regions and gradient confusion. The method involves measuring the number of distinct activation patterns during training, computing gradient confusion bounds, and tracking how positional encoding affects these metrics. The authors use a coordinate-based MLP with ReLU activations and compare training dynamics with and without positional encoding on synthetic and real image data.

## Key Results
- Dense low-dimensional inputs lead to fewer activation regions and higher gradient confusion
- Positional encoding disperses inputs across more activation regions, reducing confusion
- Higher frequency encodings create wider activation regions that may improve convergence but risk overfitting

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Dense low-dimensional inputs restrict ReLU networks to fewer activation regions, increasing gradient confusion and slowing convergence for high-frequency components.
- Mechanism: When many inputs share the same or nearby activation regions, the network has limited expressive capacity. This causes conflicting gradient directions during training (high gradient confusion), which slows convergence for rapidly oscillating target values.
- Core assumption: The target signal contains high-frequency components that require many distinct linear regions to represent accurately.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "limited expressive power, caused by dense low-dimensional inputs, leads to higher gradient confusion and slower convergence for high-frequency components"
  - [section 3] "Analyzing these activation regions are important because they provide a measure of the expressive power of the network"
  - [section 4.1] "If yi is highly oscillatory for each xi ∈ R(f,A), then confusion will occur in any situation where yi > fA(xi) and yj < fA(xj)"
- Break condition: If inputs are sparse or uniformly distributed across activation regions, the confusion mechanism weakens and spectral bias becomes less severe.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Positional encoding disperses inputs across more activation regions by increasing dimensionality and scaling inter-point distances with frequency, reducing gradient confusion.
- Mechanism: High-frequency sinusoidal encoding transforms dense low-dimensional coordinates into higher-dimensional space where points are farther apart. This creates more distinct activation patterns, allowing the network to find unique gradient solutions for each input rather than sharing regions.
- Core assumption: The encoding function γ(v) successfully maps inputs to a space where distances better reflect the frequency structure of the target function.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Positional encoding alleviates this by dispersing inputs across more activation regions, reducing confusion and mitigating spectral bias"
  - [section 3] "With positional encoding, the density is alleviated since the distance λ = ||γ(xi) - γ(xj)|| for each xi,xj can be scaled by the frequency"
  - [section 4.2] "Positional encoding can increase the distinctiveness between activation patterns (from the reasoning in section 3), drastically reducing confusion"
- Break condition: If the encoding frequency is too low or improperly scaled, inputs may still cluster in similar regions, limiting the reduction in confusion.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Higher frequency encodings create wider activation regions with more parallel hyperplanes, which may improve training convergence but risk overfitting by reducing pattern distinctiveness.
- Mechanism: As encoding frequency increases, the network learns to align more hyperplanes in similar directions, creating larger activation regions. This reduces the number of boundaries between regions, making gradients more positively correlated but potentially causing the network to lose fine-grained distinctions between inputs.
- Core assumption: The network can effectively utilize the increased parallelism without sacrificing the ability to represent the target function accurately.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5] "Higher frequency encodings allow for an increase in orthogonal hyperplane directions in the beginning of training (higher dimensional), then become increasingly parallel as training progresses"
  - [section 5] "This can result in wider activation regions, which may reduce the distinctiveness between activation patterns in exchange for positively correlated solutions"
  - [corpus] Weak - related papers discuss spectral bias and positional encoding but don't directly validate this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the target function requires fine-grained distinctions between nearby inputs, the wider regions may prevent accurate representation, leading to underfitting despite faster training.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input coordinates -> Positional encoding (optional) -> Hidden layers with ReLU -> Output layer -> Loss calculation -> Adam optimizer updates

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize network with standard uniform initialization
  2. For each training iteration:
     - Compute forward pass
     - Calculate loss and gradients
     - Update parameters
  3. Monitor activation regions and gradient confusion
  4. Visualize hyperplane arrangements and input distances

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Network depth vs. width: Deeper networks create more regions but may increase gradient confusion
  - Encoding frequency: Higher frequencies reduce confusion but may cause overfitting
  - Input normalization: Affects how coordinates map to activation regions

- Failure signatures:
  - High gradient confusion persists despite positional encoding
  - Network fails to learn high-frequency components even with encoding
  - Activation regions don't grow during training (dying ReLU issue)
  - Overfitting occurs with high-frequency encodings

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare training loss curves for coordinates vs. positional encoding on a synthetic high-frequency image
  2. Measure gradient confusion bounds and hamming distances between activation patterns during training
  3. Visualize activation region growth and hyperplane arrangements for different encoding frequencies

## Limitations
- The gradient confusion metric may not capture all aspects of optimization difficulty
- Theoretical bounds may not fully translate to practical finite-sample scenarios
- Limited analysis of long-term behavior with high-frequency encodings beyond initial training phases

## Confidence
- High confidence in core mechanism linking activation regions to gradient confusion and spectral bias
- Medium confidence in claims about higher-frequency encodings causing overfitting through wider activation regions
- Major uncertainties around practical applicability of theoretical bounds and long-term behavior
