# SANE: The phases of gradient descent through Sharpness Adjusted Number of Effective parameters

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18490
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18490
- Reference count: 40
- The paper introduces SANE (Sharpness Adjusted Number of Effective parameters), a new metric that measures effective dimensionality of neural network solutions by combining Hessian eigenvalues with a sharpness-adjusted scaling factor.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces SANE (Sharpness Adjusted Number of Effective parameters), a novel metric that measures effective dimensionality of neural network solutions by combining Hessian eigenvalues with a sharpness-adjusted scaling factor. Unlike existing sharpness-based measures, SANE remains stable during training with large learning rates, where traditional metrics like Neff and λmax degrade due to instability spikes. The authors characterize gradient descent instabilities as occurring in distinct phases and demonstrate SANE's strong correlation with generalization performance across various learning rate regimes and architectures.

## Method Summary
SANE combines the number of effective parameters (Neff) with a sharpness-adjusted scaling factor λscale to create a robust measure of effective dimensionality. The method uses Hessian eigenvalue analysis through Lanczos iteration with Hessian-vector products to compute eigenvalues, then applies an outlier-bulk decomposition to characterize training phases. For deeper networks, a k-layer Hessian approximation maintains relative scaling properties while enabling computational feasibility. The metric is validated across multiple datasets including FMNIST and CIFAR-10, showing superior stability and correlation with validation loss compared to traditional sharpness measures.

## Key Results
- SANE remains stable during training with large learning rates, while Neff and λmax spike and degrade during instabilities.
- Strong correlation with validation loss (0.87-0.94) across learning rate regimes, compared to Neff (-0.60 to -0.88) and λmax (-0.59 to -0.71).
- Characterizes gradient descent instabilities into distinct phases: stable, peak, and cooling phases.
- k-layer Hessian approximation maintains relative scaling properties needed for SANE computation in deeper networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SANE remains stable during training with large learning rates because it scales effective dimensionality by an eigenvalue λscale that is less sensitive to spikes than λmax.
- Mechanism: SANE combines Neff with a sharpness-adjusted scaling factor λscale, so while Neff and λmax spike during instabilities, SANE's combined measure dampens those spikes by incorporating multiple eigenvalues and adjusting for relative sharpness.
- Core assumption: The second-largest eigenvalue λ2 (used as λscale) is more stable than λmax during gradient descent instabilities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Unlike existing sharpness-based measures, SANE remains stable during training with large learning rates, where traditional metrics like Neff and λmax degrade due to instability spikes."
  - [section 3.1] "Since each eigenvector of the Hessian controls specific degrees of freedom, SANE is an attempt at measuring effective dimensionality. For our experiments in this work, we use λscale = λ2..."
- Break condition: If λ2 also becomes unstable during certain training regimes, SANE would lose its stability advantage.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The phases of gradient descent instabilities are characterized by distinct Hessian shifts that SANE can track.
- Mechanism: During the stable phase, λmax oscillates toward the Edge of Stability without causing large Hessian rotations. In the peak phase, λmax exceeds the Edge, causing sharp spikes in loss and Hessian metrics. The cooling phase involves dropping λmax below the Edge while maintaining similar loss basins.
- Core assumption: The Edge of Stability (2/η) acts as a threshold that triggers phase transitions in gradient descent dynamics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The authors characterize gradient descent instabilities as occurring in distinct phases: stable, peak, and cooling phases, with SANE providing insights into these transitions."
  - [section 3.2] "As λmax crosses the Edge of Stability, we enter the peak phase, which is accompanied by rises in λmax, SANE, S(v), σλbulk, and |g| during this time."
- Break condition: If the Edge of Stability concept doesn't hold for certain architectures or loss functions, the phase characterization would need revision.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The k-layer Hessian approximation maintains relative scaling properties needed for SANE computation while being computationally feasible for deeper networks.
- Mechanism: By computing the Hessian on the first k layers closest to the output, the approximation preserves the relative ordering of eigenvalues along training trajectories even though absolute scales differ.
- Core assumption: The output layer contributes most significantly to the effective dimensionality measure, so approximating with k layers captures the essential behavior.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "For deeper networks, they introduce an approximation to the full Hessian using k-layer truncations, which maintains relative scaling properties needed for SANE computation."
  - [section 5] "Empirically, while this approximation drastically changes the absolute scale of the resulting eigen-spectrum, the relative scales of measures along the training trajectory are accurate."
- Break condition: If deeper layers contribute disproportionately to effective dimensionality, the k-layer approximation would miss critical information.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal choice of λscale in the SANE formula, and how does it affect the measure's performance across different architectures and datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using λscale = λ2 as a choice, but encourages exploration of other options.
- Why unresolved: The paper only uses λ2 as a preliminary choice and acknowledges that other choices could be explored. There is no systematic study of different λscale values.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study comparing SANE performance with various λscale choices (e.g., λ3, λ4, average of top k eigenvalues) across multiple architectures and datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the precise mechanism by which large learning rates cause Hessian rotations and phase transitions in gradient descent, and how can this knowledge be used to design better learning rate schedules?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper characterizes phases of gradient descent instabilities and studies Hessian shifts across loss basins, but does not provide a complete theoretical explanation.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides empirical evidence for phase transitions and Hessian shifts, it does not offer a comprehensive theoretical framework to explain these phenomena.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical model that explains the relationship between learning rate, Hessian rotations, and phase transitions, along with empirical validation using various architectures and datasets.

## Limitations

- The choice of λscale = λ2 is shown to work well empirically, but the sensitivity of SANE to different choices of scaling eigenvalues remains unexplored.
- The k-layer Hessian approximation's effectiveness for very deep networks (>50 layers) is not validated, as experiments focus on architectures with fewer layers where full Hessian computation remains feasible.
- The relationship between SANE and generalization in transfer learning scenarios or out-of-distribution settings is not examined, limiting the metric's applicability claims.

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in SANE's stability advantage over Neff and λmax during large learning rate training - this is directly demonstrated through multiple experimental regimes with clear quantitative comparisons.
- **Medium confidence** in the phase characterization of gradient descent instabilities - while the Edge of Stability framework provides theoretical grounding, the exact boundaries between phases may be architecture-dependent.
- **Medium confidence** in the k-layer approximation's relative scaling preservation - empirical validation shows the property holds for tested architectures, but theoretical guarantees are lacking.
