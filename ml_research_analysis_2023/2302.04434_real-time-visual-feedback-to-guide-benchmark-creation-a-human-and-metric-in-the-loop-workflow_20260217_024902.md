# Real-Time Visual Feedback to Guide Benchmark Creation: A Human-and-Metric-in-the-Loop Workflow

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.04434
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04434
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: VAIDA system reduces artifacts and improves sample quality in benchmark creation through real-time visual feedback, achieving 31.3% performance decrease for BERT, 22.5% for RoBERTa, and 14.98% for GPT-3 on adversarial samples.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces VAIDA, a novel approach to improve benchmark creation by providing real-time visual feedback and recommendations to crowdworkers. The method leverages a Data Quality Index (DQI) to assess and guide sample creation, reducing artifacts and improving sample quality. A user study with NASA TLX evaluation showed that VAIDA decreased mental, temporal, and effort demands while increasing performance for both crowdworkers and analysts. The study also demonstrated that samples created with VAIDA were adversarial across models, leading to significant performance decreases.

## Method Summary
VAIDA implements a human-and-metric-in-the-loop workflow for benchmark creation that combines real-time visual feedback with quality assessment metrics. The system uses a Data Quality Index (DQI) evaluating seven linguistic components to assess sample quality and provide traffic signal color-coded feedback to crowdworkers. AutoFix incrementally improves low-quality samples while preserving intended labels, and TextFooler adversarially transforms samples for analyst review. The workflow includes specialized interfaces for both crowdworkers (sample creation with DQI feedback) and analysts (batch review with multi-granular visualizations).

## Key Results
- VAIDA decreased mental, temporal, and effort demands on both crowdworkers and analysts as measured by NASA TLX evaluation
- Samples created with VAIDA were adversarial across models, causing 31.3% performance decrease for BERT, 22.5% for RoBERTa, and 14.98% for GPT-3
- The system successfully reduced artifacts in created samples while maintaining label integrity

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Real-time visual feedback via traffic signal color coding helps crowdworkers understand and correct artifacts as they create samples.
- Mechanism: The DQI evaluates seven components of text quality and assigns a color (red/yellow/green) to each. Hovering over a color shows specific artifact types and their locations in the text, enabling immediate correction.
- Core assumption: Crowdworkers can interpret color-coded feedback and make appropriate corrections in real-time.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "V AIDA facilitates sample correction by providing realtime visual feedback and recommendations to improve sample quality."
  - [section]: "V AIDA uses an intuitive trafﬁc signal color coding (high>>moderate>>low) to indicate levels of artifacts (i.e., quality) in samples."
- Break condition: If feedback is too frequent or overwhelming, users may ignore it or become frustrated, negating the benefit.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: AutoFix incrementally modifies low-quality samples to improve their DQI score while preserving the intended label.
- Mechanism: AutoFix masks each word in the hypothesis, ranks words by importance using model influence, and replaces the most influential word to achieve at least moderate quality.
- Core assumption: The model used for importance ranking is well-calibrated to the DQI components and task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "AutoFix... sequentially masks each word in the hypothesis and ranks words based on their inﬂuence on model output, i.e., their importance. The hypothesis word of highest importance is replaced, to achieve at least moderate quality."
- Break condition: If AutoFix's word replacement changes the sample's meaning or introduces new artifacts, the approach fails.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Analyst visualizations at multiple granularities enable detailed inspection of sample quality and dataset-level trends.
- Mechanism: For each DQI component, analysts get tailored visualizations (treemaps, heatmaps, parallel coordinates) that show how adding a sample affects the overall dataset quality.
- Core assumption: Analysts can interpret complex visualizations and make informed decisions about sample acceptance/rejection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Each visualization allows the analyst to simulate how adding one or more submitted samples affects the benchmark’s quality."
  - [corpus]: Weak - the paper shows visualizations but doesn't provide strong evidence that analysts consistently make correct decisions based on them.
- Break condition: If visualizations are too complex or misleading, analysts may make poor decisions that hurt dataset quality.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Data Quality Index (DQI)
  - Why needed here: DQI quantifies artifact presence across seven linguistic components, providing the foundation for both feedback and quality assessment.
  - Quick check question: What are the seven DQI components used to evaluate sample quality?

- Concept: Real-time feedback loops
  - Why needed here: Immediate feedback allows crowdworkers to learn and correct artifacts during sample creation rather than after submission.
  - Quick check question: How does the traffic signal color coding system help crowdworkers identify and fix artifacts?

- Concept: Adversarial sample transformation
  - Why needed here: TextFooler helps analysts salvage low-quality samples by transforming them to flip labels, then reverting to the original label to identify artifacts.
  - Quick check question: What is the purpose of using TextFooler to adversarially transform samples during the analyst review process?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Crowdworker interface (sample creation, DQI feedback) -> Backend (DQI calculator, AutoFix module, TextFooler module) -> Analyst interface (batch review, visualizations) -> Data storage
- Critical path: Crowdworker creates sample → DQI calculates quality → Traffic signal feedback displayed → Crowdworker revises or submits → Analyst reviews batch → Decision made (accept/reject/modify)
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Real-time feedback vs. system performance (DQI calculations must be fast)
  - Granular visualizations vs. analyst cognitive load (too many views can be overwhelming)
  - AutoFix automation vs. preserving sample meaning (aggressive changes may alter intent)
- Failure signatures:
  - Crowdworkers ignore feedback or game the system to get green lights without improving quality
  - Analysts become overwhelmed by visualizations and make inconsistent decisions
  - DQI calculations are too slow, breaking the real-time feedback loop
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test DQI calculation speed with varying dataset sizes to ensure real-time feedback is feasible
  2. Validate AutoFix doesn't change sample meaning by having humans review AutoFix outputs
  3. Test analyst decision consistency by having multiple analysts review the same samples and comparing outcomes

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the effectiveness of VAIDA compare when used for dataset creation in different domains, such as biomedical NLP versus general NLP?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the definitions of what constitutes high and low quality data are dependent on applications, and that biomedical NLP might have lower tolerance levels for spurious bias than general NLP.
- Why unresolved: The paper primarily demonstrates VAIDA's effectiveness using the SNLI dataset and does not extensively explore its performance across different domains or applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting user studies and expert reviews with VAIDA in different domains, such as biomedical NLP, and comparing the quality of created datasets and model performance against benchmarks created using conventional methods.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of crowdworkers and analysts required for large-scale dataset creation to ensure timely feedback and maintain data quality?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests that a minimum of 30 annotators would be needed for large-scale data creation to ensure timely feedback, but does not provide a detailed analysis of the optimal number of analysts required.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide empirical data or analysis on the impact of varying the number of crowdworkers and analysts on the efficiency of the VAIDA system and the quality of the created datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different ratios of crowdworkers to analysts and measuring the time taken for sample creation, review, and overall dataset quality, as well as user satisfaction and performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the learning curve for analysts change when they start with a large number of pre-existing samples versus a cold start condition?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions a secondary study where participants created/analyzed samples for varying numbers of pre-accepted samples, observing differences in the learning curve compared to the cold start condition.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides initial observations on the learning curve differences but does not offer a comprehensive analysis of the long-term impact on analyst performance and satisfaction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking analyst performance, satisfaction, and efficiency over time when starting with different numbers of pre-existing samples, and comparing these results to those from a cold start condition.

## Limitations

- The paper doesn't report time metrics to assess whether quality improvements come at the cost of workflow efficiency
- Evaluation focuses exclusively on Natural Language Inference using SNLI, limiting generalizability to other NLP tasks
- Weak evidence that analyst visualizations actually improve decision-making quality compared to traditional methods

## Confidence

- High Confidence: Real-time visual feedback using traffic signal color coding is well-supported with clear NASA TLX evaluation results showing decreased mental, temporal, and effort demands
- Medium Confidence: Claims about improved sample quality and adversarial samples are supported but limited to three specific models on one task
- Low Confidence: Assertion that visualizations enable better analyst decision-making lacks strong evidence and validation

## Next Checks

1. **Timing Study**: Conduct a controlled experiment measuring actual time-to-completion for crowdworkers creating samples with and without VAIDA, accounting for learning curves and feedback interpretation overhead.

2. **Cross-Task Generalization**: Apply VAIDA to at least two different NLP tasks (e.g., question answering and summarization) and evaluate whether the seven DQI components remain effective or require task-specific modifications.

3. **Analyst Decision Audit**: Implement a study where multiple analysts review the same samples using VAIDA versus traditional methods, then evaluate agreement rates and dataset quality outcomes to measure whether the visualizations improve decision consistency and quality.
