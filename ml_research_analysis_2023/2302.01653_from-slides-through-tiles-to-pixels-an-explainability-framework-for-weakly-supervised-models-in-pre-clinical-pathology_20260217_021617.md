# From slides (through tiles) to pixels: an explainability framework for weakly supervised models in pre-clinical pathology

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.01653
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01653
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MIL model trained on slide-level labels produces pixel-level heatmaps that correlate with pathologist annotations and segmentation predictions, with IoU > 0.4 even at high thresholds

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a contextualized explainable AI framework for weakly supervised deep learning models applied to whole slide images in digital pathology. The method uses a multi-instance learning model trained only on slide-level labels to rank slides by likelihood of containing lesions like necrosis. To explain predictions, the framework computes attribution maps at multiple network layers and aggregates them to produce pixel-level heatmaps indicating influential regions. These heatmaps are validated against both pathologist annotations and a segmentation model's predictions, showing strong correlation and stability across overlapping tiles. The work bridges the gap between weakly supervised learning and interpretable pathology analysis, enabling pathologists to understand model decisions and prioritize slide review.

## Method Summary
The framework combines multi-instance learning with contextual explainability for whole slide image analysis. First, tissue filtering and tiling extracts 512x512 patches from WSIs, which are normalized and classified by a ResNet50-based MIL model using max-pooling across tile scores. For explainability, the framework computes attribution maps at multiple convolutional layers using activation×gradient, then aggregates them using mean absolute value or variance strategies. These are normalized via percentile ranking and thresholded to create binary masks for comparison. The MIL model achieves ROC AUC ~0.85 and Precision ~0.93, while the explainability heatmaps show IoU > 0.4 against ground truth annotations and segmentation predictions, demonstrating stability across overlapping tiles.

## Key Results
- MIL model achieves ROC AUC ~0.85 and Precision ~0.93 on slide-level necrosis classification
- Explainability heatmaps show IoU > 0.4 against pathologist annotations even at high thresholds
- Explanations are stable across overlapping tiles and correlate with model confidence scores
- Framework requires only slide-level labels, avoiding expensive pixel-level annotation

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The MIL model can accurately rank slides by necrosis likelihood using only slide-level labels without requiring pixel-level annotations.
- Mechanism: The MIL architecture uses a tile classifier to score individual patches, then applies max-pooling across tiles to determine slide-level presence/absence of necrosis. This allows the model to learn from weak supervision by identifying which tiles contain lesions without explicit pixel annotations.
- Core assumption: The lesion is present in at least one tile of a positive slide, and the model can identify these tiles through learning morphological patterns.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "trained solely on slide-level labels, without the need for pixel-level annotations"; [section 2.2] "The implemented MIL model strategy consists of a classifier, whose goal is to solve the task on individual bag elements, and a pooling strategy which predicts the global bag label given the collection of individual element predictions"
- Break condition: If lesions are too small or diffuse to be captured by any single tile, or if the model cannot learn discriminative morphological features from slide-level supervision alone.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The contextual explainability framework produces pixel-level heatmaps that correlate with pathologists' annotations despite not being trained on pixel-level data.
- Mechanism: The framework computes attribution maps at multiple convolutional layers, aggregates them using mean absolute value (MAV) or variance strategies, and scales them to produce saliency maps indicating which regions influenced classification. The multi-layer approach captures both local and global morphological features.
- Core assumption: Different convolutional layers capture different levels of morphological features, and aggregating across layers provides a more complete explanation than single-layer approaches.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "computes attribution maps at multiple layers of the network and aggregates them to produce pixel-level heatmaps"; [section 2.4] "We adopt the approach of contextual explanation of [28], and use these layer attributions as a morphologically meaningful feature basis for our explanations"
- Break condition: If the aggregation strategy fails to preserve meaningful signal, or if the model's learned features are too abstract to map to interpretable pixel regions.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The explainability heatmaps show stability across overlapping tiles and increase accuracy with higher model confidence.
- Mechanism: The framework demonstrates robustness by showing high IoU scores between explanations from overlapping tiles, and the explanations correlate positively with model prediction scores, indicating faithfulness.
- Core assumption: The model's certainty about predictions reflects the accuracy of its feature identification, and overlapping tiles should produce consistent explanations.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "The explanations are shown to be stable across overlapping tiles and increase in accuracy with higher model confidence"; [section 4.3] "We compute the IoU score to quantify the degree of agreements of the explanation on the overlapping area"
- Break condition: If overlapping tiles produce contradictory explanations, or if explanations are equally confident regardless of actual prediction quality.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Multi-Instance Learning (MIL) paradigm
  - Why needed here: The problem involves classifying whole slide images based on the presence of lesions, where only slide-level labels are available but the lesion could be in any tile. MIL naturally handles this "bag of instances" scenario.
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes MIL from traditional supervised learning when dealing with whole slide images?

- Concept: Weakly supervised learning
  - Why needed here: Obtaining pixel-level annotations from pathologists is time-consuming and expensive, but slide-level diagnoses are readily available. Weak supervision leverages this abundant weak label data.
  - Quick check question: How does the trade-off between annotation effort and model performance manifest in the MIL approach versus fully supervised segmentation?

- Concept: Attribution methods and saliency maps
  - Why needed here: To make the model's decisions interpretable to pathologists, we need to highlight which regions influenced the classification. Attribution methods provide this pixel-level explanation capability.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between using activation×gradient versus other attribution methods like Grad-CAM for explaining MIL predictions?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Tissue filtering -> tiling -> stain normalization -> tile classification -> MIL model (ResNet50 + fully connected classifier + max-pooling) -> attribution map generation -> heatmap creation -> validation against ground truth
- Critical path: Tiling and tissue filtering → MIL classification → Attribution map generation → Heatmap creation → Validation against ground truth
- Design tradeoffs: Using non-overlapping tiles simplifies computation but may miss lesions that span tile boundaries; choosing aggregation strategy (MAV vs variance) affects explanation density; thresholding for binary masks is arbitrary but necessary for comparison
- Failure signatures: Low IoU scores between explanations and annotations; high variance in IoU across overlapping tiles; explanations that don't correlate with model confidence; poor performance when lesion area is small relative to tile size
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test different tile sizes (256, 512, 1024) and measure impact on classification accuracy and explanation quality
  2. Compare MAV vs variance aggregation strategies on the same model predictions
  3. Vary threshold values for creating binary masks and plot precision-recall curves against ground truth annotations

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How do the explanations from the MIL model compare quantitatively with human expert annotations beyond the current evaluation metrics?
- What are the implications of the model's focus on tissue boundaries rather than lesion areas for clinical applications?
- How robust are the explanations to variations in slide preparation, staining, and imaging protocols across different laboratories?
- Can the explainability framework be extended to other types of lesions or organs beyond rat liver necrosis?
- What are the computational trade-offs of using contextual explanations at multiple layers versus simpler methods?

## Limitations
- The explainability framework requires a small annotated subset for validation, partially contradicting the "no pixel-level annotations needed" premise
- Tile size selection (512x512) may miss small lesions or fail to capture larger morphological patterns
- The validation relies on a U-Net segmentation model as proxy ground truth, introducing potential bias in IoU measurements

## Confidence
- High: The MIL model achieves reliable slide-level classification performance (ROC AUC ~0.85, Precision ~0.93) and produces stable explanations across overlapping tiles
- Medium: The attribution aggregation strategy (MAV vs variance) and thresholding approach significantly impact explanation quality, but optimal parameters remain dataset-dependent
- Low: The generalizability of this framework to other pathology domains (different organs, stains, or lesion types) without retraining or architectural modifications

## Next Checks
1. Ablation study on MIL architecture: Compare max-pooling vs attention-based pooling strategies on the same dataset to quantify impact on both classification accuracy and explanation quality.

2. Cross-domain transferability test: Apply the trained framework to a different pathology dataset (e.g., breast cancer WSIs) and measure degradation in both classification and explanation performance.

3. Multi-organ patch analysis: Generate heatmaps on individual tiles from multiple organs and evaluate whether the framework produces consistent explanations for similar morphological patterns across different tissue types.
