# Layered State Discovery for Incremental Autonomous Exploration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.03789
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03789
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Introduces Layered Autonomous Exploration (LAE) with sample complexity $\tilde{O}(LS_{L(1+\epsilon)}^\rightarrow\Gamma_{L(1+\epsilon)}A\ln^{12}(S_{L(1+\epsilon)}^\rightarrow)/\epsilon^2)$, improving over previous work by a factor of $L^2$, and achieves minimax-optimal sample complexity $\tilde{O}(LS_LA\ln^{12}(S_L^\rightarrow)/\epsilon^2)$ under an identifiability assumption.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the autonomous exploration (AX) problem in reinforcement learning, where the goal is to discover a set of $\epsilon$-optimal policies reaching incrementally L-controllable states. The authors introduce a novel layered decomposition of these states based on iterative application of a state-expansion operator. This decomposition enables the design of Layered Autonomous Exploration (LAE), an algorithm that improves upon previous approaches by a factor of $L^2$ in sample complexity. Under an identifiability assumption, LAE achieves the minimax-optimal sample complexity, matching the lower bound up to logarithmic factors.

## Method Summary
The Layered Autonomous Exploration (LAE) algorithm combines layer-aware state discovery with policy consolidation. The core component is LASD+ for layer discovery, which iteratively applies a state-expansion operator to discover states incrementally controllable in L steps. VISGO is used as a subroutine for computing optimistic value functions and policies during exploration. Policy evaluation tests if candidate policies can reach goals within L steps, while policy consolidation post-processes discovered states to achieve AX+ guarantees. The algorithm adaptively collects samples for each layer rather than using a fixed sample budget, improving sample efficiency over previous approaches like DISCO.

## Key Results
- Achieves sample complexity of $\tilde{O}(LS_{L(1+\epsilon)}^\rightarrow\Gamma_{L(1+\epsilon)}A\ln^{12}(S_{L(1+\epsilon)}^\rightarrow)/\epsilon^2)$
- Improves over previous work by a factor of $L^2$
- Under identifiability assumption, achieves minimax-optimal sample complexity $\tilde{O}(LS_LA\ln^{12}(S_L^\rightarrow)/\epsilon^2)$
- Matches the lower bound up to logarithmic factors

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The layered decomposition of incrementally L-controllable states allows algorithms to discover states incrementally without needing to explore the entire state space.
- **Mechanism**: The state-expansion operator TL is iteratively applied starting from {s0}, generating layers K⋆j = TL(K⋆j-1). Each layer contains states reachable in L steps from the previous layer using only incrementally controllable states.
- **Core assumption**: The TL operator is expansive and converges to S→L as a fixed point, and this process can be learned without exponential sample complexity.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "A novel layered decomposition of the set of incrementally L-controllable states that is based on the iterative application of a state-expansion operator"
  - [section] "TL acts as an expansive operator over sets (i.e., T j({s0})⊂ T j+1({s0})) until the setS→L is built"
- **Break condition**: If TL has multiple fixed points not equal to S→L, or if learning TL requires exponential samples, the layered approach fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: LASD's policy evaluation phase can distinguish between states in S→L and those outside S→L(1+ε) using optimistic value estimates.
- **Mechanism**: VISGO computes optimistic value functions for candidate goals. If a candidate goal g⋆ ∉ S→L(1+ε), its true value V⋆K,g⋆(s0) > L(1+ε). The policy evaluation uses λ = NDEV(32L, ε/256, δ) rollouts to estimate hitting time.
- **Core assumption**: The optimistic value VK,g⋆(s0) ≤ V⋆K,g⋆(s0) + O(εL) with high probability, and the policy evaluation can reliably detect when Vπg⋆g⋆(s0) > L(1+ε).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "The sample complexity of LAE is bounded as Õ(LS→L(1+ε)ΓL(1+ε)AL/ε²)"
  - [section] "we have that ˆτ≥ L(1 + ε/2)≥ Vπg⋆g⋆(s0)− Lε/2 if g⋆∈U\S→L(1+ε)"
- **Break condition**: If the concentration bounds fail or the policy evaluation cannot reliably distinguish near-boundary states, incorrect states may be added to K.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Under the identifiability assumption, LASD exactly replicates the TL operator, achieving minimax-optimal sample complexity.
- **Mechanism**: Assumption 2 states that for any j ≥ 2 and g ∉ K⋆j, V⋆K⋆j-1,g(s0) > L(1+ε). This means states not in the next layer are "far" from the current layer.
- **Core assumption**: The layers K⋆j are uniquely identifiable by their distance from previous layers, and no state outside K⋆j+1 can be reached within L(1+ε) steps from K⋆j-1.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "under a certain identifiability assumption, LAE achieves minimax-optimal sample complexity"
  - [section] "This means that each layer K⋆j can be identiﬁed exactly by an algorithm run with accuracy ε"
- **Break condition**: If Assumption 2 is violated (e.g., there exist states g with V⋆K⋆j-1,g(s0) ≤ L(1+ε) but g ∉ K⋆j), LASD may add incorrect states or miss correct ones.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with RESET actions
  - Why needed here: The problem setting requires a RESET action to return to s0 from any state, ensuring the agent can always restart exploration. This is crucial for goal-conditioned policies and incremental exploration.
  - Quick check question: What happens to the exploration algorithm if the RESET action is removed? Can the agent still guarantee reaching s0 after exploring?

- **Concept**: Hitting time and value functions Vπg(s)
  - Why needed here: The performance metric for goal-conditioned policies is the expected number of steps to reach a goal state g from state s. Understanding Vπg(s) and its properties (e.g., boundedness, monotonicity) is essential for analyzing policy evaluation and consolidation.
  - Quick check question: How does the value function Vπg(s) relate to the optimal value function V⋆K,g(s) when π is restricted on K?

- **Concept**: Concentration inequalities for martingales and empirical processes
  - Why needed here: The algorithm relies on concentration bounds to ensure that estimated transition probabilities and value functions are close to their true values with high probability. Bernstein-type inequalities are used to handle the variance of value functions.
  - Quick check question: Why is a Bernstein-type concentration inequality preferred over Hoeffding's inequality in this setting? What role does the variance of the value function play?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: LASD core -> VISGO -> Policy evaluation -> State consolidation -> Policy consolidation
- **Critical path**: LASD → VISGO → Policy evaluation → State consolidation → Policy consolidation
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Sample complexity vs. exploration efficiency**: LASD trades off sample complexity by adaptively collecting samples for each layer rather than prescribing a fixed number upfront (unlike DISCO).
  - **Optimality vs. identifiability**: LASD achieves AX+ under Assumption 2 but only AXL otherwise; removing the assumption requires exploring a larger set of candidate states.
  - **Logarithmic dependencies**: LASD + replaces log(S) with log(S→L(1+ε)) by filtering states and scaling confidence bounds, but requires additional machinery (ComputeU, RTEST).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Incorrect state discovery**: If VISGO's optimism fails or policy evaluation is unreliable, K may include states not in S→L(1+ε).
  - **Layer misidentification**: Without Assumption 2, LASD may not replicate TL exactly, leading to suboptimal sample complexity.
  - **Infinite loop**: If nmin is not properly scaled (e.g., too small), RTEST may always fail, preventing policy evaluation.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Synthetic MDP with known S→L**: Construct a simple MDP where S→L is known (e.g., a line or tree). Run LASD and verify that K = S→L and Vπg(s0) ≤ L(1+ε) for all g ∈ K.
  2. **MDP violating Assumption 2**: Create an MDP where some state g has V⋆K⋆j-1,g(s0) ≤ L(1+ε) but g ∉ K⋆j. Run LASD and measure how often it adds g to K (should be rare if the failure test works).
  3. **Countably infinite state space**: Design an MDP with infinitely many states but finite S→L(1+ε) (e.g., a branching process truncated at depth L(1+ε)). Run LASD + and verify it terminates with K ⊇ S→L.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can we design an algorithm for autonomous exploration with minimax-optimal sample complexity without relying on the layer identifiability assumption (Assumption 2)?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note that under Assumption 2, LAE achieves the minimax-optimal sample complexity, but this assumption requires that states not in the next layer are more than L(1 + ε)-steps away from the current layer. They explicitly suggest designing an algorithm without this assumption as a future direction.
- **Why unresolved**: The layer identifiability assumption is crucial for removing the ΓL(1+ε) factor in the sample complexity bound. Without it, the analysis becomes more challenging as the algorithm cannot uniquely identify layers, making concentration inequalities harder to apply.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A new algorithm that achieves the lower bound O(LS_LA/ε²) without Assumption 2, with rigorous sample complexity analysis.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can the autonomous exploration framework be extended to continuous state spaces with function approximation?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors mention extending the problem to continuous states and function approximation as a natural future direction in the conclusion.
- **Why unresolved**: The current algorithms assume countable state spaces and tabular representations. Continuous state spaces require different techniques for exploration and generalization.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An algorithm that works with continuous state spaces, provides theoretical guarantees on sample complexity, and demonstrates practical performance on benchmark continuous control tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What practical benchmarks can be used to evaluate progress towards the autonomous exploration capability?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors identify identifying benchmarks as an open problem in the conclusion, noting that practical evaluation of autonomous exploration progress is currently lacking.
- **Why unresolved**: Existing RL benchmarks focus on goal-conditioned RL or exploration with extrinsic rewards, not the specific problem of incrementally discovering and learning to reach increasingly difficult states.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A suite of benchmark environments specifically designed to test autonomous exploration capabilities, with metrics for measuring layer discovery and policy consolidation performance.

## Limitations
- The layered decomposition approach relies on the state-expansion operator TL being efficiently learnable, but Lim & Auer (2012) show that general state-expansion operators require exponential sample complexity.
- Assumption 2 (identifiability) is crucial for achieving minimax optimality but is not empirically verified.
- The paper lacks concrete MDP examples to validate the theoretical bounds.

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The layered decomposition framework is mathematically sound and the asymptotic improvement from O(L²) to O(L) factors in sample complexity is correctly derived under the stated assumptions.
- **Medium confidence**: The algorithm's practical performance depends on the efficiency of learning TL and the validity of Assumption 2, which are not empirically verified.
- **Low confidence**: The comparison to DISCO's sample complexity is somewhat indirect, as DISCO's bounds are derived under different assumptions about the MDP structure.

## Next Checks

1. **Synthetic MDP verification**: Construct a simple MDP where S→L is known (e.g., a line or tree) and verify that LASD correctly identifies all states in S→L(1+ε) with Vπg(s0) ≤ L(1+ε).

2. **Assumption 2 violation test**: Create an MDP where Assumption 2 is violated and measure how often LASD incorrectly adds states to K. This would validate the failure test mechanism's effectiveness.

3. **Computational efficiency analysis**: Implement both LASD and DISCO on the same MDP family and empirically compare their sample complexities and runtime performance to verify the claimed O(L²) improvement.
