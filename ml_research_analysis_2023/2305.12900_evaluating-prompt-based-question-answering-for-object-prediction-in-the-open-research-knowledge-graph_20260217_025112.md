# Evaluating Prompt-based Question Answering for Object Prediction in the Open Research Knowledge Graph

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12900
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12900
- Reference count: 27
- This paper introduces prompt-based training for object prediction in scholarly knowledge graphs, achieving up to 40% performance boosts.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of predicting objects in scholarly knowledge graphs using transformer models. It introduces a novel approach that leverages prompt-based training of BERT variants on a newly created dataset, Prompt-ORKG, derived from the Open Research Knowledge Graph. The method involves generating question-answer pairs in SQuAD format from scholarly abstracts and predicates. Experiments show that prompt-based training significantly improves model performance, achieving up to 40% boosts in relaxed evaluation settings. The best-performing model, RoBERTa, achieves F1 scores close to 35% and accuracies above 45% in the optimal settings. The study highlights the potential of transformer models for scholarly domain tasks and suggests directions for future research, such as improving question generation and exploring instructional prompting methods.

## Method Summary
The study employs template-based question generation to convert structured predicate-object pairs from scholarly knowledge graphs into SQuAD-format question-answer pairs. These pairs, along with corresponding scholarly abstracts, form the Prompt-ORKG corpus. Pre-trained transformer models (BERT-base, RoBERTa, MiniLM) are first fine-tuned on SQuAD data, then further trained on the Prompt-ORKG corpus using prompt-based learning. The models are evaluated using F1-score and accuracy metrics in both exact match and relaxed settings, with relaxed matching allowing partial credit for containing the answer.

## Key Results
- Prompt-based training achieves up to 40% performance boosts compared to vanilla models
- RoBERTa outperforms other BERT variants, achieving F1 scores close to 35% and accuracies above 45%
- The "Which" question type variant performs slightly better than other variants
- Models struggle with sentence-type objects, indicating challenges with longer answer spans

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Template-based question generation enables effective domain transfer for transformer models.
- Mechanism: By converting structured predicate-object pairs from scholarly knowledge graphs into SQuAD-format question-answer pairs, the models can leverage their pre-trained QA capabilities in a new domain without requiring large labeled datasets.
- Core assumption: The structural similarity between SQuAD-format questions and scholarly predicate-based questions is sufficient for knowledge transfer.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "template-based unsupervised generation of structured data similar to SQuAD QA data"
  - [section] "Template-based Questions Generation... Based on the predicate, we generate four variants... in an unsupervised manner"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the corpus neighbors don't directly address template-based generation
- Break condition: If the predicates in scholarly knowledge graphs don't map well to natural language questions, or if the abstract contexts don't contain the answer objects, the transfer will fail.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Prompt-based fine-tuning provides performance boosts in low-resource settings.
- Mechanism: The fine-tuned SQuAD models are further trained on the scholarly QA data using the original task format, allowing them to adapt to the new domain while maintaining their QA capabilities.
- Core assumption: The pre-trained knowledge in the models is sufficiently general to be adapted to scholarly domain through prompt-based learning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "prompt-based training of the models achieve performance boosts of up to 40% in a relaxed evaluation setting"
  - [section] "structural prompt-based learning over state-of-the-art SQuAD-specific fine-tuned transformer models for the scholarly domain"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence in corpus neighbors
- Break condition: If the pre-trained models have domain-specific knowledge that conflicts with the scholarly domain, or if the few-shot setting is too limited for effective adaptation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multiple question type variants improve model robustness and coverage.
- Mechanism: By generating four variants (What, Which, How, and cloze-style) for each predicate, the models learn to handle different question formulations, improving their ability to extract answers across various scholarly contexts.
- Core assumption: Different question types elicit different aspects of the model's knowledge and improve generalization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We have chosen the SQuAD-based task formulation for three mains reasons... to exploit the vast collection of options to choose from of state-of-the-art language models fine-tuned on the QA task"
  - [section] "Based on the predicate, we generate four variants of our dataset based on the fprompt function: 1) 3 variants with the 3 most common 'Wh' question words... and 2) 1 variant as a cloze-style question"
  - [corpus] No direct evidence in corpus neighbors
- Break condition: If the additional question types introduce noise rather than useful variation, or if the model cannot distinguish between the different formulations effectively.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Prompt-ORKG corpus generation pipeline (template-based question generation, abstract fetching, object typing) -> Pre-trained transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa, MiniLM) with SQuAD fine-tuning -> Evaluation framework with strict and relaxed matching settings

- Critical path: The most critical path is from predicate selection to question generation to model training and evaluation. Any failure in generating suitable question-answer pairs will cascade through the entire pipeline.

- Design tradeoffs: The choice between strict and relaxed evaluation settings represents a key tradeoff between precision and recall. The relaxed setting allows partial credit for containing the answer, while strict requires exact matches.

- Failure signatures: Poor performance on sentence-type objects indicates the models are not generalizing to longer answer spans. Low performance on the "unchanged" dataset variant suggests the SQuAD-format formulation is crucial for success.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare vanilla model performance (no prompt-based training) on the unchanged dataset vs. the cloze-style dataset to quantify the impact of SQuAD-format formulation
  2. Test all four dataset variants (What, Which, How, none) with RoBERTa to determine which question type yields best performance
  3. Evaluate object type performance breakdown to identify which categories the models handle well vs. poorly, focusing on sentence vs. noun phrase distinctions

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How effective are instructional prompting methods compared to the template-based approach used in this study for scholarly domain object prediction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest testing instructional prompting methods as a future direction, noting their effectiveness in zero-shot learning scenarios and potential to broaden the range of pre-trained models that could be tested.
- Why unresolved: This study only tested template-based prompting with SQuAD fine-tuned models. Instructional prompting was not explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments showing F1 scores and accuracy for both instructional prompting and template-based prompting methods on the same scholarly domain object prediction task.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do scholarly domain pre-trained models like SciBERT or BioBERT perform compared to general domain models like BERT and RoBERTa when fine-tuned on scholarly data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors recommend systematically investigating scholarly domain pre-trained models like SciBERT or BioBERT in future work, suggesting an experimental setup that combines scholarly pretraining with SQuAD fine-tuning and Prompt-ORKG fine-tuning.
- Why unresolved: This study only tested general domain models (BERT, RoBERTa, MiniLM) on scholarly data. Scholarly domain models were not included.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments showing F1 scores and accuracy for both scholarly domain models and general domain models on the same scholarly domain object prediction task.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal question generation strategy for the scholarly domain - should it be automated selection of question words based on predicates and answers, or is the current approach of using fixed templates sufficient?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the "which" variant of their dataset performed slightly better than others and suggest this indicates directions for future work to examine automated generation of questions by selecting and applying the most suitable question type for predicates.
- Why unresolved: The current study used fixed templates for question generation. The optimal strategy for the scholarly domain remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments showing F1 scores and accuracy for different question generation strategies (automated vs. template-based) on the same scholarly domain object prediction task.

## Limitations
- The Prompt-ORKG corpus is relatively small at approximately 2,500 question-answer pairs, potentially limiting generalizability
- Template-based question generation may produce less natural questions compared to human-authored ones
- Relaxed evaluation settings may overestimate practical utility by awarding partial credit for approximate matches
- BERT variants have token length limitations that may impact performance on longer scholarly contributions
