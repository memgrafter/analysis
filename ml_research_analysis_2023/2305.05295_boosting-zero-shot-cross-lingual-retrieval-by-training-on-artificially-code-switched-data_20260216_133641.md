# Boosting Zero-shot Cross-lingual Retrieval by Training on Artificially Code-Switched Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.05295
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05295
- **Reference count**: 16
- **Key outcome**: Code-switching improves zero-shot cross-lingual retrieval by 5.1 MRR@10 in CLIR and 3.9 MRR@10 in MLIR without hurting monolingual performance.

## Executive Summary
Zero-shot cross-lingual information retrieval models typically suffer performance drops when query and document are in different languages due to monolingual overfitting. This paper proposes training rankers on artificially code-switched data generated using bilingual lexicons from word embeddings or Wikipedia titles. This approach regularizes monolingual overfitting and improves zero-shot transfer to cross-lingual (CLIR) and multilingual (MLIR) retrieval while maintaining monolingual (MoIR) performance. Evaluated on 36 language pairs from mMARCO, code-switching yields consistent gains and generalizes well to unseen languages.

## Method Summary
The approach generates code-switched training data by randomly translating English tokens into target languages using bilingual lexicons derived from MUSE multilingual embeddings or parallel Wikipedia page titles. Cross-encoders are trained on this artificially mixed-language data with a translation probability of p=0.5. The method uses three code-switching strategies: Bilingual CS (BL-CS), Multilingual CS (ML-CS), and Wiki CS. Models are evaluated across 36 language pairs covering monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual retrieval scenarios using MRR@10 as the primary metric.

## Key Results
- Code-switching yields 5.1 MRR@10 improvement in cross-lingual retrieval (CLIR) and 3.9 MRR@10 in multilingual retrieval (MLIR)
- Gains are especially pronounced for distant language pairs and generalize to unseen languages (2.9-3.8 MRR@10 improvements)
- Performance is robust across different code-switching ratios, with benefits observed even with partial translation
- Monolingual retrieval performance is maintained or slightly improved while significantly boosting cross-lingual capabilities

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Training on code-switched data regularizes monolingual overfitting by forcing the ranker to learn cross-lingual semantic relationships instead of relying on exact keyword matches.
- Mechanism: The code-switched training instances mix languages at the token level, so the model cannot simply memorize that "query in EN" matches "document in EN" but must learn to bridge semantic gaps across languages.
- Core assumption: Monolingual overfit occurs because zero-shot models trained on same-language pairs learn spurious lexical correlations that don't generalize to CLIR/MLIR.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We hypothesize that training on queries and documents from the same language leads to monolingual overfitting where the ranker learns features, such as exact keyword matches, which are useful in MoIR but do not transfer well to CLIR and MLIR setups due to the lack of lexical overlap"
  - [section] "In fact, as shown by Roy et al. (2020) on bi-encoders, representations from zero-shot models are weakly aligned between languages, where models prefer non-relevant documents in the same language over relevant documents in a different language"
  - [corpus] "Weak evidence: No direct citations found for monolingual overfit in IR literature; this is inferred from cross-lingual representation literature."
- Break condition: If the bilingual lexicons are too sparse or low quality, the code-switched instances may become semantically incoherent, causing the regularization effect to break down.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Code-switching improves alignment between representations of different languages by exposing the model to parallel semantic content in mixed language contexts.
- Mechanism: By randomly translating tokens from one language to another during training, the model learns that different surface forms can represent the same underlying concept, improving cross-lingual semantic alignment.
- Core assumption: Multilingual encoders have weakly aligned representations across languages; mixed-language training strengthens these alignments.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "To address this problem, we propose to use code-switching as an inductive bias to regularize monolingual overfitting in CEs"
  - [section] "We hypothesize that training on queries and documents from the same language leads to monolingual overfitting where the ranker learns features, such as exact keyword matches, which are useful in MoIR but do not transfer well to CLIR and MLIR setups due to the lack of lexical overlap"
  - [corpus] "No direct evidence found in corpus; this is inferred from multilingual representation literature on code-switching."
- Break condition: If the translation probability is too high, the input becomes mostly non-English and the model may lose the pivot-language benefit of training on English data.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Code-switching creates synthetic multilingual training data without requiring parallel corpora, making it scalable to many language pairs.
- Mechanism: By using bilingual lexicons derived from word embeddings or Wikipedia titles, the approach generates training instances that span multiple language pairs without needing explicit translation pairs.
- Core assumption: High-quality bilingual lexicons can be induced from monolingual resources (embeddings or Wikipedia inter-language links).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We rely on bilingual dictionaries D induced from cross-lingual word embeddings... We use the lexicons provided by the linguatools project"
  - [section] "For BL-CS and ML-CS we use multilingual MUSE embeddings to induce bilingual lexicons... For Wiki-CS, we use the lexicons provided by the linguatools project"
  - [corpus] "Weak evidence: Lexicons exist but quality varies; corpus doesn't evaluate lexicon quality impact."
- Break condition: If the lexicon coverage is poor for certain language pairs, code-switching will fail to generate meaningful training instances for those pairs.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Bilingual lexicon generation from MUSE/Wikipedia → Code-switching data generation → Cross-encoder training on mMARCO → Evaluation across 36 language pairs
- **Critical path**: Lexicon generation → Code-switching data generation → Cross-encoder training → Evaluation
- **Design tradeoffs**: Higher translation probability increases cross-lingual exposure but may reduce semantic coherence; using Wikipedia titles provides larger lexicons but may include less precise translations than MUSE-based embeddings
- **Failure signatures**: (1) No improvement over zero-shot baseline suggests lexicons are poor quality or translation probability is misconfigured; (2) Degradation in MoIR indicates over-regularization; (3) Poor performance on distant language pairs suggests insufficient lexicon coverage
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Verify bilingual lexicon quality by sampling translations and checking semantic coherence
  2. Test different translation probabilities (0.25, 0.5, 0.75) to find optimal balance between regularization and coherence
  3. Compare MUSE-based vs Wikipedia-based lexicons on a small set of language pairs to determine which provides better semantic alignment

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal ratio of code-switched tokens to original tokens for different language pairs and task types?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper investigates different translation probabilities (p = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0) and shows that code-switching a smaller portion of tokens is already beneficial for zero-shot transfer into CLIR, with gains being robust towards different values of p.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides insights into the impact of translation probability on performance, it does not determine the optimal ratio for each language pair and task type. The optimal ratio may vary depending on factors such as language similarity, dataset size, and model architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting extensive experiments with different translation probabilities for each language pair and task type, and analyzing the performance trade-offs, would help determine the optimal ratio of code-switched tokens to original tokens.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of code-switching methods compare to other cross-lingual and multilingual retrieval approaches, such as cross-lingual pre-training, multilingual query expansion, or pivot language tuning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that code-switching is complementary to existing approaches like weak supervision, pivot language tuning, multilingual query expansion, and cross-lingual pre-training, but does not provide a direct comparison of performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the effectiveness of code-switching methods and does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other cross-lingual and multilingual retrieval approaches. It is unclear how code-switching methods perform relative to these alternative approaches in terms of effectiveness, efficiency, and scalability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments that directly compare code-switching methods with other cross-lingual and multilingual retrieval approaches on the same datasets and evaluation metrics would help determine the relative performance and advantages of each method.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of code-switching methods vary with the size and quality of bilingual lexicons used for generating code-switched data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses bilingual lexicons induced from cross-lingual word embeddings and parallel Wikipedia page titles to generate code-switched data. It mentions that the Wikipedia-based lexicons are several times larger than the MUSE vocabulary, but does not investigate the impact of lexicon size and quality on performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore how the size and quality of bilingual lexicons affect the performance of code-switching methods. It is unclear whether using larger and higher-quality lexicons would lead to further improvements in cross-lingual and multilingual retrieval.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with different sizes and qualities of bilingual lexicons, and analyzing the performance impact, would help determine the relationship between lexicon characteristics and code-switching effectiveness.

## Limitations

- The approach relies heavily on the quality and coverage of bilingual lexicons, which are not directly evaluated in the paper
- The optimal translation probability (p=0.5) is not extensively tuned and may vary by language pair and task type
- The method is evaluated only on cross-encoder architectures, leaving questions about applicability to more efficient bi-encoder models

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The empirical improvements in MRR@10 across 36 language pairs are well-supported by the experimental results. The consistent gains in CLIR (5.1 MRR@10) and MLIR (3.9 MRR@10) without degrading MoIR performance are clearly demonstrated.
- **Medium Confidence**: The mechanism explanations linking code-switching to regularization of monolingual overfitting are theoretically sound but rely on indirect evidence from related work rather than direct ablation studies within this paper.
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that this approach is "cheap" relative to alternatives lacks quantitative comparison. The paper doesn't compare training time, memory requirements, or inference efficiency against other regularization methods for cross-lingual IR.

## Next Checks

1. **Lexicon Quality Impact**: Conduct controlled experiments varying lexicon quality (e.g., using different seed word pair counts for MUSE embeddings) to quantify how lexicon coverage affects code-switching effectiveness across language pairs.

2. **Translation Probability Sensitivity**: Systematically evaluate the impact of different translation probabilities (p ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}) on both CLIR/MLIR gains and MoIR preservation to identify optimal regularization strength.

3. **Cross-Encoder vs Bi-Encoder Transfer**: Replicate the experiments using bi-encoder architectures to determine whether code-switching regularization transfers to more efficient retrieval models, and if the optimal translation probability differs between architectures.
