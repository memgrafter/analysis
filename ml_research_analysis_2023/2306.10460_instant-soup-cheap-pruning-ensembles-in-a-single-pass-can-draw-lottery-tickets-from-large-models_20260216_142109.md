# Instant Soup: Cheap Pruning Ensembles in A Single Pass Can Draw Lottery Tickets from Large Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.10460
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10460
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: ISP achieves comparable or better performance than iterative magnitude pruning while requiring only a fraction of the computational cost

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Instant Soup Pruning (ISP), a novel pruning method that addresses the computational expense of finding lottery tickets in large pre-trained transformers. Inspired by model soups, ISP generates high-quality sparse subnetworks by superposing multiple weak and noisy subnetworks, effectively averaging out the noise. The method requires only a fraction of the computational cost of traditional iterative magnitude pruning (IMP) while achieving comparable or even better performance. Extensive experiments on CLIP and BERT across various vision and language tasks validate the effectiveness of ISP compared to state-of-the-art pruning methods.

## Method Summary
Instant Soup Pruning (ISP) generates sparse subnetworks by superposing multiple weak and noisy subnetworks, inspired by model soups. The method creates an ensemble of individually poor subnetworks through single-pass magnitude pruning at different sparsity levels, then combines them by averaging their weights. This superposition effectively averages out the noise inherent in individual pruning decisions, producing a lottery ticket that performs comparably or better than subnetworks found through expensive iterative pruning methods. The approach requires only a single forward pass through the model at each pruning iteration, dramatically reducing computational cost while maintaining or improving performance.

## Key Results
- ISP achieves comparable or better performance than state-of-the-art pruning methods including iterative magnitude pruning
- The method requires only a fraction of the computational cost of traditional pruning approaches
- Extensive experiments on CLIP and BERT across vision and language tasks demonstrate consistent improvements
- The proposed Instant Model Soup extension improves fine-tuning performance of dense pre-trained models at minimal training cost

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ISP works by leveraging the principle that averaging multiple weak and noisy predictions can produce a stronger, more accurate result. Individual subnetworks generated through single-pass pruning contain pruning noise - suboptimal decisions made by the pruning algorithm. When multiple such noisy subnetworks are superimposed through weight averaging, the independent noise tends to cancel out while the underlying signal (the true importance of weights) is preserved and amplified. This creates a high-quality lottery ticket without the need for expensive iterative pruning cycles. The method effectively transforms the computational challenge of finding a good subnetwork into the simpler task of generating many noisy subnetworks and averaging them together.

## Foundational Learning
- **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis**: Pre-trained dense networks contain subnetworks that can be trained in isolation to match or exceed the performance of the original model. Why needed: Provides the theoretical foundation that motivates pruning research and validates the goal of finding sparse, trainable subnetworks.
- **Model Soups**: Superposition of multiple pre-trained models to improve generalization. Why needed: The core inspiration for ISP's approach of combining multiple weak subnetworks into a stronger ensemble.
- **Magnitude Pruning**: Pruning weights based on their absolute values, removing the smallest magnitude weights first. Why needed: The fundamental operation used in ISP to generate individual weak subnetworks from the pre-trained model.
- **Single-Pass vs Iterative Pruning**: Comparison of one-time pruning decisions versus multiple pruning-retraining cycles. Why needed: ISP's key innovation is avoiding iterative pruning while still achieving competitive performance.
- **Weight Averaging in Ensembles**: The technique of combining multiple models by averaging their parameters. Why needed: The mathematical operation that creates the final high-quality subnetwork from multiple weak ones.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Pre-trained model -> Multiple single-pass pruning operations -> Superposition (weight averaging) -> Final subnetwork

**Critical Path**: The most time-consuming operation is the initial forward pass through the pre-trained model to compute weight magnitudes for pruning decisions. All subsequent operations (multiple pruning operations and weight averaging) are computationally inexpensive.

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- Computational efficiency vs. potential information loss from single-pass decisions
- Number of subnetworks to average (more improves quality but increases computation)
- Sparsity levels to sample (should span a range to capture diverse pruning patterns)
- Whether to fine-tune the final subnetwork or use it directly

**Failure Signatures**: 
- If individual subnetworks are too similar, noise averaging may not occur effectively
- If pruning thresholds are poorly chosen, the superposition may average out useful signal along with noise
- If the original model is not well-trained, the method may amplify existing problems rather than improve them

**First Experiments**:
1. Generate 10 subnetworks at different sparsity levels and compare their individual performance to the ISP-ensemble performance
2. Vary the number of subnetworks in the superposition (5, 10, 20, 50) to identify the optimal ensemble size
3. Test different pruning criteria (magnitude, movement, gradient-based) to assess sensitivity to the base pruning method

## Limitations
- Experimental validation remains limited to specific pre-trained model families (CLIP and BERT), leaving uncertainty about performance transfer to other transformer architectures
- The theoretical justification relies on the assumption of uncorrelated errors, but this correlation structure is not rigorously quantified in practice
- The method depends on the availability of pre-trained models from which to sample subnetworks, requiring additional initialization costs for novel architectures

## Confidence
**High Confidence**: The core empirical observation that superposing multiple pruned subnetworks improves performance over individual subnetworks is well-supported by controlled experiments across multiple tasks and datasets.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that ISP achieves comparable or better performance than state-of-the-art pruning methods is supported by experiments, but the comparison set is limited and may not represent all competitive approaches.

**Medium Confidence**: The theoretical motivation connecting model soups to lottery ticket discovery through noise averaging is plausible but lacks rigorous mathematical derivation and empirical validation of the underlying assumptions.

## Next Checks
1. **Architecture Transfer Validation**: Test ISP on additional transformer architectures beyond CLIP and BERT, including decoder-only models like GPT variants and encoder-decoder models like T5, to assess generalizability across the transformer family.

2. **Correlation Structure Analysis**: Quantify the correlation between pruned subnetwork errors through ablation studies that systematically vary the degree of overlap in pruned weights, testing whether the averaging effect holds when correlations are high.

3. **Dynamic Subnetwork Sampling**: Implement a validation protocol that examines whether the quality of ISP-derived subnetworks improves with the diversity of the initial subnetwork pool, testing the hypothesis that heterogeneous subnetworks contribute more to the final performance.
