# Know your Enemy: Investigating Monte-Carlo Tree Search with Opponent Models in Pommerman

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.13206
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13206
- **Reference count**: 33
- **Primary result**: SP-MCTS and TP-MCTS significantly improve performance in Pommerman FFA mode compared to baselines

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of scaling Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to multiplayer games by proposing techniques that transform general-sum multiplayer games into single-player and two-player games using deterministic opponent models. The core idea is to reduce the exponential branching factor in multiplayer settings by treating other players as part of the environment and using learned models to predict their actions. Experiments in Pommerman demonstrate that both Single-Player MCTS (SP-MCTS) and Two-Player MCTS (TP-MCTS) achieve proficient levels of play, with SP-MCTS consistently outperforming TP-MCTS.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes two MCTS variants: SP-MCTS, which uses opponent models for all other players, and TP-MCTS, which additionally explores moves of a selected opponent. Both methods use deterministic opponent models to generate actions for other players, transforming n-player games into single- or two-player games. The approach is combined with a neural network that provides policy and value estimates to guide the search. Training follows an AlphaZero-like framework with supervised learning from demonstrations followed by reinforcement learning on self-generated samples. The methods are evaluated in Pommerman's Free For All mode against baseline agents.

## Key Results
- SP-MCTS and TP-MCTS significantly improve win rates compared to baseline agents in Pommerman FFA
- SP-MCTS consistently outperforms TP-MCTS across all configurations tested
- Both methods achieve proficient levels of play, with SP-MCTS showing particularly strong performance
- The approaches scale effectively to the 4-player Pommerman environment

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Opponent models reduce the branching factor in multiplayer games.
- Mechanism: Instead of expanding all players' actions in the search tree, the method treats other players as part of the environment and uses deterministic opponent models to generate their moves. This transforms an n-player game into a single-player or two-player game, drastically reducing the number of nodes that need to be expanded.
- Core assumption: The opponent models accurately capture the behavior of real opponents, or at least provide a reasonable approximation that allows the search to focus on relevant action sequences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "techniques that transform general-sum multiplayer games into single-player and two-player games that consider other agents to act according to given opponent models."
  - [section] "To address this issue, we propose two simple yet effective methods that reduce multiplayer games to single and two-player games. They allow for the application of AlphaZero-like frameworks without major adjustments."
  - [corpus] Weak or missing evidence directly supporting this specific claim.
- Break condition: If the opponent models diverge significantly from actual opponent behavior, the search may focus on irrelevant or suboptimal paths, leading to poor decision-making.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Single-player MCTS allows for deeper search within limited computational resources.
- Mechanism: By limiting the search to only the player agent's actions and using opponent models for others, the branching factor per step is reduced to |A|, where |A| is the action space size. This allows the search to explore deeper into the game tree within the same computational budget compared to full multiplayer search.
- Core assumption: The reduction in branching factor outweighs any loss in search quality due to the use of deterministic opponent models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "scaling the search to an arbitrary number of players presents a computational challenge, especially if decisions have to be planned over a longer time horizon."
  - [section] "This allows for the application of AlphaZero-like frameworks without major adjustments."
  - [corpus] Weak or missing evidence directly supporting this specific claim.
- Break condition: If the opponent models are inaccurate, the deeper search may explore paths that are irrelevant or suboptimal in the real game environment.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Two-player MCTS balances search depth and opponent modeling accuracy.
- Mechanism: This approach expands moves for a selected opponent in addition to the player agent, while other opponents use deterministic models. This provides a balance between the deeper search of single-player MCTS and the more accurate opponent modeling of full multiplayer search.
- Core assumption: The selected opponent is the most relevant one to consider during search, or at least provides a significant improvement in search quality over single-player MCTS.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose techniques that transform general-sum multiplayer games into single-player and two-player games that consider other agents to act according to given opponent models."
  - [section] "To overcome these limitations to some extend, we propose an approach which we call two-player search. This approach expands our single-player search by exploring the moves of a selected opponent in each step."
  - [corpus] Weak or missing evidence directly supporting this specific claim.
- Break condition: If the selected opponent is not the most relevant one, or if the opponent models for other players are inaccurate, the search may not provide a significant improvement over single-player MCTS.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Environment -> Opponent Models -> MCTS -> Neural Network -> RL Loop

- Critical path:
  Initialize opponent models and neural network → Perform MCTS search using the opponent models and neural network guidance → Generate a move based on the search results → Execute the move in the environment and observe the outcome → Update the neural network using the observed outcome and search results → Repeat until the game is over or a stopping criterion is met

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Search depth vs. accuracy: Deeper search can lead to better decisions, but may be computationally expensive. Using opponent models can allow for deeper search, but may introduce inaccuracies if the models are not perfect.
  - Single-player vs. two-player MCTS: Single-player MCTS allows for deeper search, but may not consider the most relevant opponents. Two-player MCTS balances search depth and opponent modeling accuracy, but may be more computationally expensive.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor performance against opponents with strategies that differ significantly from the opponent models.
  - Overly defensive or passive playing style due to inaccurate opponent models.
  - Computational constraints preventing deep enough search for effective decision-making.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper mentions extending the approach to team and radio modes of Pommerman as an interesting direction for future work, noting challenges with partial observability and communication.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is limited to Pommerman's FFA mode, leaving generalizability to other multiplayer domains uncertain
- The work relies on deterministic opponent models, which may not capture complex opponent behaviors accurately
- The paper does not explore the impact of model quality on search performance through systematic ablation studies

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1**: Medium - The theoretical soundness of opponent modeling reducing search complexity is clear, but specific performance claims lack detailed experimental validation
- **Mechanism 2**: Medium - The concept is sound, but the effectiveness depends heavily on opponent model quality which isn't thoroughly quantified
- **Mechanism 3**: Low - The paper provides limited evidence for why this specific balance between search depth and opponent modeling is optimal
