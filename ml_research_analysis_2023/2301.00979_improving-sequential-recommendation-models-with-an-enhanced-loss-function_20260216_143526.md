# Improving Sequential Recommendation Models with an Enhanced Loss Function

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.00979
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00979
- Reference count: 8
- Key outcome: CCE loss achieved average improvements of 125.63%, 69.90%, and 33.24% in terms of full ranking NDCG@5 for GRU4Rec, SASRec, and S3Rec respectively

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses limitations in loss functions for sequential recommendation models, specifically their focus on only the last timestamp of training sequences and reliance on negative sampling. The authors propose a novel Cumulative Cross-Entropy (CCE) loss that considers all timestamps and avoids negative sampling by calculating loss across the entire item set. Experimental results demonstrate significant performance improvements when applying CCE to three state-of-the-art models (GRU4Rec, SASRec, S3Rec) across five benchmark datasets.

## Method Summary
The CCE loss function computes cross-entropy loss across all timestamps in a sequence rather than focusing only on the final prediction. For each timestamp t, it calculates the softmax probability over all items in the catalog, then accumulates the negative log-likelihood of the positive item. This approach eliminates the need for negative sampling while ensuring the model learns from all intermediate predictions. The method is implemented by replacing standard BCE/CE/BPR losses in existing sequential recommendation frameworks with the CCE formulation: l∑t=1 MASK log exp(rt,pos)/∑|I|j=1exp(rt,j), where MASK handles variable-length sequences.

## Key Results
- CCE loss achieved average improvements of 125.63%, 69.90%, and 33.24% in terms of full ranking NDCG@5 for GRU4Rec, SASRec, and S3Rec respectively
- The method demonstrated consistent improvements across five benchmark datasets: Sports, Toys, Yelp, Beauty, and LastFM
- CCE improved training efficiency by eliminating the overhead of negative sampling operations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: CCE loss avoids the vanishing gradient problem caused by negative sampling in BPR and TOP1 losses.
- Mechanism: CCE loss computes softmax scores over the entire item set rather than a small sampled negative set, ensuring gradients remain informative across all items.
- Core assumption: The gradient magnitude depends on the relative scale of positive vs negative scores, and when negatives are randomly sampled, many may be far from the positive item, causing near-zero gradients.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "CCE is simple and direct, which enjoys the virtues of painless deployment, no negative sampling, and effective and efficient training." [section] "Lbpr =− 1 Ns Ns∑neg=1 logσ(rl,pos−rl,neg)" and analysis of vanishing gradients when Ns increases.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: CCE loss captures temporal dependencies across all timestamps, improving intermediate sequence representation.
- Mechanism: By summing cross-entropy loss over all timestamps in the sequence, CCE ensures that the model learns accurate predictions not just at the final timestamp but also at intermediate steps.
- Core assumption: In sequential recommendation, each item in the sequence should be predicted from its preceding context, so ignoring intermediate timestamps wastes valuable training signal.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "CCE loss that considers all timestamps and avoids negative sampling by calculating loss across the entire item set." [section] "Fig. 1(c) visualizes the NDCG@10 scores of GRU4Rec on each timestamp... the GRU4Rec model trained with BCE loss optimizes all timestamps of the training sequence, which results in performance improvements over vanilla GRU4Rec on the validation and test data."

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: CCE loss improves training efficiency by eliminating the overhead of negative sampling operations.
- Mechanism: Instead of repeatedly sampling negatives for each timestamp, CCE computes loss using the full softmax distribution once per timestamp, reducing sampling-related computational overhead.
- Core assumption: Sampling negatives requires additional computation and introduces variance in training, while full softmax provides deterministic gradients.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "CCE loss that considers all timestamps and avoids negative sampling by calculating loss across the entire item set." [section] "Second, negative sampling is a widely-used approach to improve performance... increasing the number of negative samples will reduce the computational efficiency."

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input layer -> Embedding layer -> Model backbone (GRU4Rec/SASRec/S3Rec) -> Prediction layer -> Loss function (CCE) -> Masking layer

- Critical path:
  1. Sequence input → embeddings → model forward pass
  2. Output scores for all items at each timestamp
  3. Apply masking to ignore padding positions
  4. Compute CCE loss across all timestamps
  5. Backpropagate gradients through entire sequence

- Design tradeoffs:
  - CCE vs BCE: CCE avoids sampling but requires full softmax computation
  - Sequence length: Longer sequences increase CCE computation but provide more training signal
  - Item catalog size: Larger catalogs make full softmax expensive

- Failure signatures:
  - Training instability: May indicate numerical issues with large softmax computations
  - Poor convergence: Could suggest learning rate too high for CCE gradients
  - Memory issues: Full softmax over millions of items may exceed GPU memory

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replace BCE with CCE in SASRec on a small dataset (e.g., Beauty) and compare training curves
  2. Measure inference time per epoch with CCE vs BCE on medium-sized dataset
  3. Test CCE with different sequence lengths to find optimal trade-off between performance and computation

## Limitations
- Computational efficiency claims assume reasonable item catalog sizes; performance may degrade with extremely large catalogs where full softmax becomes prohibitive
- The method's effectiveness relies on the assumption that all timestamps in training sequences contain useful signal, which may not hold for noisy or very long sequences
- The paper does not extensively analyze the trade-off between sequence length and performance when using CCE

## Confidence
- High confidence: Performance improvements demonstrated across multiple datasets and models; the mechanism of avoiding negative sampling is straightforward and well-supported
- Medium confidence: Training efficiency claims - while theoretically sound, real-world performance depends heavily on specific hardware and catalog size
- Low confidence: The claim that CCE completely avoids the vanishing gradient problem - this needs more rigorous theoretical analysis, particularly for very large item sets
