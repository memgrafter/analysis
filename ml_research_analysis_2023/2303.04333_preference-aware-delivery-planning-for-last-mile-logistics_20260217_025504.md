# Preference-Aware Delivery Planning for Last-Mile Logistics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.04333
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04333
- Reference count: 32
- Primary result: Hierarchical routing with Bayesian-optimized weights achieves 50% improvement over baselines on Amazon dataset

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a hierarchical route optimization approach for last-mile logistics that incorporates driver preferences into delivery planning. The method combines a zone-level Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) with a stop-level Shortest Hamiltonian Path (SHP) problem, using Bayesian optimization to tune feature weights based on historical high-quality routes. Tested on a real-world Amazon dataset, the approach achieves an average score of 0.0496 on testing data, outperforming baselines by nearly 50%.

## Method Summary
The method uses a hierarchical route optimizer that first solves a zone-level TSP with learnable weights on zone features, then solves a stop-level SHP problem within each zone. Bayesian optimization adjusts the weights on zone features by modeling the relationship between feature weights and route similarity to historical high-quality routes. The approach uses a Savings algorithm for both the zone-level and stop-level problems, with the evaluation metric combining Sequence Distance (SD) and Earth Mover's Distance (ERP) to measure route similarity to benchmark routes.

## Key Results
- Achieved average score of 0.0496 on testing data, outperforming baselines by nearly 50%
- Identified route-related features that impact performance: actual travel time, package sizes, and travel time variations
- Tested on real-world Amazon dataset across multiple cities with consistent improvements

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Hierarchical decomposition (zone-level TSP → stop-level SHP) reduces problem complexity and aligns with driver behavior.
- Mechanism: By first fixing a sequence of zones, the full routing problem is split into two smaller TSP-like problems. This mirrors the observation that drivers naturally visit stops in zone blocks, which reduces the number of decisions the solver needs to make at once and focuses optimization on more tractable subproblems.
- Core assumption: Drivers' preferred routes tend to group stops by zones, and this grouping can be learned from historical data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our hierarchical router first solves a zone-level Traveling Salesman Problem with learnable weights on various zone-level features; with the zone visit sequence fixed, we then solve the stop-level vehicle routing problem as a Shortest Hamiltonian Path problem."
  - [section] "What we see from the comparison is strong visual evidence that drivers prefer to schedule their visits in blocks of zones, i.e., visiting all stops within a zone before moving on to the next zone."
- Break condition: If actual driver behavior does not cluster by zones (e.g., in very dispersed or mixed delivery areas), the zone-level simplification could misrepresent optimal routes and degrade performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Bayesian optimization tunes zone-level feature weights to reflect drivers' implicit preferences.
- Mechanism: A Gaussian process models the relationship between feature weights and route similarity to historical high-quality routes. The acquisition function (expected improvement) selects new weight combinations to minimize the average route score, thereby learning which zone features matter most to drivers.
- Core assumption: The quality of a route can be effectively modeled as a smooth function of feature weights, and sufficient historical high-quality routes exist to train this model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The Bayesian optimization approach is then introduced to allow us to adjust the weights to be assigned to different zone features used in solving the zone-level Traveling Salesman Problem."
  - [section] "We then utilize the Bayesian optimization approach (see Algorithm 1) to update the feature weights iteratively to minimize the average score."
- Break condition: If the objective function is highly discontinuous or noisy, the Gaussian process assumption fails, leading to poor weight updates and suboptimal routes.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using route similarity (SD + ERP) rather than pure travel cost aligns optimization with human preferences.
- Mechanism: The evaluation metric measures how closely a generated sequence matches a benchmark sequence in both order (SD) and travel distance (ERP). This encourages the optimizer to produce routes that "look like" drivers' choices, not just ones that are shortest in distance or time.
- Core assumption: High-quality historical routes are a valid proxy for preferred routes, and drivers' preferences are consistent enough to be captured by sequence similarity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "The evaluation of generated routes depends on how closely a generated route resembles a highly rated benchmark route from history."
  - [section] "The evaluation metric is whether a generated route closely resembles the highly-rated benchmark route."
- Break condition: If the historical high-quality routes are not representative of general driver preferences, or if driver preferences are highly individual, the similarity-based objective may misguide the optimizer.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Historical routes -> Zone-level TSP solver (with Bayesian-optimized weights) -> Stop-level SHP solver -> Route evaluation (SD + ERP) -> Feedback to Bayesian optimizer
- Critical path:
  1. Train Bayesian optimizer on high-quality historical routes
  2. Solve zone-level TSP with learned weights
  3. For each zone, solve stop-level SHP with appropriate start/end stops
  4. Evaluate and iterate if necessary
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Hierarchical routing reduces complexity but assumes zone-based clustering; if this doesn't hold, performance suffers
  - Bayesian optimization is sample-efficient but can be slow to converge; the Savings algorithm is fast but heuristic
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor route scores on testing data indicate either bad feature weights or invalid hierarchical assumptions
  - High variance in scores across cities suggests model doesn't generalize well to different layouts
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the HR-LP solver on a small, known route and verify zone ordering matches driver intuition
  2. Compare Bayesian-optimized weights to random weights to confirm improvement
  3. Evaluate route scores on a held-out city to check generalization

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal value of hyperparameter h (number of candidate starting/ending stops to enumerate) for maximizing route quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper tested h values of 2-6 and found minimal impact on outcomes
- Why unresolved: The study only tested a limited range of h values (2-6) and found minimal differences, but did not explore whether larger values might yield better results or identify an optimal value
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing a wider range of h values (e.g., 2-20) across diverse route instances to identify potential optimal values

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the hierarchical routing approach be extended to incorporate dynamic real-time updates to routes during delivery execution?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper presents a static hierarchical approach but doesn't address real-time route adjustments
- Why unresolved: The current method assumes fixed route planning without considering real-time changes like traffic conditions, new orders, or cancellations
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing a dynamic version of the hierarchical approach that can adapt to real-time changes while maintaining preference-aware routing

- Why unresolved: The study only considers a limited set of features and doesn't explore how additional practical constraints like time windows or customer preferences might impact route quality
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing the model with expanded feature sets including time windows, customer preferences, and other real-world constraints to measure performance impact

## Limitations
- Zone-based Hierarchical Assumption: The method assumes drivers naturally cluster deliveries by zone, but this may not hold in all geographies or delivery scenarios.
- Representative Benchmark Routes: The approach relies on historical high-quality routes as benchmarks, but their representativeness of broader driver preferences is uncertain.
- Generalizability Across Cities: While tested on four cities, the wide variation in performance suggests the model's results may depend heavily on local delivery patterns.

## Confidence
- Route Score Improvement (High): The reported 50% improvement over baselines and consistent score distribution on testing data provide strong evidence for the method's effectiveness on the given dataset.
- Bayesian Optimization Tuning (Medium): The use of Bayesian optimization is well-grounded in machine learning literature, but its success depends on the smoothness and structure of the objective function, which is not fully explored.
- Hierarchical Decomposition Benefit (Medium): The approach is logically sound and matches observed driver behavior in the dataset, but its advantage over non-hierarchical methods in different contexts is not proven.
