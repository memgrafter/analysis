# Guiding Text-to-Text Privatization by Syntax

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01471
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01471
- Reference count: 15
- One-line primary result: Improves downstream task performance by up to 4.66% while preserving differential privacy guarantees via syntactic guidance in text privatization

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of text-to-text privatization under metric differential privacy, where substituting words based on embedding proximity often results in grammatically incoherent surrogate texts. The authors find that privatized texts are dominated by nouns and lack syntactic structure. To remedy this, they propose directing substitutions to words matching the original word's grammatical category, effectively turning privatization into a constrained candidate selection problem. Experiments on GLUE tasks demonstrate significant performance gains without substantially weakening privacy guarantees.

## Method Summary
The method extends metric differential privacy text privatization by incorporating grammatical category constraints during word substitution. After perturbing word embeddings with multivariate Laplace noise (MADLIB mechanism), instead of selecting the nearest neighbor in the embedding space, the method searches among the k=20 nearest neighbors for a word sharing the same universal POS tag. This syntactic guidance prevents implausible substitutions (e.g., adjectives replaced by numerals) and preserves sentence structure. The vocabulary consists of 24,525 words with 11 grammatical categories, using 100-dimensional GloVe embeddings, and evaluation is performed on IMDb and GLUE benchmark tasks.

## Key Results
- Performance improvement: Up to 4.66% gain in downstream BERT classification accuracy on GLUE tasks by constraining substitutions to matching grammatical categories.
- Privacy preservation: Marginal changes to plausible deniability statistics (Nw and Sw) indicate grammatical constraints do not significantly weaken differential privacy guarantees.
- Syntactic coherence: Prevents implausible substitutions like "before" being replaced by "1979" by enforcing grammatical consistency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The proposed method improves downstream task performance by directing substitutions toward words with matching grammatical categories, rather than only relying on semantic proximity in embedding space.
- Mechanism: The mechanism generalizes the nearest-neighbor search from k=1 to k=20 and selects the nearest neighbor whose grammatical category matches the original word. This constraint increases the likelihood that substitutions preserve syntactic roles.
- Core assumption: Maintaining grammatical consistency in substitutions leads to better preservation of sentence structure and therefore higher performance in downstream tasks like classification and similarity scoring.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We demonstrate a substantial improvement in the performance of downstream tasks by up to 4.66% while retaining comparative privacy guarantees."
  - [section 4.4] "By broadening the candidate pool to k = 20 and directing the substitution to words with matching grammatical categories, BERT trained on surrogate texts ranks at 60.11%. This corresponds to narrowing down the performance loss by 4.66%."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct citations or empirical comparisons in the neighbor papers to this exact mechanism.
- Break condition: If the candidate pool k is too small to contain a word of the matching grammatical category, the mechanism reverts to unrestricted nearest-neighbor substitution, potentially degrading syntactic coherence.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The grammatical constraint does not significantly compromise differential privacy guarantees.
- Mechanism: By keeping the distance metric and noise calibration identical to the baseline, and only restricting the candidate selection to matching grammatical categories, the privacy budget ε remains effectively the same.
- Core assumption: Restricting candidate selection does not increase the probability of self-substitution or reduce the effective support enough to noticeably weaken plausible deniability.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3] "Directing the substitution to words with a matching grammatical category result in marginal changes to the plausible deniability."
  - [section 4.3] "This is visually recognizable by the distribution shift. The grammatical constraint risks slightly more self-substitutions and reduced effective support."
  - [corpus] Weak - neighboring papers focus on LDP or semantic bias, not the privacy analysis of grammatical constraints in metric DP.
- Break condition: If the vocabulary distribution is highly skewed toward certain grammatical categories, the candidate pool may often lack matching words, increasing self-substitution and reducing effective support.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The method prevents implausible substitutions such as replacing adjectives with numerals, which improves semantic and syntactic coherence.
- Mechanism: The lookup table of grammatical categories ensures that words like "before" (ADP) are not replaced by "1979" (NUM), preserving syntactic roles that embeddings alone cannot guarantee.
- Core assumption: Some grammatical mismatches are more harmful to downstream task performance than others; specifically, category mismatches involving numbers or punctuation are particularly damaging.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1] "We note that our syntactic guidance prevents words from being misleadingly replaced by numbers (and vice versa), as in the case of before being replaced by 1979."
  - [section 3] "We find that privatization produces texts that consist to a large extent of incoherent nouns."
  - [corpus] Weak - neighboring papers do not directly address category-specific substitution failures in DP text privatization.
- Break condition: If the embedding space does not cluster words by grammatical category (e.g., if embeddings are poorly trained), the grammatical constraint may fail to find suitable candidates within the candidate pool.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Universal part-of-speech tagging
  - Why needed here: The method relies on mapping each word to a grammatical category to guide substitutions; without accurate POS tagging, the constraint cannot function.
  - Quick check question: Given the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps", what POS tags would you assign to each word using the universal tagset?

- Concept: Multivariate Laplace mechanism for metric differential privacy
  - Why needed here: The privatization step adds noise calibrated by the embedding distance metric; understanding this mechanism is essential to reason about privacy guarantees and noise levels.
  - Quick check question: If a word's embedding is perturbed by noise sampled from a multivariate Laplace distribution, how does the magnitude of the noise relate to the privacy budget ε?

- Concept: Nearest-neighbor search in high-dimensional spaces
  - Why needed here: After noise addition, the method must project the noisy vector back to a discrete vocabulary via nearest-neighbor search; efficiency and correctness depend on understanding this search.
  - Quick check question: In a 100-dimensional GloVe embedding, how does increasing the candidate pool size k from 1 to 20 affect the probability of finding a word with the same grammatical category?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Embedding lookup → Noise injection (multivariate Laplace) → Nearest-neighbor search with grammatical constraint → Output surrogate text.
- Critical path: Embedding → Noise → Candidate selection → Substitution.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Larger k improves chances of finding grammatically matching words but increases computation and may reduce privacy guarantees slightly.
  - Using universal POS tags simplifies the constraint but may misclassify words without context, weakening the constraint's effectiveness.
- Failure signatures:
  - If downstream task performance does not improve, suspect either the candidate pool is too small or the POS tagger is inaccurate.
  - If privacy metrics show degradation, suspect the grammatical constraint is too restrictive relative to the vocabulary distribution.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run the baseline (k=1) and constrained (k=20) methods on a small validation set and compare both downstream task accuracy and grammatical category preservation.
  2. Vary k from 1 to 50 and plot the trade-off between performance gain and self-substitution rate.
  3. Replace the universal POS tagger with a context-aware tagger and measure the change in substitution quality and downstream performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the precise impact of the grammatical constraint on the privacy budget when considering information leakage beyond the effective support and self-substitution statistics?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the grammatical constraint may incur additional information leakage not accounted for by the current privacy analysis, but does not quantify this leakage.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides approximate statistics for plausible deniability (Nw and Sw) and acknowledges that the constraint may introduce unaccounted information leakage, but does not perform a more rigorous privacy analysis to measure this leakage.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal privacy analysis, such as using the exponential mechanism, that quantifies the additional information leakage introduced by the grammatical constraint and compares it to the baseline mechanism.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance gain from the grammatical constraint vary across different types of downstream tasks beyond sentiment analysis and the GLUE benchmark?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper focuses on sentiment analysis and GLUE tasks, but acknowledges that the preservation of syntactic roles may be relevant to a variety of downstream tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates the performance gain on a limited set of downstream tasks, leaving open the question of how the constraint generalizes to other tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments evaluating the performance gain on a wider range of downstream tasks, such as text summarization, question answering, and machine translation, to determine the generalizability of the grammatical constraint.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the size of the candidate pool k affect the trade-off between privacy and utility, and what is the optimal value of k for different privacy budgets and downstream tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a fixed candidate pool size of k=20, but acknowledges that k should be chosen based on the geometric properties of the embedding.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the impact of varying k on the trade-off between privacy and utility, nor does it provide guidance on how to choose an optimal k for different scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments varying k across a range of values and privacy budgets, and evaluating the impact on both privacy (e.g., using formal privacy metrics) and utility (e.g., downstream task performance) to determine the optimal k for different use cases.

## Limitations
- Lack of direct comparison with other privacy-preserving text generation methods, especially those using semantic or contextual constraints.
- Privacy analysis relies on approximate statistics (Nw, Sw) rather than rigorous formal bounds, leaving potential information leakage from the grammatical constraint unaccounted for.
- Universal POS tagging without context may introduce systematic substitution errors for words with multiple possible grammatical roles.

## Confidence

- High confidence: The mechanism of improving downstream task performance via grammatical constraint selection (4.66% improvement) is well-supported by quantitative results and reproducible across GLUE tasks.
- Medium confidence: The claim that grammatical constraints do not significantly compromise differential privacy guarantees is supported by marginal changes in plausible deniability statistics, but lacks formal privacy accounting.
- Low confidence: The assertion that category mismatches involving numbers or punctuation are particularly harmful is based on anecdotal examples rather than systematic ablation studies across grammatical categories.
