# Pre-processing training data improves accuracy and generalisability of convolutional neural network based landscape semantic segmentation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14625
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14625
- Reference count: 0
- The study found that stratified random sampling substantially increased accuracy for small classes in imbalanced datasets, and that data augmentation and scaling were imperative to avoid overfitting and improve model generalization and transferability.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how data preprocessing techniques affect the accuracy and generalizability of U-Net convolutional neural networks for semantic segmentation of land use/land cover (LULC) features in high-resolution aerial imagery. The research systematically evaluates different training patch generation methods, data augmentations, and prediction strategies to optimize model performance, particularly for underrepresented classes in imbalanced datasets. By applying these optimized preprocessing steps, the final model achieved high accuracy metrics (kappa of 0.84, user accuracy of 0.81, and producer accuracy of 0.87) when applied to imagery from a different date and sensor.

## Method Summary
The study employs a U-Net CNN architecture for multi-class semantic segmentation of LULC features in RGB aerial imagery. The method involves generating training patches using stratified random sampling based on class area, applying data augmentations (flips, rotations, contrast changes, noise, geometric distortions, blur, and artificial clouds), and scaling pixel values. The model is trained on 512x512 pixel patches with batch size 20, and predictions are generated using a multiple-pass strategy with patch rotations and weighted averaging. The approach is validated on test imagery from a different date and sensor to assess transferability.

## Key Results
- Stratified random sampling improved accuracy for underrepresented classes, with 512x512 pixel patches showing the highest kappa and F1-score.
- Data augmentations and scaling increased kappa by >0.24 and F1-score by 0.19, improving model generalization and transferability.
- Multiple-pass prediction with patch rotations and weighted averaging produced more accurate and aesthetically pleasing classifications.
- The final optimized model achieved kappa of 0.84, user accuracy of 0.81, and producer accuracy of 0.87 on unseen data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Stratified random sampling based on class area improves accuracy for underrepresented classes.
- Mechanism: By allocating training patches proportional to the log area of each class, smaller classes receive more training samples relative to their spatial extent, improving model learning for these classes.
- Core assumption: The log transformation adequately balances the representation of very small classes without oversampling.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "a stratified random sampling approach for producing training patches improved the accuracy of classes with a smaller area"
  - [section] "The stratified random sampling method showed the 512 x 512 pixel patch size produced higher accuracy for both the kappa statistic and F1-Score compared to other patch sizes."
- Break condition: If the log transformation fails to balance representation, extremely small classes may still be under-sampled.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Data augmentations improve model generalization and transferability to unseen data from different sensors and dates.
- Mechanism: Random augmentations (contrast changes, noise, geometric distortions, blur, artificial clouds) simulate real-world variations in imagery, forcing the model to learn robust features rather than overfitting to specific training conditions.
- Core assumption: The augmentations chosen (contrast, noise, geometry, blur, clouds) are representative of real-world variations in remote sensing imagery.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "applying data augmentations and scaling are imperative in creating a generalised model able to accurately classify LULC features in imagery from a different date and sensor"
  - [section] "There was a >0.24 increase in kappa and 0.19 increase in F1-Score" when comparing models with and without augmentations.
- Break condition: If augmentations are too aggressive, they may create unrealistic training samples that degrade model performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multiple-pass prediction with patch rotations and weighted averaging produces more accurate and aesthetically pleasing classifications.
- Mechanism: By averaging predictions from multiple rotated versions of each patch and applying a second pass offset by half a patch, the method reduces edge effects and captures more contextual information, leading to smoother and more accurate outputs.
- Core assumption: The weighted averaging based on distance from patch center effectively combines multiple predictions without introducing bias.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "producing the output classification by averaging multiple grids of patches and three rotated versions of each patch produced and more accurate and aesthetic result"
  - [section] "the multiple pass classification is more aesthetic with the elimination of patch edge effects"
- Break condition: If the offset and weighting scheme is poorly chosen, it may introduce artifacts or reduce accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the inclusion of additional LULC classes affect model accuracy and training time compared to training separate models for each class?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions this as an outstanding question in the conclusion, noting that future work should analyze the effect on model accuracy with additional LULC classes and compare training separate models for each class versus one model for all classes.
- Why unresolved: The study was limited to eight LULC classes due to time restrictions, preventing analysis of the impact of additional classes on accuracy and training time.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training and evaluating models with additional LULC classes beyond the eight studied, comparing the accuracy and training time of a single multi-class model versus separate models for each class.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of augmented versions of each patch to use when generating output classifications through multiple-pass prediction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests this as an area for future research, noting that they did not examine if averaging patch classifications with three augmented versions has any advantage over one or two augmented versions.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested averaging three augmented versions of each patch, without comparing the results to using fewer or more augmentations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing the accuracy and efficiency of output classifications generated using different numbers of augmented patch versions, such as one, two, three, and more.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can additional ancillary data like climate, elevation, and soil information be integrated to improve LULC classification accuracy, especially over broad areas?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper suggests integrating additional ancillary data as a future research direction, noting that such data may assist in LULC prediction, particularly over broad areas.
- Why unresolved: The study only used aerial imagery data for classification and did not explore the potential benefits of incorporating additional contextual information.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training and evaluating models that incorporate various types and combinations of ancillary data, such as climate, elevation, and soil information, and comparing their accuracy to models using only aerial imagery data.

## Limitations

- The study focused on a single geographic region with specific crop types and spatial configurations, limiting generalizability to different biomes or land cover patterns.
- The reliance on manually digitized training data, while providing high-quality labels, limits scalability and introduces potential human bias.
- Computational optimization was achieved through brute-force testing of patch sizes and batch numbers, which may not identify globally optimal configurations.
