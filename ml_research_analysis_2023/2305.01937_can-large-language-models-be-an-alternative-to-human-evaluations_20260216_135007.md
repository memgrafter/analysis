# Can Large Language Models Be an Alternative to Human Evaluations?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.01937
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01937
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: LLM evaluation using text-davinci-003 and ChatGPT produces results consistent with expert human evaluation for text quality assessment.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can serve as an alternative to human evaluation for assessing text quality in NLP tasks. The authors propose presenting LLMs with the same instructions, samples, and questions used in human evaluation, and parsing their generated responses as ratings. Experiments on story generation and adversarial attack tasks demonstrate that LLM evaluation produces results consistent with expert human judgments, while offering advantages in reproducibility and cost. The approach leverages zero-shot in-context learning capabilities of LLMs to perform evaluation tasks without fine-tuning.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune GPT-2 on the WritingPrompts dataset to generate stories, then collect human evaluation from English teachers on story quality attributes. They present identical evaluation prompts to LLMs (text-davinci-003, ChatGPT, T0) and parse the generated responses to extract numeric ratings. The LLM ratings are compared to human ratings using statistical measures like Kendall's τ correlation. The evaluation process involves preparing prompts with task instructions and samples, sending them to LLMs with various sampling parameters, parsing the outputs, and analyzing the correlation with human judgments.

## Key Results
- LLM evaluation using text-davinci-003 and ChatGPT produces ratings consistent with expert human evaluation for story quality.
- Despite small variations, LLM evaluation remains stable across different instructions and sampling parameters.
- ChatGPT can rank adversarial text quality similarly to human experts, though it tends to be less harsh on unnatural adversarial samples.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LLMs can reproduce human evaluation outcomes when given identical task instructions, samples, and questions.
- Mechanism: LLMs perform zero-shot in-context learning by conditioning on the provided evaluation setup and generating a rating response, which is then parsed to extract a numeric score.
- Core assumption: The LLM's internal representations capture the same qualitative dimensions that human evaluators use.
- Evidence anchors: LLM ratings are consistent with human expert ratings for story quality; text-davinci-003 rates human-written stories higher than model-generated stories.
- Break condition: If the LLM lacks exposure to relevant linguistic features or instructions are ambiguous, ratings will diverge from human judgments.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The variance in LLM evaluation results is small when changing task instructions or sampling parameters.
- Mechanism: Minor variations in wording or sampling temperature do not materially alter the relative ranking of text quality, preserving evaluation validity.
- Core assumption: The LLM's rating distribution is stable enough that small perturbations do not flip qualitative judgments.
- Evidence anchors: Small variations due to different instructions do not change the conclusion that LLMs rate human-written stories higher; average ratings slightly drop with lower temperature but preference order remains.
- Break condition: If instructions introduce strong personal bias or temperature is set too low, relative rankings could be affected.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLM evaluation can be used to rank adversarial text quality similarly to human experts.
- Mechanism: LLMs assess adversarial examples on fluency and meaning preservation by comparing benign and perturbed text, generating scores that align with human quality judgments.
- Core assumption: The LLM can detect semantic shifts and fluency issues introduced by synonym substitution attacks.
- Evidence anchors: ChatGPT rates adversarial samples lower than benign samples and agrees with English teachers on relative quality of different adversarial attack methods.
- Break condition: If adversarial perturbations preserve fluency but change meaning imperceptibly to the LLM, or if the LLM is overly lenient, rankings may not match human evaluations.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Zero-shot in-context learning
  - Why needed here: The entire approach relies on the LLM's ability to perform evaluation tasks without fine-tuning, just by conditioning on the task description.
  - Quick check question: Can the LLM generate a coherent rating when given only the instruction and sample, without any parameter updates?

- Concept: Likert scale interpretation
  - Why needed here: The LLM must map qualitative textual output to a discrete numeric rating that can be parsed and compared to human scores.
  - Quick check question: Does the parsing rule reliably extract the intended numeric value from the LLM's generated text across multiple samples?

- Concept: Inter-annotator agreement metrics
  - Why needed here: To quantify the consistency of LLM-generated ratings and compare them to human agreement levels.
  - Quick check question: How does the LLM's rating variance across multiple generations compare to the variance among human evaluators on the same sample?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input layer (instructions, sample, question) -> LLM core (zero-shot in-context evaluation) -> Parser (numeric rating extraction) -> Comparison module (statistical analysis against human ratings) -> Output (numeric score and correlation metrics)

- Critical path: 1. Prepare evaluation prompt with instruction, sample, and question 2. Send prompt to LLM with sampling parameters 3. Parse LLM's generated response to extract numeric rating 4. Compare parsed ratings to human ratings using statistical measures

- Design tradeoffs: Using free-form generation allows flexibility but requires robust parsing rules; structured classification would be more stable but less adaptable. Sampling multiple responses per sample increases reliability but also cost and latency. Parsing based on regex is fast but brittle to unexpected output formats.

- Failure signatures: Parser fails to extract a number → output is discarded or default value used. LLM refuses to answer or generates off-topic text → no valid rating obtained. High variance across repeated generations → indicates instability in rating.

- First 3 experiments: 1. Run LLM evaluation on a small set of stories with known human ratings; verify that mean and std of LLM scores match human patterns. 2. Vary the sampling temperature (T=0.3, 0.7, 1.0) on the same sample; observe changes in ratings and IAA. 3. Modify the evaluation question slightly (e.g., add persona prompt); check if relative rankings of text quality change.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the relative ranking of LLM evaluation results remain consistent when using different LLMs on the same task?
- Basis in paper: The paper demonstrates that text-davinci-003 and ChatGPT both rate human-written stories higher than GPT-2-generated stories, but their absolute rating scales differ. Different LLMs do not consistently prefer human-written stories.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not systematically compare the relative rankings of multiple LLMs across a wider range of tasks and text qualities.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study comparing the relative rankings of multiple LLMs across diverse NLP tasks and text qualities would clarify the consistency of LLM evaluation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the training data and fine-tuning process of LLMs impact their performance on LLM evaluation tasks?
- Basis in paper: The paper uses different LLMs with varying training processes (e.g., fine-tuning on task mixtures, reinforcement learning from human feedback), and performance differences suggest training influences evaluation ability.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the relationship between LLM training data, fine-tuning methods, and their performance on evaluation tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: A controlled study comparing LLMs with different training data compositions and fine-tuning strategies on standardized evaluation tasks would reveal the impact of training on evaluation performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can LLM evaluation be used to assess the quality of texts in languages other than English, and how does performance vary across languages?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on English texts and English-speaking evaluators, while multilingual LLMs like BLOOM exist but are not explored for evaluation tasks.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not address the generalizability of LLM evaluation to other languages or whether performance extends to languages with different linguistic structures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating the same texts using multilingual LLMs and human evaluators proficient in those languages would reveal performance across different languages and highlight any language-specific challenges.

## Limitations
- Dependence on LLM's ability to generalize evaluation criteria from human instructions without explicit fine-tuning.
- Parsing mechanism for extracting numeric ratings from free-form LLM output introduces brittleness and potential for incorrect or missing ratings.
- Limited to specific tasks tested (story generation and adversarial attack detection), leaving generalizability to other NLP tasks unproven.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The core finding that LLM evaluation can reproduce human expert ratings for story quality assessment is well-supported by statistical analysis.
- Medium confidence: The claim about stability across different instructions and sampling parameters is supported by experimental results but limited to narrow variations.
- Low confidence: The generalizability of LLM evaluation to arbitrary NLP tasks beyond the two tested domains remains unproven.

## Next Checks
1. Test the LLM evaluation approach on a third, distinct NLP task (e.g., summarization quality) to assess generalizability beyond story generation and adversarial attack detection.
2. Conduct a human evaluation study where evaluators are blind to whether ratings come from humans or LLMs, to test if LLM ratings are indistinguishable from human judgments.
3. Implement and test multiple parsing strategies (e.g., structured output format vs. regex extraction) to quantify the impact of parsing methodology on rating reliability.
