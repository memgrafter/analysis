# Towards Measuring and Scoring Speaker Diarization Fairness

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.09991
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09991
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Introduced first fairness protocol for speaker diarization, finding 5% gender bias and accent disparities using DFR metric

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the first fairness protocol and metric for speaker diarization systems. The authors apply a state-of-the-art pipeline to single-speaker utterances from CommonVoice and measure the probability of detecting 0, 1, or multiple speakers across demographic groups. They define a Diarization Fairness Rate (DFR) that isolates clustering bias from voice activity detection errors. Results reveal systematic biases: male voices are 5% more likely to be correctly diarized than female voices, and African/Singaporean accents show lower fairness scores. Short utterances frequently go undetected.

## Method Summary
The authors developed a fairness evaluation protocol that applies a standard speaker diarization pipeline to single-speaker utterances from the CommonVoice dataset. The pipeline consists of voice activity detection (C-RNN), x-vector embedding extraction (trained on VoxCeleb and CnCeleb), and Bayesian HMM clustering. For each utterance, they record whether the system detects zero, one, or multiple speakers. These probabilities (p₀, p₁, p₊) are calculated for each demographic group and used to compute the Diarization Fairness Rate: DFR = p₁ / (1 - p₀ - p₊). This metric normalizes for overall system performance while isolating demographic bias in the clustering stage.

## Key Results
- Gender bias: 5% more male voices correctly diarized than female voices
- Accent bias: African and Singaporean accents show significantly lower DFR scores
- Utterance length: Short utterances (<10 characters) frequently undetected due to VAD failure
- Protocol validation: DFR successfully isolates clustering bias from voice activity detection errors

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The protocol measures fairness by comparing correct speaker detection probability across demographic groups
- Mechanism: Applies diarization pipeline to single-speaker utterances, tracking 0, 1, or ≥2 speaker detections broken down by demographics
- Core assumption: Single-speaker utterances provide controlled setting where deviations from detecting exactly one speaker are measurable errors
- Break condition: Imbalanced dataset or violated single-speaker assumption may not reflect real-world fairness gaps

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: DFR normalizes for overall performance while isolating demographic bias
- Mechanism: DFR = p₁ / (1 - p₀ - p₊) measures correct detection proportion among cases where system outputs speaker
- Core assumption: Voice activity detection and clustering contribute independently to errors
- Break condition: Large p₀ or p₊ for certain groups makes DFR unstable; biased VAD invalidates separation assumption

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Controlling for utterance length and speaker identity reduces confounding factors
- Mechanism: Single-speaker utterances remove speaker turn complexity and overlapping speech
- Core assumption: Multi-speaker conversations introduce variability that obscures demographic bias
- Break condition: Real-world diarization differs significantly from single-speaker settings, limiting generalizability

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Utterance → Voice Activity Detection (C-RNN) → X-vector Embedding Extractor → Bayesian HMM Clustering → Speaker count
- Critical path: Utterance → VAD → Embeddings → Clustering → Speaker count
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single-speaker utterances simplify bias measurement but may not reflect real-world complexity
  - Fixed open-source pipeline ensures reproducibility but may not be optimal for all accents/genders
  - DFR metric is interpretable but unstable when p₀ or p₊ are large
- Failure signatures:
  - High p₀ indicates VAD failure (e.g., short utterances)
  - High p₊ suggests clustering errors (e.g., male voices misclustered as multiple speakers)
  - Low DFR for a group indicates systematic underperformance
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce gender bias result using provided CommonVoice subset and baseline pipeline
  2. Measure DFR for short (<10 char) vs. long (>100 char) utterances to confirm VAD bias
  3. Swap VAD component with alternative (e.g., WebRTC VAD) and observe changes in p₀ and DFR across groups

- Question: How can fairness evaluation protocols for speaker diarization be adapted to handle conversations with multiple speakers, rather than just single-speaker utterances?

- Why unresolved: Current protocol designed for single-speaker utterances to minimize variability, limiting real-world applicability
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of fairness protocol for multi-speaker conversations demonstrating ability to identify/quantify biases in datasets with labeled multi-speaker interactions

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are specific contributions of VAD, embedding extraction, and clustering components to observed biases, and how can these be improved to enhance fairness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Protocol results may vary with specific VAD and clustering systems; bias origins could be linked to training data or physiological differences
- Why unresolved: Paper doesn't analyze how each component contributes to biases or explore potential improvements
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive study isolating impact of each diarization component on fairness, followed by development/testing of improved components that reduce biases

### Open Question 3

- Why unresolved: Paper contains no experiments or results related to audio-visual speaker diarization
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental comparison of fairness metrics between audio-only and audio-visual systems demonstrating whether visual cues reduce biases related to gender, age, accent, or utterance length

## Limitations
- Protocol relies on single-speaker utterances which may not capture real-world diarization challenges with overlapping speech
- Observed biases are statistically significant within CommonVoice but may not generalize to other datasets or acoustic conditions
- DFR metric becomes unstable when p₀ or p₊ are large for certain groups, potentially masking or exaggerating bias effects

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Measurement methodology (tracking p₀, p₁, p₊ across demographic groups) is sound and reproducible
- **Medium confidence**: DFR metric meaningfully isolates clustering bias from VAD errors, though edge cases exist
- **Medium confidence**: Observed gender and accent biases are real within tested conditions, but magnitude may vary with different systems or datasets
