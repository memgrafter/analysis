# A Survey on Explainability of Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.01958
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01958
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Comprehensive survey categorizing GNN explainability methods into factual and counterfactual approaches with applications across multiple domains

## Executive Summary
This survey provides a systematic overview of explainability methods for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), addressing the critical need to interpret complex non-linear GNN models. The paper categorizes existing methods into factual (self-interpretable and post-hoc) and counterfactual approaches, with detailed taxonomies and discussions of their strengths and limitations. It covers various techniques including decomposition-based, gradient-based, surrogate, perturbation-based, and generation-based methods, along with special categories like temporal GNNs and causality-based explainers. The survey also discusses applications in computer security, social networks, computational biology, and chemistry, while providing insights into evaluation metrics and future research directions.

## Method Summary
The survey methodology involves comprehensive literature review and systematic categorization of existing GNN explainability methods. The authors organized methods into a taxonomy based on their fundamental approach: factual methods that explain predictions without altering them, and counterfactual methods that identify minimal input changes to flip predictions. For each category, they provide detailed descriptions of individual methods, their mechanisms, strengths, and limitations. The survey also includes discussions on evaluation metrics, widely used datasets, and application domains to provide a holistic view of the current state of GNN explainability research.

## Key Results
- Comprehensive taxonomy of GNN explainability methods organized into factual and counterfactual approaches
- Detailed analysis of various techniques including decomposition-based, gradient-based, surrogate, perturbation-based, and generation-based methods
- Discussion of special categories such as temporal GNNs, global explainers, and causality-based explainers
- Coverage of applications across computer security, social networks, computational biology, and chemistry domains

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Factual explainers separate explanation generation from model training to maintain black-box compatibility.
- Mechanism: Post-hoc methods treat the GNN as a fixed function and optimize a separate explainer architecture to extract subgraphs or node importance scores without altering the original model weights.
- Core assumption: The GNN's prediction behavior is stable and does not depend on training-time interventions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Post-hoc methods do not have the explainable architecture inbuilt into the model to attribute a model's prediction to the input."
  - [section] "Post-hoc methods do not have the explainable architecture inbuilt into the model to attribute a model's prediction to the input."
- Break condition: If the GNN requires continuous adaptation during inference, the fixed black-box assumption fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Self-interpretable explainers integrate explanation extraction into the model's forward pass via information or structural constraints.
- Mechanism: The model learns to produce both a prediction and an interpretable subgraph by jointly optimizing for task loss and a constraint (e.g., mutual information bottleneck or cardinality limit).
- Core assumption: The explanation can be encoded in the same forward computation without harming prediction accuracy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "In self-interpretable methods, the explainable procedure is intrinsic to the model... Both modules are trained together with an objective L(f ◦ g(G), Y) to minimize the loss between prediction f ◦ g(G) and label Y."
  - [abstract] "In self-interpretable methods, the subgraph extraction module (the function g) uses constraints to find an informative subgraph Gs from the input graph G."
- Break condition: If the added explanation constraint introduces too much inductive bias, model performance degrades.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Counterfactual explainers find minimal input changes that flip predictions, enabling contrastive reasoning.
- Mechanism: Perturbation-based counterfactual methods apply a mask to edges or node features and search for the smallest change that causes a label shift, using loss functions that balance prediction change and perturbation size.
- Core assumption: Small, well-defined perturbations are sufficient to produce meaningful alternative predictions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Counterfactual methods provide an explanation by identifying the minimal alteration in the input graph that results in a change in the model's prediction."
  - [section] "Perturbation-based methods apply a mask to edges or node features and search for the smallest change that causes a label shift."
- Break condition: If the model's decision boundary is highly non-linear or noisy, small perturbations may not yield consistent label changes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input graph G(V, E, X) → Explainer module → Explanation subgraph E or node/edge mask
  - Explainer module categories:
    - Decomposition-based: needs model weights
    - Gradient-based: needs gradient computation
    - Surrogate: builds local dataset + interpretable model
    - Perturbation-based: samples subgraphs via masks
    - Generation-based: learns to generate explanations
    - Self-interpretable: trains joint prediction + explanation
    - Counterfactual: searches minimal changes to flip prediction

- Critical path: For perturbation-based explainers, the sequence is: mask generation → subgraph extraction → model prediction on masked graph → scoring function → backpropagate to refine mask.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Black-box vs white-box: White-box methods need internal weights but can be more precise; black-box methods are more flexible but may miss subtle internal patterns.
  - Global vs local: Global explainers provide high-level insights but may miss instance-specific details; local explainers are precise but not scalable.
  - Perturbation vs generation: Perturbation-based methods can be faster but risk invalid graph structures; generation-based methods can enforce validity but are computationally heavier.

- Failure signatures:
  - Decomposition-based: Poor explanations if model uses non-linear feature interactions not captured by layer-wise relevance propagation.
  - Gradient-based: Vanishing gradients or saturated activations lead to misleading importance scores.
  - Surrogate: If local dataset is too sparse or unrepresentative, surrogate model fails to approximate true decision boundary.
  - Perturbation: Over-aggressive masking may yield invalid or trivial subgraphs.
  - Generation: Mode collapse or invalid graph generation if constraints are too loose.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we effectively extend explainability methods for GNNs to handle combinatorial optimization problems on graphs, such as node ranking or graph matching, where the prediction task is not classification but rather a structured output?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions this as a future direction, noting that while GNNs are increasingly used for combinatorial optimization problems, their explainability is largely missing.
- Why unresolved: Combinatorial optimization problems involve complex, structured outputs that differ significantly from standard classification tasks. Existing explanation methods are primarily designed for classification, and adapting them to explain solutions for optimization problems requires new approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating novel explanation methods that can effectively interpret the predictions of GNNs for combinatorial optimization problems on graphs, such as providing insights into why a particular solution was chosen or how the model arrived at it.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the most effective strategies for designing global explanation methods for GNNs that can provide high-level insights across large datasets, considering the challenges of human cognitive limitations in processing numerous individual explanations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the need for global explanation methods to complement local explanations and mentions the challenge of human cognitive limitations when dealing with large datasets.
- Why unresolved: Global explanation methods aim to capture common patterns across a dataset, but designing them effectively requires balancing the need for generality with the preservation of important local details. Additionally, ensuring that these explanations are easily understandable by humans is crucial.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies evaluating different global explanation methods on large-scale graph datasets, assessing their ability to capture meaningful patterns and provide insights that are both informative and interpretable by humans.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can we design explanation methods for temporal GNNs that can effectively capture the complex interplay between graph structures and their evolution over time, especially for continuous-time dynamic graphs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies temporal GNNs as a special category and mentions the need for future work on explaining continuous-time dynamic graphs.
- Why unresolved: Temporal GNNs operate on graphs that evolve over time, making their explanations inherently more complex. Existing methods primarily focus on discrete-time models, and extending them to continuous-time scenarios requires new techniques to capture the dynamic nature of the data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating explanation methods specifically designed for temporal GNNs that can effectively handle continuous-time dynamic graphs, providing insights into how the model's predictions are influenced by the temporal evolution of the graph structure.

## Limitations
- The survey's taxonomy may not capture all emerging approaches that combine multiple explanation paradigms
- Evaluation relies heavily on proxy metrics that may not fully capture human interpretability
- Focus is primarily on supervised GNN settings, potentially overlooking unsupervised or reinforcement learning contexts

## Confidence
- Taxonomy framework (High): The categorization of explainability methods into self-interpretable, post-hoc, and counterfactual approaches is well-grounded in the literature and consistently applied throughout the survey
- Mechanism descriptions (Medium): While the survey provides detailed explanations of various methods, some implementation details and hyperparameter choices are not fully specified, limiting reproducibility
- Evaluation criteria (Medium): The discussion of evaluation metrics is comprehensive, but the relative importance and practical utility of different metrics could be more explicitly addressed
- Application coverage (High): The survey demonstrates strong coverage of applications across computer security, social networks, computational biology, and chemistry domains
