# Neuron to Graph: Interpreting Language Model Neurons at Scale

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19911
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19911
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: N2G builds graph representations that more accurately predict neuron activations than baseline methods across all layers of a 6-layer Transformer model

## Executive Summary
Neuron to Graph (N2G) is an automated approach for interpreting neurons in large language models by converting their behavior into interpretable graphs. The method extracts highly activating examples, prunes irrelevant context, identifies key tokens via saliency methods, and augments the dataset with similar examples to create a graph representation. These graphs can be visualized for human interpretation and used to generate predicted activations for automatic validation. N2G scales to build representations for all neurons in a 6-layer Transformer model using a single GPU, enabling systematic neuron-level interpretability research at scale.

## Method Summary
N2G takes a target neuron and its highly activating examples as input, then applies a series of processing steps to build an interpretable graph. The method first identifies the minimal context needed for activation through pruning, then computes token saliency to determine importance weights. It augments the dataset by generating similar examples through token substitutions using DistilBERT, and finally constructs a trie-based graph representation. The resulting graph enables both visualization of neuron behavior and generation of predicted activations on new text for automated validation.

## Key Results
- N2G's graph representations achieved higher F1 scores than baseline methods across all layers of a 6-layer Transformer model
- The method showed particularly strong performance in early layers where neuron behavior is simpler and more consistent
- N2G successfully scaled to build graph representations for all neurons in the model using a single Tesla T4 GPU in approximately 48 hours

- Mechanism: N2G extracts highly activating examples, prunes irrelevant context, identifies important tokens via saliency, and augments with similar examples to create a graph. The graph structure enables both human visualization and automated activation prediction.
- Core assumption: A neuron's behavior can be approximated by analyzing its activations on a finite set of examples and that the resulting graph structure captures the neuron's essential activation patterns.

- Claim: The graph representation enables both human interpretation and automated analysis.
- Mechanism: The graph can be visualized with activating tokens in red and context tokens in blue, allowing human inspection. It can also be used to predict token activations on new text, enabling automated validation by comparing predicted activations to ground truth neuron activations.
- Core assumption: The graph structure is sufficient to encode the neuron's behavior such that it can accurately predict activations on unseen text.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "These graphs can be visualised to aid researchers' manual interpretation, and can generate token activations on text for automatic validation by comparison with the neuron's ground truth activations."

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input -> Pruning -> Saliency Identification -> Augmentation -> Graph Building -> Output
- Critical path: The most critical steps are the pruning and saliency identification, as they directly impact the quality of the graph representation. Errors in these steps will propagate through the augmentation and graph building, leading to poor overall results.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Computational cost vs. accuracy: More examples and complex augmentation strategies could improve accuracy but increase computational cost.
  - Graph complexity vs. interpretability: A more detailed graph might capture more nuances but be harder to visualize and interpret.
  - Token-level vs. concept-level matching: The current approach uses exact token matching, but a concept-level approach might be more robust to variations in language.
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor F1 scores on activation prediction: Indicates the graph is not capturing the neuron's behavior well.
  - Disconnected subgraphs in the visualization: Suggests the neuron is highly polysemantic and the current approach is not handling it effectively.
  - Extremely long processing times: Indicates the method is not scaling well to larger models or datasets.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run N2G on a single, well-understood neuron (e.g., a neuron known to respond to a specific token) and verify the output graph matches expectations.
  2. Compare the F1 scores of N2G to the baseline methods on a small subset of neurons to ensure it is providing a benefit.
  3. Visualize the graph for a neuron known to be polysemantic and inspect if the polysemanticity is captured in the structure (e.g., as disconnected subgraphs).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How well does N2G perform on language models with non-SoLU activation functions, which tend to have more polysemantic neurons?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that they evaluated N2G on a SoLU model specifically because it has fewer polysemantic neurons, and acknowledge that polysemanticity could reduce N2G's effectiveness on other architectures.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide results or analysis of N2G's performance on models using ReLU, GeLU, or other common activation functions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing N2G on multiple language models with different activation functions and comparing the quality of the resulting neuron graphs and their predictive accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can N2G's graph representations be used to automatically identify and map circuits of neurons that work together to perform specific tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors suggest this possibility, mentioning that later-layer neurons might be "composed" of earlier-layer neurons, and that identifying neurons with subset behaviors could help discover circuits.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides preliminary evidence through a case study comparing similar neurons, but does not demonstrate systematic circuit discovery or mapping.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and validating an algorithm that uses N2G graphs to automatically identify chains or clusters of neurons that collectively perform a function, and verifying these findings against known circuits or manual analysis.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal size and diversity of the training dataset for each neuron to maximize N2G's performance, especially for complex neurons in later layers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that N2G's performance decreases in later layers, which they attribute to the training examples not fully capturing the complex behavior of these neurons. They also mention that augmentation helps but doesn't fully solve the problem.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a fixed dataset size (top 20 examples) for all neurons and doesn't explore how varying this affects results, particularly for neurons with more complex or abstract behaviors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the number and diversity of training examples for neurons across different layers, measuring how this impacts graph quality and predictive accuracy.

## Limitations

- The method may struggle with highly polysemantic neurons where behavior varies dramatically across contexts, potentially leading to oversimplified or fragmented graph representations.
- N2G operates at the token level, which may miss higher-level semantic patterns that neurons capture, limiting its ability to generalize to semantically related but non-identical tokens.
- While the method scales to 6-layer models, computational requirements for much larger models remain uncertain and may require significant resources or architectural modifications.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core claim that N2G produces graph representations that better predict neuron activations than baseline methods is well-supported by quantitative F1 score comparisons across all layers (Claims supported by Figures 3 and 4, and Table 1).
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that N2G enables new forms of automated interpretability research (e.g., finding specific neuron types, comparing neurons) is plausible based on the graph structure but requires further empirical validation to demonstrate practical utility.
- **Medium Confidence**: The assertion that the graph representations are interpretable by humans is supported by the visualization methodology, but the paper doesn't provide user studies or qualitative assessments of interpretability by researchers.

## Next Checks

1. **Stress Test on Polysemantic Neurons**: Select neurons known to exhibit high polysemanticity and evaluate whether N2G's graph representations capture all activation patterns or collapse them into a single representation. This would validate the method's robustness to one of its stated limitations.

2. **Semantic Generalization Test**: For neurons responding to specific concepts (e.g., "king"), evaluate whether N2G's predicted activations generalize to semantically related tokens ("queen," "monarch") that weren't in the original dataset. This would test whether the token-level approach captures higher-level semantic patterns.

3. **Cross-Model Generalization**: Apply N2G to a neuron graph from the 6-layer SoLU model and test whether it accurately predicts activations on a different model architecture (e.g., GPT-2) or the same model fine-tuned on a different dataset. This would validate whether N2G captures general neuron behavior rather than dataset-specific quirks.
