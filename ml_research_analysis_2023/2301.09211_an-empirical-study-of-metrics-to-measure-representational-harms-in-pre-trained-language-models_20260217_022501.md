# An Empirical Study of Metrics to Measure Representational Harms in Pre-Trained Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.09211
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09211
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: Introduces safety score metric showing that depth-over-width prioritization in PTLMs mitigates representational harms, with weak correlations to existing bias metrics suggesting capture of different harm notions.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a new metric to quantify implicit representational harms in pre-trained language models (PTLMs) towards 13 marginalized demographics. The metric leverages language modeling likelihood of implicitly harmful versus benign statements, using scaled perplexity and Mann-Whitney U-test to produce a safety score. Experiments on 24 widely used PTLMs reveal that models exhibit higher propensities for harm towards certain demographics, with safety scores below 0.5 for most groups. The metric shows weak or negative correlations with existing gender stereotyping metrics, suggesting it captures different notions of bias. Further analysis indicates that prioritizing depth over width in PTLMs mitigates representational harms, especially in auto-encoder models.

## Method Summary
The study proposes a safety score metric that computes scaled perplexity (perplexity normalized by toxicity scores) for implicitly harmful and benign sentences from the ToxiGen dataset, then applies Mann-Whitney U-test to compare their distributions. The metric is evaluated across 24 PTLMs including BERT, RoBERTa, GPT-2, ALBERT, ELECTRA, and XLNet variants. Safety scores are compared with existing bias metrics like Regard, CMA, and StereoSet, and analyzed across different model architectures and training objectives.

## Key Results
- Safety scores below 0.5 for most demographics across 24 PTLMs indicate higher propensity for harm
- Weak or negative correlations with existing gender stereotyping metrics suggest measurement of distinct harm notions
- Depth prioritization over width improves safety scores, particularly in auto-encoder models like ALBERT
- Safety scores show no consistent correlation with toxic language detectors, confirming capture of implicit rather than explicit toxicity

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The metric uses language modeling likelihood to detect implicit representational harms.
- Mechanism: It computes scaled perplexity by normalizing token probabilities with toxicity scores, then applies Mann-Whitney U-test to compare distributions of harmful vs. benign sentences.
- Core assumption: Higher perplexity for harmful sentences indicates lower likelihood of generation by the model, implying reduced propensity for harm.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "leverage the primary task of PTLMs, i.e., language modeling, and propose a new metric to quantify manifested implicit representational harms"
  - [section 3.2.2]: "leverage language modeling objective which is the pre-training task in large-scale PTLMs"
  - [corpus]: Weak correlation with existing toxic language detectors suggests the metric captures a different notion of harm.
- Break condition: If scaled perplexity fails to differentiate between harmful and benign distributions, or if toxicity annotations are unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Depth over width prioritization mitigates representational harms in auto-encoder models.
- Mechanism: Increased depth with parameter sharing (as in ALBERT) improves model capacity to learn nuanced representations while reducing harmful associations.
- Core assumption: Deeper architectures with shared parameters can better generalize from pre-training data, reducing memorization of harmful patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "prioritizing depth over width in PTLMs mitigates representational harms, especially in auto-encoder models"
  - [section 4.6]: "In auto-encoder models, average safety scores have higher negative correlation with the width of the network compare to its depth"
  - [corpus]: Distilled-BERT models with deeper architectures show better safety scores than wider ones.
- Break condition: If parameter sharing causes loss of representational capacity or if deeper models overfit to specific harmful patterns.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Safety scores correlate differently with intrinsic and extrinsic bias metrics, indicating unique measurement of harm.
- Mechanism: The metric captures toxicity notion of harm rather than stereotypes, leading to weak or negative correlations with stereotype-focused metrics.
- Core assumption: Implicit toxicity and stereotyping are distinct dimensions of representational harm, requiring different measurement approaches.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.4]: "Our metric is negatively correlated with CMA and SS metrics in auto-encoder models"
  - [section 4.4]: "Our metric is positively correlated with CMA and Regard metrics" in auto-regressive models
  - [corpus]: Weak correlation with toxic language detectors confirms capture of implicit rather than explicit toxicity.
- Break condition: If the metric fails to differentiate between various types of representational harms or shows inconsistent correlations across model families.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the safety score metric perform when applied to datasets that contain implicit bias towards intersecting marginalized demographics (e.g., Black women, Middle Eastern LGBTQ individuals)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors mention that their evaluation dataset does not cover intersections of marginalized demographics and suggest this as an extension for future work.
- Why unresolved: The current evaluation dataset only includes single demographic groups, so the metric's performance on intersectional groups is unknown.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the safety score metric correlate with other intrinsic metrics for measuring representational harms, such as CrowS-Pairs and StereoSet?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors compare their safety score metric with several existing metrics, but not with CrowS-Pairs and StereoSet.
- Why unresolved: The correlation between the safety score metric and other intrinsic metrics is not explored in the paper.
- What evidence would resolve it: Calculating the correlation between the safety score metric and other intrinsic metrics using the same evaluation datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the safety score metric perform when applied to pre-trained language models that have been fine-tuned on downstream tasks, such as sentiment analysis or natural language inference?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors focus on measuring representational harms in pre-trained language models using the language modeling task, but do not explore how the safety score metric performs on fine-tuned models.
- Why unresolved: The safety score metric is designed to measure representational harms in pre-trained language models, but its performance on fine-tuned models is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying the safety score metric to pre-trained language models that have been fine-tuned on downstream tasks and comparing the results to the pre-trained models.

## Limitations
- The metric's reliance on human-annotated toxicity scores introduces potential bias and subjectivity in safety score calculation
- Study focuses on 13 marginalized demographics, which may not fully represent all groups subject to representational harms
- Findings may be specific to chosen datasets and model architectures, limiting broader applicability

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The mechanism by which scaled perplexity combined with Mann-Whitney U-test quantifies safety scores is well-established. The observation that prioritizing depth over width improves safety scores in auto-encoder models is supported by multiple experiments and aligns with known architectural principles.
- **Medium Confidence**: The interpretation of weak or negative correlations with existing gender stereotyping metrics as evidence of measuring a distinct notion of harm requires careful consideration. The metric may capture a different aspect of bias, but alternative explanations exist, such as measurement artifacts or dataset-specific effects.
- **Low Confidence**: The generalizability of findings across all possible marginalized demographics and language contexts remains uncertain. The study's results may be specific to the chosen datasets and model architectures, limiting broader applicability.

## Next Checks
1. **Dataset Generalization Test**: Evaluate the metric's consistency across multiple toxicity annotation frameworks (e.g., Perspective API, human raters) to assess robustness to annotation variations and ensure the safety score captures a stable notion of harm.

2. **Architectural Ablation Study**: Systematically vary depth and width parameters in controlled experiments (e.g., fixed parameter count with different depth/width ratios) to isolate the specific architectural factors driving the observed safety improvements.

3. **Cross-Demographic Analysis**: Conduct detailed analysis of safety score distributions across all 13 demographics to identify any systematic biases or anomalies in the metric's behavior, particularly for demographics where scores deviate significantly from the mean.
