# Problems and shortcuts in deep learning for screening mammography

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.16417
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16417
- Reference count: 0
- A model trained only on view markers achieved 0.691 AUC, revealing spurious shortcuts in mammography AI.

## Executive Summary
This work identifies and addresses spurious shortcuts that can inflate performance in deep learning models for screening mammography. The authors demonstrate that models can exploit correlations between dataset attributes (view markers, dataset source, exam type, scanner model) and cancer prevalence, creating performance illusions. They propose training and analysis methods to mitigate these shortcuts, including removing view markers, balancing cancer prevalence across datasets, and training only on screening exams. The study reveals the "AUC paradox" where models show high combined performance but fail on individual datasets, highlighting the need for stratified evaluation in medical AI.

## Method Summary
The authors trained an EfficientNet-B0 model on 120,112 US mammography exams (3,467 cancers) and 16,693 UK exams (5,655 cancers). The model used a two-stage training approach: first training on 512x512 patches, then on whole images (1664x1280). They implemented mitigation strategies including removing view markers from images, balancing cancer prevalence equally across datasets during training, and training only on screening exams while excluding diagnostic exams. Performance was evaluated using AUC with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals on test sets containing 11,593 US exams and 1,880 UK exams.

## Key Results
- Model trained only on view markers achieved 0.691 AUC, confirming spurious shortcut potential
- Original model showed AUC paradox: 0.945 on combined US+UK vs. 0.838 (US) and 0.892 (UK)
- Dataset balancing mitigated shortcut: improved individual dataset AUCs to 0.887 (US) and 0.894 (UK)
- Similar AUC paradox (0.903) occurred between screening (0.862) and diagnostic (0.861) exams
- Removing diagnostic exams during training alleviated this bias

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: View Marker Removal
The model exploits spurious correlations between view marker presence and cancer prevalence across datasets. View markers have different cancer prevalence in WUSTL (1.2% vs 2.6%) and OPTIMAM (20.4% vs 17.7%). Removing these markers eliminates this shortcut by preventing the model from using this non-clinical signal.

### Mechanism 2: Dataset Balancing
When datasets have unequal cancer prevalence, the model learns to assign higher scores to images from high-prevalence datasets. Balancing cancer prevalence during training ensures equal representation, preventing the model from developing dataset-specific biases that inflate combined performance.

### Mechanism 3: Screening Exam Filtering
Diagnostic exams have much higher cancer prevalence (10.2% vs 0.9% for WUSTL), creating a shortcut where the model associates exam type with cancer risk. Training only on screening exams removes this bias and improves screening-specific performance.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Simpson's Paradox
  - Why needed here: The AUC paradox is a manifestation of Simpson's paradox where combined dataset performance differs from individual dataset performance.
  - Quick check question: If Dataset A has 90% cancer prevalence with AUC 0.8 and Dataset B has 10% cancer prevalence with AUC 0.8, what happens to combined AUC if the model learns to score Dataset A higher?

- Concept: AUC as probability
  - Why needed here: Understanding that AUC represents the probability that a random positive scores higher than a random negative is crucial for understanding the AUC paradox.
  - Quick check question: If a model always scores positives from Dataset A higher than positives from Dataset B, and negatives from Dataset B higher than negatives from Dataset A, what happens to combined AUC?

- Concept: Dataset bias vs Model bias
  - Why needed here: Distinguishing between dataset bias (unequal cancer prevalence across attribute values) and model bias (model learning to assign higher probabilities to certain attribute values) is essential for identifying shortcuts.
  - Quick check question: If an attribute has equal cancer prevalence across values but the model still assigns different scores, is this dataset bias or model bias?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Image → EfficientNet-B0 → Feature vector → Breast probability (average) → Exam probability (max) → Binary classification
- Critical path: Image → EfficientNet-B0 → Feature vector → Breast probability (average) → Exam probability (max) → Binary classification
- Design tradeoffs: Using EfficientNet-B0 provides good performance with reasonable computational cost vs larger models; averaging breast scores vs separate models for each breast; max vs average for exam scores
- Failure signatures: High AUC on combined dataset but low AUC on individual datasets (AUC paradox); large performance differences across scanner types or datasets; ability to predict dataset source from model features
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train on images with only view markers (no breast tissue) and evaluate AUC to verify view marker shortcut
  2. Train with balanced cancer prevalence across datasets and compare individual dataset AUCs to original model
  3. Train only on screening exams and evaluate performance difference between screening and diagnostic subsets

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we develop debiasing methods that don't require pre-specifying the attributes of interest and don't require removing crucial cancer cases like diagnostic exams during training?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions this as a limitation in the discussion section, stating "Future studies will investigate debiasing methods not requiring pre-specification of the attributes of interest and proposes shortcut mitigations without removing crucial cancer cases like diagnostic exams."
- Why unresolved: Current methods either require knowing the specific attributes that might cause shortcuts or involve removing important data (like diagnostic exams) to mitigate these shortcuts. A more general approach that doesn't require this prior knowledge or data removal would be valuable for real-world applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: A successful debiasing method that can identify and mitigate shortcuts without prior knowledge of specific attributes or without requiring the removal of diagnostic exams would resolve this question.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the AUC paradox manifest when combining datasets from more than two sources with varying cancer prevalences and model biases?
- Basis in paper: The paper discusses the AUC paradox in the context of combining two datasets (US and UK) and provides a simulation study. However, it doesn't explore scenarios with more than two datasets.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides insights into the AUC paradox with two datasets, real-world scenarios often involve multiple data sources. Understanding how the paradox behaves with more complex dataset combinations is crucial for evaluating AI models trained on diverse data.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study combining multiple datasets with varying cancer prevalences and model biases, along with corresponding simulations, would help understand the AUC paradox in more complex scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can we develop methods to identify shortcuts in continuous attributes, not just discrete ones?
- Basis in paper: The paper states "This work focused on shortcuts for attributes labeled with discrete classes (e.g., HS vs SD). In reality, these models may find shortcuts on hidden attributes or continuous attributes."
- Why unresolved: Current methods for identifying shortcuts are limited to discrete attributes, but real-world data often contains continuous attributes that could also lead to shortcuts. Developing methods to detect shortcuts in continuous attributes would provide a more comprehensive understanding of model behavior.
- What evidence would resolve it: A method that can successfully identify and quantify shortcuts in continuous attributes would resolve this question.

## Limitations

- The analysis focuses on discrete attributes, potentially missing shortcuts in continuous or hidden attributes
- Methods require pre-specification of attributes that might cause shortcuts
- Some mitigation strategies involve removing important data (like diagnostic exams) from training
- Limited evaluation of clinical impact and real-world integration with radiologist workflows

## Confidence

- High: Existence of view marker shortcut and effectiveness of removal (backed by AUC comparison: 0.691 for view markers only vs. higher for full model)
- High: Dataset balancing effectiveness in mitigating the AUC paradox (original model: 0.945 combined vs. 0.838 US/0.892 UK; balanced model: 0.887 US/0.894 UK)
- Medium: Generalization of the AUC paradox framework to other medical imaging tasks and modalities
- Low: Clinical impact assessment of the proposed mitigation strategies on radiologist-AI collaboration and patient outcomes
