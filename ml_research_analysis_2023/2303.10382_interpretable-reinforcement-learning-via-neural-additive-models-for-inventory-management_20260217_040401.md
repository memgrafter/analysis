# Interpretable Reinforcement Learning via Neural Additive Models for Inventory Management

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.10382
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10382
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: NAM-based policy achieves comparable performance to MLP-PPO in inventory management, with slightly higher mean cumulative reward (792.69 vs. 730.27 IQM)

## Executive Summary
This work addresses the challenge of learning interpretable, dynamic inventory ordering policies for multi-echelon supply chains. It introduces Neural Additive Models (NAMs) as the policy architecture within a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) framework, replacing opaque multi-layer perceptrons with additive, single-feature contributions. The NAM-based policy achieves comparable performance to standard MLP-PPO in a three-echelon inventory benchmark, with slightly higher mean cumulative reward (792.69 vs. 730.27 IQM). The interpretable structure allows inspection of feature importances and shape functions, revealing how inventory levels and past actions drive ordering decisions. This approach bridges the gap between interpretable static policies and flexible RL policies, enabling stakeholder communication and insight into complex supply chain behaviors.

## Method Summary
The method employs Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Neural Additive Models (NAMs) as the policy architecture for multi-echelon inventory management. The NAM replaces traditional multi-layer perceptrons by decomposing the policy into additive, single-feature contributions, where each feature's contribution is computed independently by a separate neural network (subnet). The final action is the sum of these contributions, avoiding the need to learn feature interactions explicitly. The approach is evaluated on the OR-Gym INVMANAGEMENT-V0 environment, a three-echelon linear supply chain with stochastic customer demand. Hyperparameters are optimized via random search over 30 configurations, each evaluated with 3 random seeds for 60 steps, with final evaluation using 20 seeds and 50 rollouts each.

## Key Results
- NAM-PPO achieves comparable performance to MLP-PPO with mean cumulative reward of 792.69 IQM vs 730.27 IQM
- NAM policies maintain interpretability through per-feature shape functions showing contribution vs feature value relationships
- Both NAM-PPO and MLP-PPO show similar degradation under demand distribution disruptions
- Feature importance analysis reveals inventory levels and past actions as key drivers of ordering decisions

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: NAM-PPO policies achieve comparable performance to MLP-PPO by linearly combining univariate feature contributions without modeling higher-order interactions.
- Mechanism: Each feature's contribution to the policy output is computed independently by a separate neural network (subnet), and the final action is the sum of these contributions. This avoids the need to learn feature interactions explicitly, which can be difficult and unstable.
- Core assumption: The inventory management decision problem can be approximated well by additive feature contributions without strong interaction effects.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The interpretable structure allows inspection of feature importances and shape functions, revealing how inventory levels and past actions drive ordering decisions."
  - [section 3] "Using a NAM to represent the agent’s policy, we can leverage its interpretability to gain insights into the learned features of the policy"
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct comparisons to MLP-PPO in neighbors, but MORSE: Multi-Objective RL via Strategy Evolution for Supply Chain Optimization uses additive models for interpretability.
- Break condition: If the true policy requires strong feature interactions (e.g., inventory level x lead time), NAM performance will degrade significantly compared to MLP.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Interpretability via shape functions enables stakeholder communication and validation of learned policies.
- Mechanism: The NAM architecture naturally produces per-feature shape functions (contribution vs feature value plots) that can be extracted and analyzed without additional explanation methods.
- Core assumption: Supply chain managers can understand and trust policies when shown individual feature contributions and their relationships to decisions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The interpretable structure allows inspection of feature importances and shape functions, revealing how inventory levels and past actions drive ordering decisions."
  - [section 3] "The shape functions describe the contribution of the corresponding features exactly as the prediction is given by a simple point-wise addition of feature contributions"
  - [section 4.2] "To this end, the shape functions of the NAM-based policies can be visualized as shown in Figure 3"
- Break condition: If shape functions are too complex or counterintuitive for stakeholders to understand, the interpretability benefit is lost.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: NAM-PPO is robust to disruptions in customer demand patterns, maintaining stable performance across varying disruption strengths.
- Mechanism: The additive structure allows the policy to adapt to sudden demand changes by adjusting individual feature contributions without requiring complete retraining.
- Core assumption: NAM can generalize to out-of-distribution demand patterns through its flexible, non-linear feature contributions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "we evaluate the robustness of the trained MLP- and NAM-PPO agents under sudden changes in customer demand"
  - [section 4.2] "We see that for both MLP- and NAM-PPO the IQM of the reward degrades very similarly as we increase the disruption strength"
  - [corpus] Weak - no explicit robustness analysis in neighbors, but Iterative Multi-Agent RL: A Novel Approach Toward Real-World Multi-Echelon Inventory Optimization mentions robustness as a goal.
- Break condition: If demand disruptions create patterns that NAM cannot capture through its univariate contributions, performance will degrade more severely than MLP.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Environment -> Policy Network (NAM/MLP) -> Action -> Environment Reward -> Value Network (MLP) -> PPO Update

- Critical path:
  1. Environment generates state (inventory levels + past actions)
  2. Policy network (MLP or NAM) processes state and outputs reorder quantities
  3. Environment executes actions, generates reward, and provides next state
  4. PPO updates policy and value networks using collected trajectories

- Design tradeoffs:
  - NAM vs MLP: Interpretability vs potential performance (NAM cannot model feature interactions)
  - Fixed subnet count (S=30) vs adaptive: Computational efficiency vs flexibility
  - Raw features vs feature extraction: Interpretability vs parameter efficiency

- Failure signatures:
  - NAM: Poor performance on tasks requiring feature interactions, inability to capture complex patterns
  - MLP: Lack of interpretability, difficulty in stakeholder communication
  - Both: Instability with long episode lengths, sensitivity to hyperparameter choices

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train MLP-PPO and NAM-PPO on default environment, compare IQM and confidence intervals
  2. Extract and visualize shape functions for NAM policy, analyze feature importances
  3. Test both policies under demand disruption scenarios, measure robustness and performance degradation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do higher-order feature interactions impact the performance and interpretability of Neural Additive Models (NAMs) in reinforcement learning for inventory management?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that NAMs can only linearly combine feature contributions, potentially missing higher-order interactions that MLPs can capture.
- Why unresolved: The paper mentions this limitation but does not explore the impact of adding higher-order features to NAMs or compare their performance with standard MLPs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing NAMs with and without higher-order feature interactions against standard MLPs in terms of performance and interpretability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do Neural Additive Model (NAM)-based policies generalize to longer time horizons compared to standard multi-layer perceptron (MLP) policies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper reports that NAM policies fail to remain profitable in the long run while MLP policies remain stable and profitable when the episode length is increased.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides preliminary results but does not investigate the underlying reasons for this difference in generalization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further analysis of the learned policies' behavior over extended time horizons, including the impact of higher-order feature interactions and the role of feature importance in long-term decision-making.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do disruptions in customer demand distribution affect the robustness of Neural Additive Model (NAM)-based policies compared to standard multi-layer perceptron (MLP) policies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that both NAM and MLP policies are similarly affected by changes in the demand distribution, but hardening the policies against disruptions makes them more brittle.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides initial results but does not explore the mechanisms behind the brittleness of hardened policies or investigate potential strategies to improve robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Further experiments on the impact of different types and magnitudes of demand disruptions on policy performance, as well as analysis of the learned policies' decision-making processes under stress conditions.

## Limitations

- Limited generalizability to complex supply chain topologies beyond the three-echelon benchmark
- Lack of empirical validation of interpretability claims through stakeholder comprehension studies
- Weak corpus support with no direct comparisons to MLP-PPO in related work
- Limited investigation of robustness under demand disruptions and the brittleness of hardened policies

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The comparative performance results between NAM-PPO and MLP-PPO (IQM of 792.69 vs 730.27) are well-supported by the experimental design with multiple random seeds and bootstrap confidence intervals.

**Medium Confidence**: The interpretability claims are reasonable given the NAM architecture's properties, but the practical utility for stakeholder communication is not empirically validated beyond shape function visualization.

**Low Confidence**: The robustness claims under demand disruptions are based on limited experimental scenarios. The paper states both methods degrade similarly, but does not provide evidence that NAM can effectively handle out-of-distribution demand patterns through its additive structure.

## Next Checks

1. **Interaction Effects Test**: Systematically evaluate whether NAM-PPO performance degrades significantly on tasks requiring strong feature interactions (e.g., inventory level x lead time) compared to MLP-PPO.

2. **Stakeholder Comprehension Study**: Conduct user studies with supply chain managers to assess whether the shape functions are actually interpretable and actionable for decision-making.

3. **Generalization Benchmark**: Test NAM-PPO performance across multiple supply chain topologies (longer chains, parallel branches) to evaluate whether the interpretability-accuracy tradeoff holds in more complex environments.
