# GFlowNets with Human Feedback

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07036
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07036
- Reference count: 10
- Key outcome: GFlowHF learns a policy proportional to human scores rather than focusing on highest scores, achieving better exploration and higher average reward than RLHF baselines in multimodal reward tasks.

## Executive Summary
GFlowHF introduces a novel approach that uses GFlowNets to learn a policy proportional to human ratings instead of just maximizing the highest scores. Unlike RLHF, which focuses on optimizing for the best outcomes, GFlowHF models the entire distribution of human feedback through a flow network trained with a flow matching loss. Experiments on a multimodal Point-Robot task show GFlowHF discovers multiple high-reward targets and demonstrates superior exploration while being more robust to noisy human labels.

## Method Summary
GFlowHF combines human feedback with GFlowNets by training a reward model on human scores and then using a flow network to learn a policy proportional to these scores. The flow network is trained via flow matching loss to satisfy π(sf) ∝ score(sf), ensuring that trajectories with higher human scores are sampled more frequently in proportion to their scores. This approach contrasts with RLHF's focus on maximizing expected reward, which tends to collapse onto a single high-scoring mode. The method involves collecting trajectories, training a reward network on human scores, sampling flows to compute the flow matching loss, updating the flow network, and deriving the policy from the flow network.

## Key Results
- GFlowHF discovers both high-reward targets in a multimodal Point-Robot task while RLHF baselines only find one.
- GFlowHF achieves higher average reward than RLHF methods in the multimodal reward setting.
- GFlowHF demonstrates stronger robustness to noisy human labels, maintaining performance even with incorrect high-score labels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GFlowHF learns a policy proportional to the full distribution of human ratings rather than just the top-ranked outcomes.
- Mechanism: The flow network Fθ is trained via flow matching loss to satisfy π(sf) ∝ score(sf), ensuring that trajectories with higher human scores are sampled more frequently in proportion to their scores. This is different from RLHF, which maximizes expected reward and thus tends to collapse onto a single high-scoring mode.
- Core assumption: The reward model rφ accurately captures human preferences across the full reward spectrum, not just extremes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The goal of GFlowHF is to learn a policy that is strictly proportional to human ratings, instead of only focusing on human favorite ratings like RLHF."
  - [section] Equation (1) defines the flow matching loss to enforce π(sf) ∝ score(sf).
- Break condition: If the reward model rφ is poorly calibrated or only trained on sparse extreme labels, the proportionality assumption fails and GFlowHF will not explore effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: GFlowHF is more robust to noisy human labels than RLHF.
- Mechanism: By modeling the entire reward distribution, GFlowHF is less sensitive to outliers. A single high-scoring incorrect label does not dominate policy updates because the flow network balances multiple reward levels during training.
- Core assumption: The flow network aggregates rewards over many trajectories, diluting the impact of individual noise.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "GFlowHF has a stronger ability to resist noisy human labels."
  - [section] Figure 4 shows GFlowHF still discovers correct answers despite a wrong high-score label at (7,10).
- Break condition: If noise is systematic (e.g., all high scores for wrong answers), GFlowHF will still converge to the wrong distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: GFlowHF achieves better exploration in multimodal reward tasks.
- Mechanism: Because the policy samples proportionally to scores, multiple high-scoring modes (e.g., two goal coordinates) are all represented in the learned policy. RLHF, optimizing for maximum reward, collapses to one mode.
- Core assumption: The task has multiple distinct high-reward states that should be explored.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "For a multimodal reward distribution, i.e. there are many different high-scoring answers. We can see that GFlowHF can demonstrate an exploration ability far beyond RLHF."
  - [section] Figure 3 shows GFlowHF explores both targets (5,10) and (10,5), while RLHF methods only find one.
- Break condition: In unimodal reward landscapes, the advantage over RLHF disappears.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Human labeler → scores → reward network rφ
  - Flow network Fθ trained via flow matching loss
  - Policy π(at|st) = Fθ(st,at) / Fθ(st)
  - Environment interaction to collect trajectories

- Critical path:
  1. Collect trajectories and get human scores.
  2. Train reward network rφ on scores.
  3. Sample K flows and compute flow matching loss.
  4. Update flow network Fθ.
  5. Derive policy π from Fθ.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Flow networks require careful tuning of the number of sampled flows K; too few leads to high variance, too many to inefficiency.
  - Reward model accuracy is critical; poor rφ degrades exploration.
  - Training on continuous vs discrete spaces changes the flow matching formulation.

- Failure signatures:
  - Policy collapses to one mode → check reward model calibration.
  - No improvement in exploration → check flow sampling and loss computation.
  - High variance in rewards → increase K or batch size.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate the Point-Robot multimodal task; verify GFlowHF finds both goal coordinates.
  2. Add a single noisy high-score label; confirm GFlowHF still finds correct modes.
  3. Compare average reward and number of distinct high-reward trajectories between GFlowHF and RLHF baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does GFlowHF perform in high-dimensional state-action spaces compared to RLHF?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only evaluates GFlowHF on a simple 2D continuous Point-Robot task. There is no mention of scalability to higher dimensions.
- Why unresolved: The experiments are limited to a toy environment. Real-world applications like language modeling involve extremely high-dimensional spaces where exploration is much harder.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments applying GFlowHF to high-dimensional tasks such as Atari games, robotics with many joints, or text generation would demonstrate scalability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the sample efficiency of GFlowHF compared to RLHF in terms of human feedback required?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] While the paper mentions human feedback is used to fit a reward model, it does not quantify how many human labels are needed for effective training versus RLHF.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not report the number of human labels used during training or compare this to the number RLHF would require for similar performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies showing performance as a function of the number of human labels, compared directly to RLHF baselines, would clarify sample efficiency.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How sensitive is GFlowHF to the choice of hyperparameters like the number of sampled flows and buffer sizes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The appendix provides specific hyperparameters used but does not explore sensitivity or provide ablation studies.
- Why unresolved: The paper presents results with fixed hyperparameters without discussing robustness to hyperparameter choices.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive hyperparameter sweeps showing performance across a range of values for key parameters like number of flows, buffer sizes, and learning rates would demonstrate sensitivity.

## Limitations

- Empirical evaluation is limited to a single synthetic multimodal reward task, leaving uncertainty about performance in real-world human feedback scenarios.
- Scalability of flow networks to high-dimensional continuous action spaces is not demonstrated.
- Computational overhead of sampling K flows for each update may become prohibitive in larger environments.

## Confidence

- **High confidence** in the mechanism showing GFlowHF learns a proportional policy over human ratings (supported by theoretical flow matching framework and clear mathematical formulation).
- **Medium confidence** in the robustness claim to noisy labels (supported by one synthetic counterexample, but lacks systematic noise analysis across different noise levels).
- **Medium confidence** in the exploration advantage (demonstrated in controlled multimodal setting, but generalizability to more complex multimodal distributions unverified).
