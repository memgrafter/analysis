# Evaluating Large Language Models with NeuBAROCO: Syllogistic Reasoning Ability and Human-like Biases

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.12567
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12567
- Reference count: 9
- Large language models struggle more with syllogistic reasoning problems involving belief biases, conversion errors, and atmosphere effects.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the NeuBAROCO dataset to evaluate whether large language models exhibit human-like biases in syllogistic reasoning. The dataset, derived from BAROCO and available in both English and Japanese, contains 375 syllogistic inference problems annotated for inference type and belief consistency. The study examines three types of biases observed in human reasoning: belief biases, conversion errors, and atmosphere effects. Results show that current LLMs, including GPT-3.5, RoBERTa, and BART, struggle more with problems involving these biases, with GPT-3.5 performing better overall but still exhibiting notable difficulties.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates three large language models (RoBERTa, BART, and GPT-3.5) on the NeuBAROCO dataset using zero-shot inference. Models are prompted to classify syllogistic conclusions as entailment, contradiction, or neutral based on given premises. The dataset contains problems with different bias types - belief-consistent vs. inconsistent, conversion errors, and atmosphere effects. Performance is measured by accuracy across the full dataset and within each bias category, with results compared to established human reasoning patterns.

## Key Results
- GPT-3.5 achieves 76.27% accuracy overall, significantly outperforming RoBERTa (46.13%) and BART (42.93%)
- Models perform significantly worse on belief-inconsistent problems compared to belief-consistent ones
- GPT-3.5 shows particular difficulty with conversion errors and atmosphere effects, though it outperforms the other models on these challenging categories

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Large language models perform better on syllogistic reasoning tasks when the conclusions are consistent with common-sense beliefs.
- Mechanism: Belief bias causes humans to incorrectly accept conclusions that align with their beliefs and reject those that don't, regardless of logical validity. LLMs appear to exhibit similar patterns, struggling more with problems where the conclusion contradicts common-sense beliefs.
- Core assumption: LLMs have been trained on human-generated text that contains these belief biases, causing them to internalize similar reasoning patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Our findings demonstrate that current large language models struggle more with problems involving these three types of biases."
  - [section 4.1] "It is widely recognized that people tend to have trouble in determining whether an inference is valid when it includes a sentence contrary to common sense."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - only shows related papers on reasoning biases, not direct evidence of belief bias mechanisms.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LLMs make conversion errors by incorrectly interpreting quantifier relationships in syllogisms.
- Mechanism: When presented with premises like "All A are B" and "All B are C," LLMs may incorrectly convert this to "All B are A" and then draw invalid conclusions, similar to human conversion errors documented in cognitive psychology.
- Core assumption: The models haven't learned the non-convertibility of certain quantifier relationships, treating them as symmetric when they're not.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.2] "There are at least two types of errors, called illicit conversion... The tendency to interpret All A are B as equivalent to All B are A"
  - [section 5.2.2] "While RoBERTa and BART hardly answered correctly (0% and 1.43%), GPT-3.5 performed better, answering correctly almost a quarter of the cases"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - corpus shows related work on conversion errors but no direct evidence of LLM behavior.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLMs exhibit atmosphere effects by allowing the "mood" of premises to influence conclusion validity judgments.
- Mechanism: When premises contain particular quantifiers (some) or negative terms, LLMs are more likely to judge conclusions as invalid or particular, regardless of actual logical validity - mirroring the human tendency to be influenced by premise characteristics.
- Core assumption: The training data contains patterns where certain premise types correlate with certain conclusion types, leading models to learn spurious associations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.3] "Atmosphere effects are one of the inferential biases... It can be interpreted as two principles... The principle of quality... The principle of quantity"
  - [section 5.2.2] "While RoBERTa and BART hardly provide correct answer to atmosphere cases (0% and 0.96%), GPT-3.5 performed better (39.42%)"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers exist but don't directly support atmosphere effect mechanisms in LLMs.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: NeuBAROCO dataset creation -> Model inference (RoBERTa, BART, GPT-3.5) -> Bias category evaluation -> Comparative analysis with human reasoning patterns
- Critical path: Data preparation → Model inference → Bias category evaluation → Comparative analysis. The bottleneck is typically the manual annotation of belief consistency and bias categories.
- Design tradeoffs: Using Japanese and English datasets increases generalizability but requires translation quality control. Testing multiple models provides breadth but limits depth on any single architecture.
- Failure signatures: Low accuracy on inconsistent problems suggests belief bias issues; zero accuracy on conversion problems indicates fundamental misunderstanding of quantifier relationships; low atmosphere accuracy suggests spurious pattern learning.
- First 3 experiments:

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of large language models on syllogistic reasoning change with few-shot learning, particularly for problems involving belief biases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that further research on few-shot learning would be insightful and cites Dasgupta et al. (2022) who found improved performance with few-shot learning.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments tested models without any solved examples, so the impact of few-shot learning remains unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing model performance on syllogistic reasoning with and without few-shot learning, particularly focusing on belief-biased problems.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Do large language models exhibit similar biases when solving extended forms of natural logic inferences, such as relational syllogisms with transitive verbs or comparatives?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper concludes by suggesting that examining extended forms of natural logic inferences is an interesting direction for future work.
- Why unresolved: The current study focused only on basic syllogisms and did not test extended forms of natural logic inferences.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing large language models on extended forms of natural logic inferences and comparing their performance to basic syllogisms.

### Open Question 3
- Question: At what level do large language models make errors in conversion problems - at the sentence interpretation level or the logical inference level?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that there is room for discussion on which level the models made mistakes in conversion problems.
- Why unresolved: The current experiments did not investigate the specific source of errors in conversion problems.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of model outputs and intermediate representations to determine where errors occur in the reasoning process for conversion problems.

## Limitations

- The study uses a relatively small dataset of 375 syllogistic problems, which may not fully capture the complexity of logical reasoning biases.
- Belief consistency annotations were performed by the authors, introducing potential subjectivity in what constitutes "belief-inconsistent" problems.
- Only three model architectures were tested, all of which are decoder-only or encoder-decoder transformers, limiting generalizability to other model types.

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The observation that GPT-3.5 outperforms RoBERTa and BART on syllogistic reasoning tasks is well-supported by the experimental results. The dataset construction methodology and evaluation procedure are clearly described.

**Medium confidence**: The claim that LLMs exhibit human-like biases in syllogistic reasoning is supported by the performance patterns, but the mechanisms explaining why these biases emerge (e.g., training data influences) remain speculative without direct evidence.

**Low confidence**: The assertion that belief bias is the primary driver of poor performance on belief-inconsistent problems, while conversion errors and atmosphere effects are secondary, lacks sufficient comparative analysis to establish relative importance.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-linguistic validation**: Test whether the observed bias patterns hold when translating the Japanese problems to English (or vice versa) to ensure the results aren't language-specific artifacts.

2. **Controlled training experiments**: Train a model from scratch on logically valid syllogisms while systematically varying the inclusion of belief-inconsistent examples to determine if the biases can be mitigated through targeted training.

3. **Human benchmark replication**: Conduct a parallel human evaluation on the same problems to establish baseline human performance levels and confirm that the "human-like" biases are actually similar in magnitude and pattern.
