# Revisiting Inferential Benchmarks for Knowledge Graph Completion

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04814
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04814
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 10
- Key outcome: Proposed inferential benchmarks show significantly worse performance for state-of-the-art KG completion models compared to standard benchmarks, validating their effectiveness at measuring inference pattern learning.

## Executive Summary
Standard knowledge graph completion benchmarks use random splitting of triples, which fails to capture the causal dependencies inherent in inference patterns. This paper proposes a principled approach to construct inferential benchmarks that explicitly incorporate logical rules representing desired inference patterns. The method ensures models witness both premises and conclusions during training while generating challenging negative examples that penalize unintended rule learning. Evaluation shows state-of-the-art models perform significantly worse on these benchmarks compared to standard ones, revealing weaknesses in their ability to learn inference patterns.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a three-step pipeline for constructing inferential benchmarks. First, rules are generated from selected inference patterns using the KG and SP ARQL engine, selecting rules with largest support. Second, these rules are applied to the KG and the derived triples are distributed into training, validation, and test sets (8:1:1 ratio) while ensuring both premises and conclusions are present in training. Third, negative examples are generated using one of three methods - relevance-based sampling, position-aware corruption, or query-guided sampling - designed to discourage learning of unintended rules. The benchmarks are evaluated on FB15K-237, WN18RR, and LUBM using embedding-based, GNN-based, and rule mining models.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art KG completion models show significantly worse performance on inferential benchmarks compared to standard benchmarks
- Rule-based systems excel on simple inference patterns but struggle with complex ones
- Performance heavily depends on negative example generation strategy, with corruption-based methods yielding near-perfect results while proposed methods expose model weaknesses
- The benchmarks successfully evaluate models' ability to learn inference patterns rather than just memorizing facts

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Random splitting fails to capture causal dependencies inherent in inference patterns.
- Mechanism: Standard benchmarks split triples randomly, so premises and conclusions for a rule can end up in different sets, preventing models from learning the rule's causal structure.
- Core assumption: Inference patterns require witnessing both premises and conclusions during training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Standard completion benchmarks...are a random split of a given KG and hence do not capture the causality of inference patterns."
  - [section 1] "During training, models should witness both the premise and conclusion triples for selected rules instantiating a pattern of interest..."
- Break condition: If rules are not identifiable or the KG lacks sufficient support for rule premises, the approach cannot ensure correct distribution.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Corruption-based negative sampling leads to trivial classification and fails to penalize unintended rule learning.
- Mechanism: Corruption replaces entities in positive triples, generating negatives that are easy to detect, allowing models to learn simpler rules that entail target rules without penalty.
- Core assumption: Negative examples must witness rules the model should not learn, especially those logically entailing target rules.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "negative examples are designed to discourage the models from learning rules not entailed by the rule set."
  - [section 1] "Corruption-based negative sampling...would most likely not generate negative examples that penalize the model for learning the unintended rule."
- Break condition: If negative sampling fails to produce truly challenging examples, models will achieve near-perfect performance on trivial negatives.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Rule generation with maximal support ensures sufficient positive examples for learning.
- Mechanism: The pipeline selects rules with largest support in the KG, ensuring enough witnessing premises to distribute across training/validation/test sets.
- Core assumption: Rules with larger support provide more positive examples and better learning opportunities.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1] "The first step generates rules for the selected inference patterns with large number of witnessing premises in the KG."
  - [section 3.1] "One approach is to select into R a fixed predefined number of relations with the largest support..."
- Break condition: If the KG is too sparse, even rules with maximal support may not provide enough examples for meaningful learning.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: KG -> Rule generation module -> Distribution module -> Negative generation module -> Evaluation module
- Critical path: KG → Rule generation → Rule application → Distribution → Negative generation → Benchmark ready
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Rule selection: Larger k1 gives more rule diversity but may include irrelevant rules
  - Sample size: Larger k2 increases benchmark size but requires more computation
  - Negative method: Query-guided is most challenging but also most computationally intensive
- Failure signatures:
  - Benchmark too small: k1/k2 too low or KG lacks sufficient support
  - Models achieve perfect scores: Negative generation too easy (likely using corruption)
  - Data leakage: Incorrect distribution causing overlap between train/test sets
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Generate a simple symmetry benchmark on a small KG and verify that both premise and conclusion triples are in training
  2. Compare performance using corruption vs position-aware negative sampling on the same benchmark
  3. Test rule mining systems' ability to extract benchmark rules and measure entailment percentages

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can benchmark construction methods be adapted to evaluate the ability of KG completion models to learn inference patterns involving more complex logical structures, such as negation, disjunction, and higher-arity relations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on inference patterns involving conjunctions of atoms and inequalities in bodies, but does not address more complex logical structures. The authors note that their approach can be extended to arbitrary inference patterns in principle, but this remains unexplored.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the feasibility or challenges of extending the benchmark construction methodology to handle more complex logical structures. The impact of such extensions on model performance and the development of appropriate negative sampling strategies are unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing model performance on benchmarks incorporating complex logical structures versus standard benchmarks, along with an analysis of the challenges in constructing such benchmarks and the effectiveness of different negative sampling strategies.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of negative sampling strategy affect the performance of different KG completion models, and what are the underlying reasons for these differences?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the importance of negative sampling strategies and shows that the choice of method significantly impacts model performance, with corruption-based methods yielding nearly perfect results while the proposed methods expose model weaknesses. However, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the reasons behind these differences.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the specific reasons why different negative sampling strategies lead to varying levels of model performance. Understanding the underlying mechanisms and biases introduced by different strategies is crucial for developing more effective and fair benchmarks.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive analysis of the impact of different negative sampling strategies on model performance, including an investigation of the specific patterns and dependencies that each strategy captures or fails to capture. This could involve experiments with controlled datasets and a detailed examination of model predictions.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can KG completion benchmarks be designed to better evaluate the ability of models to learn rules that are logically entailed by other rules, but are not explicitly present in the training data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper introduces the concept of query-guided sampling to generate negative examples that prevent models from learning unintended rules, but it does not fully explore the challenges and potential solutions for evaluating models' ability to learn logically entailed rules.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the specific difficulties in designing benchmarks that effectively test models' ability to learn logically entailed rules. The impact of different benchmark designs on model performance and the development of appropriate evaluation metrics are unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing model performance on benchmarks with varying levels of rule entailment, along with an analysis of the specific patterns and dependencies that models struggle to learn in such scenarios. This could involve experiments with synthetic datasets and a detailed examination of model predictions and rule extraction capabilities.

## Limitations
- The exact SP ARQL implementation details and specific parameters k1 and k2 are not fully specified, making exact reproduction challenging
- The benchmarks may simply make the task more difficult rather than specifically measuring inference pattern learning
- Limited analysis of why different negative sampling strategies lead to varying model performance

## Confidence
- Claim: Standard benchmarks fail to capture causal dependencies inherent in inference patterns → High
- Claim: Proposed benchmarks better measure inference pattern learning → Medium
- Claim: Proposed negative sampling methods are superior to corruption-based approaches → Low
