# Resource-Efficient Fine-Tuning Strategies for Automatic MOS Prediction in Text-to-Speech for Low-Resource Languages

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.19396
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19396
- Reference count: 0
- Pre-training on BVCC before fine-tuning on SOMOS outperforms training on either dataset alone for MOS prediction in low-resource TTS.

## Executive Summary
This study explores resource-efficient fine-tuning strategies for automatic MOS prediction in text-to-speech (TTS) systems for low-resource languages (LRLs). Using a wav2vec 2.0-based MOS prediction model, the research compares two large open-access MOS datasets (BVCC and SOMOS) to identify optimal fine-tuning approaches. Key findings include the effectiveness of pre-training on BVCC before fine-tuning on SOMOS, a 30% data threshold for sufficient fine-tuning, and promising results from single-listener fine-tuning for pilot tests.

## Method Summary
The study employs a wav2vec 2.0 Base model (95M parameters) with a linear layer for MOS prediction, trained using L1 loss and SGD optimization (learning rate 1e-4, momentum 0.9). Experiments involve zero-shot and fine-tuned predictions on neural TTS samples in West Frisian, with data split into 40%/10%/50% train/validation/test sets across 10 random splits. The research compares pre-training on BVCC vs. SOMOS, varying fine-tuning data percentages (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%), and single-listener vs. all-listener fine-tuning approaches.

## Key Results
- Pre-training on BVCC before fine-tuning on SOMOS yields the best MOS prediction accuracy for both fine-tuned and zero-shot scenarios.
- No significant improvement in accuracy beyond using 30% of available MOS data for fine-tuning.
- Single-listener fine-tuning shows promising system-level LCC/SRCC (>0.6) despite high utterance-level MSE, suggesting viability for pilot tests.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pre-training on BVCC before fine-tuning on SOMOS outperforms training on either dataset alone for MOS prediction in low-resource TTS.
- Mechanism: BVCC provides diverse audio quality patterns from many TTS and VC systems, which helps the model handle the variability in LRL TTS outputs. SOMOS then specializes the model to neural TTS characteristics, combining breadth and depth.
- Core assumption: The MOS prediction model benefits from first learning broad audio quality patterns, then refining to neural TTS-specific traits.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "pre-training on BVCC before fine-tuning on SOMOS leads to the best accuracy for both fine-tuned and zero-shot prediction"
  - [section] "BVCC-SOMOS outperformed BVCC and SOMOS in almost all cases"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't directly address MOS dataset combination strategies.
- Break condition: If LRL TTS artifacts are too distinct from BVCC patterns, pre-training may introduce irrelevant noise instead of helpful generalization.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fine-tuning with 30% of available MOS data is sufficient for good system-level MOS prediction accuracy in LRL TTS.
- Mechanism: Beyond 30%, additional data yields diminishing returns due to listener bias variability dominating the signal, and the model has already captured the key relationships between audio features and MOS scores.
- Core assumption: Listener bias variance limits the benefit of more data beyond a certain threshold.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "using more than 30 percent of the total data does not lead to significant improvements"
  - [section] "all accuracy measures stopped improving after the 30%-step, i.e., adding training data beyond this produced no statistically significant improvement"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't provide specific evidence about optimal fine-tuning data percentages.
- Break condition: If the LRL TTS system produces highly variable outputs, more data may be needed to capture the full range of quality patterns.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Fine-tuning with MOS ratings from a single highly proficient listener can yield useful system-level MOS predictions for early-stage TTS development in LRLs.
- Mechanism: A single listener's ratings across all TTS systems provide consistent relative rankings, allowing the model to learn the relative quality order even if absolute scores vary from the broader listener population.
- Core assumption: A highly proficient listener's relative rankings are sufficiently representative for pilot evaluation purposes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "fine-tuning with data from a single listener shows promising system-level accuracy"
  - [section] "system-level LCC and SRCC were interestingly higher than in the all-listener models"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus doesn't specifically address single-listener fine-tuning strategies.
- Break condition: If the single listener has unusual preferences or the LRL community has diverse evaluation criteria, the model may learn biased rankings.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - wav2vec 2.0 Base (95M parameters) as feature extractor
  - Linear layer for MOS score prediction
  - L1 loss function for training
  - SGD optimizer with learning rate 1e-4 and momentum 0.9
  - Early stopping after 20 epochs of no validation loss improvement

- Critical path: Data preprocessing → wav2vec 2.0 feature extraction → linear layer MOS prediction → L1 loss computation → SGD optimization → evaluation metrics

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using wav2vec 2.0 Base vs. larger variants: balances performance with computational efficiency
  - L1 loss vs. other loss functions: simpler implementation, less sensitive to outliers
  - Early stopping threshold: prevents overfitting but may miss later improvements

- Failure signatures:
  - High utterance-level MSE but reasonable system-level LCC/SRCC: listener bias dominates individual predictions
  - No improvement beyond 30% data: listener bias variance limits additional data benefit
  - Single listener fine-tuning produces high MSE but good system-level metrics: relative ranking learned but absolute scores off

- First 3 experiments:

  3. Compare single-listener vs. all-listener fine-tuning on the same 20% data subset to validate the pilot test approach

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does pre-training on BVCC consistently improve MOS prediction accuracy across different languages and TTS systems beyond West Frisian and Chinese?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that their experiments in Frisian and Chinese suggest similar results could be achieved in other languages, but acknowledge this remains an assumption requiring further validation.
- Why unresolved: The study only tested two languages, limiting generalizability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Additional experiments testing pre-training on BVCC followed by fine-tuning on SOMOS across a diverse set of languages and TTS architectures.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Is there an optimal threshold for the amount of fine-tuning data that balances resource efficiency with MOS prediction accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The study found no significant improvement in accuracy beyond using 30% of total MOS data for fine-tuning.
- Why unresolved: The "sweet spot" may vary depending on the specific TTS system, language, and dataset characteristics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the proportion of fine-tuning data across multiple languages, TTS systems, and MOS datasets to identify consistent thresholds.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can MOS prediction models effectively incorporate contextual appropriateness metrics beyond naturalness ratings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors cite previous work arguing that naturalness is ambiguous and that more nuanced evaluation metrics, such as contextual appropriateness, should be considered.
- Why unresolved: Current MOS prediction models primarily focus on naturalness, neglecting other important aspects of speech quality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of MOS prediction models that integrate contextual appropriateness metrics alongside naturalness ratings.

## Limitations
- Findings rely on two specific MOS datasets (BVCC and SOMOS) and a single low-resource language (West Frisian), limiting generalizability.
- The wav2vec 2.0 Base model used may not capture all relevant audio quality patterns for diverse LRL TTS systems.
- Single-listener fine-tuning results may not translate to other listeners or LRL communities with different evaluation preferences.

## Confidence
- High Confidence: The 30% data threshold finding is well-supported by statistical analysis showing no significant improvement beyond this point. The BVCC-SOMOS pre-training benefit is demonstrated across multiple evaluation metrics.
- Medium Confidence: The single-listener fine-tuning approach shows promise but requires validation across different listeners and languages to confirm robustness. The wav2vec 2.0-based model's effectiveness for LRL MOS prediction needs broader testing.
- Low Confidence: The specific 95M parameter wav2vec 2.0 Base model choice and SGD hyperparameters may not be optimal for all LRL TTS scenarios. The study's conclusions about resource efficiency depend heavily on the availability and quality of MOS data.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-linguistic validation: Test the BVCC-SOMOS pre-training and 30% fine-tuning thresholds on at least two additional low-resource languages to assess generalizability.
2. Listener diversity test: Validate single-listener fine-tuning results using multiple listeners with varying proficiency levels to determine if the approach works across different listener profiles.
3. Model architecture comparison: Evaluate alternative SSL models (e.g., HuBERT, WavLM) and fine-tuning strategies to confirm that wav2vec 2.0 Base with SGD represents the most resource-efficient approach for LRL MOS prediction.
