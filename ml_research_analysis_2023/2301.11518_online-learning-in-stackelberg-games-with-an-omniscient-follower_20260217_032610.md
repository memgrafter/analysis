# Online Learning in Stackelberg Games with an Omniscient Follower

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.11518
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11518
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: An omniscient follower's best-response behavior can exponentially increase the leader's regret even in linear reward settings

## Executive Summary
This paper studies online learning in decentralized cooperative Stackelberg games where a leader takes actions followed by an omniscient follower who best-responds to the leader's action. The key finding is that an omniscient follower can dramatically increase the leader's regret complexity from polynomial to exponential, even when rewards are linear. However, by observing the follower's responses, the leader can achieve significantly improved regret bounds through carefully designed algorithms.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a UCB algorithm that leverages side information from observing the follower's responses. For each round, the algorithm maintains confidence sets based on both reward observations and response observations, then selects actions to maximize the upper confidence bound. The approach differs from standard bandit algorithms by explicitly using the follower's behavior as informative features to guide exploration.

## Key Results
- Exponential lower bound on regret (Ω(T^{(d-3)/(d-2)})) even for linear rewards
- UCB with side information achieves O(log²(T)) regret for imitation learning
- Improved regret O(√T) for expert-guided exploration with side information
- Polynomial bandit case: regret Θ(√d^{2k-1}T) with side information vs. Θ(√d^{2k}T) without

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: An omniscient follower's best-response behavior can exponentially increase the leader's regret even in linear reward settings
- Mechanism: The follower's expert behavior masks the true reward landscape from the leader, preventing effective exploration
- Core assumption: The follower always best-responds and is omniscient about the true reward function
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the existence of the omniscient follower may change the sample complexity drastically, from constant to exponential"
  - [section 3.1] Example 3.2 shows how the best response destroys linearity for leader's observations
  - [corpus] Related papers on learning in structured Stackelberg games confirm this challenge
- Break condition: If the follower is non-myopic or has imperfect information about the reward

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Side information from observing follower responses can dramatically reduce sample complexity
- Mechanism: The leader can use the follower's actions as informative features that guide exploration
- Core assumption: The follower's response is correlated with the reward structure and provides meaningful information
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "improved regret (O(log²(T))) for imitation learning" with side information
  - [section 4.2.1] Example 4.4 shows UCB achieves O(log²(T)) regret using response observations
  - [corpus] "ReLExS: Reinforcement Learning Explanations for Stackelberg No-Regret Learners" suggests this is an active research area
- Break condition: If the follower's response is noisy or uncorrelated with the reward structure

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: UCB with side information fails in polynomial bandit settings due to optimism bias
- Mechanism: Optimism principle leads to suboptimal actions when the best response function is non-linear
- Core assumption: The reward is a polynomial activation on a linear family, making the best response a lower-degree polynomial
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] Example 5.1 shows UCB achieves suboptimal sample complexity compared to algorithms using response observations exclusively
  - [section 5.2] Example 5.6 demonstrates optimism-based methods fail dramatically in certain problems
  - [corpus] "Neural Operators Can Play Dynamic Stackelberg Games" suggests neural approaches may be needed for complex response structures
- Break condition: When the polynomial degree is low or the response structure is simple

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Leader agent: Takes actions, receives rewards, observes (possibly noisy) follower responses
  - Follower agent: Always best-responds to leader's actions with full knowledge of true reward
  - Learning algorithm: Maintains confidence sets, selects actions to minimize regret
  - Side information processor: Uses follower responses to refine confidence sets

- Critical path:
  1. Leader selects action at
  2. Follower observes at and best-responds with bt
  3. Leader observes (noisy) reward rt and response ˆbt
  4. Learning algorithm updates confidence sets Ht
  5. Algorithm selects next action at+1

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using response observations vs. ignoring them (exponential vs polynomial complexity)
  - UCB optimism vs. pessimistic approaches in polynomial settings
  - Perfect vs. noisy observation of follower responses

- Failure signatures:
  - Regret growing exponentially with dimension (curse of expertise)
  - UCB consistently choosing suboptimal actions in polynomial cases
  - Confidence sets failing to contain true parameter

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can a general framework be developed to systematically synthesize response and reward observations for UCB-type algorithms in Stackelberg games with omniscient followers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that designing confidence sets based on specific response-reward structures is challenging and a general design is left for future research.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a systematic approach and leaves it as an open problem.
- What evidence would resolve it: A unified algorithm or theoretical framework that can handle various response-reward structures without requiring per-instance design.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal strategy for a non-myopic expert follower to maximize long-term reward while facilitating the leader's learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how a myopic best-response follower can hinder the leader's learning and suggests that a non-myopic follower might be beneficial.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore or analyze the behavior of non-myopic followers.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis or simulations comparing the learning outcomes of myopic vs. non-myopic followers in various Stackelberg game settings.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can a unified analysis provide a sharp upper bound on regret for all examples considered in the paper?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that a general framework of analysis that can provide a unified yet sharp upper bound on the examples is valuable.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides separate analyses for different examples but does not unify them.
- What evidence would resolve it: A single theoretical framework or algorithm that can derive optimal or near-optimal regret bounds for all considered examples.

## Limitations
- Exponential lower bounds rely on specific linear reward structures that may not generalize
- Practical performance on real-world problems is not demonstrated
- Construction of confidence sets for complex response-reward structures beyond simple linear cases is left unspecified

## Confidence
- High confidence: Exponential lower bound results and fundamental challenge posed by omniscient followers
- Medium confidence: UCB algorithm's performance guarantees for imitation learning under idealized conditions
- Low confidence: Practical applicability of proposed algorithms to complex real-world Stackelberg games
