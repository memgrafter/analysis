# Say What You Mean! Large Language Models Speak Too Positively about Negative Commonsense Knowledge

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.05976
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05976
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: LLMs correctly answer yes-or-no questions about negative commonsense knowledge but frequently fail to generate sentences expressing the same knowledge, revealing a "belief conflict" caused by statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language models handle negative commonsense knowledge through two probing tasks: constrained sentence generation and Boolean question answering. The study reveals a "belief conflict" where models correctly answer yes-or-no questions about negative knowledge but frequently fail to generate sentences that express the same knowledge. The authors find this conflict stems from statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias in pre-training, where frequent word co-occurrence patterns mislead models. The problem can be partially alleviated using Chain-of-Thought prompting techniques like deductive reasoning and fact comparison. This work highlights a significant gap between what LLMs know about negative knowledge and what they can express, raising important questions about their true reasoning capabilities.

## Method Summary
The study uses in-context learning with LLMs (GPT-3.5, Codex, InstructGPT) to probe negative commonsense knowledge without fine-tuning. The CSK-PN dataset contains 4,000 relational triples (positive and negative pairs) from ConceptNet across 6 relation types. Two tasks are evaluated: Boolean QA (yes/no answers) and constrained sentence generation (keyword-to-sentence). Performance is measured using TP/TN/Acc metrics, with focus on belief conflict - the gap between QA and CG performance on negative knowledge. The study varies in-context examples (0-32) and tests Chain-of-Thought techniques to alleviate the conflict.

## Key Results
- LLMs show belief conflict: correctly answer negative knowledge questions but frequently generate positive statements when prompted to create sentences
- Statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias from pre-training cause models to favor positive knowledge generation despite knowing negative facts
- Chain-of-Thought prompting (deductive reasoning, fact comparison) partially alleviates belief conflict by forcing explicit reasoning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The belief conflict arises because statistical co-occurrence patterns in training data bias LLMs toward generating positive knowledge, even when they "know" the negative is true.
- **Mechanism**: During pretraining, LLMs learn statistical shortcuts where certain subject-object pairs frequently co-occur in positive contexts. When generating sentences, these learned associations override the model's internal belief about negation, causing hallucination of positive statements.
- **Core assumption**: The model's generation is driven more by learned statistical patterns than by its internal representation of truth values.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Our further analysis shows that statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias from language modeling pre-training cause this conflict."
  - [section 5.2] "We conclude that the hard-to-generate negative knowledge for LLMs tend to be those in which they have seen many subjects and objects appear together."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the paper mentions co-occurrence buckets but doesn't provide detailed corpus statistics to support the strength of this bias.
- **Break condition**: If the model is explicitly prompted to reason deductively or compare related facts, it can override the statistical shortcut and generate the correct negative statement.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Chain-of-Thought prompting can alleviate the belief conflict by forcing explicit reasoning that overrides implicit statistical biases.
- **Mechanism**: CoT techniques like deductive reasoning (modus tollens) and fact comparison make the reasoning process explicit, allowing the model to logically derive the correct negation rather than relying on learned statistical patterns.
- **Core assumption**: LLMs have the latent capability to reason about negation but need explicit prompting to access this capability.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "This problem can be partially alleviated using Chain-of-Thought prompting techniques like deductive reasoning and fact comparison."
  - [section 5.4] "Both CoT instances improve LLMs' performance on TN, showing the benefit of explicit reasoning for deriving negative knowledge."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the paper doesn't explore how CoT affects the model's internal representations or whether this improvement generalizes beyond the specific prompting examples.
- **Break condition**: If the CoT examples provided are not representative of the task at hand, or if the model lacks the necessary commonsense knowledge to perform the reasoning, the technique may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The imbalance in positive vs. negative examples in the training data leads to reporting bias, where the model overgenerates positive statements.
- **Mechanism**: Because positive knowledge is more frequently expressed in text, LLMs learn to associate certain keywords with positive statements. This creates a bias where, even when the model knows a statement is negative, it defaults to generating the more common positive form.
- **Core assumption**: The training data distribution directly influences the model's generation bias, and adjusting this distribution can mitigate the problem.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Our further analysis shows that statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias from language modeling pre-training cause this conflict."
  - [section 5.3] "With more E−s, LLMs are encouraged to generate more negations."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - the paper doesn't provide detailed analysis of the training data distribution or how it correlates with the model's generation bias.
- **Break condition**: If the model has already overfit to the positive bias during pretraining, simply adjusting the in-context examples may not be sufficient to overcome the ingrained bias.

- **Critical path**: The critical path for generating a valid negative statement is: 1) The LLM receives the prompt with examples, 2) It processes the keywords and task instruction, 3) It generates a sentence, and 4) The evaluation metric checks for negation cues.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different types of negative knowledge (e.g., physical impossibility vs. social norms) affect the belief conflict in LLMs?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that negative knowledge includes statements like "lions don't live in the ocean" but doesn't explore how different categories of negative knowledge might behave differently.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on relational commonsense knowledge from ConceptNet but doesn't systematically categorize or compare different types of negative knowledge.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing LLMs on datasets containing various types of negative knowledge (physical impossibility, social norms, temporal constraints, etc.) and comparing their performance across these categories would reveal if some types are more prone to belief conflict than others.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the minimum amount of in-context examples needed to significantly reduce the belief conflict for different LLM sizes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that adding more examples helps generate both positive and negative commonsense knowledge, but the gap between TP and TN in the CG task still exists.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests up to 32 examples and doesn't systematically measure the relationship between example quantity and belief conflict reduction across different model sizes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with varying numbers of examples (e.g., 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) across different model sizes would reveal the scaling relationship and identify the point of diminishing returns.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does fine-tuning LLMs on negative knowledge data permanently resolve the belief conflict, or does it merely create a different form of bias?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests that increasing negated texts in training data could overcome the belief conflict, but this is presented as a hypothetical rather than tested approach.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses in-context learning to probe fixed LLMs rather than fine-tuning them, leaving open the question of whether training-time interventions would provide lasting solutions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Fine-tuning experiments where LLMs are trained on datasets enriched with negative knowledge, followed by testing on both the original and new datasets, would reveal whether fine-tuning creates lasting changes or merely shifts the bias pattern.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the belief conflict manifest in other generative tasks beyond constrained sentence generation, such as story generation or dialogue systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that hallucinated generation of negative knowledge is seen in downstream tasks like explanation generation.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on a controlled generation task and only briefly mentions downstream applications without systematic investigation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating LLMs on diverse generative tasks (story generation, dialogue systems, summarization) where negative knowledge is important, measuring both the accuracy of negative knowledge and the consistency between what models say they know and what they generate.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Can adversarial training or debiasing techniques specifically designed for negation reporting bias effectively eliminate the belief conflict?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper identifies statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias as causes of the belief conflict, suggesting these are addressable problems.
- Why unresolved: While the paper mentions that Chain-of-Thought prompting can alleviate the problem, it doesn't explore specialized training techniques or adversarial approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training LLMs with adversarial examples that specifically target negation reporting bias, or applying debiasing techniques designed for negation phenomena, followed by testing whether these approaches reduce the belief conflict in both generation and QA tasks.

## Limitations

- The study relies on a relatively small dataset of 4,000 relational triples from ConceptNet, which may not capture the full complexity of negative commonsense knowledge.
- Evaluation metrics for generation tasks are coarse, detecting only explicit negation cues without capturing nuanced or weak negations.
- The analysis of statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias lacks detailed corpus-level statistics to definitively establish these mechanisms as causal factors.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The core observation of belief conflict - that LLMs correctly answer yes/no questions about negative knowledge but fail to generate corresponding negative statements - is well-supported by empirical results across multiple model families.

**Medium Confidence**: The attribution of belief conflict to statistical shortcuts and negation reporting bias is supported by analysis but lacks definitive causal evidence.

**Medium Confidence**: The effectiveness of Chain-of-Thought prompting in alleviating belief conflict is demonstrated but limited to specific prompting strategies without exploring the full space of potential techniques.

## Next Checks

1. **Corpus Analysis Validation**: Conduct detailed analysis of pre-training corpora to quantify the frequency distribution of positive vs. negative statements for subject-object pairs, directly testing the statistical shortcut hypothesis with concrete corpus statistics.

2. **Cross-Domain Generalization**: Test the belief conflict and CoT mitigation strategies on knowledge bases beyond ConceptNet (e.g., ATOMIC, Wikidata) to determine if the phenomenon generalizes across different types of relational knowledge and domains.

3. **Fine-Grained Evaluation**: Develop and implement a more nuanced evaluation metric for negative statement generation that can detect weak negations, implicit negations, and semantic negations beyond explicit negation cue detection, providing a richer understanding of model capabilities.
