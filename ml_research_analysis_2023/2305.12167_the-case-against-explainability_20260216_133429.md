# The Case Against Explainability

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12167
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12167
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: End-user Explainability fails to fulfill legal reason-giving functions and may cause more harm than good

## Executive Summary
This study critically examines the legal and regulatory demand for AI explanations by analyzing the role of "reason-giving" in law. The analysis reveals that end-user Explainability largely fails to fulfill the first three legal functions (better decisions, due process, human agency) because AI replaces human decision-makers. While Explainability can enhance authority, it risks misleading users through manipulation, automation bias, and LLMs' persuasive yet unreliable explanations. The study concludes that end-user Explainability is inadequate for fulfilling legal explanation rights and may cause more harm than good, urging regulators and ML practitioners to reconsider its widespread pursuit.

## Method Summary
The paper deconstructs reason-giving's legal purposes (promoting better decisions, facilitating due process, respecting human agency, enhancing authority) and systematically analyzes whether end-user Explainability can fulfill each function. It examines current Explainability techniques, their limitations in producing legally-valid explanations, and the risks of manipulation when explanations enhance authority without substance. The method combines legal analysis with technical evaluation of XAI capabilities and risk assessment of authority manipulation.

## Key Results
- End-user Explainability fails to fulfill the first three legal functions of reason-giving because AI replaces human decision-makers
- Current Explainability techniques cannot produce legally-valid explanations that support meaningful contestation and due process
- Explainability excels at enhancing authority but creates risks of manipulation and misplaced trust through automation bias

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: End-user Explainability fails to fulfill legal reason-giving functions because AI replaces human decision-makers, removing the impact of explanations on the decision process
- Mechanism: Legal reason-giving depends on explanations influencing human decision-makers to improve decisions, respect agency, and ensure due process. When AI makes decisions, these functions become irrelevant
- Core assumption: The legal system's reliance on reason-giving assumes a human decision-maker who can be influenced by explanations
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "reason-giving's functions rely on its impact over a human decision maker... end-user Explainability fails, or is unsuitable, to fulfil the first, second and third legal function."
  - [section 4.1]: "If one of reason-giving's main roles is to impact the decision-making process itself... then it is hard to grasp in what form this purpose might be fulfilled given a machine now replaces a human decision maker."
- Break condition: If AI systems were designed to incorporate human oversight in a way that explanations could influence the final decision, this mechanism would fail

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Current Explainability techniques do not produce "explanations" that satisfy legal standards for meaningful contestation and due process
- Mechanism: Legal explanations require transparency and reliability that current XAI methods (LIME, SHAP, etc.) cannot provide, as they generate approximations rather than true causal understanding
- Core assumption: Legal systems require explanations that are both understandable to laypeople and reliable enough to support contestation rights
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "a persistent gap exists between the need to execute a meaningful right to explanation vs. the ability of Machine Learning systems to deliver on such a legal requirement."
  - [section 4.2.1]: "scholars argue it is still a long way from producing layperson understandable explanations... Most current Explainability techniques are non-accessible to a human lacking technological literacy."
- Break condition: If XAI techniques evolved to produce genuinely understandable and reliable explanations, or if legal standards for explanations were redefined, this mechanism would fail

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: End-user Explainability excels at enhancing decision-makers' authority but this creates risks of manipulation and misplaced trust
- Mechanism: Explanations can make AI systems appear more trustworthy and legitimate, but this authority is built on potentially misleading or unreliable explanations that can manipulate users through automation bias
- Core assumption: Users tend to over-trust machine-generated explanations, making them vulnerable to manipulation
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "end-user Explainability excels in the fourth function... raises serious risks considering recent end-user Explainability research trends, Large Language Models' capabilities, and the ability to manipulate end-users."
  - [section 4.4]: "Research has shown Explainability's potential to cause human over-reliance on the system... as well as the opportunity for wrongdoing and manipulation by promoting misguided trust."
- Break condition: If users developed critical literacy about AI explanations or if regulatory frameworks addressed manipulation risks, this mechanism would fail

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific mechanisms could replace end-user Explainability to achieve the legal purposes of reason-giving (better decisions, due process, human agency, authority)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper concludes that end-user Explainability is inadequate for fulfilling legal explanation rights and calls for reconsideration of its widespread pursuit.
- Why unresolved: The paper critiques existing approaches but doesn't propose specific alternatives that could achieve the same legal objectives.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies comparing alternative mechanisms (e.g., human oversight, different transparency frameworks) against legal standards for reason-giving.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we measure whether AI-generated explanations actually promote "appropriate trust" rather than manipulation or automation bias?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies risks of manipulation and automation bias when AI systems enhance their authority through explanations.
- Why unresolved: Current research focuses on perceived trustworthiness rather than actual trustworthiness, and there's no established methodology to distinguish between beneficial and harmful trust.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking user behavior and decision outcomes when using AI explanations, distinguishing between warranted and unwarranted trust.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Under what specific conditions might the harms of AI explanations outweigh their benefits?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper concludes that in some cases the right to AI explanation "could bring more harm than good."
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies general risks but doesn't specify thresholds or contexts where these harms become predominant.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of harm-benefit ratios across different AI application domains, user populations, and explanation quality levels.

## Limitations

- Analysis assumes current legal frameworks are static and cannot evolve to accommodate new forms of AI explanations, potentially underestimating regulatory adaptability
- Limited empirical evidence on real-world harm from AI explanations, with most claims based on theoretical risks rather than documented cases
- Doesn't fully address whether alternative accountability mechanisms could fulfill legal functions without traditional Explainability

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: The distinction between legal reason-giving functions and current Explainability capabilities is well-established and methodologically sound
- **Medium confidence**: Claims about Explainability's failure to support due process and human agency, as these depend on contested interpretations of legal requirements
- **Low confidence**: Assertions about manipulation risks from LLMs' explanations, as this relies heavily on theoretical concerns rather than empirical evidence
