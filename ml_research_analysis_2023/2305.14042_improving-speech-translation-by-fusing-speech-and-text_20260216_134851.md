# Improving speech translation by fusing speech and text

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.14042
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14042
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: 34.0 BLEU on MuST-C En→De/Es/Fr, outperforming SOTA by 1.1 BLEU

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of improving speech translation by leveraging complementary information from speech and text modalities. The authors propose Fuse-Speech-Text (FST), a cross-modal model that supports speech, text, and fused speech-text inputs. By employing techniques like contrastive learning, cross-attentive regularization, and knowledge distillation, FST achieves state-of-the-art results on multiple benchmarks while uniquely improving both speech translation and machine translation performance simultaneously.

## Method Summary
The FST model uses a shared Transformer encoder-decoder architecture with Wav2vec2.0 for speech encoding. Speech representations are downsampled by a factor of 4 using convolutional layers to match text sequence lengths. The model incorporates prompt tags (<golden>/<asr>) to guide cross-modal fusion based on transcript quality. Training combines multiple objectives including speech translation, machine translation, forced alignment, and ASR, with cross-modal contrastive learning and regularization losses to align representations across modalities.

## Key Results
- Achieves 34.0 BLEU on MuST-C En→De/Es/Fr benchmark
- Outperforms state-of-the-art by 1.1 BLEU
- Improves machine translation performance by 3.2 BLEU compared to pre-trained MT model
- Demonstrates effectiveness on GigaST and newstest benchmarks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Speech representations are substantially longer than text representations, creating an input length mismatch that degrades cross-modal fusion.
- Mechanism: The model applies two convolutional layers with stride 2 to reduce speech sequence length by a factor of 4, aligning it more closely with text sequence length.
- Core assumption: Downsampling speech by a factor of 4 preserves essential information while making lengths comparable to text.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The length of speech input representation obtained from speech pre-trained model frequently exceeds that of the corresponding transcript input representation by an order of magnitude or more."
  - [section] "To reduce the difference in representation length between speech and text as much as possible, we use two additional convolutional layers with stride 2 to shrink the length by a factor of 4."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence about convolutional downsampling effectiveness.
- Break condition: If downsampling removes critical temporal information or if text representations are longer than expected (e.g., long sentences), the alignment benefit disappears.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: ASR transcripts contain errors that mislead translation, while speech contains complementary information that can correct these errors.
- Mechanism: The model fuses speech and text inputs with prompt tags (<golden>/<asr>) that signal transcript quality, implicitly guiding the model to rely more on speech when transcripts are error-prone.
- Core assumption: The model can learn to appropriately weight speech vs. text information based on prompt tags during training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The prompt tags implicitly guide the model to utilize more speech information when the transcript is inaccurate."
  - [section] "The prompt tags (<golden>/<asr>) to indicate whether the transcript contains errors. The prompt tags implicitly guide the model to utilize more speech information when the transcript is inaccurate."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence about prompt tag effectiveness.
- Break condition: If the model fails to learn the correlation between prompt tags and actual transcript quality, or if the ASR errors are too severe for speech to correct.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Different modalities produce distinct hidden state distributions that need alignment for effective cross-modal fusion.
- Mechanism: The model uses Cross-Attentive Regularization (CAR) to align encoder states between speech, text, and fused representations, plus Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) to align decoder output distributions.
- Core assumption: Regularizing hidden states and output distributions across modalities reduces modality gaps and improves translation quality.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We utilize Cross-Attentive Regularization (CAR) to align the states of the encoder and Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) loss to align the distribution of the decoder."
  - [section] "We utilize Cross-Attentive Regularization (CAR) to align the states of the encoder and Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) loss to align the distribution of the decoder."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence about CAR and JSD effectiveness.
- Break condition: If regularization forces representations to converge too much, losing modality-specific information that's beneficial for translation.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Speech representation preprocessing
  - Why needed here: Speech representations are much longer than text representations, requiring dimensionality reduction for effective cross-modal fusion.
  - Quick check question: Why does the model apply convolutional downsampling to speech representations before fusion?

- Concept: Cross-modal contrastive learning
  - Why needed here: Speech and text representations need to be brought closer in semantic space for the model to effectively combine their complementary strengths.
  - Quick check question: What is the purpose of the N-pair contrastive loss between speech and text representations?

- Concept: Knowledge distillation from fused to unimodal models
  - Why needed here: The fused model can achieve better translation quality, and distilling this knowledge to speech-only and text-only models improves their performance.
  - Quick check question: How does the knowledge distillation loss help improve speech translation and machine translation performance?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Wav2vec2.0 speech encoder -> 2-layer convolutional downsampling -> Prompt-based speech-text fusion -> Shared Transformer encoder (6 layers) -> Shared Transformer decoder (6 layers) -> Translation output

- Critical path: Raw speech → Wav2vec2.0 → Conv downsampling → Fusion with text → Shared encoder → Shared decoder → Translation output

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Shared vs. separate encoders/decoders: Sharing reduces parameters and enables knowledge transfer but may limit modality-specific specialization
  - Prompt-based vs. learned fusion: Prompts provide explicit guidance but may be less flexible than learned attention mechanisms
  - Downsampling factor: Factor of 4 balances alignment with information preservation

- Failure signatures:
  - Performance degradation on speech translation but not machine translation: Likely issue with speech encoder or fusion mechanism
  - Performance degradation on both tasks: Likely issues with shared encoder/decoder or conflicting training objectives
  - Model fails to learn from ASR transcripts: Likely issues with prompt tags or contrastive learning

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Ablation: Remove convolutional downsampling to test if length alignment is necessary
  2. Ablation: Remove prompt tags to test if explicit guidance is needed for error correction
  3. Ablation: Remove CAR and JSD regularization to test if hidden state alignment is beneficial

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How effective would the FST approach be when applied to low-resource languages or dialects where high-quality speech and text data are scarce?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates success on high-resource languages (English, German, Spanish, French, Chinese) but does not address low-resource scenarios. The authors mention that speech data is "exceptionally scarce" and obtaining it for many languages is challenging, but do not explore performance degradation in such settings.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on benchmarking with well-resourced datasets (MuST-C, GigaST) without evaluating model performance on low-resource language pairs or under limited data conditions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments on low-resource language pairs (e.g., English→Swahili, English→Wolof) with varying amounts of training data, and analysis of performance degradation relative to high-resource baselines.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Would removing the dependency on an external ASR system for transcription errors improve latency and end-to-end performance, and if so, how?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge this limitation: "our model still relies on an ASR system to transcribe speech into text, which does not address the issue of high latency in the cascade system."
- Why unresolved: While the authors recognize this as a limitation, they do not explore alternatives such as using end-to-end models that bypass explicit transcription or integrating the ASR functionality directly into their architecture.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing FST with a fully end-to-end approach that eliminates the ASR intermediate step, measuring both latency improvements and translation quality changes.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of discrete speech features extracted by advanced pre-trained models compare to continuous features in the FST framework?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors experimented with Codebook-Gumbel and Codebook-Kmeans methods but found that mapping continuous speech features to discrete space led to decreased BLEU scores. They speculate that better discrete feature extraction from speech pre-trained models could improve performance.
- Why unresolved: The current speech pre-trained models (Wav2vec2.0, HuBERT) extract continuous features, and the authors did not explore newer models that might extract high-quality discrete features directly.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments using discrete feature extraction from advanced pre-trained models (e.g., VQ-Wav2vec, HuBERT with discrete outputs) versus continuous features, measuring impact on translation quality in the FST framework.

## Limitations

- Architectural reproducibility: The prompt-based fusion mechanism lacks sufficient implementation details for faithful reproduction
- Training objective sensitivity: Multi-task learning framework combines 8 loss functions with unspecified weighting parameters
- Modality gap claims: Evidence supporting identified modality gaps is primarily theoretical rather than empirically validated

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The observation that speech and text have different input lengths is well-established in the literature and directly observable from the data. The claim that downsampling speech by a factor of 4 reduces this gap is mechanically sound and verifiable.

**Medium Confidence**: The general approach of using contrastive learning and regularization to align cross-modal representations is theoretically justified. However, the specific effectiveness of the N-pair contrastive loss and cross-attentive regularization depends heavily on implementation details not fully specified in the paper.

**Low Confidence**: The claim that prompt tags effectively guide the model to utilize more speech information when transcripts are inaccurate lacks empirical validation. The paper asserts this mechanism works but doesn't provide evidence showing how the model actually responds to different prompt tag combinations.

## Next Checks

1. **Prompt Tag Effectiveness Analysis**: Conduct controlled experiments varying prompt tag combinations (<golden>, <asr>, <fused>) while keeping all other factors constant. Measure the model's attention patterns and translation outputs to verify whether it appropriately weights speech vs. text information based on transcript quality.

2. **Cross-Modal Alignment Ablation**: Systematically remove each alignment component (contrastive learning, cross-attentive regularization, JSD) individually and in combination. Compare the resulting modality gaps using quantitative measures of representation similarity (e.g., KL divergence between speech and text embeddings) to establish causal relationships.

3. **Knowledge Distillation Validation**: Compare three training scenarios: (a) joint training with shared encoder/decoder only, (b) joint training with knowledge distillation, and (c) pre-training on MT data then fine-tuning on ST. This isolates the contribution of knowledge distillation from the joint training framework and determines whether improvements are additive or redundant.
