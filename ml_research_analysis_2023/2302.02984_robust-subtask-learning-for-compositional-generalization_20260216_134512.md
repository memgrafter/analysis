# Robust Subtask Learning for Compositional Generalization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2302.02984
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02984
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ROSAC and AROSAC outperform baselines in compositional RL tasks under adversarial subtask sequences

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of learning subtask policies that are robust to arbitrary task sequences in compositional reinforcement learning. The authors propose a novel approach that formulates the problem as a two-agent zero-sum Markov game where an adversary selects task sequences to minimize performance while the agent learns policies that maximize worst-case performance. They develop two algorithms - ROSAC (synchronous) and AROSAC (asynchronous) - that solve this game by training subtask policies in parallel. Experiments on rooms navigation and F1/10th racing car environments demonstrate superior performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

## Method Summary
The paper formulates compositional RL as a two-agent zero-sum Markov game where the agent learns subtask policies and the adversary selects task sequences. ROSAC adapts Soft Actor-Critic to this game by maintaining separate value functions and policies for each subtask, with entropy bonuses encouraging exploration. AROSAC parallelizes the computation by using separate replay buffers for each subtask. The stagewise game structure allows independent computation of value functions for each subtask. The algorithms are evaluated against baselines including NAIVE, DAGGER, MADDPG, and PAIRED on rooms navigation and F1/10th racing environments.

## Key Results
- ROSAC and AROSAC achieve higher success rates than baselines when evaluated under both random and adversarial task sequences
- The methods demonstrate robustness to arbitrary task sequences, with success rates decreasing less steeply as sequence length increases
- AROSAC provides computational speedup through parallel training while maintaining performance comparable to ROSAC

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The adversarial formulation forces subtask policies to be robust to arbitrary task sequences by making the adversary choose the worst possible sequence of subtasks. The problem is modeled as a two-agent zero-sum Markov game where the adversary picks subtasks to minimize reward and the agent learns policies to maximize worst-case performance. This creates a min-max optimization problem where the learned policies are robust to any task sequence.

### Mechanism 2
The stagewise game structure allows parallel training of subtask policies, reducing computational cost. The game has a stagewise structure where agent 1 acts when the current subtask is not completed (S1 states) and agent 2 acts when the subtask is completed (S2 states). This allows computing the value function for each subtask independently in parallel.

### Mechanism 3
The entropy bonus in Soft Actor-Critic encourages exploration and prevents premature convergence to suboptimal policies. The reward function is augmented with an entropy term that encourages the policy to maintain high entropy (explore) while still maximizing expected reward. This helps avoid getting stuck in local optima.

  - Why needed here: The stagewise structure of the game allows computing value functions for each subtask in parallel using asynchronous updates

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Subtask policies → Value function estimators → Replay buffers (per subtask for AROSAC) → Environment
- **Critical path:** 1) Initialize subtask policies and value functions 2) Execute current subtask policy 3) When subtask completes, adversary implicitly selects next subtask 4) Update value functions and policies using observed transitions 5) Repeat until convergence
- **Design tradeoffs:** Synchronous vs asynchronous training (ROSAC simpler but slower, AROSAC parallelizable but complex), single vs multiple replay buffers (AROSAC better sample efficiency but more memory), entropy weight (balances exploration vs exploitation)
- **Failure signatures:** Policies stuck in local optima (increase entropy weight), training instability (check learning rates), poor worst-case performance (verify adversary selects challenging sequences)
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) Sanity check on rooms environment with 2-room version 2) Parallelization test comparing ROSAC vs AROSAC 3) Robustness evaluation against random and adversarial task sequences

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of ROSAC and AROSAC scale with the number of subtasks in the task sequence?
- Basis in paper: The paper demonstrates effectiveness on tasks with up to 25 subtasks in the F1/10th environment, but does not explore scaling beyond this
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide experiments or analysis on tasks with significantly longer sequences of subtasks
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing performance degradation (or lack thereof) as the number of subtasks in a sequence increases beyond 25

### Open Question 2
- Question: How sensitive are ROSAC and AROSAC to the choice of hyperparameters like learning rates, entropy weight, and Polyak rate?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions using grid search for hyperparameter selection but does not provide sensitivity analysis
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore how variations in these hyperparameters affect the performance of the algorithms
- What evidence would resolve it: Sensitivity analysis showing performance changes across a range of hyperparameter values for both ROSAC and AROSAC

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of ROSAC and AROSAC compare to other hierarchical RL methods like MAXQ or option-critic in compositional tasks?
- Basis in paper: The paper focuses on comparing against non-hierarchical baselines and PAIRED, but does not include comparisons with other hierarchical RL approaches
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a direct comparison between the proposed methods and established hierarchical RL algorithms
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing ROSAC and AROSAC against hierarchical RL methods like MAXQ or option-critic on the same compositional tasks

## Limitations
- The robustness guarantees depend on the adversary having perfect state observability, which may not hold in practice
- The stagewise parallel training assumes subtask independence that may not hold in real-world applications
- The approach has only been validated on relatively simple environments with limited subtask types

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The basic algorithmic framework (ROSAC/AROSAC) and its implementation in the two tested environments is sound and reproducible
- **Medium confidence**: The claimed robustness to arbitrary task sequences under adversarial selection, as this depends heavily on the adversary's capabilities and state observability
- **Low confidence**: The generalizability of the approach to significantly more complex environments with longer task sequences or more interdependent subtasks

## Next Checks
1. **State observability validation**: Test the algorithms with partial observability where the adversary cannot perfectly observe the current state, measuring how robustness degrades
2. **Subtask dependency stress test**: Design environments where subtask completion affects the difficulty of subsequent subtasks, measuring whether the stagewise parallel training still converges
3. **Adversary capability boundary**: Compare performance against increasingly sophisticated adversaries (random → MCTS → learned adversaries) to identify where robustness breaks down
