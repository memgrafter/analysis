# Attacks on Robust Distributed Learning Schemes via Sensitivity Curve Maximization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.14024
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14024
- Reference count: 28
- Key outcome: Sensitivity curve maximization attacks effectively disrupt robust aggregation schemes in distributed learning, even under moderate contamination rates.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a new attack method targeting robust aggregation schemes in distributed learning systems. The attack exploits the sensitivity curves of aggregation functions to find minimal perturbations that maximally influence the learning outcome. Through extensive experiments, the authors demonstrate that even established robust aggregation methods like α-trimmed mean, Talwar, and Biweight Tukey estimators can be broken by carefully crafted attacks, raising serious concerns about the security of distributed learning systems.

## Method Summary
The authors propose sensitivity curve maximization (SCM) as a targeted attack framework against robust aggregation schemes. The attack works by computing the sensitivity curve of the aggregation function for given benign samples, then finding the minimal perturbation that maximizes this sensitivity curve. The attack is evaluated in a decentralized learning setup with K=32 agents in an Erdős–Rényi graph, using linear models with Gaussian regressors and noise. Different contamination rates (9-38%) are tested to assess the attack's effectiveness under various conditions.

## Key Results
- The SCM attack successfully disrupts α-trimmed mean, Talwar, and Biweight Tukey estimators even at moderate contamination rates
- Attack effectiveness varies across aggregation schemes, with some being more vulnerable than others
- The attack maintains stealth while achieving significant disruption to model convergence
- Different SCM variants (α-SCM, Talwar-SCM, Tukey-SCM) show varying degrees of success against their respective targets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Sensitivity curve maximization allows attackers to find minimal perturbations that maximally influence robust aggregation schemes.
- Mechanism: The attack computes the sensitivity curve (SC) for each aggregation scheme, which quantifies how much an outlier affects the estimator. By maximizing the SC and minimizing the perturbation size, the attacker identifies optimal values that introduce the greatest bias while remaining undetected.
- Core assumption: The attacker has complete knowledge of the benign samples and the aggregation scheme being used.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We present a new attack based on sensitivity curve maximization (SCM), and demonstrate that it is able to disrupt existing robust aggregation schemes by injecting small, but effective perturbations."
  - [section III] "Finding the smallest value zopt m , which maximizes the SC, requires solving zopt m = min zm∈Cm |zm| Cm ≜ arg max zm (SC(Ym, Zm)) (12)"
- Break condition: The attack fails if the attacker lacks complete knowledge of benign samples or if the aggregation scheme's sensitivity curve cannot be accurately modeled.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Robust aggregation schemes can be broken by carefully crafted perturbations that exploit their sensitivity curves.
- Mechanism: Different aggregation schemes have different sensitivity curves. For example, the α-trimmed mean removes outliers beyond a certain threshold, so the attack injects values just below this threshold to maximize influence. For M-estimators like Talwar and Biweight Tukey, the attack calculates optimal values based on the tuning constant c to maximize the ψ function.
- Core assumption: The attacker can accurately model the sensitivity curve of the target aggregation scheme.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section III] "Talwar and Biweight Tukey: Both attack schemes will lead to a similar solution, which only differs in a constant c0. By solving c0 = arg max x ψ (x) (13)"
  - [section III] "Instead of using the (Nk − αN k − 1)-th weight, it is also possible to use the (Nk − αN k)-th weight minus some small value."
- Break condition: The attack fails if the sensitivity curve cannot be accurately modeled or if the aggregation scheme has a different underlying mechanism than assumed.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Even moderate contamination rates can break robust aggregation schemes when using sensitivity curve maximization attacks.
- Mechanism: The attack works iteratively over multiple rounds of learning, introducing small but steady distortions that accumulate over time. Even when the contamination rate is moderate (e.g., 9-38%), the targeted perturbations can cause the aggregation schemes to diverge.
- Core assumption: The learning process is iterative and the same aggregation scheme is used consistently across rounds.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section IV-B] "It can be observed that the aggregation scheme which is under attack usually has the worst performance or a significantly worse performance, compared to other attack schemes."
  - [section IV-B] "Even under moderate contamination rates the aggregation schemes fail, if a targeted SCM is applied."
- Break condition: The attack fails if the aggregation scheme changes between rounds or if there are mechanisms to detect and correct accumulated distortions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data collection -> Sensitivity curve computation -> Optimization -> Attack injection -> Aggregation

- Critical path:
  1. Collect benign samples Y
  2. Compute sensitivity curve SC(Y,z) for candidate perturbations z
  3. Solve optimization problem to find zopt that maximizes SC while minimizing |z|
  4. Inject zopt into aggregation step
  5. Repeat across dimensions and learning rounds

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs. stealth: Larger perturbations have greater impact but are more likely to be detected
  - Computational complexity: Computing sensitivity curves for all dimensions can be expensive
  - Knowledge requirements: Complete knowledge of benign samples yields best results but may not be realistic

- Failure signatures:
  - Aggregation scheme converges despite contamination
  - Detection of anomalous values at the fusion center
  - Performance degradation that doesn't match expected sensitivity curve patterns

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement sensitivity curve computation for sample mean, median, and α-trimmed mean on synthetic data
  2. Test the attack on a simple distributed learning setup with known aggregation scheme
  3. Compare attack effectiveness across different contamination rates and aggregation schemes

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How effective are random aggregation scheme selection and parameter obfuscation in preventing SCM attacks in real-world distributed learning scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions MixTailor [21] as a potential defense, which randomly selects aggregation schemes from a predefined set, and suggests combining this with randomly adapting tuning variables to make it harder for attackers to design optimal attack patterns.
- Why unresolved: While the paper discusses the potential of MixTailor as a defense, it does not provide experimental results demonstrating its effectiveness against SCM attacks in practical settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments comparing the performance of distributed learning systems using MixTailor against SCM attacks versus those using static aggregation schemes, measuring the impact on convergence and model accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the SCM attack be adapted to target non-element-wise aggregation schemes, and how would its effectiveness compare to element-wise attacks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on element-wise aggregation schemes but does not explore the possibility of extending the SCM attack to non-element-wise schemes, leaving open the question of its applicability and effectiveness in such scenarios.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate the feasibility or impact of applying SCM attacks to non-element-wise aggregation schemes, which are also commonly used in distributed learning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing SCM attack variants for non-element-wise aggregation schemes, comparing their effectiveness to element-wise attacks in terms of disrupting model convergence and accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do SCM attacks perform against adaptive robust aggregation schemes that dynamically adjust their parameters based on the observed data distribution?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper does not address the interaction between SCM attacks and adaptive robust aggregation schemes, which could potentially detect and mitigate the effects of such attacks in real-time.
- Why unresolved: The effectiveness of SCM attacks against adaptive robust aggregation schemes is unknown, as the paper does not explore this interaction or the potential for these schemes to counteract the attack.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing adaptive robust aggregation schemes and evaluating their resilience against SCM attacks, measuring the ability of the schemes to maintain model performance and convergence in the presence of the attack.

## Limitations

- The attack assumes complete knowledge of benign samples and aggregation scheme parameters, which may not be realistic in many practical scenarios
- The experimental setup uses synthetic data with specific statistical properties that may not generalize to real-world federated learning applications
- The attack's computational complexity for high-dimensional problems is not thoroughly discussed, raising questions about scalability

## Confidence

- High confidence in the theoretical framework of sensitivity curve maximization and its mathematical soundness
- Medium confidence in the attack's effectiveness across different aggregation schemes, as experimental results are based on controlled synthetic environments
- Medium confidence in the practical implications, as real-world deployment would face additional constraints and countermeasures
