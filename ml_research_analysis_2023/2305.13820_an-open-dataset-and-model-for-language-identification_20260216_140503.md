# An Open Dataset and Model for Language Identification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.13820
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13820
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Macro-average F1 score of 0.93 and false positive rate of 0.033 on FLORES-200 benchmark

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a language identification (LID) model trained on a manually audited dataset of 201 languages, achieving state-of-the-art performance with a macro-average F1 score of 0.93 and false positive rate of 0.033. The authors address the critical issue of data quality in LID by curating monolingual data from reliable sources and manually auditing a sample from each source and language. By using fastText with standard hyperparameters and focusing on high-quality, domain-matched training data, the model significantly outperforms existing open models, particularly for lower-resource languages.

## Method Summary
The authors curate a dataset of 121 million lines across 201 languages from reliable sources including news sites, Wikipedia, and religious texts, avoiding web-crawled datasets to ensure label reliability. They manually audit a sample from each source and language to eliminate noise and incorrect labels. The data is preprocessed using Moses scripts, filtered by script, and sampled proportionally to p^0.3 to mitigate class imbalance. A fastText model with n-grams 2-5, embedding dimension 256, and other standard hyperparameters is trained for 2 epochs. The model is evaluated on the FLORES-200 benchmark, achieving a macro-average F1 score of 0.93 and false positive rate of 0.033.

## Key Results
- Macro-average F1 score of 0.93 and false positive rate of 0.033 on FLORES-200 benchmark
- Significant improvement on lower-resource languages compared to prior open models
- Strong performance across language classes, with particular challenges for Arabic dialects and Chinese languages

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Manual auditing of training data ensures higher label accuracy, which leads to better model performance.
- Mechanism: By manually checking a sample of data from each source and language, the authors eliminate noise and incorrect labels that commonly plague web-crawled datasets.
- Core assumption: Human inspection can reliably identify language mismatches even in unfamiliar scripts using heuristics like diacritics, word patterns, and script consistency.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We achieve this by training on a curated dataset of monolingual data, the reliability of which we ensure by auditing a sample from each source and each language manually."
  - [section]: "We then carried out a manual audit of a random sample of all data sources and languages: one a native Bulgarian speaker (able to read Cyrillic and Latin scripts and Chinese characters), and the other a native English speaker (able to read Latin, Arabic and Hebrew scripts)."
- Break condition: If the auditing sample is too small or unrepresentative, systematic errors may persist undetected.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using domain-matched, high-quality sources improves generalization across language classes.
- Mechanism: Selecting data from reliable domains (news sites, Wikipedia, religious texts) with trusted language labels reduces domain shift between training and test data.
- Core assumption: Formal text domains provide cleaner language signals than web-crawled data with mixed content.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "We wanted to be as confident as possible that our dataset had reliable language labels... We therefore avoided web-crawled datasets and instead chose sources where we felt the collection methodology made it very likely that the language labels were correct."
  - [section]: "We checked that each dataset was either under an open license for research purposes or described as free to use."
- Break condition: If the test set contains informal or code-switched language, the model's performance may degrade.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Proportional sampling by language class size (p^0.3) mitigates class imbalance effects.
- Mechanism: By sampling data proportionally to p^0.3 (where p_l is the fraction of lines in the dataset which are in language l), the authors reduce the dominance of high-resource languages in training.
- Core assumption: This sampling strategy preserves enough examples for each class while still providing sufficient data for training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Finally, we followed Arivazhagan et al. (2019) and Costa-jussà et al. (2022) and sampled proportionally to p^0.3 l , where p_l is the fraction of lines in the dataset which are in language l. This aims to ameliorate class skew issues."
- Break condition: If the exponent is too low, high-resource languages may still dominate; if too high, low-resource classes may lack sufficient examples.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: The model uses character-level n-grams to capture language-specific orthographic patterns, which is effective for LID tasks.

  - Why needed here: Macro-averaged F1 and false positive rate are used to fairly assess performance across all languages, including low-resource ones.
  - Quick check question: Why is macro-average preferred over micro-average when evaluating LID systems with many languages?

  - Why needed here: Ensures the reliability of language labels, which is critical for building a high-quality LID model.
  - Quick check question: What are the key indicators a human auditor should look for when verifying language labels in an unfamiliar script?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: fastText model -> character n-gram embeddings -> linear classifier -> output probabilities for 201 languages
- Critical path: Data collection -> manual auditing -> preprocessing -> proportional sampling -> fastText training -> evaluation
- Design tradeoffs: Manual auditing ensures data quality but is time-consuming; using only formal text domains limits domain coverage but increases label reliability.
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on closely related languages, high false positive rates for certain classes, domain mismatch between training and test data.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train the model without manual auditing to quantify the impact of data quality on performance.
  2. Test the model on informal text or code-switched data to assess domain robustness.
  3. Vary the sampling exponent (p^l) to find the optimal balance between class representation and overall performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can language identification systems be improved for distinguishing closely related languages and dialects, particularly Arabic varieties and Chinese languages?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that Arabic dialects and Chinese languages pose particular challenges, with F1 scores for Arabic dialects as low as 0.03-0.49 and confusion between Yue Chinese and other Chinese varieties.
- Why unresolved: Despite extensive work on LID, these language families remain difficult to distinguish due to high linguistic similarity, sparse training data for dialects, and domain mismatches between training and test data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of domain-specific training data, exploration of cross-lingual transfer learning, and development of dialect-specific features could provide insights into better handling of closely related languages.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal balance between training data quantity and quality for low-resource languages in LID systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that Pearson correlation between training data size and F1 score is only 0.0242, suggesting quality and domain matching matter more than quantity, especially for lower-resourced languages.
- Why unresolved: While the authors manually audit their dataset, they cannot quantify the exact impact of their quality assurance process versus data quantity, as they lack access to the training data used by other models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying both data quality (through manual auditing) and quantity for the same languages would reveal the relative importance of each factor.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can LID systems be made more robust across different domains and writing styles?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The case study of Yue Chinese reveals a domain mismatch between colloquial training data and formal test data, leading to poor performance.
- Why unresolved: Most LID test sets use formal text, but real-world applications often encounter informal or domain-specific language, and current systems show sensitivity to these variations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Creation and evaluation of LID test sets spanning multiple domains (news, social media, literature, etc.) would reveal domain robustness issues and guide improvements.

## Limitations

- The manual auditing process introduces potential human bias and scalability constraints, and the sample size for auditing is not explicitly specified.
- The model's performance on informal text, code-switched content, and domain-specific jargon remains untested, as the training data is limited to formal sources.
- The proportional sampling strategy (p^0.3) is empirically chosen without rigorous justification for the exponent value.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The reported macro-F1 score of 0.93 and false positive rate of 0.033 on the FLORES-200 benchmark, as these are based on standardized evaluation.
- **Medium Confidence**: Claims about improved performance on lower-resource languages, as this requires careful analysis of class-specific metrics and comparison with prior work.
- **Medium Confidence**: The assertion that training data quality and domain matching are critical factors, as this is supported by qualitative analysis but would benefit from controlled ablation studies.
