# SAM Meets Robotic Surgery: An Empirical Study in Robustness Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2304.14674
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14674
- **Reference count**: 23
- **Key outcome**: SAM achieves superior zero-shot generalization with bounding box prompts for surgical instrument segmentation but requires domain-specific fine-tuning before practical deployment

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates the Segment Anything Model (SAM) for surgical instrument segmentation in robotic surgery scenarios using the EndoVis 2017 and 2018 datasets. The study investigates SAM's performance with different prompting strategies, its robustness to various data corruptions, and compares it against state-of-the-art supervised models. Results demonstrate that while SAM shows strong zero-shot performance with bounding box prompts, it struggles with point-based prompts and complex surgical scenarios involving overlapping instruments and image artifacts. The model exhibits significant performance degradation under common data corruptions, indicating the need for further domain adaptation before clinical deployment.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates SAM's zero-shot performance on robotic surgical instrument segmentation tasks using two benchmark datasets (EndoVis 2017 and 2018). The evaluation examines three prompting strategies: bounding box prompts, point prompts, and unprompted settings. Performance is measured using standard segmentation metrics (Dice, mIoU) and compared against state-of-the-art supervised models. The study also assesses robustness by applying various data corruptions (noise, blur, weather, digital) and analyzing failure modes in complex surgical scenarios involving blood, reflection, and occlusion. Qualitative analysis examines SAM's ability to segment instrument parts (shaft, jaws, wrist) in challenging conditions.

## Key Results
- SAM outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods with bounding box prompts in both binary and instrument-wise segmentation tasks
- SAM struggles significantly with point-based prompts and unprompted settings, failing to segment entire instruments or distinguish overlapping instruments
- Performance degrades substantially under various data corruptions, with accuracy drops ranging from 10-30% depending on corruption type and severity
- SAM cannot accurately predict certain instrument parts (jaws, wrist) in complex surgical scenarios involving blood, reflection, blur, and shade

  - *Why needed*: Enables rapid deployment without expensive annotation requirements
  - *Quick check*: Measure performance drop when applying model to out-of-distribution data

- **Robustness Testing**: Systematic evaluation of model performance under various data corruptions
  - *Why needed*: Real-world surgical scenarios involve many image quality variations
  - *Quick check*: Apply standardized corruption benchmarks and measure degradation patterns

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Image Encoder -> Prompt Encoder -> Mask Decoder -> Output Masks

**Critical Path**: Image input → Feature extraction → Prompt conditioning → Mask prediction → Post-processing

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- SAM trades computational efficiency for flexibility in prompt-based segmentation
- Zero-shot capability comes at the cost of performance compared to specialized models
- Bounding box prompts provide better localization but require more user input than point prompts

**Failure Signatures**: 
- Inability to segment entire instruments with point prompts
- Confusion between overlapping instruments in complex scenes
- Significant accuracy drops under image corruptions (noise, blur, weather)
- Failure to detect instrument parts in presence of blood/reflection

**Three First Experiments**:
1. Compare bounding box vs point prompt performance across all segmentation metrics
2. Measure accuracy degradation under progressive levels of Gaussian noise
3. Evaluate segmentation of individual instrument parts (shaft, jaws, wrist) in clean vs corrupted images

- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that SAM "requires further domain-specific fine-tuning before being ready for downstream surgical tasks" and demonstrates significant performance degradation under various data corruptions and complex surgical scenarios.
- Why unresolved: While the paper identifies the need for fine-tuning, it does not specify what architectural changes or domain adaptation techniques would be most effective for surgical instrument segmentation.

- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates SAM's zero-shot performance and does not investigate its performance after fine-tuning on surgical instrument datasets.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the optimal prompt strategy for SAM when segmenting surgical instruments, particularly for distinguishing between overlapping instruments and handling complex surgical scenarios?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that SAM "struggles to segment the whole instrument with point-based prompts and unprompted settings" and "encounters difficulties when applied to more complicated scenes, as it struggles to differentiate between the entirety of instrument articulating parts accurately."
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates SAM's limitations with different prompt types, it does not explore alternative prompt strategies or investigate what combination of prompts would be optimal for surgical instrument segmentation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing various prompt strategies (e.g., combinations of bounding boxes and points, region-based prompts, multi-point prompts) on surgical instrument segmentation tasks, with quantitative evaluation of performance improvements.

## Limitations
- Limited evaluation to only four types of data corruptions without exploring surgical-specific artifacts like smoke, steam, or varying lighting conditions
- Qualitative rather than quantitative analysis of failure modes in complex surgical scenarios, making clinical impact assessment difficult
- No investigation of computational efficiency or real-time performance constraints for surgical applications
- Comparison with supervised models doesn't account for potential performance gains from domain-specific fine-tuning of SAM
