# Revisiting Self-Training with Regularized Pseudo-Labeling for Tabular Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.14013
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14013
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Regularized curriculum pseudo-labeling consistently achieves lowest rank in performance comparisons across diverse tabular datasets and models.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses limitations in pseudo-labeling approaches for semi-supervised learning on tabular data by introducing two novel methods: curriculum pseudo-labeling and regularized pseudo-labeling. Curriculum pseudo-labeling progressively adds pseudo-labeled samples based on confidence percentile rather than a fixed threshold, while regularized pseudo-labeling incorporates feature likelihoods into confidence scores to ensure pseudo-labels lie in high-density regions. Extensive experiments on diverse binary and multiclass tabular datasets with multiple models demonstrate that both methods outperform existing approaches, with regularized curriculum pseudo-labeling achieving the most consistent improvements.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes self-training with two novel pseudo-labeling strategies for tabular data. Curriculum pseudo-labeling replaces fixed confidence thresholds with percentile-based selection that gradually incorporates more samples during training cycles. Regularized pseudo-labeling enhances confidence scores by incorporating empirical feature likelihoods given the pseudo-label, ensuring selected samples lie in high-density regions consistent with the cluster assumption. The methods are tested across diverse tabular datasets using XGBoost, LightGBM, TabTransformer, and neural networks, with performance evaluated using F1-score for imbalanced datasets and accuracy for others.

## Key Results
- Curriculum pseudo-labeling improves performance across multiple models and datasets compared to fixed threshold approaches
- Regularized pseudo-labeling achieves lowest average rank in performance comparisons, demonstrating consistent effectiveness
- Both methods are compatible with various models including gradient boosting and neural networks without requiring architectural constraints

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Curriculum pseudo-labeling progressively adds pseudo-labeled samples based on confidence percentile rather than a fixed threshold, improving performance across multiple models and datasets.
- Mechanism: Instead of using a fixed threshold for pseudo-label selection, the method starts with the top r% of samples by confidence and gradually increases the percentile during the self-training cycle until all unlabeled data is used. This approach adapts to the evolving quality of the model's predictions.
- Core assumption: Confidence percentiles better capture the distribution of prediction quality over time than a static threshold, allowing more reliable pseudo-labels to be selected as training progresses.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "First, it introduces curriculum pseudo-labeling, which progressively adds pseudo-labeled samples based on confidence percentile rather than a fixed threshold, improving performance across multiple models and datasets."
  - [section 2.2]: "Curriculum pseudo-labeling does not use a fixed threshold which is widely used in the tabular domain, but instead uses percentile scores to decide which samples to add."
- Break condition: If the model's confidence scores become poorly calibrated or if the percentile threshold grows too quickly, low-quality pseudo-labels could be incorporated, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Regularized pseudo-labeling incorporates feature likelihoods into the confidence score to ensure pseudo-labels lie in high-density regions, aligning with the cluster assumption.
- Mechanism: The confidence score is adjusted by a likelihood term computed from the empirical distribution of each feature given the pseudo-label. This regularization favors pseudo-labels that are both confident and likely under the observed feature distributions.
- Core assumption: In tabular data, the position of each feature has a fixed meaning, so the likelihood of a feature value given a class can be reliably estimated from the training data. High-density regions in feature space correspond to high likelihood.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Second, it proposes regularized pseudo-labeling, which incorporates feature likelihoods into the confidence score to ensure pseudo-labels lie in high-density regions, aligning with the cluster assumption."
  - [section 3.2]: "We propose a regularized pseudo-labeling approach that uses a regularized confidence score that can guarantee the cluster assumption by generating pseudo-labels that have high confidence scores and likelihoods."
- Break condition: If feature independence is violated or if the empirical distributions are poorly estimated (e.g., due to limited data or outliers), the likelihood term could mislead the selection process.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The proposed methods are compatible with various models and datasets, outperforming existing methods in exhaustive experiments.
- Mechanism: By avoiding model-specific architectural constraints and dataset composition requirements, the methods can be applied to any classifier, including gradient boosting decision trees and neural networks, on diverse tabular datasets.
- Core assumption: The effectiveness of pseudo-labeling does not depend on the specific model architecture or dataset characteristics, as long as the classifier can produce reasonable confidence scores.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Experiments on diverse binary and multiclass tabular datasets with models like XGBoost, LightGBM, TabTransformer, and neural networks show that both curriculum and regularized pseudo-labeling outperform existing methods."
  - [section 4.3]: Multiple tables and figures demonstrate consistent performance gains across models and datasets.
- Break condition: If a model cannot produce calibrated confidence scores or if the dataset is too small or imbalanced, the pseudo-labeling may not be effective.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Classifier -> Pseudo-labeler -> Likelihood estimator (for regularized method) -> Self-training loop

- Critical path:
  1. Train initial classifier on labeled data
  2. Generate pseudo-labels using the pseudo-labeler
  3. Combine labeled and pseudo-labeled data
  4. Retrain classifier on combined data
  5. Repeat until convergence or maximum iterations

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fixed threshold vs. curriculum percentile: Fixed threshold is simpler but may be suboptimal; curriculum percentile adapts but requires tuning of initial and step sizes.
  - Likelihood regularization: Improves reliability of pseudo-labels but adds computational overhead and depends on accurate feature likelihood estimation.
  - Early stopping and reinitialization: Helps prevent confirmation bias but may slow convergence.

- Failure signatures:
  - Performance degradation over iterations (confirmation bias)
  - High variance in results across folds (unstable pseudo-label selection)
  - No improvement over supervised baseline (poor confidence scores or insufficient unlabeled data)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement and test fixed threshold pseudo-labeling as a baseline on a small tabular dataset with XGBoost.
  2. Replace the fixed threshold with curriculum pseudo-labeling and compare performance.
  3. Add likelihood regularization to the curriculum method and evaluate the impact on a binary classification task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the regularized pseudo-labeling method perform on tabular datasets with non-independent features, where the assumption of feature independence may not hold?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper assumes feature independence when computing likelihoods in the regularized pseudo-labeling approach, but acknowledges this may not reflect real-world tabular data characteristics.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide empirical results on datasets with correlated features to validate the robustness of the method when this assumption is violated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing regularized pseudo-labeling performance on datasets with both independent and correlated features would demonstrate its effectiveness across different feature dependency scenarios.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the curriculum pseudo-labeling approach be further optimized by dynamically adjusting the initial and stepping thresholds based on dataset characteristics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses fixed initial and stepping thresholds for curriculum pseudo-labeling, which may not be optimal for all datasets.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore adaptive threshold strategies that could potentially improve performance on diverse tabular datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Results comparing fixed versus adaptive threshold strategies on a wide range of tabular datasets would show whether dynamic adjustment leads to significant performance gains.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of regularized pseudo-labeling scale with extremely high-dimensional tabular data, such as those with thousands of features?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using feature selection to reduce computational complexity but does not address performance in very high-dimensional spaces.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide experiments or analysis on datasets with extremely high feature dimensions, leaving uncertainty about scalability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments on high-dimensional tabular datasets with and without feature selection would demonstrate the method's effectiveness and computational feasibility in such scenarios.

## Limitations

- Fixed threshold percentile increment rate is not specified, leaving potential performance variability unaddressed
- Feature likelihood estimation assumes feature independence, which may not hold in real tabular datasets
- The impact of sample size on empirical distribution estimation for likelihoods is not quantified
- Cross-dataset hyperparameter transferability is assumed but not empirically validated
