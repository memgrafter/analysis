# Monge, Bregman and Occam: Interpretable Optimal Transport in High-Dimensions with Feature-Sparse Maps

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.04065
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04065
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Feature-sparse optimal transport maps for interpretable high-dimensional analysis without dimensionality reduction

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to optimal transport (OT) in high-dimensional spaces by constructing translation-invariant cost functions with sparsity-inducing regularizers. The authors propose using elastic-type costs h(z) = 1/2||z||^2 + τ(z), where τ is a sparsity-inducing function, and demonstrate a surprising connection between the resulting transport maps and Bregman centroids. Their method produces feature-sparse displacement vectors that adapt to input data, enabling interpretable OT in extremely high-dimensional spaces without dimensionality reduction.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a new family of translation-invariant costs for optimal transport, built on elastic-type regularizers that combine a strongly-convex quadratic term with a sparsity-inducing norm. These costs induce feature-sparse displacement vectors in the resulting transport maps, which can be computed using entropic regularization and expressed as Bregman centroids. The method is validated on synthetic datasets and applied to single-cell RNA sequencing data with 34,000 dimensions, demonstrating improved performance and interpretability compared to traditional ℓ2^2 costs.

## Key Results
- Proposed method produces feature-sparse displacement vectors that adapt to input data
- Successfully recovers meaningful transport maps in extremely high-dimensional spaces without dimensionality reduction
- Applied to single-cell RNA sequencing data (34,000 dimensions), showing improved performance and interpretability
- Method enables direct interpretation at the gene level by maintaining sparse displacement patterns

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Using translation-invariant elastic costs with sparsity-inducing regularizers produces transport maps with feature-sparse displacements
- Mechanism: The elastic cost function h(z) = 1/2||z||^2 + τ(z) combines a strongly convex quadratic term with a sparsity-inducing regularizer τ. When τ is chosen as an ℓ1 norm or similar sparsity-inducing function, the resulting transport maps naturally produce sparse displacement vectors where only a subset of features change between source and target points.
- Core assumption: The sparsity-inducing regularizer τ has a well-defined proximal operator and the overall cost h remains strongly convex
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose a new model for transport maps, built on a family of translation invariant costs c(x, y):=h(x-y), where h:=1/2||·||^2+τ and τ is a regularizer."
  - [section]: "When h is an elastic-type regularizer, the sum of a strongly-convex term ||·||^2/2 and a sparsifying norm τ, we show that such centroids are obtained using the proximal operator of τ."
- Break condition: If τ is not chosen to be sparsity-inducing or if the proximal operator becomes ill-defined for the chosen τ

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The entropic map estimator for elastic-type costs produces Bregman centroids relative to the divergence generated by h
- Mechanism: The generalized entropic map Th,ε(x) can be expressed as x - Ch((x-yj)j, (pj(x))j), where Ch represents a Bregman centroid. This formulation naturally produces sparse displacements because the Bregman centroid computation with elastic costs inherently promotes sparsity through the proximal operator.
- Core assumption: The cost function h is strongly convex and its convex conjugate is differentiable everywhere
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "When h is the ℓ2^2 cost, and when µ and ν can be accessed through samples, a convenient estimator for f* and subsequently T* is the entropic map. We generalize these estimators for arbitrary costs h."
  - [section]: "The entropic map estimator for h evaluated at x ∈ Ω_ν̂m(h) is x - ∇h* ◦ ∇fε(x). This simplifies to: Th,ε(x) := x - Ch((x - yj)j, (pj(x))j)"
- Break condition: If h is not strongly convex or if the Bregman centroid computation fails to converge

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The proposed method enables interpretable OT in extremely high-dimensional spaces without dimensionality reduction
- Mechanism: By constructing transport maps that are sparse in feature space rather than sample space, the method preserves interpretability at the original feature level. Each displacement vector directly indicates which features (e.g., genes in single-cell data) changed during transport, allowing direct interpretation without losing information through dimensionality reduction.
- Core assumption: The original feature space contains meaningful structure that can be captured through sparse displacements
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We showcase the ability of our method to estimate meaningful OT maps for high-dimensional single-cell transcription data, in the 34000-d space of gene counts for cells, without using dimensionality reduction, thus retaining the ability to interpret all displacements at the gene level."
  - [section]: "We show that this approach succeeds in recovering meaningful maps in extremely high-dimension."
- Break condition: If the original feature space is too noisy or irrelevant features dominate the transport problem

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Sinkhorn algorithm -> computes entropic dual potentials fε and gε
  - Bregman centroid computation -> calculates the transport map using the gradient of dual potentials
  - Sparsity-inducing proximal operator -> applies element-wise shrinkage based on the chosen regularizer τ
  - Evaluation pipeline -> measures performance using MSE, support error, and interpretability metrics

- Critical path:
  1. Compute kernel matrix K from input samples and cost function h
  2. Run Sinkhorn algorithm to obtain optimal dual potentials
  3. Calculate the generalized entropic map using Bregman centroids
  4. Apply sparsity through the proximal operator of τ
  5. Evaluate the resulting transport map's sparsity and accuracy

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Regularization strength vs. sparsity: Higher regularization produces sparser displacements but may reduce transport accuracy
  - Choice of sparsity-inducing norm: Different norms (ℓ1, k-support, STVS) offer different sparsity patterns and computational costs
  - Dimensionality vs. interpretability: The method trades computational efficiency for maintaining interpretability in high dimensions

- Failure signatures:
  - Transport maps that are too sparse (almost all displacements zero) indicate over-regularization
  - Maps that fail to capture the target distribution suggest insufficient regularization or poor choice of cost function
  - Computational instability during Sinkhorn iterations may indicate numerical issues with the chosen cost function

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the theoretical guarantees for the convergence and stability of entropic map estimators when using elastic-type costs with sparsity-inducing regularizers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the entropic map estimators are defined almost everywhere and highlights a connection with Bregman centroids, but does not provide rigorous convergence guarantees or stability analysis for the resulting transport maps.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on the practical implementation and empirical validation of the method but does not delve into the theoretical analysis of convergence and stability properties, which are crucial for understanding the reliability of the approach in various scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Rigorous mathematical proofs demonstrating the convergence of the entropic map estimators to the true optimal transport map, along with stability analysis showing how perturbations in the data affect the estimated maps, would provide the necessary theoretical guarantees.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of sparsity-inducing regularizer (e.g., ℓ1, STVS, k-overlap) impact the interpretability and performance of transport maps in different application domains?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces three sparsity-inducing regularizers (ℓ1, STVS, k-overlap) and applies them to single-cell RNA sequencing data, showing that they produce sparse displacement patterns. However, it does not systematically compare their performance across different domains or provide guidelines for choosing the most appropriate regularizer.
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method in a specific application, it does not explore how the choice of regularizer affects the interpretability and performance of transport maps in other domains, such as image processing or natural language processing.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive study comparing the performance and interpretability of transport maps obtained using different sparsity-inducing regularizers across various application domains would provide insights into the strengths and limitations of each regularizer and guide their selection in practice.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the proposed method be extended to handle time-varying optimal transport problems, where the source and target distributions change over time?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on static optimal transport problems, but the concept of Bregman centroids and entropic maps could potentially be adapted to handle time-varying scenarios by incorporating temporal dynamics into the cost function or regularization terms.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore the application of the proposed method to time-varying optimal transport problems, which are relevant in many real-world scenarios, such as tracking cell development over time or analyzing evolving social networks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing a framework that extends the proposed method to handle time-varying optimal transport problems, along with empirical validation on datasets with temporal dynamics, would demonstrate the applicability and effectiveness of the approach in this setting.

## Limitations
- Limited theoretical analysis of convergence and stability properties for the proposed method
- Empirical validation primarily focused on single-cell RNA sequencing data, raising questions about generalization to other domains
- Scalability to extremely high-dimensional data may be limited by computational complexity of proximal operators
