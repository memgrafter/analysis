# Reinterpreting causal discovery as the task of predicting unobserved joint statistics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.06894
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06894
- Reference count: 35
- The paper reinterprets causal discovery as predicting statistical properties of unobserved joint distributions

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a novel reinterpretation of causal discovery as a standard prediction problem. Rather than viewing causal discovery as inferring the underlying causal structure, it frames the task as predicting statistical properties (like conditional independences or correlations) for variable combinations that have never been jointly observed. This pragmatic view allows application of statistical learning theory to derive generalization bounds for causal discovery. The paper demonstrates that causal models can serve as predictors, with their expressiveness characterized by VC dimension, and validates this approach through experiments showing empirical error converging to expected error as predicted by theory.

## Method Summary
The paper reinterprets causal discovery by treating statistical properties of variable subsets as labels in a prediction task. Given multiple datasets with overlapping but different variable sets, the goal is to predict statistical properties for unobserved combinations. Causal models like DAGs or additive noise models are viewed as predictors that map from variable subsets to their statistical properties. The richness of the causal model class is quantified via VC dimension, enabling application of statistical learning theory to derive generalization bounds. The framework focuses on two types of statistical properties: conditional independences (for DAG models) and additive noise model parameters (for polytrees). The approach leverages the fact that causal models induce constraints on observable statistics, which can be learned from data and used to predict unobserved statistics.

## Key Results
- Conditional independence tests from a DAG model can reliably predict unseen independences in synthetic experiments
- Polytree models can predict additive noise model properties with bounded generalization error
- Empirical error approaches expected error as predicted by learning theory bounds when the number of training tests increases

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works because causal models impose deterministic constraints on statistical properties. Once a causal model is learned from observed data, it encodes relationships that allow prediction of unseen statistical properties. The VC dimension of the model class provides a measure of expressiveness that bounds generalization error. By treating statistical properties as labels and causal models as predictors, the problem becomes amenable to standard learning theory analysis, allowing derivation of PAC-style generalization bounds for causal discovery.

## Foundational Learning
- **VC dimension**: Measures the expressiveness of a hypothesis class; needed to bound generalization error in learning theory. Quick check: Can be computed for linear classifiers but often hard for complex models.
- **PAC learning**: Framework for probably approximately correct learning; provides theoretical guarantees on generalization. Quick check: Requires finite hypothesis space or bounded VC dimension.
- **Conditional independence testing**: Core statistical tool for causal discovery; identifies d-separation in DAGs. Quick check: Can be performed using various statistical tests (e.g., chi-square, mutual information).
- **DAG models**: Directed acyclic graphs representing causal structure; encode conditional independence constraints. Quick check: Every DAG induces a set of d-separation constraints.
- **Additive noise models**: Causal models where effects are functions of causes plus independent noise; identifiable from observational data. Quick check: Y = f(X) + N where N is independent of X.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Observed datasets -> Statistical property estimation -> Causal model learning -> Generalization bound computation -> Prediction of unobserved properties
- **Critical path**: The most time-consuming step is typically learning the causal model from statistical tests, especially for large variable sets
- **Design tradeoffs**: Expressiveness vs. generalization - more complex models can fit training data better but may generalize worse
- **Failure signatures**: High empirical error despite low training error suggests overfitting; inability to predict observed properties indicates model misspecification
- **First experiments**:
  1. Verify VC dimension bounds for simple DAG classes (e.g., polytrees)
  2. Test conditional independence prediction on synthetic data with known structure
  3. Compare prediction performance across different causal model classes

## Limitations
- Only applies to causal models expressible as deterministic functions of statistical properties
- Theoretical framework relies on finite VC dimension, which may not hold for expressive model classes
- Empirical validation limited to synthetic datasets and small-scale problems

## Confidence
- VC dimension application to causal discovery: High
- Generalization bounds derivation: High
- Empirical validation on synthetic data: Medium
- Applicability to real-world causal discovery: Low
