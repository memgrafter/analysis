# Investigating the Utility of Surprisal from Large Language Models for Speech Synthesis Prosody

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.09814
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09814
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Surprisal values from LLMs show moderate correlation with word prominence but provide minimal improvement in speech synthesis prosody.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether word surprisal values derived from large language models (LLMs) can improve speech synthesis prosody by making prominence patterns more appropriate. Using the LJ Speech dataset and GPT-2/GPT-J models, the researchers examine the relationship between surprisal and word prominence across varying context lengths and model sizes. While surprisal and prominence show moderate correlation, the results indicate that surprisal-based conditioning provides only minimal improvement over baseline speech synthesis, with longer contexts and larger models generally underpredicting prominent words.

## Method Summary
The researchers extracted word surprisal values from GPT-2 and GPT-J models for context lengths of 0-5 sentences, then computed prominence estimates using continuous wavelet transform (CWT) on the LJ Speech dataset. They trained FastPitch models with local conditioning on surprisal values and compared performance against baseline and prominence-conditioned systems. Objective evaluation was conducted using RMSE and Pearson correlation for f0 and duration predictions, along with correlation analysis between surprisal and prominence/acoustic features.

## Key Results
- Word surprisal and word prominence show moderate correlation, though the relationship is described as weak
- Longer contexts and larger models generally underpredict the prominence of given words
- Surprisal-conditioned speech synthesis provides minimal improvement over baseline, suggesting limited effectiveness for eliciting appropriate prominence patterns

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Surprisal values can capture contextual predictability of words.
- Mechanism: LLMs assign probabilities to word sequences, with surprisal computed as negative log-likelihood. Lower surprisal indicates higher predictability.
- Core assumption: LLM probability estimates accurately reflect human perception of word predictability.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "word surprisal, a measure of the predictability of a word in a given context"; [section] "surprisal has been shown in the literature to be connected to the impression of highlighting, and in prosodic terms, to the phenomenon of prosodic prominence"
- Break condition: If LLM probability estimates diverge significantly from human perception.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Prominence in speech correlates with surprisal values.
- Mechanism: Words with high surprisal (low predictability) are more likely to be realized with prosodic prominence as they carry more new information.
- Core assumption: The relationship between surprisal and prominence is consistent enough to be useful for speech synthesis.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Our results show that word surprisal and word prominence are moderately correlated"; [section] "The general observation was that prominence correlated with surprisal rather weakly"
- Break condition: If the correlation between surprisal and prominence is too weak to be useful.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Conditioning a speech synthesizer with surprisal values can improve prosody.
- Mechanism: Incorporating surprisal values as additional features during training allows the synthesizer to adjust prosodic parameters based on word predictability.
- Core assumption: The synthesizer can effectively use surprisal values to modify its output, leading to more natural prosody.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We demonstrate that, in line with these findings, a speech synthesizer conditioned with surprisal values provides a minimal improvement over the baseline"; [section] "The synthesis results also suggest a limited efficacy of using surprisal values for eliciting appropriate prominence patterns"
- Break condition: If the improvement over the baseline is too minimal to justify the additional complexity.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: LLM (GPT-2 or GPT-J) -> FastSpeech model -> CWT prominence estimation -> LJ Speech dataset
- Critical path:
  1. Extract word surprisal values from LLM for each word in the corpus
  2. Compute prominence values using CWT on the speech signal
  3. Train FastSpeech model with or without surprisal conditioning
  4. Evaluate synthesized speech using objective metrics (RMSE, correlation)
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using larger LLMs vs. smaller ones: Larger models may capture more context but can under-predict prominence
  - Longer context vs. shorter context: Longer context may improve givenness modeling but can also lead to under-prediction
  - Continuous vs. discrete prominence values: Continuous values may be more informative but harder to model
- Failure signatures:
  - Minimal improvement over baseline: Indicates that surprisal values are not effectively improving prosody
  - Inconsistent or erratic behavior: Suggests that the synthesizer is not properly utilizing the surprisal conditioning
  - Poor correlation between surprisal and prominence: Implies that the underlying assumption of the mechanism is flawed
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train FastSpeech with surprisal conditioning using GPT-2 small and compare to baseline
  2. Train FastSpeech with surprisal conditioning using GPT-J and longer context, compare to baseline

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Why do larger language models consistently underpredict the prominence of given words in longer contexts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "LLMs consistently underestimate the prominence of given words in longer contexts" and notes this is "partly due to their better memory, but the words that are repeated are also often worth repeating, and thus uttered prominently."
- Why unresolved: The exact mechanisms behind why larger models' superior memory leads to underprediction of prominence for repeated words, despite their tendency to be emphasized in speech, remain unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing how different model sizes handle repeated words in various contexts, combined with human perception studies on prominence patterns.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we effectively integrate phrase-level prosodic patterns (like phrase endings and first names vs surnames) into surprisal-based models for better prominence prediction?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that "the language models naturally tend to assign low surprisal for the phrase endings and first names instead of surnames, contradicting the typical English pattern of assigning stress to the final word."
- Why unresolved: Current surprisal models struggle to capture these inherent phrase-level prosodic patterns that don't align with surprisal values, leading to incorrect prominence predictions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing hybrid models that combine surprisal with explicit phrase-level prosodic features and evaluating their performance on prominence prediction tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the role of human interaction and communication context in shaping prosodic patterns that surprisal-based models cannot capture?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that "human interaction and context of communication, namely, what is informative and relevant to the audience, plays a significant role in determining prosody, something that the introspective surprisal values can not model."
- Why unresolved: Surprisal models are trained on static text corpora and lack the dynamic, audience-aware aspects of human communication that influence prosodic choices.
- What evidence would resolve it: Studies comparing prominence patterns in texts with different intended audiences or communication purposes, and incorporating these contextual factors into language models.

## Limitations
- Single-speaker dataset limits generalizability of findings across different speakers
- Objective evaluation only, without human perceptual validation of prosodic naturalness
- Fixed synthesis architecture may not reveal whether limited effectiveness is architecture-specific

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The experimental methodology and technical implementation are sound. The moderate correlation between surprisal and prominence is empirically demonstrated with appropriate statistical measures.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim that surprisal provides "minimal improvement" over baseline is supported by objective metrics but lacks human perceptual validation. The interpretation of why longer contexts and larger models under-predict prominence is plausible but not definitively proven.

**Medium Confidence**: The conclusion that "LLM-based surprisal offers limited effectiveness" for improving prominence patterns is reasonable given the experimental results, though it may be premature without exploring alternative architectures or conditioning strategies.

## Next Checks

**Validation Check 1**: Conduct a human perceptual evaluation study comparing baseline, surprisal-conditioned, and prominence-conditioned synthesis outputs to determine whether minimal objective improvements translate to perceptible differences in naturalness and appropriateness of prominence patterns.

**Validation Check 2**: Replicate the experiments on a multi-speaker dataset to assess whether the relationship between surprisal and prominence varies across speakers, helping determine if findings are generalizable beyond the single-speaker LJ Speech corpus.

**Validation Check 3**: Test alternative conditioning strategies, such as incorporating surprisal at different levels of the synthesis architecture or using hierarchical conditioning approaches, to reveal whether limited effectiveness is due to the specific conditioning method rather than the surprisal feature itself.
