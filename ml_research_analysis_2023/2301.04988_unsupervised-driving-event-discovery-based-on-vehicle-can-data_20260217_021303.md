# Unsupervised Driving Event Discovery Based on Vehicle CAN-data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2301.04988
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04988
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: Unsupervised method achieves macro F1 of 0.469 on 13 driving event types from Tesla Model 3 CAN-data using VAME* generative SSL model.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents an unsupervised approach to discover driving events from vehicle CAN-data by simultaneously clustering and segmenting multivariate time series. The method uses self-supervised learning (SSL) to encode each time step into similar representations for events of the same type, enabling fine-grained clustering and segmentation. Evaluated on a two-hour annotated Tesla Model 3 driving session with 13 event labels, the generative SSL model VAME* achieves the best performance with a macro F1 score of 0.469. The approach is designed to support human annotators by automatically discovering frequently occurring driving events and can be applied to other multivariate time series domains.

## Method Summary
The approach preprocesses CAN-data by resampling to 10Hz and Z-normalizing 9 relevant signals from the original 113. A sliding window technique extracts fixed-size subsequences, which are encoded using SSL models (generative: VAE, Drive2Vec, AE; contrastive: T-Loss, TNC) into low-dimensional embeddings. These embeddings form a new time series preserving temporal order. Clustering (k-means or TICC) is applied to the embeddings, with segmentation occurring at cluster changepoints. The method is evaluated on a two-hour annotated test trip from a Tesla Model 3, achieving macro F1 scores ranging from 0.338 to 0.469 depending on the SSL model and clustering method.

## Key Results
- VAME* generative SSL model achieves the highest macro F1 score of 0.469 using k-means clustering
- Contrastive SSL models (T-Loss and TNC) perform comparably with macro F1 scores of 0.392 and 0.338
- Window sizes in [5, 10, 15, 20] and embedding dimensions in [3, 5, 10, 15, 20] are explored
- TICC clustering performs worse than k-means on this dataset, suggesting limited benefit from temporal dependencies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SSL enables encoding time steps of the same driving event into similar representations, making clustering possible at the timestamp level.
- Mechanism: The encoder maps sliding windows to a lower-dimensional embedding space where SSL loss ensures windows from the same event type are nearby. This learned similarity enables k-means or TICC to cluster time steps independently, simultaneously segmenting and clustering the entire time series.
- Core assumption: The temporal neighborhood of windows within an event is statistically similar, allowing SSL loss to pull them together in embedding space.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] mentions SSL for distinguishing driving events in latent space; [section] describes VAME with future decoder and k-means objective; [corpus] provides weak evidence as related papers don't explicitly discuss representation similarity.
- Break condition: If event boundaries are not stationary or events are very short relative to window size, similarity assumption breaks and SSL fails to produce discriminative embeddings.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Fixed-size sliding windows preserve temporal context while enabling fine-grained clustering.
- Mechanism: For each time step t, a window St-(w-1),t is extracted and encoded to zt. Concatenating these zt's produces a time series of the same length as the original but in transformed embedding space, with each point carrying local neighborhood context.
- Core assumption: Window size w is large enough to capture meaningful driving event patterns but small enough to allow fine segmentation.
- Evidence anchors: [section] defines sliding window and evaluates w in [5, 10, 15, 20]; [corpus] provides weak evidence as related papers don't discuss window size selection.
- Break condition: If w is too large, method cannot resolve short events; if too small, embeddings lack context and become noisy.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Generative SSL models outperform contrastive models on CAN-data because they better capture temporal dynamics and future context.
- Mechanism: Generative models learn to reconstruct input or predict future values from embeddings, forcing the encoder to capture current and future event dynamics. This aligns well with the non-stationary nature of driving events, while contrastive models assume local stationarity.
- Core assumption: Future context is a strong signal for differentiating driving events in CAN-data.
- Evidence anchors: [section] describes TNC and T-Loss as contrastive methods; [section] explains VAME is trained with present and future decoder; [corpus] provides weak evidence as related papers don't compare generative vs. contrastive SSL for CAN-data.
- Break condition: If dataset lacks sufficient future context or events are too short, predictive power of generative models diminishes and contrastive models may catch up.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Multivariate time series preprocessing and Z-normalization
  - Why needed here: CAN-data from multiple sensors must be normalized to ensure each channel contributes equally to learned representations.
  - Quick check question: After resampling to 10Hz and applying Z-normalization, what is the mean and standard deviation of each channel in the training set?

- Concept: Sliding window technique for sequence modeling
  - Why needed here: Each timestamp-level embedding requires local context from neighboring samples to capture event dynamics.
  - Quick check question: Given a window size w=10 and a time series of length 100, how many sliding windows will be generated?

- Concept: Clustering with temporal dependency (TICC vs. k-means)
  - Why needed here: TICC incorporates temporal dependencies between clusters, which can improve segmentation when events have smooth transitions.
  - Quick check question: What is the main difference between k-means and TICC in terms of how they treat temporal order in the data?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data Ingestion → Preprocessing (resampling, normalization) → Sliding Window Extraction → Encoder Network (SSL model) → Embedding Time Series → Clustering (k-means or TICC) → Segmentation → Event Discovery
- Critical path: Sliding Window → Encoder → Clustering. Any delay or failure here directly impacts final event discovery.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Window size vs. segmentation granularity
  - Embedding dimension vs. clustering quality and computational cost
  - Generative vs. contrastive SSL: better temporal modeling vs. simpler training
- Failure signatures:
  - Poor clustering F1 → check SSL model training, window size, or normalization
  - Over-segmentation → reduce window size or increase embedding dimension
  - Under-segmentation → increase window size or switch to TICC
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train AE with window size 5, embedding dimension 10 on a small subset; check reconstruction loss and visualize embeddings with t-SNE.
  2. Replace AE with VAME*, same hyperparameters; compare macro F1 on validation set.
  3. Switch clustering from k-means to TICC; observe change in segmentation quality and run time.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How can contrastive SSL methods be further improved to better capture non-stationary driving events in CAN-data?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors suggest better neighborhood estimation method for TNC could be developed to capture non-stationary nature of events.
  - Why unresolved: Current TNC method assumes time series stationarity, which doesn't suit dynamic driving events. Paper doesn't provide specific solution.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Developing and testing a new neighborhood estimation method that accounts for non-stationarity and comparing performance with existing methods on CAN-data.

- Question: Can the proposed approach be effectively applied to vehicles with combustion engines or other domains like HAR and EEG?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors intend to evaluate approach for combustion engine vehicles and other domains (e.g., HAR, EEG) in the future.
  - Why unresolved: Current evaluation is limited to Tesla Model 3, and authors haven't explored applicability to other vehicle types or domains.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Conducting experiments with combustion engine vehicles and applying approach to HAR and EEG datasets to assess generalization.

- Question: How can the approach be extended to discover driver behavior patterns for sequences of events?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors mention possibility of discovering different driver behavior patterns for events or sequences using, for instance, a language model.
  - Why unresolved: Current approach focuses on individual driving events, and paper doesn't provide detailed method for analyzing sequences.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Implementing and evaluating a method that incorporates sequence analysis, such as a language model, to identify behavior patterns in driving event sequences.

## Limitations
- SSL model architectures and training hyperparameters are not fully specified beyond references
- No ablation on temporal neighborhood size or temporal dependency modeling
- Limited comparison with supervised baselines or domain-specific methods
- Performance (macro F1 ~0.47) remains modest, suggesting unresolved noise or class imbalance issues

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (SSL for embedding similarity): **Medium** - supported by method description but no empirical verification of embedding separability
- Mechanism 2 (sliding window for context): **High** - standard technique, hyperparameters explored
- Mechanism 3 (generative SSL advantage): **Low** - stated but not rigorously validated against contrastive SSL
