# Plan-Based Derivation of General Functional Structures in Product Design

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.04600
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04600
- Reference count: 33
- Key outcome: Automated functional decomposition using AI planning techniques

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach for automated functional decomposition in product design by leveraging planning techniques from Artificial Intelligence. The method models functional decomposition as a planning problem, representing functions as actions and product entities as states, allowing established planning algorithms to automatically compute functional structures. The approach was evaluated using two examples - a coffee maker and a siege engine - demonstrating successful automated decomposition with correct and complete function sequences.

The work represents a significant bridge between AI planning methods and functional decomposition in engineering design, offering a new perspective on automating this crucial early design step. By applying a modified Partial Order Planning algorithm, the approach provides a systematic method for deriving general functional structures that could potentially streamline the product design process and reduce the reliance on designer intuition and experience.

## Method Summary
The approach represents functional decomposition as a planning problem by modeling functions as actions and product entities as states. The authors developed a modified Partial Order Planning (POP) algorithm to handle this representation, using Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) to encode the functional structure generation problem. The method takes an initial system state and a desired target state as inputs, then automatically computes a sequence of functions that transform the initial state into the target state through the application of function actions.

## Key Results
- Successfully demonstrated automated functional decomposition for a coffee maker example
- Achieved correct and complete function sequences for a siege engine example
- Validated that AI planning algorithms can effectively handle functional decomposition problems
- Showed the approach can generate general functional structures without designer intervention

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works by transforming the functional decomposition problem into a formal planning problem where functions become actions and system states become world states. The modified POP algorithm can then systematically explore the state space to find valid sequences of function applications that transform an initial state into a desired target state. This leverages well-established AI planning theory and algorithms that have been refined over decades for optimal pathfinding and state-space exploration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Functional decomposition**: Breaking down complex systems into simpler functional elements; needed to understand the core problem being solved
- **Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL)**: A standardized language for expressing planning problems; needed to represent the functional decomposition as a planning problem
- **Partial Order Planning (POP)**: A planning algorithm that generates partially ordered sequences of actions; needed to find valid function sequences
- **State-space representation**: Modeling problems as transitions between states; needed to represent product entities and their transformations
- **Automated reasoning**: Using computational methods to derive logical conclusions; needed to replace manual designer intuition
- **Product lifecycle functions**: The various roles a product plays from creation to disposal; needed to define the scope of decomposition

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Initial state -> PDDL representation -> Modified POP algorithm -> Function sequence -> Target state

Critical path:
State definition → PDDL encoding → Planning algorithm execution → Sequence validation → Output generation

Design tradeoffs:
- Generality vs. specificity in function representation
- Computational complexity vs. solution optimality
- Automation vs. designer control and verification
- Formalization requirements vs. practical applicability

Failure signatures:
- Inability to find valid function sequences (planning failure)
- Generation of incomplete or incorrect function sequences
- Excessive computational time for complex products
- Poor scalability with increasing product complexity

First experiments:
1. Apply the approach to a simple household appliance (e.g., toaster) to verify basic functionality
2. Test with products requiring multiple interacting functions to evaluate handling of dependencies
3. Measure computational performance on progressively more complex product examples

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the proposed approach handle non-functional requirements and insufficiently formalized requirements in functional decomposition?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that non-functional requirements and insufficiently formalized requirements were not considered further, but notes that interactions between humans and assistance in decomposition could lead to further insights.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed methodology or framework for incorporating non-functional requirements into the automated functional decomposition process.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study or experiment demonstrating the integration of non-functional requirements into the PDDL representation and the impact on the decomposition results would provide clarity.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of using different planning algorithms (besides POP) on the quality and efficiency of the functional decomposition?
- Basis in paper: The paper uses a modified POP algorithm and mentions that further research may be done concerning the used algorithm, questioning what other algorithms are suited to find solutions for the approach.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not compare the performance of the POP algorithm with other planning algorithms for functional decomposition tasks.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative study evaluating multiple planning algorithms (e.g., A*, Dijkstra's, etc.) on various functional decomposition problems would provide insights into their relative performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the approach be adapted to solve more specialized decomposition problems like Intensity and Quantity functional structures?
- Basis in paper: The paper suggests that further research may be invested in the ability to adapt to different decomposition problems, specifically mentioning Intensity and Quantity functional structures.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a methodology or examples of how the approach can be modified to handle these specialized decomposition problems.
- What evidence would resolve it: A case study or experiment demonstrating the successful application of the approach to Intensity and Quantity functional structures would provide evidence of its adaptability.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can external interdependencies (e.g., requirements lists, physical effects, effect models, principle solutions, and design solutions) be incorporated into the automated functional decomposition process?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that external interdependencies were not directly considered due to their high complexity and the need for preprocessing steps, but suggests that further research could explore how these interdependencies affect decomposition.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a framework or methodology for incorporating these interdependencies into the PDDL representation or the decomposition algorithm.
- What evidence would resolve it: A study or experiment demonstrating the integration of external interdependencies into the automated functional decomposition process and the impact on the results would provide clarity.

## Limitations
- The approach's performance on complex or dissimilar products beyond the tested examples remains unverified
- Computational efficiency and scalability for larger, more complex products has not been demonstrated
- The method requires well-formalized requirements and may struggle with ambiguous or incomplete specifications
- Limited validation with actual engineering designers to assess practical utility in real design workflows

## Confidence
- High confidence in the novelty of applying AI planning to functional decomposition
- Medium confidence in the demonstrated feasibility for specific examples
- Low confidence in generalizability to complex or diverse product domains
