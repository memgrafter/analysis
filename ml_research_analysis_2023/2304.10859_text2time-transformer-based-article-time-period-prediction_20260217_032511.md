# Text2Time: Transformer-based Article Time Period Prediction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.10859
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10859
- Reference count: 10
- The BERT model achieves 82% accuracy in predicting article publication decade from text

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Text2Time, a transformer-based model that predicts the publication decade of news articles from their text content. The authors constructed a dataset of over 350,000 articles from The New York Times spanning six decades (1960s-2010s) and developed a BERT-based classifier that achieves 82% accuracy, significantly outperforming a Naive Bayes baseline at 63%. The model demonstrates robustness to date removal and article length normalization, indicating it captures genuine decade-specific linguistic patterns rather than just explicit date mentions or length cues.

## Method Summary
The researchers fine-tuned a pre-trained BERT-Base (cased) model for multiclass text classification, using Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 4e-5 and batch sizes of 16 for training and 8 for testing. They trained for 2 epochs due to GPU time constraints. The approach involved preprocessing articles to remove publication dates and default phrases, then tokenizing with BERT's tokenizer before passing through the frozen base encoder and a trainable classification head.

## Key Results
- BERT model achieves 82% accuracy versus 63% for Naive Bayes baseline
- Model remains robust at ~79% accuracy when publication dates are removed from text
- Accuracy stays consistent when articles are truncated to uniform length

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: BERT outperforms Naive Bayes because it captures contextual and stylistic linguistic shifts over decades, not just keyword frequency.
- Mechanism: Pretrained BERT embeddings encode rich contextual features from six decades of diverse article categories, allowing fine-tuning to recognize decade-specific writing patterns, topical vocabularies, and linguistic evolution beyond simple term counts.
- Core assumption: The corpus contains sufficient decade-specific linguistic signals (vocabulary, style, length) that are distinguishable in embeddings and learnable during fine-tuning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The BERT model outperforms the baseline across all article categories and remains robust when dates are removed or articles are truncated to uniform length, indicating the model captures decade-specific linguistic patterns beyond just date mentions or article length."
  - [section] "It can be seen that the accuracy varies by the category of the article... Words in low accuracy articles might be changing across decades."
  - [corpus] Weak: corpus shows related work on news and transformer models but no direct evidence BERT captures stylistic patterns vs keywords.
- Break condition: If linguistic patterns are too subtle or uniform across decades, or if article content is dominated by static named entities, BERT's advantage would diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Removing publication years from article text does not significantly degrade BERT accuracy, proving the model relies on linguistic cues rather than explicit dates.
- Mechanism: BERT's attention and contextual embeddings capture era-specific language without needing explicit year references; the model generalizes decade identity from surrounding vocabulary and style.
- Core assumption: The dataset's linguistic features (word choice, syntax, topical focus) are strongly correlated with publication decade even when years are absent.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The model remains robust when dates are removed or articles are truncated to uniform length, indicating the model captures decade-specific linguistic patterns beyond just date mentions or article length."
  - [section] "To prove that there is no effect of the years in the dataset. Dataset was processed to remove all the years in the articles... The model trained and tested on this dataset has an accuracy of around 79% which almost similar to the original dataset."
  - [corpus] Weak: no direct corpus evidence; inference from experimental results only.
- Break condition: If decades overlap heavily in vocabulary or if named entities dominate, removing dates could cause accuracy to drop sharply.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Longer article lengths in later decades provide additional decade signals that BERT leverages.
- Mechanism: Statistical increase in article word count per decade creates a length-based feature that BERT embeddings encode; this length trend correlates with decade and aids classification.
- Core assumption: Article length variation across decades is significant and consistent enough to serve as a discriminative feature for BERT.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We can observe that the average and median word length of articles is increasing with each decade... Additionally, the standard deviation of word lengths also has increased significantly in latter decades."
  - [section] "It is possible that our BERT model picked up on this phenomenon of word length of the article being correlated with the decade in which it was published."
  - [corpus] Weak: corpus contains no evidence that length is used by models; only observational dataset stats.
- Break condition: If articles are truncated to uniform length, accuracy remains high (shown in paper), weakening this mechanism's role.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Text preprocessing and cleaning (removing default phrases, dates, standardizing format).
  - Why needed here: Raw scraped articles contain metadata, dates, and boilerplate text that leak decade information or add noise, biasing the model.
  - Quick check question: If publication dates remain in the text, what accuracy effect would you expect and why?

- Concept: Multinomial Naive Bayes as a baseline for text classification.
  - Why needed here: Provides a simple, interpretable benchmark to measure whether more complex models like BERT add meaningful predictive power.
  - Quick check question: Why might Naive Bayes achieve ~63% accuracy even without capturing deep linguistic patterns?

- Concept: Fine-tuning pretrained language models for downstream classification.
  - Why needed here: BERT's contextual embeddings capture nuanced language features; fine-tuning adapts them to the decade prediction task while freezing base layers for efficiency.
  - Quick check question: What would happen to training time and accuracy if all BERT layers were unfrozen instead of just the classification head?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Dataset preprocessing pipeline → Tokenization → BERT base encoder (frozen) → Feed-forward classification head (trainable) → Softmax output (6 decades)
- Critical path: Clean raw text → Split into train/validation/test → Tokenize with BERT tokenizer → Feed to BERT → Pass pooled output to classifier → Compute cross-entropy loss → Backpropagate only through classifier layers
- Design tradeoffs: Using frozen BERT reduces training time and GPU memory but limits adaptation; smaller model size trades off with potential accuracy gains from deeper tuning
- Failure signatures: Low accuracy despite training could indicate preprocessing errors (dates not removed), class imbalance, or insufficient linguistic variation in the dataset
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train Naive Bayes baseline on cleaned dataset and record accuracy
  2. Fine-tune BERT on full dataset, evaluate accuracy, and compare to baseline
  3. Remove all year mentions from text, retrain BERT, and compare accuracy to original BERT run to confirm robustness

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How much does the variation in article length across decades impact the BERT model's performance, and is this effect consistent across all article categories?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows that article length increases with each decade and that the BERT model's accuracy remains robust when articles are truncated to uniform length, but it does not explore the impact of varying article lengths across different categories in depth.
- Why unresolved: The study tested uniform length but did not analyze whether certain categories are more susceptible to length-based biases or if the model's reliance on length varies by topic.
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed ablation studies comparing model performance on articles of varying lengths within each category, or controlled experiments isolating length as a feature.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the model rely on topical keywords versus stylistic features when predicting the publication decade of an article?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Error analysis reveals that the model sometimes misclassifies based on topical keywords (e.g., "Soviet" leading to 1980s prediction) or historical references, suggesting both keyword and stylistic features drive predictions.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not quantify the relative contribution of keywords versus stylistic patterns to the model's accuracy, nor does it explore methods to disentangle these influences.
- What evidence would resolve it: Feature importance analysis, ablation studies removing keywords or stylistic markers, or interpretability techniques like attention visualization to identify dominant predictive features.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the model's performance be improved by incorporating domain-specific fine-tuning for different article categories, and would this approach generalize across decades?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that accuracy varies by category (e.g., higher for Sports and Movies, lower for Science and Lifestyle) but does not explore whether category-specific fine-tuning could enhance performance.
- Why unresolved: The study used a single BERT model for all categories and did not investigate whether tailoring the model to specific domains or adjusting for decade-specific trends within categories would yield better results.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing baseline BERT performance with category-specific or decade-specific fine-tuned models, along with cross-category generalization tests.

## Limitations
- Relies on a single news source (The New York Times), limiting generalizability to other publications
- Does not fully characterize whether predictions are driven by keywords versus stylistic features
- Six-decade span may not capture sufficient linguistic evolution for reliable classification

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: BERT outperforms Naive Bayes baseline by ~19 percentage points; accuracy remains stable when dates are removed from text (79% vs 82%); results are consistent across article categories.
- **Medium Confidence**: BERT captures decade-specific linguistic patterns rather than just date mentions or article length; length-based features contribute to predictions; model robustness holds when articles are truncated to uniform length.
- **Low Confidence**: The exact mix of keyword-based versus stylistic features driving decade predictions; generalizability to other news sources or text domains; whether six decades provide sufficient linguistic separation for reliable classification.

## Next Checks
