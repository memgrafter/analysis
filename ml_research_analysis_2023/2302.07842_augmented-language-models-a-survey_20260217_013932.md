# Augmented Language Models: a Survey

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.07842
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07842
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: ALMs significantly improve reasoning performance (e.g., GSM8K accuracy up to 65.4%) by integrating reasoning and tool-use capabilities

## Executive Summary
This survey comprehensively reviews approaches to augment language models with reasoning and tool-use capabilities. It classifies methods into eliciting reasoning through prompting (few-shot, recursive, and explicit teaching), using external tools and actions (retrieval, computation, and environment interaction), and learning these skills via supervision or reinforcement learning. Key findings include that chain-of-thought prompting significantly improves reasoning performance, retrieval-augmented models achieve strong few-shot learning with fewer parameters, and reinforcement learning enables language models to act as agents. The survey highlights that ALMs offer advantages like improved truthfulness, interpretability, and enhanced capabilities compared to traditional LMs. It also discusses ethical concerns and identifies open research questions, such as developing self-supervised methods for tool-use and combining reasoning with actions.

## Method Summary
The survey synthesizes findings from 30 papers examining augmented language models (ALMs) that integrate reasoning and tool-use capabilities. Methods are categorized into three main approaches: eliciting reasoning through prompting techniques like chain-of-thought and recursive prompting; using external tools and actions such as retrieval systems, calculators, and environment interactions; and learning these skills through supervision or reinforcement learning from human feedback. The analysis covers various model architectures including GPT-3, PaLM, and LaMDA, evaluating performance on benchmarks like GSM8K for reasoning and MMLU for general language understanding.

## Key Results
- Chain-of-thought prompting significantly improves reasoning performance, with GSM8K accuracy reaching up to 65.4%
- Retrieval-augmented models like RETRO and Atlas achieve strong few-shot learning with fewer parameters
- Reinforcement learning enables language models to act as agents (e.g., WebGPT answering questions via web browsing)
- ALMs offer advantages like improved truthfulness, interpretability, and enhanced capabilities compared to traditional LMs

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Augmented Language Models (ALMs) outperform traditional LMs by integrating reasoning and tools into their context processing.
- Mechanism: ALMs decompose complex tasks into simpler subtasks (reasoning) and leverage external tools (e.g., calculators, retrievers) to handle subtasks beyond the LM's core capabilities. This combination improves accuracy and interpretability compared to pure LMs.
- Core assumption: Decomposing tasks and using tools increases the likelihood of correct token prediction by enriching context.

- Break condition: If the decomposition or tool integration adds noise rather than useful context, or if the LM cannot effectively utilize the augmented context.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting significantly improves reasoning performance in large LMs.
- Mechanism: By providing intermediate reasoning steps in the prompt, CoT guides the LM to generate a logical chain of thought before arriving at the final answer. This process helps the model avoid common pitfalls in complex reasoning.
- Core assumption: Large LMs can effectively utilize intermediate reasoning steps to improve final answer accuracy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "chain-of-thought prompting significantly improves reasoning performance (e.g., GSM8K accuracy up to 65.4%)."
  - [section]: "Wei et al. (2022c) introduced chain-of-thought (CoT), a few-shot prompting technique for LMs. The prompt consists of examples of a task, with inputs followed by intermediate reasoning steps leading to the final output."
  - [corpus]: Found 25 related papers. Average neighbor FMR=0.469, average citations=0.0. Top related titles include works on reasoning and chain-of-thought.
- Break condition: If the model is too small (less than 100B parameters as noted in the paper) or if the reasoning steps are not logically sound, CoT may not provide significant improvement.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) enables LMs to align with complex human preferences and values.
- Mechanism: RLHF uses human preferences (e.g., rankings, likes/dislikes) as rewards to optimize the LM's behavior. This process allows the model to learn subtle nuances in human judgment that are difficult to capture with hard-coded reward functions.
- Core assumption: Human feedback can be effectively used to guide the LM's learning process and improve its alignment with human values.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "reinforcement learning enables language models to act as agents (e.g., WebGPT answering questions via web browsing)."
  - [section]: "RLHF works by using a pre-trained LM to generate text, which is then evaluated by humans by, for example, ranking two model generations for the same prompt."

- Break condition: If human feedback is noisy, inconsistent, or difficult to obtain at scale, RLHF may not be effective or may lead to biased models.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Core LM -> Reasoning module -> Tool interface -> Context manager -> Reward model (for RLHF)
- Critical path: 1. Receive input prompt 2. Generate reasoning steps (if applicable) 3. Call relevant tools 4. Integrate tool outputs and reasoning into context 5. Generate final output
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Model size vs. performance: Larger models generally perform better with ALM techniques
  - Tool integration complexity vs. benefit: More sophisticated tools can improve performance but add complexity
  - Reasoning depth vs. computational cost: Deeper reasoning can improve accuracy but increases inference time
- Failure signatures:
  - Incorrect tool usage or outputs
  - Inconsistent or illogical reasoning steps
  - Over-reliance on tools for simple tasks
  - Hallucinations despite tool usage
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement chain-of-thought prompting on a simple arithmetic task (e.g., GSM8K)
  2. Integrate a basic calculator tool and test on math word problems
  3. Implement a simple retriever and test on a question-answering task

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can ALMs be calibrated to decide when to use tools versus relying on their own knowledge?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses the tradeoff between memorizing information and querying tools, suggesting that some situations require tools while others benefit from memorization.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide empirical evidence or clear guidelines for when a model should choose one approach over the other.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing ALM performance with and without tool usage in various scenarios, and developing a calibration mechanism for tool usage.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the combination of reasoning and tool-use be effectively integrated in ALMs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights that few works combine reasoning and tools, suggesting this as a promising research direction.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore specific methodologies for integrating reasoning and tool-use, nor does it provide evidence of successful integration.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and evaluation of ALMs that seamlessly integrate reasoning and tool-use, demonstrating improved performance over models with only one of these capabilities.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can ALMs be trained in a fully self-supervised manner without relying on human annotations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that most works rely on human annotation, which may not be scalable, and identifies self-supervised training as an open research question.
- Why unresolved: Current ALMs often require human-written demonstrations or feedback, which is costly and time-consuming.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful development of ALMs that can learn to reason and use tools without human supervision, using techniques like self-play, bootstrapping, or unsupervised learning.

## Limitations

- Heterogeneous implementations across different papers make direct performance comparison challenging
- Many empirical claims rely on results from individual papers rather than systematic benchmarking
- Limited ablation studies that isolate the contribution of specific ALM components

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The survey accurately represents the landscape of ALM approaches and correctly identifies chain-of-thought prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, and RLHF as major techniques.

**Medium Confidence**: Specific performance numbers are accurately reported from source papers, but cross-method comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to implementation differences.

**Low Confidence**: Long-term predictions about ALM development trajectories and untested combinations of techniques have high uncertainty given the field's rapid evolution.

## Next Checks

1. **Systematic Benchmarking**: Conduct controlled experiments comparing different ALM techniques (CoT, retrieval, RLHF) on identical model architectures and datasets to establish more reliable performance hierarchies.

2. **Component Ablation Studies**: For each ALM method, perform systematic ablations to quantify the contribution of individual components (e.g., separating the effects of reasoning steps vs. tool usage in CoT+retrieval systems).

3. **Scaling Law Analysis**: Investigate how ALM performance scales with model size across different augmentation techniques to identify whether smaller models can effectively use ALMs or if benefits are primarily realized at scale.
