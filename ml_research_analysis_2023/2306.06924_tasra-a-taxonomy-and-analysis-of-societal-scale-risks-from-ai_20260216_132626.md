# TASRA: a Taxonomy and Analysis of Societal-Scale Risks from AI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.06924
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06924
- Reference count: 4
- Key outcome: Exhaustive taxonomy of societal-scale AI risks based on accountability framework with six distinct risk types

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces TASRA, an exhaustive taxonomy for classifying societal-scale risks from AI technology. The taxonomy is built on an accountability framework that examines whose actions cause risks, whether actors are unified, and whether actions are deliberate. This creates six distinct risk types: diffusion of responsibility, unanticipated impacts, willful indifference, criminal weaponization, and state weaponization. For each risk type, the paper provides illustrative scenarios and discusses both technical and policy interventions. The framework aims to help policymakers and researchers systematically analyze and address the various ways AI could pose risks to humanity.

## Method Summary
The taxonomy is constructed using a decision tree based on three accountability questions: whose actions lead to the risk, are the actors unified, and are they deliberate? This creates an exhaustive classification system with exactly six risk types. The paper supports each risk type with illustrative stories and discusses potential technical and policy solutions. The approach draws from fault tree analysis principles to ensure comprehensive coverage of possible risk scenarios.

## Key Results
- Creates an accountability-based decision tree with exactly six risk types covering all combinations of actor unity and deliberateness
- Identifies three previously under-recognized risk types (Types 1-3) involving systemic and collective behaviors
- Provides concrete narrative scenarios for each risk type to aid policymaker understanding
- Links each risk type to specific technical and policy intervention strategies

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: TASRA's accountability-based decision tree ensures exhaustiveness in risk classification.
- Mechanism: By structuring risks around three questions - whose actions, unity of actors, and deliberateness - the taxonomy creates a decision tree with exactly six leaves, covering all possible combinations of these dimensions.
- Core assumption: These three questions are truly orthogonal and jointly exhaustive for categorizing societal-scale AI risks.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The taxonomy reveals previously under-recognized risk types (Types 1-3) that require different interventions than deliberate misuse.
- Mechanism: By including risks from diffusion of responsibility and unanticipated impacts, the taxonomy surfaces systemic risks that arise from collective behaviors rather than individual intentions, highlighting the need for regulatory and technical solutions beyond criminal law.
- Core assumption: Risks from collective/systemic behaviors are distinct enough from intentional misuse to warrant separate classification.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The illustrative stories make abstract risk categories concrete and actionable for policymakers.
- Mechanism: By providing narrative scenarios for each risk type, the paper translates technical concepts into accessible formats that help stakeholders understand potential failure modes and motivate appropriate interventions.
- Core assumption: Narrative scenarios effectively communicate complex technical risks to non-technical audiences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can indistinguishability obfuscation (IO) techniques be made efficient enough for practical use in AI systems?
- Basis in paper: The paper mentions that current IO techniques are "currently too inefficient to be practical" but could be promising for future improvements.
- Why unresolved: Making IO efficient enough for real-world AI applications is a significant technical challenge that requires further research and development.
- What evidence would resolve it: Demonstration of IO techniques that can obfuscate AI-relevant programs (e.g., neural networks) with reasonable computational overhead and without compromising security.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we effectively regulate algorithms and their societal impacts, similar to how food and drugs are regulated?
- Basis in paper: The paper discusses the need for regulatory oversight of algorithms, mentioning the lack of a "pervasive and influential regulatory body for algorithms" and the challenges of classifying and testing algorithms for regulatory purposes.
- Why unresolved: Developing a comprehensive regulatory framework for algorithms is a complex task that involves balancing innovation, privacy, and societal well-being, and requires input from various stakeholders.
- What evidence would resolve it: Establishment of a regulatory body with the authority to oversee algorithms, along with the development of standardized methods for classifying, testing, and auditing algorithms based on their potential societal impacts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can AI technology be used to facilitate fair and effective negotiations between conflicting parties or nations?
- Basis in paper: The paper explores the potential of AI-assisted negotiation, mentioning the development of mediation tools and the challenges of balancing fairness and successful negotiation outcomes.
- Why unresolved: AI-assisted negotiation is a complex problem that involves understanding human preferences, beliefs, and decision-making processes, and developing AI systems that can navigate these complexities while promoting fairness and mutually beneficial outcomes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Successful implementation and evaluation of AI-assisted negotiation tools in real-world scenarios, demonstrating their ability to facilitate fair and effective negotiations between conflicting parties or nations.

## Limitations

- The taxonomy's exhaustiveness claim lacks empirical validation against real-world AI incidents
- Boundary conditions between risk types (particularly Types 2-3 and Types 4-6) may be ambiguous in practice
- The paper does not provide a detailed methodology for constructing the taxonomy or assessing its exhaustiveness

## Confidence

- High confidence: The accountability-based framework for classifying risks is methodologically sound and provides a useful structure for thinking about AI risks
- Medium confidence: The claim of exhaustiveness, while theoretically justified by the decision tree structure, lacks empirical validation
- Medium confidence: The illustrative stories effectively communicate risk concepts, though their effectiveness for diverse audiences is not tested
