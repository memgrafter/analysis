# Deep Gaussian Mixture Ensembles

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.07235
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07235
- Reference count: 18
- Primary result: Novel probabilistic deep learning technique for quantifying epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty using Gaussian mixture ensembles trained via EM algorithm

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Deep Gaussian Mixture Ensembles (DGMEs), a probabilistic deep learning technique that models the data generating process as a Gaussian mixture. DGMEs use an Expectation-Maximization algorithm to learn model parameters, enabling them to approximate complex probability distributions including heavy-tailed and multimodal distributions. The approach outperforms state-of-the-art uncertainty quantifying deep learning models on both synthetic and real-world datasets.

## Method Summary
DGMEs train a weighted ensemble of neural networks using the EM algorithm. The method initializes mixture weights equally and updates posterior probabilities (E-step) using Bayes' theorem, then optimizes network parameters (M-step) using weighted negative log-likelihood. Dropout is applied to account for epistemic uncertainty, and sampling from the predictive distribution involves first selecting a mixture component, then sampling network parameters with dropout, and finally sampling from the Gaussian component. The approach is evaluated on synthetic regression tasks and real-world datasets using NLL and RMSE metrics.

## Key Results
- DGMEs achieve better NLL and RMSE performance compared to DEs, MDNs, and MCD baselines on synthetic regression tasks with heavy-tailed and bimodal noise
- The EM training procedure enables learning of mixture weights, which is not commonly done in ensembles
- DGMEs can capture complex distributions (heavy-tailed, multimodal) by modeling the conditional density as a Gaussian mixture
- Experimental results show improved uncertainty quantification on UCI regression benchmark datasets and financial time series forecasting

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: DGMEs outperform DEs by directly maximizing the joint data likelihood rather than a lower bound
- Mechanism: The EM algorithm optimizes the full Gaussian mixture likelihood, whereas DEs train each ensemble member independently under a Gaussian assumption
- Core assumption: The data generating process can be modeled as a Gaussian mixture with unknown mixture weights
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our contributions include the derivation of an expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm used for learning the model parameters, which results in an upper-bound on the log-likelihood of training data over that of standard deep ensembles."
  - [section 4.3]: "Proposition 4.3 shows that maximizing the data likelihood directly as in DGMEs achieves an equal or better likelihood than maximizing each ensemble member's likelihood separately as in DEs."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence for direct likelihood comparison with DEs in broader literature
- Break condition: If the true data generating process is unimodal Gaussian, the mixture modeling adds unnecessary complexity without performance gain

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: DGMEs can capture complex distributions (heavy-tailed, multimodal) by modeling the conditional density as a Gaussian mixture
- Mechanism: Gaussian mixtures are universal approximators for smooth probability density functions, allowing DGMEs to learn more complex data generating processes than single Gaussian assumptions
- Core assumption: The underlying conditional density can be well-approximated by a finite Gaussian mixture
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "By assuming the data generating process follows that of a Gaussian mixture, DGMEs are capable of approximating complex probability distributions, such as heavy-tailed or multimodal distributions."
  - [section 4.1]: "Since Gaussian mixtures are universal approximators for smooth probability density functions [Bacharoglou, 2010, Goodfellow et al., 2016], modeling the conditional density pθ(y|x) as a Gaussian mixture allows for learning more complex distributions."
  - [corpus]: Limited direct evidence in corpus for Gaussian mixture universality in deep learning contexts
- Break condition: If the true distribution is extremely complex or has discontinuities, a finite mixture may not provide adequate approximation

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The EM training procedure enables learning of mixture weights, which is not commonly done in ensembles
- Mechanism: The E-step computes posterior probabilities of mixture component membership, and the M-step analytically updates mixture weights based on these posteriors
- Core assumption: Mixture weights are independent of input features and can be learned as global parameters
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Additionally, the proposed EM training procedure allows for learning of mixture weights, which is not commonly done in ensembles."
  - [section 4.1]: "The optimization of the mixture weights (π1, ..., πK) can be carried out analytically and done independently of optimizing the mixture parameters {θ1, ..., θK}."
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence for mixture weight learning in ensemble methods in broader literature
- Break condition: If the optimal mixture weights are highly input-dependent, the global weight assumption becomes limiting

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  K neural networks (mixture components) -> Mixture weight parameters π1,...,πk -> EM training loop (E-step, M-step) -> Dropout layer for epistemic uncertainty

- Critical path:
  1. Initialize mixture weights equally and neural network parameters randomly
  2. E-step: Compute posterior probabilities γk,n for each training example
  3. M-step: Update mixture weights analytically and network parameters via weighted negative log-likelihood
  4. Repeat until convergence or max EM steps
  5. At inference: Sample mixture component, apply dropout, sample from selected component's predictive distribution

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Number of mixture components K vs. model complexity and overfitting risk
  - Dropout probability pd vs. epistemic uncertainty quantification quality
  - Number of EM steps J vs. computational cost and convergence
  - Shared vs. separate feature extractors for mean and variance functions

- Failure signatures:
  - Training NLL increases with more EM steps (overfitting)
  - Test NLL much higher than training NLL (poor generalization)
  - Predictive distributions too narrow/wide compared to ground truth
  - Mixture weights converging to degenerate values (some πk → 0)

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Toy regression with Gaussian noise: Verify DGMEs match DEs performance when true distribution is simple
  2. Toy regression with heavy-tailed noise: Demonstrate DGMEs' ability to capture non-Gaussian distributions
  3. Toy regression with bimodal noise: Show DGMEs can learn multimodal distributions while DEs cannot

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do DGMEs perform on classification tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Techniques like MDNs and DGMEs are not suited for dealing with classiﬁcation tasks, since the output of both models is a mixture of Gaussian distributions. For classiﬁcation tasks, we instead can consider a mixture of categorical distributions, rather than a mixture of Gaussian distributions."
- Why unresolved: The paper only discusses the extension of DGMEs to classification tasks in the supplementary material and does not provide experimental results
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing DGMEs to other uncertainty quantification methods on classification tasks

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal number of EM rounds for DGMEs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "We can see empirically that for a ﬁxed computational budget, there is tradeoff between the performance and the effective number of EM rounds. The tradeoff is more apparent when considering the more difﬁcult examples (i.e., heavy-tailed unimodal noise and normal bimodal noise)."
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that there is a tradeoff between the number of EM rounds and performance, but does not provide a clear answer on the optimal number of EM rounds
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing the performance of DGMEs with different numbers of EM rounds on various datasets

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of DGMEs compare to other uncertainty quantification methods on high-dimensional data?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only evaluates DGMEs on low-dimensional datasets (e.g., UCI regression benchmark datasets and financial time series forecasting)
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide experimental results on high-dimensional data, which is a common scenario in real-world applications
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing DGMEs to other uncertainty quantification methods on high-dimensional datasets

## Limitations
- Computational complexity of EM training compared to standard ensemble methods
- Assumption of global mixture weights that may not capture input-dependent uncertainty patterns
- Potential for overfitting when modeling complex distributions with limited data
