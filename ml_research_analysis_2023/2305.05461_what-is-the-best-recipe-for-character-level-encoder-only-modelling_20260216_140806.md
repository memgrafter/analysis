# What is the best recipe for character-level encoder-only modelling?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.05461
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05461
- Reference count: 11
- The paper finds that a Charformer-CANINE architecture with tokeniser-based masking outperforms BERT on multiple tasks while eliminating the need for complex tokenisers during inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically compares character-level encoder models and pretraining objectives to find the best-performing combination. It shows that a Charformer-CANINE architecture trained with a tokeniser-based masking scheme outperforms a BERT baseline on multiple tasks, demonstrating character-level models' readiness for widespread adoption. However, optimal performance still depends critically on tokeniser quality, introducing a hidden dependency. The study highlights that character-level models can match or exceed token-level models on tasks like NER and QA while offering benefits like easier annotation and removal of complex tokenisers.

## Method Summary
The paper benchmarks three character-level architectures (CANINE, Charformer, Perceiver) with BERT-small as a token-level baseline. Models are trained on multilingual Wikipedia data for 250k steps using 32 TPUv3 chips. The study compares different pretraining objectives including tokeniser-based masking, tokeniser-free masking, and autoregressive vs. independent character prediction. Evaluation includes morphological probing (Universal Dependencies), WikiANN NER, and TyDi-QA. The best configuration combines Charformer downsampling with CANINE upsampling, trained with tokeniser-based masking and token-level prediction targets.

## Key Results
- Charformer-CANINE architecture outperforms BERT on downstream tasks while being moderately slower to pretrain
- Tokeniser-based masking schemes consistently outperform tokeniser-free alternatives
- Learnt character embeddings significantly improve model stability and performance compared to hash embeddings
- Larger tokeniser vocabularies during pretraining lead to better downstream performance, even for character-level models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Character-level models with appropriate architecture and training can match or exceed token-based models on downstream tasks.
- Mechanism: The combination of Charformer encoder and CANINE decoder leverages locality-aware downsampling and upsampling to efficiently process characters while preserving semantic information, allowing direct learning from orthography without tokenization bottlenecks.
- Core assumption: Character-level representations can capture sufficient linguistic structure when the architecture properly aggregates local character information and the training objective aligns with downstream task requirements.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Our best performing character-level model exceeds the performance of a token-based model trained with the same settings on the same data"
  - [section]: "we find that the best recipe combines the Charformer and CANINE model architectures"
  - [corpus]: Weak - no direct corpus evidence supporting this specific mechanism
- Break condition: If the locality assumptions break down for languages with non-local morphological composition or if the character embedding space becomes too sparse to learn meaningful representations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Learnt character embeddings significantly improve model performance and stability compared to fixed hash embeddings.
- Mechanism: Learnt embeddings capture orthographic patterns (phonetics, shape) and semantic relationships between characters that random hash embeddings cannot represent, providing better initialization for downstream tasks.
- Core assumption: Character embeddings can learn meaningful relationships that transfer to downstream tasks, and these relationships are more informative than random initialization.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "we note that the lack of innate bias in the Perceiver model components renders it less data efficient"
  - [section]: "Learnt character embeddings improve results... results for TyDi-QA drop to 64.48± 24.56, and for WikiANN drop to 87.98± 0.06"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus doesn't provide direct evidence about embedding quality
- Break condition: When character embeddings become too large to train effectively or when the character-level information becomes irrelevant for the downstream task.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Tokeniser quality during pretraining significantly impacts downstream performance even when tokenisers are not used during evaluation.
- Mechanism: Tokenisers provide supervision about meaningful linguistic units during pretraining, and higher quality tokenisers (larger vocabularies) provide better alignment with natural language structure, improving the learned representations.
- Core assumption: Tokenisers capture meaningful linguistic boundaries that benefit representation learning, and this benefit persists even when the tokeniser is discarded.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "the best method to train character-level models still relies on a subword-level tokeniser during pretraining"
  - [section]: "Larger vocabulary sizes consistently lead to better downstream task performance for both models, even the character-level model"
  - [corpus]: Weak - corpus evidence doesn't directly support tokeniser dependency
- Break condition: When tokenisers introduce harmful biases or when the downstream task benefits more from character-level patterns than token-level supervision.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Character embeddings → Downsampling (Charformer/CANINE/Perceiver) → BERT core → Upsampling (CANINE/Perceiver) → Character-level outputs
- Critical path: Input characters → Learnt embeddings → Local attention/convolution → BERT processing → Upsampling → Final representations
- Design tradeoffs: Character-level models trade sequence length efficiency for removal of tokenisation complexity; locality assumptions vs. flexibility; compute efficiency vs. performance
- Failure signatures: Poor morphology probing but good downstream tasks suggests overspecialization; unstable training indicates embedding issues; poor performance across all tasks suggests architectural problems
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare Charformer-CANINE with different downsampling configurations on morphology probing to isolate architectural impact
  2. Test different tokeniser vocabulary sizes during pretraining to measure tokeniser dependency
  3. Compare autoregressive vs. independent character prediction under different masking schemes to identify optimal training objective

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the use of character-level models significantly improve performance on low-resource languages compared to subword-level models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions the potential of character-level models for massively multilingual models, as subword tokenisers have limited vocabulary size per language, impacting non-European scripts disproportionately.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates on a limited set of languages and does not specifically focus on low-resource languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments on a diverse set of low-resource languages with varying scripts and morphological complexity.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the performance of character-level models scale with increasing model size and training data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that the Charformer-CANINE model outperforms BERT on downstream tasks while being moderately slower to pretrain. However, the impact of scaling up model size and training data on character-level models is not explored.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on comparing different model architectures and pretraining objectives rather than exploring scaling laws.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with larger character-level models and more training data to determine the scaling behavior.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Are there alternative pretraining objectives or masking schemes that can outperform tokeniser-based masking for character-level models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper investigates tokeniser-free masking schemes and prediction targets, but finds that tokeniser-based masking still performs the best. The authors suggest that more work is necessary to discover better ways to predict open-vocabulary masked targets.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores a limited set of masking schemes and prediction targets. There may be other combinations that can achieve better performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Exploration of alternative masking schemes and prediction targets, such as using semantic similarity or linguistic structure to guide the masking process.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the quality of the character embeddings impact the performance of character-level models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that using learnt character embeddings improves model performance and stability compared to using only hash embeddings. However, the impact of different character embedding initialization methods or incorporating additional character-level features is not explored.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on comparing different model architectures and pretraining objectives rather than exploring the impact of character embeddings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with different character embedding initialization methods, such as using pre-trained character embeddings or incorporating phonetic or shape information, and evaluating their impact on model performance.

## Limitations

- The best-performing character-level models still depend on high-quality tokenisers during pretraining, contradicting the goal of eliminating tokenisation complexity
- Morphological probing results show weaker performance compared to downstream tasks, suggesting potential overspecialization
- The study doesn't explore low-resource languages or truly multilingual scenarios where character-level models might show the most benefit

## Confidence

**High Confidence Claims:**
- Charformer-CANINE architecture consistently outperforms other character-level configurations
- Learnt character embeddings significantly improve model stability and performance
- Token-level prediction during pretraining yields better downstream performance

**Medium Confidence Claims:**
- Character-level models can match or exceed token-based models on downstream tasks
- Tokeniser-based masking scheme is optimal for character-level pretraining
- Computational efficiency improvements are meaningful for practical deployment

**Low Confidence Claims:**
- The exact mechanism by which tokenisers improve character-level pretraining remains speculative
- Whether morphology probing weaknesses represent fundamental limitations or evaluation issues
- Long-term stability and generalization beyond evaluated task suite

## Next Checks

1. **Tokeniser Dependency Stress Test**: Train the best Charformer-CANINE model with progressively degraded tokenisers (smaller vocabularies, corrupted boundaries, language-specific tokenisers) to quantify the actual dependency strength and identify breaking points where character-level advantages disappear.

2. **Cross-Lingual Generalization Probe**: Evaluate the character-level models on truly unseen languages with no pretraining data to determine whether the purported benefits for multilingual scenarios hold when tokeniser supervision is completely absent.

3. **Alternative Pretraining Objective Validation**: Systematically test whether the tokeniser-based masking advantage persists when using different pretraining data sources (e.g., synthetic character sequences with known linguistic structure) to isolate whether the benefit comes from tokeniser quality or the pretraining data distribution itself.
