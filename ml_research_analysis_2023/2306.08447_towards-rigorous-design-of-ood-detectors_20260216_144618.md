# Towards Rigorous Design of OoD Detectors

## Quick Facts

## Executive Summary
This position paper addresses a critical gap in autonomous systems safety by highlighting that current out-of-distribution (OoD) detection metrics like expected calibration error do not adequately capture the safety requirements needed for real-world deployment. The authors argue that OoD detectors should be designed and validated according to explicit safety requirements based on set containment relations between the detector's decision boundary, the system's operational domain, and the DNN's certainty boundary. This framework aims to bridge the divide between traditional machine learning evaluation and rigorous safety engineering practices.

The paper proposes a systematic approach to OoD detector design that focuses on meeting safety requirements rather than achieving high performance on abstract metrics. By leveraging statistical hypothesis testing and Dempster-Shafer theory for confidence propagation, the authors present a methodology for rigorously assessing OoD detector effectiveness in terms of actual system safety. The work emphasizes the need to align technical implementations with explicit safety specifications, particularly for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a framework for rigorous OoD detector design based on four safety requirements derived from set containment relations. The methodology involves first defining the operational domain (HSOD) and DNN certainty boundary, then establishing containment relationships between these sets and the OoD detector's decision boundary. The authors suggest using statistical hypothesis testing to verify these containment relations and Dempster-Shafer theory to propagate confidence measures. The framework emphasizes that OoD detection should be validated against explicit safety requirements rather than traditional performance metrics, with a focus on ensuring that the detector's behavior aligns with the system's safety envelope.

## Key Results
- OoD detection metrics like expected calibration error fail to capture actual safety requirements for autonomous systems
- Four safety requirements based on set containment relations provide a rigorous foundation for OoD detector design
- Statistical hypothesis testing and Dempster-Shafer theory offer promising approaches for validating OoD detector effectiveness against safety requirements

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework works by grounding OoD detection in explicit safety requirements rather than abstract performance metrics. By defining the operational domain and DNN certainty boundary as mathematical sets, and requiring the OoD detector's decision boundary to maintain specific containment relationships with these sets, the approach ensures that OoD detection directly supports system safety. The use of statistical hypothesis testing provides rigorous validation methods, while Dempster-Shafer theory enables principled confidence propagation that accounts for uncertainty in both the DNN outputs and the detector's own confidence estimates.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: HSOD Specification -> DNN Certainty Boundary -> OoD Detector -> System Safety Validation

Critical path: The critical path flows from HSOD specification through DNN certainty quantification to OoD detector design, with safety validation as the final gate. Each stage must successfully complete before the next can proceed.

Design tradeoffs: The framework trades off traditional performance metrics (accuracy, calibration error) for explicit safety guarantees. This may result in more conservative systems with higher false positive rates but lower risk of missing true OoD scenarios.

Failure signatures: Primary failure modes include: 1) HSOD specification mismatch with actual operational environment, 2) Incorrect quantification of DNN certainty boundary, 3) OoD detector decision boundary violating containment requirements, 4) Statistical tests failing to reject false hypotheses.
