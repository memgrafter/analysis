# CrossSpeech: Speaker-independent Acoustic Representation for Cross-lingual Speech Synthesis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.14370
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14370
- Reference count: 0
- CrossSpeech achieves 7.0% EER in cross-lingual speaker similarity versus 34.6% for baseline FastPitch

## Executive Summary
CrossSpeech addresses speaker-language entanglement in cross-lingual text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis by disentangling speaker and language representations at the acoustic feature level. The method introduces a two-generator architecture with a speaker-independent generator (SIG) and speaker-dependent generator (SDG) to separate speaker identity from language-specific acoustic patterns. By using mix-dynamic speaker layer normalization (M-DSLN) and speaker generalization loss, CrossSpeech achieves significant improvements in speaker similarity metrics for cross-lingual scenarios while maintaining comparable quality to baseline models in intra-lingual settings.

## Method Summary
CrossSpeech implements a two-stage architecture built on FastPitch backbone with online aligner. The speaker-independent generator (SIG) processes text through M-DSLN to produce speaker-independent acoustic representations, while the speaker-dependent generator (SDG) handles speaker-specific attributes through DSLN and separate pitch prediction. The model employs speaker generalization loss to ensure consistency between mixed and original speaker-conditioned representations. Training uses combined objectives including reconstruction loss, alignment loss, duration prediction loss, speaker generalization loss, speaker-independent pitch loss, and speaker-dependent pitch loss, optimized with LAMB for 800 epochs. Mel-to-audio conversion uses a pre-trained Fre-GAN model.

## Key Results
- Cross-lingual speaker similarity: 7.0% EER versus 34.6% for baseline FastPitch
- Maintains comparable quality to baseline in intra-lingual scenarios
- Residual connection from speaker-independent to speaker-dependent representations improves pronunciation accuracy
- Mix-DSLN and speaker generalization loss critical for cross-lingual performance gains

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Speaker-language entanglement in cross-lingual TTS degrades speaker similarity because acoustic representations become biased toward the source language speaker distribution.
- Mechanism: CrossSpeech decomposes the speech generation pipeline into speaker-independent (SIG) and speaker-dependent (SDG) generators, ensuring speaker identity is preserved through separate modeling of speaker and language information at the acoustic feature level.
- Core assumption: Speaker and language information can be effectively disentangled in the acoustic feature space without information loss.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Mix-Dynamic Speaker Layer Normalization (M-DSLN) improves speaker generalization by preventing text encoding from being biased to specific speaker attributes.
- Mechanism: M-DSLN mixes the filter weight and bias with randomly shuffled speaker embeddings using a Beta distribution parameter, creating speaker-generalized representations that improve cross-lingual performance.
- Core assumption: Random mixing of speaker information during training forces the model to learn speaker-invariant features.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Residual connection from speaker-independent to speaker-dependent representations improves pronunciation accuracy and boosts speaker-language disentanglement.
- Mechanism: The speaker-independent acoustic representation hsi is projected and added as a residual to the final mel-spectrogram, providing text-related information while allowing SDG to focus on speaker-dependent attributes.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Text Encoder → M-DSLN → SIP Predictor → FFT Decoder → hsi → DSLN (with es) → SDP Predictor → FFT Decoder → hsd → + hsi_residual → Mel-spectrogram

- Critical path: Text → M-DSLN → SIP → Duration → Upsample → FFT → hsi → DSLN → SDP → FFT → hsd → + hsi_residual → Mel-spectrogram

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Two-generator approach increases model complexity but provides better disentanglement
  - M-DSLN requires additional computation during training but improves generalization
  - Separate pitch predictors for speaker-independent and dependent components add parameters but improve pitch accuracy

- Failure signatures:
  - Speaker similarity metrics degrade significantly if M-DSLN is removed
  - Pronunciation errors occur when residual connection is removed
  - Cross-lingual performance degrades more than intra-lingual when components are ablated

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replace M-DSLN with standard DSLN and measure impact on cross-lingual EER
  2. Remove speaker generalization loss (Lsgr) and evaluate speaker similarity degradation
  3. Remove residual connection from hsi and assess pronunciation accuracy and overall quality

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the proposed components in CrossSpeech generalize to languages with significantly different phonetic inventories or prosodic patterns?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper tests CrossSpeech on three languages (English, Chinese, Korean) but doesn't explore how well it would work for languages with very different characteristics.
- Why unresolved: The experimental validation is limited to languages from the same family (all using IPA).
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing CrossSpeech on a diverse set of languages with different phonetic inventories and prosodic systems.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal architecture for handling speaker-language combinations not seen during training in cross-lingual TTS?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state "it is hard to predict speech variation owing to the speaker-language combinations unseen during training."
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explore architectural variations or provide guidelines for determining the best architecture for unseen speaker-language combinations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies testing different architectural variants across multiple unseen speaker-language combinations.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of CrossSpeech scale with dataset size and speaker diversity in cross-lingual TTS?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses relatively small datasets (maximum 12 speakers) and doesn't investigate how performance changes with larger datasets.
- Why unresolved: The experiments are conducted on limited data, making it unclear how well the approach would perform with larger, more diverse datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic scaling experiments testing CrossSpeech performance across varying dataset sizes and speaker diversity levels.

## Limitations
- Proprietary datasets limit reproducibility and validation on public benchmarks
- Two-stage generator architecture increases computational complexity and training time
- Limited exploration of how performance scales with larger, more diverse datasets

## Confidence

**Medium Confidence:** Effectiveness of Mix-DSLN for speaker generalization and the speaker generalization loss formulation.

**Low Confidence:** Claims about universal applicability to other multilingual scenarios and handling of unseen speaker-language combinations.
