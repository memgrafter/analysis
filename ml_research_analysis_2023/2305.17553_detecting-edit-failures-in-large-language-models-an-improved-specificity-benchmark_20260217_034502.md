# Detecting Edit Failures In Large Language Models: An Improved Specificity Benchmark

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2305.17553
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17553
- **Reference count**: 8
- **Primary result**: Introduces COUNTER FACT+ and NKL metric to reveal previously unreported specificity problems in ROME and MEMIT model editing techniques.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a dynamic specificity benchmark called COUNTER FACT+ and a new metric, Neighborhood KL Divergence (NKL), to better detect unwanted side effects in model editing techniques. The authors find that existing benchmarks miss important specificity failures, particularly the phenomenon of "loud facts" where edited models inappropriately complete unrelated prompts when primed with the edit context. Using these improved evaluation methods, they demonstrate that popular model editing techniques like ROME and MEMIT suffer from significant specificity issues that were previously unreported.

## Method Summary
The authors extend the COUNTER FACT benchmark by creating COUNTER FACT+, which prepends the model edit context to neighborhood prompts to make the benchmark more sensitive to unwanted side effects. They introduce NKL as a metric that measures the full probability distribution divergence between edited and unedited models. The evaluation uses three model editing techniques (ROME, MEMIT, FT-L) on GPT-2 and GPT-J models, measuring specificity using NS, NM, and NKL on both original and dynamic benchmarks.

## Key Results
- ROME and MEMIT show significant specificity failures on COUNTER FACT+ that were not detected by the original benchmark
- NKL reveals distribution shifts for semantically related tokens that NS/NM miss
- FT-L demonstrates better specificity performance than ROME and MEMIT
- The "loud facts" phenomenon becomes apparent only with the dynamic benchmark

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Prepending the model edit to neighborhood prompts increases the likelihood of detecting unwanted side effects.
- Mechanism: By including the linguistic context of the model edit in the test prompt, the model is more likely to exhibit "loud facts" behavior, where the edit's object token (e.g., "Rome") is inappropriately completed for unrelated prompts.
- Core assumption: The model's behavior changes significantly when primed with the linguistic context of the model edit, revealing previously hidden side effects.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Mentioning the subject of the model edit can drastically change the behavior of the edited model, but the existing benchmark does not detect this."
  - [section] "Motivated by the example of loud facts shown in Fig. 1 and by the intuition that unwanted side effects are more likely when the model is primed with the linguistic context of the model edit, we now introduce a dynamic version of COUNTER FACT which we will refer to as COUNTER FACT+."
  - [corpus] Weak: The corpus mentions "unveiling pitfalls" but does not specifically anchor the priming mechanism.
- Break condition: If the model edit does not significantly alter the model's behavior when primed with the edit context, the dynamic benchmark will not reveal additional side effects.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using Neighborhood KL Divergence (NKL) as a metric captures unwanted side effects not detected by existing metrics.
- Mechanism: NKL measures the divergence of the entire next-token probability distribution, detecting changes in semantically related tokens (e.g., "Vatican" when "Rome" is the edit object) that NS and NM miss.
- Core assumption: Changes in the full probability distribution reflect unwanted side effects of the model edit, even when the directly implicated tokens (original and edit object) are not affected.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The edited model displays strongly changed logits not only for the original object ("Paris") and edit object ("Rome") but also for semantically related tokens ("Vatican"). Again, this would be overlooked by the current specificity evaluation since it does not consider the entire probability distribution."
  - [section] "To capture this possibility, we introduce as an additional metric the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence of the next-token distribution between the edited and unedited model, referred to as Neighborhood KL Divergence (NKL)."
  - [corpus] Weak: The corpus does not directly discuss KL divergence as a metric for model editing.
- Break condition: If the model edit does not significantly alter the full probability distribution, NKL will not detect additional side effects.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The dynamic benchmark (COUNTER FACT+) and NKL metric reveal that ROME and MEMIT have previously unreported specificity problems.
- Mechanism: By combining the priming effect of COUNTER FACT+ with the comprehensive detection of NKL, the evaluation uncovers significant drops in specificity for ROME and MEMIT that were not detected by the original COUNTER FACT benchmark and NS/NM metrics.
- Core assumption: The combination of dynamic prompting and full-distribution analysis provides a more sensitive and comprehensive evaluation of model editing specificity than existing methods.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using this improved benchmark to evaluate recent model editing techniques and find that they suffer from low specificity."
  - [section] "We use this improved benchmark to evaluate recent model editing techniques and find previously unreported side effects."
  - [corpus] Weak: The corpus mentions "evaluating the edit locality" but does not specifically anchor the combined effect of dynamic prompting and NKL.
- Break condition: If the model edit does not introduce significant unwanted side effects that are detectable by the dynamic benchmark and NKL, the improved evaluation will not reveal additional problems.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: COUNTER FACT+ dataset -> NKL metric computation -> Model editing algorithms (ROME, MEMIT, FT-L) -> GPT-2, GPT-J models
- Critical path: Generate COUNTER FACT+ dataset by prepending model edits to neighborhood prompts, compute NKL for each prompt, aggregate results to evaluate specificity across editing algorithms
- Design tradeoffs: COUNTER FACT+ is more sensitive but potentially more computationally expensive; NKL captures more side effects but may be noisier than NS/NM
- Failure signatures: If NS and NM remain high on COUNTER FACT+ but NKL is also high, it suggests the model is completing the specific tokens correctly but altering the broader distribution (a "loud facts" failure)
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate the comparison of NS on COUNTER FACT vs. COUNTER FACT+ for a simple edit (e.g., ROME on GPT-2-medium)
  2. Compute NKL for the same edit on both datasets to confirm the distribution shift
  3. Test FT-L as a baseline on COUNTER FACT+ to confirm it has fewer "loud facts" issues than ROME/MEMIT

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do model editing techniques like ROME and MEMIT perform on larger language models (LLMs) beyond GPT-J (6B), and do they eventually overcome the specificity issues observed in smaller models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions preliminary evidence that specificity might improve for larger models but also notes that this is confounded by the improved specificity of the unedited model.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates up to GPT-J (6B), and it's unclear if the trend continues or if specificity issues are completely resolved in extremely large models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical evaluation of ROME and MEMIT on significantly larger models (e.g., GPT-3, GPT-4) to assess if specificity issues persist or diminish.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What are the long-term effects of model editing on the ability of language models to capture discourse structure and fluency in generated text?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper acknowledges that it does not evaluate other types of model edits or their effects on long-term aspects of language models, such as discourse structure and fluency.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on specificity and does not explore how model edits impact the overall quality and coherence of generated text over longer contexts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Corpus-level analysis and dynamic approaches like red-teaming or dynamic benchmarking to uncover subtle adverse effects on discourse structure and fluency.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do ROME and MEMIT perform when multiple model edits are performed simultaneously, and what are the implications for specificity and overall model performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that MEMIT can handle thousands of simultaneous edits but does not evaluate this scenario or its impact on specificity.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates single model edits and does not explore the cumulative effects of multiple edits on model specificity and performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of ROME and MEMIT with multiple simultaneous edits to assess the impact on specificity and model performance.

## Limitations

- The paper's central claim about "unreported problems" is contingent on the sensitivity of the new benchmark without statistical significance tests
- NKL metric lacks clear thresholds for what constitutes problematic divergence
- Focus on GPT-2 and GPT-J models limits generalizability to other architectures
- Does not explore whether "loud facts" is an inherent limitation or a byproduct of the priming strategy

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The mechanism of prepending edit context to neighborhood prompts (COUNTER FACT+) is technically sound and well-motivated by the "loud facts" example. The NKL metric is a valid extension of standard evaluation practices.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that ROME and MEMIT have "previously unreported" specificity problems is supported by the data but could benefit from statistical validation and comparison with more baseline methods.
- **Low Confidence**: The paper's assertion that the new benchmark is definitively "better" than existing ones is not fully substantiated without broader empirical coverage or ablation studies on the priming mechanism.

## Next Checks

1. **Statistical Significance Testing**: Perform paired t-tests or bootstrap confidence intervals on NS/NM differences between COUNTER FACT and COUNTER FACT+ to determine if improvements are statistically significant across all tested edits.

2. **Ablation on Priming**: Create a variant of COUNTER FACT+ without the edit context prepending (i.e., random unrelated text) to test whether the observed improvements are specifically due to edit-relevant priming or general context effects.

3. **Threshold Calibration for NKL**: Define and validate a quantitative threshold for "problematic" NKL values by analyzing the distribution of NKL scores on randomly generated edits or on FT-L edits, which the paper claims are more specific.
