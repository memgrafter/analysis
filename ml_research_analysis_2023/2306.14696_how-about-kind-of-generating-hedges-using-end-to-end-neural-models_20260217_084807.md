# How About Kind of Generating Hedges using End-to-End Neural Models?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.14696
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14696
- Reference count: 26
- Key outcome: Reranking improves hedge F1 from 0.4-0.5 to 0.84-0.85 in tutoring dialogues

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of generating conversational hedges in tutoring dialogues by fine-tuning pre-trained language models (BART, BlenderBot, DialoGPT) and then reranking generated candidates using a hedge classifier. The two-step approach significantly outperforms fine-tuning alone, achieving F1 scores of 0.85 for BART and 0.84 for BlenderBot. Error analysis reveals that most failures stem from coherence-driven generation that prioritizes content over social goals, especially in low-rapport interactions where hedges are more frequent but under-generated. The models can learn different hedge subtypes but struggle with contextual appropriateness, highlighting the need for explicit social-state modeling.

## Method Summary
The authors employ a two-step approach: first, fine-tune pre-trained models (BART, BlenderBot, DialoGPT) on a 60-hour peer-tutoring corpus containing 701 hedge turns and 4455 non-hedge turns; second, generate 50 candidate utterances using beam search and rerank them with a hedge classifier to select the utterance matching the target hedge strategy. Performance is evaluated using F1 score for hedge generation along with BLEU, ROUGE-L, CHRF, BARTScore, BERTScore, and human evaluation.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuning alone achieves F1 scores around 0.4-0.5 for hedge generation
- Reranking improves F1 to 0.85 (BART) and 0.84 (BlenderBot)
- Error analysis shows 92.5% of over-generation errors are due to goal mismatch
- Low-rapport interactions contain 264 hedges vs. 42 in high-rapport interactions
- Models learn different hedge subtypes but struggle with contextual appropriateness

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Fine-tuning alone cannot implicitly learn hedge generation due to insufficient signal for social strategy.
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning optimizes for content coherence and token likelihood but lacks explicit social context to modulate hedging.
- Core assumption: Hedge generation requires more than surface-level pattern matching; it depends on social state modeling.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "fine-tuning state-of-the-art language models... followed by ii) reranking to select the candidate that best matches the expected hedging strategy"
  - [section]: "the performance of all three models was comparable but very limited. This suggests that the fine-tuning approach does not allow language models to learn hedge knowledge implicitly."
  - [corpus]: "our spontaneous dialogue dataset may lead the way for cutting-edge models trained on a real-world, face-to-face interactional dataset."
- Break condition: If the corpus contained richer social annotations (e.g., rapport, face threat levels), fine-tuning might capture more implicit hedge cues.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Reranking with a hedge classifier dramatically improves hedge F1 score by explicitly enforcing social strategy.
- Mechanism: A candidate pool is generated via fine-tuning, then filtered and ranked by a hedge classifier to match the target hedge label.
- Core assumption: The hedge classifier reliably detects hedge vs. non-hedge labels, and candidate pool size is sufficient to find a match.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "reranking to select the candidate that best matches the expected hedging strategy within a candidate pool using a hedge classifier"
  - [section]: "Table 2 shows that models deployed with the reranking method have relatively higher or comparable Bart scores, but greatly improved performance on the F1 score (from .54 to .85)."
  - [corpus]: "the hedge classifier selected the most suitable utterances, and yet the model still produced the wrong strategy"
- Break condition: If the hedge classifier misclassifies hedge candidates (as in 92.5% of over-generation errors), reranking effectiveness collapses.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Hedge distribution imbalance across rapport levels leads to under-generation errors in low-rapport contexts.
- Mechanism: Low-rapport interactions contain more hedges; when the model under-generates hedges, the mismatch appears more often in low-rapport cases.
- Core assumption: Hedge usage correlates with rapport levels, and the model's coherence-driven generation favors content over social strategy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "low-rapport interactions where hedges are more frequent but under-generated"
  - [section]: "We believe that this is because more hedges are found in low rapport interaction. Therefore, we count the hedge distribution of the low and high rapport interaction in the test dataset."
  - [corpus]: "264 hedges are found in low rapport interaction, and 42 in high rapport interaction"
- Break condition: If rapport annotations were unavailable, this mechanism would be invisible; imbalance effects would go undetected.

## Foundational Learning

  - Why needed here: The system needs to distinguish between hedge vs. non-hedge and hedge subcategories (apologies, shields, approximators).
  - Quick check question: What are the three main hedge subcategories used in the corpus annotations?

- Concept: Reranking with classifier filtering
  - Why needed here: Improves social strategy adherence without retraining the entire model; bridges gap between content coherence and social goals.
  - Quick check question: Why is a 50-candidate pool chosen instead of generating until a hedge is found?

- Concept: Rapport-level error distribution analysis
  - Why needed here: Reveals why certain hedge contexts are systematically under-generated, guiding targeted data augmentation or loss function redesign.
  - Quick check question: How does the hedge distribution in low-rapport vs. high-rapport interactions affect the model's error pattern?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data loader -> Corpus split -> Fine-tuning generator -> Hedge classifier -> Reranking pipeline -> Evaluation metrics
- Critical path: Input dialogue history -> Generate 50 candidates -> Classify each with hedge classifier -> Filter by target label -> Pick highest score candidate -> Output
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Fine-tuning vs. reranking: Fine-tuning cheaper but weak; reranking stronger but requires extra classifier.
  - Candidate pool size: Larger improves match chance but increases compute; 50 chosen as compromise.
- Failure signatures:
  - Low hedge F1 with reranking -> Classifier misclassifies or pool lacks matching strategy.
  - Coherence loss in reranked outputs -> Classifier over-filtering, losing token likelihood.
  - Systematically missing hedges in low-rapport -> Hedge distribution imbalance not addressed.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Compare F1 on balanced vs. unbalanced test sets to confirm imbalance impact.
  2. Vary candidate pool size (10, 50, 100) to measure F1 trade-off curve.
  3. Swap classifier for rule-based vs. ML-based to test error source.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal pool size for the reranking method to balance hedge generation accuracy and computational efficiency?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that a candidate pool size of 50 was chosen as a compromise between likelihood of obtaining a hedge and speed of generation, but does not explore other pool sizes.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide experimental results comparing different pool sizes to determine the optimal value.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying the candidate pool size and measuring the resulting hedge generation accuracy and computational time would identify the optimal pool size.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would the hedge generation performance change if the models were fine-tuned on a dataset of expert tutors rather than untrained teenage tutors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that the current dataset consists of untrained teenage tutors and suggests investigating expert tutors in future work, implying that this could impact hedge generation performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide any experimental results using expert tutor data, so the potential impact on hedge generation is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Fine-tuning the models on a dataset of expert tutor dialogues and comparing the hedge generation performance to the current results would demonstrate the impact of tutor expertise.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can large language models be augmented to better handle social goals like rapport management alongside task-oriented goals like content coherence?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The error analysis reveals that a key limitation is the models' tendency to prioritize content coherence over social goals like rapport management, leading to "Goal Mismatch" errors.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies this limitation but does not propose or test specific solutions for enabling models to balance social and task goals more effectively.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and evaluating methods to incorporate social state representations or multi-task learning objectives into the models, and measuring the resulting impact on hedge generation and rapport management, would provide insights into potential solutions.

## Limitations
- The hedge classifier architecture and training details are not fully specified, making it difficult to assess the source of performance gains
- Coherence goals trump social goals in low-rapport interactions, but the paper doesn't explore targeted solutions beyond reranking
- The suggestion that "explicit social-state modeling" is needed remains speculative without concrete architectural proposals

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Fine-tuning alone cannot implicitly learn hedge generation (F1 ~0.4-0.5 vs. 0.84-0.85 with reranking)
- **Medium Confidence**: Coherence goals trump social goals in low-rapport interactions (requires further validation)
- **Low Confidence**: "Explicit social-state modeling" is needed for future work (speculative without concrete proposals)
