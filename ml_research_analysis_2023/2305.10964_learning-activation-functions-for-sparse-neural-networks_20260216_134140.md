# Learning Activation Functions for Sparse Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10964
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10964
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Up to 15.53% absolute accuracy gains for sparse networks at high pruning ratios

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses accuracy degradation in sparse neural networks by identifying two key issues: the universal use of ReLU activation functions and the application of dense network hyperparameters to sparse architectures. The authors propose Sparse Activation Function Search (SAFS), a two-stage optimization framework that automatically learns optimal activation functions and hyperparameters for sparse networks. Through extensive experiments on multiple architectures and datasets, SAFS demonstrates significant improvements over default training protocols, particularly at high pruning ratios.

## Method Summary
SAFS combines discrete optimization for selecting activation functions with gradient-based optimization for scaling factors and hyperparameters. The framework operates in two stages: first, it uses Late Acceptance Hill Climbing (LAHC) to search for optimal activation functions per layer; second, it jointly optimizes scaling factors (α, β) and hyperparameters using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and SMAC3. The method is designed to address the specific challenges of sparse neural networks created through magnitude pruning.

## Key Results
- Up to 15.53%, 8.88%, and 6.33% absolute accuracy gains for LeNet-5, VGG-16, and ResNet-18 respectively
- Significant improvements especially at high pruning ratios (up to 99%)
- Outperforms state-of-the-art methods in accuracy-compression ratio trade-off
- Requires 47 GPU hours for VGG-16 on CIFAR-10 (vs 3.8 hours for vanilla pruning)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
ReLU activation functions are particularly harmful for sparse neural networks because they cause gradient flow blockage around zero, leading to training instability and accuracy degradation. Sparse neural networks have many zero-valued weights after pruning, and when ReLU is applied, any negative input becomes zeroed out, causing gradient flow to be blocked or significantly reduced. This creates dead neurons and prevents effective weight updates during training.

### Mechanism 2
Default dense network hyperparameters are suboptimal for sparse neural networks because sparse architectures have different optimization dynamics. Dense networks are trained with hyperparameters optimized for their dense connectivity patterns. Sparse networks, after pruning, have fundamentally different loss landscapes and gradient distributions, requiring different hyperparameter settings to navigate effectively.

### Mechanism 3
Combining activation function search with hyperparameter optimization in a joint framework provides better performance than optimizing either in isolation. The two-stage optimization (discrete search for activation functions, then continuous optimization for scaling factors and hyperparameters) allows the system to first find promising function classes and then fine-tune their parameters along with learning settings.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Dense model → Pruning → Stage 1 (activation search) → Stage 2 (scaling/HPO) → Final model
- **Critical path:** Dense model → Pruning → Stage 1 (activation search) → Stage 2 (scaling/HPO) → Final model
- **Design tradeoffs:** Search cost vs. performance (Stage 1 requires 47 GPU hours but provides significant accuracy gains), expressivity vs. search space size (using parametric activation functions balances function diversity with tractable search), discrete vs. continuous optimization (separate treatment improves efficiency)
- **Failure signatures:** Poor convergence during Stage 1 (search space may be too large or evaluation metric unreliable), Stage 2 provides minimal improvement (dense hyperparameters were already suitable or pruning ratio too low), gradient flow remains low after optimization (chosen activation functions still problematic)
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) Run SAFS on LeNet-5 on MNIST with 90% pruning to verify workflow and measure search cost vs. benefit, 2) Compare SAFS with baseline magnitude pruning on VGG-16 on CIFAR-10 at 99% pruning to measure activation function optimization impact, 3) Test different search algorithms (LAHC vs. random search) on a small problem to validate LAHC choice for Stage 1

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How do different unary operator search algorithms compare in terms of finding optimal activation functions for sparse neural networks? While LAHC performs better than alternatives for VGG-16 on CIFAR-10, it's unclear if this holds for different architectures, datasets, or pruning ratios.

- What is the impact of using SAFS on the gradient flow of sparse neural networks compared to other methods? The paper shows SAFS improves gradient flow for VGG-16 on CIFAR-10, but this limited comparison doesn't generalize to other network types or datasets.

- How does the computing cost of SAFS compare to other methods for optimizing activation functions in sparse neural networks? The reported 47 GPU hours for VGG-16 on CIFAR-10 may not scale similarly to other networks or indicate whether more efficient methods exist.

## Limitations

- Computational cost of Stage 1 (approximately 47 GPU hours) may limit practical deployment for larger models
- Effectiveness primarily evaluated on image classification tasks, raising questions about performance on other domains like NLP
- Doesn't extensively explore trade-offs between search cost and performance gains across different network architectures

## Confidence

**High Confidence:** The claim that ReLU activation functions are suboptimal for sparse neural networks is well-supported by theoretical arguments about gradient flow blockage and empirical evidence from multiple experiments showing consistent accuracy improvements when alternative activation functions are used.

**Medium Confidence:** The assertion that dense network hyperparameters are suboptimal for sparse networks is supported by ablation studies, but the analysis could benefit from more systematic exploration of the hyperparameter space and comparison with other optimization methods.

**Medium Confidence:** The overall effectiveness of SAFS compared to state-of-the-art methods is demonstrated across multiple datasets and architectures, though the comparison is limited to a specific set of baselines and doesn't explore all recent advances in sparse network training.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-domain validation:** Apply SAFS to non-image datasets (e.g., NLP tasks using BERT or LSTM architectures) to verify whether the activation function optimization benefits extend beyond computer vision applications.

2. **Search algorithm ablation:** Systematically compare LAHC with alternative discrete optimization methods (genetic algorithms, simulated annealing) and alternative hyperparameter optimization frameworks to quantify the contribution of each algorithmic choice.

3. **Scaling analysis:** Evaluate SAFS on larger models (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B7) and measure how search costs and performance gains scale with model size, including analysis of whether the two-stage optimization remains beneficial or if alternative approaches become more efficient.
