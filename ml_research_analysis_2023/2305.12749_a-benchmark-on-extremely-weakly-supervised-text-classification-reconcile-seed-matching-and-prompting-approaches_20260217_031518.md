# A Benchmark on Extremely Weakly Supervised Text Classification: Reconcile Seed Matching and Prompting Approaches

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.12749
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12749
- Reference count: 27
- Both SEED and PROMPT approaches are competitive in XWS-TC, with no clear winner

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a comprehensive benchmark comparing SEED (seed matching) and PROMPT (prompting) approaches for Extremely Weakly Supervised Text Classification (XWS-TC). Using standardized datasets, supervision, and hyperparameters, the study reveals that both approaches are competitive, with no clear winner. SEED methods demonstrate greater robustness to changes in human guidance and pre-trained language models, while PROMPT methods can handle more general types of human guidance and do not require an unlabeled corpus. A clustering post-processing step based on raw in-domain texts is shown to be a strong performance booster for both approaches.

## Method Summary
The benchmark evaluates SEED and PROMPT methods on 11 text classification datasets using standardized hyperparameters and pre-trained language models (BERT, RoBERTa, GPT-2, BART). SEED methods (X-Class, ClassKG, NPPrompt) match text to seed words and their expanded related words, while PROMPT methods (Prompt, DCPMI, ProtoCal) use generative capabilities of large language models to generate likelihoods for label words. The study controls for seed words, instructions, label words, and pre-trained models to assess robustness and scaling behavior.

## Key Results
- SEED methods are more robust to changes in human guidance (seed words, instructions, label words) than PROMPT methods
- PROMPT methods scale better with larger pre-trained language models than SEED methods
- Clustering on an unlabeled corpus is a universal performance booster for both SEED and PROMPT methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
SEED methods are more robust to changes in human guidance because they rely on matching text to seed words and their expanded related words, which are derived from statistical or embedding similarity methods. This matching process is less sensitive to the exact phrasing of seed words or instructions because it focuses on semantic similarity rather than exact text generation.

### Mechanism 2
PROMPT methods scale better with larger pre-trained language models because they use the generative capabilities of large language models to generate likelihoods for label words based on input text and instructions. As the size of the pre-trained language model increases, its ability to understand and generate more nuanced and accurate likelihoods improves, leading to better performance.

### Mechanism 3
Clustering on an unlabeled corpus is a universal performance booster because it helps to better understand the decision boundaries between classes by grouping similar texts together. This can be used to calibrate the predictions of both SEED and PROMPT methods, leading to improved performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do SEED and PROMPT methods perform on larger pre-trained language models like T0, PaLM, and ChatGPT?
- Basis in paper: The benchmark specifically excludes large language models due to lack of equivalent representation encoding language models for SEED approaches and difficulty in fair evaluation
- Why unresolved: The authors acknowledge that the current state of extremely weak supervision would be better understood with complete evaluations on state-of-the-art large language models, but they lack computational resources to perform such evaluation
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive evaluation of SEED and PROMPT methods on large language models like T0, PaLM, and ChatGPT, including their performance on various datasets and their robustness to changes in human guidance and pre-trained language models

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the quality of related words found by different SEED and verbalizer methods affect their performance and robustness?
- Basis in paper: Both SEED and verbalizer methods find related words to represent classes, but use different approaches (unlabeled corpus for SEED vs. external supervision for verbalizers)
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the quality of related words found by different methods or whether related words between methods can be used interchangeably
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparative analysis of the quality of related words found by different SEED and verbalizer methods, including their impact on performance and robustness, and an investigation into the interchangeability of related words between methods

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the clustering post-processing step be further improved to enhance the performance of both SEED and PROMPT methods?
- Basis in paper: The paper presents a study on clustering post-processing step, which was independently proposed for both SEED (X-Class) and PROMPT (ProtoCal) methods
- Why unresolved: While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of clustering post-processing step, it does not explore potential improvements or alternative clustering techniques that could further enhance performance
- What evidence would resolve it: An investigation into alternative clustering techniques and their impact on the performance of SEED and PROMPT methods, including comparisons with current clustering approaches used in X-Class and ProtoCal

## Limitations
- The benchmark uses hyperparameters tuned on a single dataset (IMDB), potentially introducing bias toward certain method families
- The evaluation framework assumes availability of unlabeled in-domain corpora, which may not be feasible in all real-world scenarios
- The exclusion of very large language models (beyond 355M parameters) limits understanding of scaling behavior at current state-of-the-art scales

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The relative performance comparison between SEED and PROMPT methods across datasets is robust, as evidenced by consistent results across multiple datasets and metrics. The finding that clustering post-processing universally improves performance is strongly supported by the experimental results.

**Medium Confidence**: The robustness analysis to different seed words, instructions, and pre-trained models shows meaningful trends but may be sensitive to the specific choices made in the experiments. The claim about SEED methods being more robust to human guidance changes is supported but could vary with different guidance formulations.

**Low Confidence**: The scaling behavior with larger pre-trained models is based on a limited range of model sizes and may not generalize to the largest available models. The trade-off between SEED and PROMPT methods' strengths in different scenarios needs further validation with more diverse datasets.
