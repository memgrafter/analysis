# Feature Interactions Reveal Linguistic Structure in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.12181
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12181
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: FIDAMs can recover grammatical structures in synthetic languages when models achieve perfect performance, but generalization to natural language (CoLA) remains limited

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a framework to evaluate feature interaction detection and attribution methods (FIDAMs) in neural networks, focusing on how well they recover linguistic structures. The authors define two key dimensions—feature removal and feature influence—to characterize existing FIDAMs and generate novel variants. Through experiments on synthetic formal languages (Identity Rule, Dyck-2, palindromes) and a natural language task (CoLA), they demonstrate that method performance is highly sensitive to baseline choice, with expected and interventional baselines often outperforming static ones. The study reveals an "Attribution Generalisation problem" where strong performance on synthetic tasks does not reliably predict success on natural language, highlighting the complexity gap between controlled and real-world linguistic structures.

## Method Summary
The authors create synthetic formal languages using probabilistic context-free grammars with known grammatical rules, then train small LSTM and Transformer models to perfect accuracy (100%). They implement multiple FIDAMs including Group Ablation, Archipelago, Shapley Interaction Index (SII), Shapley Taylor Interaction Index (STII), and Integrated Hessians, testing them with various baseline procedures (static, expected, interventional, observational). The Average Relative Rank (ARR) metric measures how well extracted feature interactions align with ground-truth grammatical dependencies. For natural language evaluation, they apply the best-performing FIDAM-baseline combinations to the CoLA acceptability classification task and compare results against dependency parse trees.

## Key Results
- FIDAMs successfully recover grammatical rules in synthetic formal languages when models achieve perfect performance, with expected and interventional baselines outperforming static ones
- Performance on synthetic tasks does not reliably predict performance on natural language (CoLA), demonstrating the Attribution Generalisation problem
- On CoLA, certain FIDAMs show moderate alignment with dependency parse trees, but this alignment is task-specific and not generalizable across methods

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Feature interaction attribution methods can recover grammatical structures when models are trained to perfect performance on synthetic formal languages.
- Mechanism: By training models to achieve 100% accuracy on synthetic formal language tasks, we ensure that the model must have learned the underlying grammatical rules. FIDAMs then extract feature interactions from the model, and if these interactions align with the known dependencies in the grammar, it demonstrates that the FIDAM faithfully reflects the model's reasoning.
- Core assumption: Perfect performance on a synthetic formal language task guarantees that the model has learned the grammatical rules and not spurious heuristics.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We show that under specific configurations, some methods are indeed able to uncover the grammatical rules acquired by a model."
  - [section] "Models are trained to perfection (100% accuracy) on both train and test set... only these results allow completely solving the task."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence linking perfect performance to grammatical knowledge.
- Break condition: If the model achieves perfect performance through memorization or spurious heuristics rather than learning the grammatical rules, the FIDAMs will not align with the true grammatical structure.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The choice of baseline procedure significantly impacts the faithfulness of FIDAMs in recovering feature interactions.
- Mechanism: Different baseline procedures (static, expected, interventional, observational) represent different assumptions about the absence of a feature. The optimal baseline depends on the specific FIDAM and the task. For example, the Identity Rule language benefits from sampling baselines, while the Dyck-2 language benefits from a fixed mapping baseline.
- Core assumption: The baseline procedure should represent a neutral value that does not introduce bias into the interaction attribution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Results show that method performance is highly sensitive to baseline choice, with expected and interventional baselines often outperforming static ones."
  - [section] "The zero-valued baseline again turns out to be a mediocre baseline: for none of the methods this results in a high ARR score."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence linking baseline choice to interaction recovery.
- Break condition: If the baseline procedure introduces systematic bias or does not represent a neutral value, the FIDAMs will not accurately reflect the true feature interactions.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The performance of FIDAMs on synthetic formal language tasks may not generalize to natural language tasks due to the Attribution Generalisation problem.
- Mechanism: Synthetic formal languages have well-defined grammatical structures and controlled complexity, allowing for strong faithfulness guarantees. Natural language is more complex and multi-faceted, with no single hierarchical structure that captures all aspects of language. Therefore, the interactions that FIDAMs recover on synthetic tasks may not align with the relevant structures in natural language.
- Core assumption: The structure of synthetic formal languages is representative of the relevant structures in natural language.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "On CoLA, certain FIDAMs align moderately with dependency parse trees, but generalization from synthetic to natural language remains limited, highlighting the Attribution Generalisation problem."
  - [section] "Our results on the formal language tasks either did not turn out to be predictive of this experiment or, alternatively, the results were predictive but the LMs made less use of dependency graph information than we might have expected."
  - [corpus] Weak - no direct corpus evidence linking synthetic task performance to natural language performance.
- Break condition: If the synthetic formal languages are not representative of the relevant structures in natural language, or if the natural language task is not well-suited for evaluating FIDAMs, the generalization will fail.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: PCFG generation -> Model training (LSTM/Transformer) -> FIDAM implementation -> Baseline application -> ARR evaluation -> Natural language analysis

- Critical path:
  1. Generate synthetic datasets using PCFGs for Identity Rule and Dyck-2 languages; train LSTM models to 100% accuracy on binary classification
  2. Implement Group Ablation, SII, STII, and Integrated Hessians FIDAMs with multiple baseline strategies (static, expected, interventional, observational)
  3. Compute Average Relative Rank scores by comparing extracted feature interactions against ground-truth grammatical rules for each dataset

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Synthetic vs. natural languages: Synthetic languages offer controlled complexity and known structures, while natural languages are more complex and multi-faceted
  - Model architecture: LSTMs and Transformers have different strengths and weaknesses in learning formal language structures
  - Baseline procedures: Different baselines represent different assumptions about the absence of a feature, and the optimal choice depends on the specific FIDAM and task

- Failure signatures:
  - Low ARR scores on synthetic formal languages indicate that the FIDAM is not faithfully reflecting the model's reasoning
  - Poor generalization from synthetic to natural language tasks indicates the Attribution Generalisation problem
  - Inconsistent results across different baseline procedures suggest that the FIDAM is sensitive to the choice of baseline

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train an LSTM on the Identity Rule language and evaluate the performance of Group Ablation, SII, and STII with different baseline procedures
  2. Train a Transformer on the Dyck-2 language and compare the performance of different FIDAMs and baseline procedures
  3. Apply the best-performing FIDAM and baseline combination from the synthetic tasks to the CoLA natural language task and analyze the results

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do feature interaction detection and attribution methods (FIDAMs) generalize from synthetic formal languages to natural language tasks like CoLA?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper highlights the Attribution Generalisation problem, noting that FIDAM performance on synthetic tasks (Dyck-2, Identity Rule) does not reliably predict performance on natural language (CoLA), and that methods like SII/STII perform well on formal languages but only moderately on CoLA.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows that synthetic tasks provide controlled environments with known grammatical structures, but natural language is more complex and multi-faceted. The gap in performance suggests that the synthetic-to-natural generalization is not straightforward.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies testing FIDAMs across diverse natural language tasks (e.g., syntax, semantics, morphology) and formal languages, with analysis of which task properties (e.g., depth of recursion, ambiguity) predict generalization success.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Which baseline selection strategy (static, expected, interventional, observational) is most effective for FIDAMs in natural language tasks, and why?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that baseline choice significantly impacts FIDAM performance, with expected and interventional baselines often outperforming static ones in synthetic tasks, but static baselines (e.g., <pad>) performing better in CoLA. The optimal baseline depends on the task and FIDAM combination.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a unified framework for baseline selection, and the effectiveness of baselines varies across tasks and FIDAMs. The causal vs. observational baseline debate remains unresolved.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies testing all baseline types across multiple FIDAMs and natural language tasks, with analysis of how baseline choice interacts with model architecture and linguistic structure.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Do higher-order feature interactions (beyond pairwise) play a significant role in modeling linguistic structure in language models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on pairwise interactions but acknowledges that linguistic structure may manifest through higher-order interactions. Methods like Archipelago can detect higher-order interactions, but the paper does not explore their effectiveness in natural language.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluates pairwise interactions, leaving open whether higher-order interactions capture additional linguistic phenomena (e.g., long-range dependencies, semantic roles).
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing pairwise vs. higher-order FIDAMs on natural language tasks, with analysis of whether higher-order interactions align with linguistic structures like syntactic trees or semantic roles.

## Limitations

- Performance on synthetic formal languages does not reliably predict performance on natural language tasks, highlighting the Attribution Generalisation problem
- Baseline choice significantly impacts FIDAM performance, suggesting results may be sensitive to methodological choices rather than intrinsic method capabilities
- Limited exploration of higher-order feature interactions beyond pairwise interactions, leaving open whether more complex linguistic phenomena can be captured

## Confidence

- **Synthetic language results**: High confidence - controlled experiments with known ground truth provide strong evidence for mechanism validity
- **Baseline sensitivity findings**: High confidence - systematic evaluation across multiple methods and tasks consistently shows baseline importance
- **Natural language generalization**: Low confidence - limited alignment with dependency structures and unclear causal relationship between synthetic and natural language performance
- **Attribution Generalisation problem**: Medium confidence - identified as a limitation but not thoroughly investigated
