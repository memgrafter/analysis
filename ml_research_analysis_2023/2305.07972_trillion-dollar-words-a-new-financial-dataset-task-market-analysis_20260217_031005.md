# Trillion Dollar Words: A New Financial Dataset, Task & Market Analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.07972
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07972
- Reference count: 13
- Key outcome: RoBERTa-large achieves 0.7113 F1 score on hawkish-dovish classification of FOMC communications

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Trillion Dollar Words, a novel dataset and task for classifying Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) communications as hawkish, dovish, or neutral. The authors construct the largest annotated dataset of FOMC speeches, meeting minutes, and press conference transcripts, and benchmark various pre-trained language models on this dataset. Using the best-performing model (RoBERTa-large), they construct a measure of monetary policy stance for FOMC document release days. The study finds that RoBERTa-large achieves an average F1 score of 0.7113 on the hawkish-dovish classification task. The constructed measure is validated by its correlation with inflation indicators (CPI and PPI) and its ability to predict treasury yields and stock market returns.

## Method Summary
The authors created a dataset of FOMC communications (1996-2022) by collecting meeting minutes, press conference transcripts, and speeches, then filtering sentences based on a predefined dictionary of monetary policy terms. They manually annotated samples to create a training dataset and implemented sentence splitting to separate contrasting sentiments. Various models were trained including rule-based approaches, LSTM, and fine-tuned PLMs (BERT-base, RoBERTa-base, RoBERTa-large, ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo). The best model (RoBERTa-large) was then used to construct a document-level hawkishness measure that was validated against macroeconomic indicators and market returns.

## Key Results
- RoBERTa-large achieves an average F1 score of 0.7113 on hawkish-dovish classification
- The constructed measure correlates positively with CPI and PPI indicators
- Trading strategies using the measure generate modest returns of 0.5-1% on treasury yields and 0.2-0.8% on stock market returns

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would the hawkish-dovish classification performance change if audio or video features from FOMC press conferences were incorporated into the model?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge that their study focuses only on text data and note that audio or video features might contain additional information that could be useful for monetary policy stance classification.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not explore multimodal approaches that combine text with audio or video data, which could potentially improve classification accuracy by capturing non-verbal cues and tone.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training and evaluating the same hawkish-dovish classification models on a multimodal dataset that includes audio and video features from FOMC press conferences, and comparing the performance metrics to the text-only models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Would a zero-shot or few-shot approach with more recent generative LLMs (beyond ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo) outperform fine-tuned PLMs on the hawkish-dovish classification task?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors benchmark ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo as a zero-shot baseline and find it underperforms fine-tuned PLMs. They suggest that a more comprehensive benchmark with open-source models could provide better comparisons.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests one generative LLM (ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo) in a zero-shot setting. More recent models like GPT-4, Claude, or open-source alternatives might perform better, especially with few-shot prompting or fine-tuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematically evaluating a range of state-of-the-art generative LLMs using zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned approaches on the hawkish-dovish classification task, and comparing their performance metrics to the current best-performing fine-tuned PLMs.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How would the performance of the hawkish-dovish classification models change if the sentence splitting approach was improved using a more sophisticated method than the simple rule-based approach used in this study?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge that their sentence splitting method is simple and rule-based, and they leave it as an open problem for future researchers to find better methods for splitting sentences with opposite tones.
- Why unresolved: The current sentence splitting approach may not optimally separate sentences with mixed tones, potentially affecting the quality of the training data and the subsequent model performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and implementing a more advanced sentence splitting method (e.g., using semantic analysis or machine learning-based approaches) and retraining the hawkish-dovish classification models on the improved dataset, then comparing the performance metrics to those obtained with the original sentence splitting approach.

## Limitations

- Dataset size for press conference data (4,010 annotated sentences) is notably smaller than for meeting minutes (25,611 sentences) and speeches (9,428 sentences), which may limit model performance and generalizability for this communication type.
- The sentence splitting heuristic, while showing improvements for meeting minutes and press conferences, relies on a fixed set of contrastive conjunctions that may not capture all nuanced sentiment shifts in FOMC communications.
- Economic validation focuses primarily on short-term market reactions (up to 1 day) without examining longer-term policy transmission mechanisms.
