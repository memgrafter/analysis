# Massively Multilingual Shallow Fusion with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.08917
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08917
- Reference count: 0
- 1.9B-parameter GLaM MoE LM achieves 5.53% average WER reduction across 43 languages via shallow fusion

## Executive Summary
This work explores using large language models (LLMs) in multilingual speech recognition through shallow fusion with an end-to-end ASR model. The approach scales to 84 languages using a mixture-of-experts LLM (GLaM) that dynamically activates two experts per decoding step to maintain constant inference computation. Compared to dense Conformer LMs of similar inference cost, GLaM reduces WER by 4.4% relative on an English long-tail test set and improves 41 out of 50 languages with an average WER reduction of 3.85% in multilingual settings. Overall, GLaM achieves an average WER reduction of 5.53% over 43 languages, demonstrating superior performance while maintaining efficient inference due to its sparse architecture.

## Method Summary
The approach trains a 1.9B-parameter GLaM mixture-of-experts LLM on combined speech-text and text-only data from 84 languages. During shallow fusion, the E2E ASR model scores are combined with GLaM scores at each beam search step. The GLaM model uses 64 experts but dynamically activates only 2 per decoding step, maintaining inference computation comparable to a 140M-parameter dense Conformer LM. Training combines Voice Search transcripts with anonymized search traffic sentences to avoid domain mismatch.

## Key Results
- GLaM-64E reduces WER by 4.4% relative on English long-tail test set compared to 140M Conformer LM
- Improves 41 out of 50 languages with average WER reduction of 3.85% in multilingual settings
- Achieves average WER reduction of 5.53% over 43 languages
- Dynamic expert selection maintains inference computation comparable to dense 140M-parameter model

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GLaM's mixture-of-experts architecture achieves superior multilingual ASR performance by dynamically activating only two experts per decoding step, maintaining constant computation while leveraging a large parameter space.
- Mechanism: During shallow fusion, GLaM uses a gating network to route each token to the two most relevant experts from a pool of 64. This selective activation allows the model to maintain rich linguistic representations for 84 languages while keeping inference computation comparable to a 140M-parameter dense Conformer LM.
- Core assumption: The gating network can effectively identify the most relevant experts for each token across diverse languages, and the dynamic routing provides better language-specific representations than a static dense model.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "When the number of experts increases, GLaM dynamically selects only two at each decoding step to keep the inference computation roughly constant." [section] "GLaM dynamically activates two experts for forward propagation, which in total amounts to less than 8% of the network, i.e., around 145M parameters"
- Break condition: If the gating network fails to route tokens effectively across languages, performance degrades. This could happen with highly similar languages or when domain mismatch causes routing errors.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Training GLaM on both speech-text data and text-only data from all 84 languages eliminates domain mismatch and improves ASR performance.
- Mechanism: By combining Voice Search transcripts (speech-text data) with anonymized search traffic sentences (text-only data) during GLaM training, the model learns representations that match the ASR task domain while maintaining multilingual coverage. This prevents the typical domain mismatch that occurs when LLMs trained on web data are applied to ASR.
- Core assumption: The combined training data provides sufficient domain-relevant coverage for all 84 languages while maintaining the linguistic diversity needed for effective multilingual modeling.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We combine transcripts from both speech-text data and text-only data from all languages in training to avoid domain mismatch." [section] "Our text data covers 75 of the 84 languages... sentences are sampled from anonymized search traffic across multiple domains such as Web, Maps, News, Play, and YouTube"
- Break condition: If text-only data doesn't adequately represent the ASR domain or if certain languages lack sufficient training data, the model may underperform on those languages.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The token-level dynamic routing in GLaM provides better handling of code-switching and proper nouns compared to dense LMs.
- Mechanism: The MoE architecture allows different experts to specialize in different linguistic phenomena. During decoding, the gating network can route code-switched tokens or rare proper nouns to experts that have learned to handle these specific cases, improving recognition accuracy.
- Core assumption: The MoE experts develop specialized capabilities for handling challenging linguistic phenomena like code-switching and proper nouns during training.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "There is one interesting example that for en ph a code-switching example with a transliterated Chinese song name 'ni yao de ai' is recognized correctly in GLaM shallow fusion but not using Conformer" [section] "GLaM wins mainly focus on person names and proper nouns"
- Break condition: If the experts don't specialize effectively or if routing becomes too rigid, the model may not provide the expected benefits for code-switching or proper noun recognition.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architecture
  - Why needed here: MoE allows scaling to 1.9B parameters while maintaining inference efficiency by activating only 145M parameters per decoding step, crucial for practical deployment
  - Quick check question: How does MoE maintain constant computation while scaling parameter count?

- Concept: Shallow Fusion in ASR
  - Why needed here: Shallow fusion combines E2E model scores with external LM scores at each beam search step, enabling integration of text-only data without retraining the E2E model
  - Quick check question: What's the key difference between shallow fusion and deep fusion in terms of model architecture?

- Concept: Multilingual Tokenization and Vocabulary Sharing
  - Why needed here: The model uses mixed transcripts pooled from all 84 languages to generate wordpieces, requiring understanding of how shared vocabularies work across languages
  - Quick check question: How does a shared vocabulary handle languages with different scripts and morphologies?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Speech-Text Data + Text-Only Data -> GLaM Training -> 2B E2E Conformer Model -> Shallow Fusion Decoding -> WER Evaluation
- Critical path: 1. Preprocess speech-text and text-only data 2. Train GLaM on combined data 3. Train E2E model 4. Apply shallow fusion during decoding 5. Evaluate on test sets
- Design tradeoffs: Storage vs computation (1.9B GLaM requires storage but inference is efficient); Expert count vs routing overhead (more experts provide better specialization but require more complex routing); Data combination vs domain mismatch (combining speech-text and text-only data helps but may introduce noise)
- Failure signatures: Poor performance on specific languages indicates routing or data issues; Increased WER on long-tail test sets suggests domain mismatch; Regression on certain languages indicates over-correction or text normalization issues
- First 3 experiments: 1. Compare 2-expert vs 64-expert GLaM on English long-tail test set 2. Test GLaM-64E on a subset of 10 languages before full-scale deployment 3. Ablation study: Train GLaM on speech-text data only vs combined data

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of GLaM scale when increasing the number of experts beyond 64, and what are the computational and quality trade-offs at different scales?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explores a 64-expert GLaM model and compares it to a 2-expert version, but does not investigate models with more than 64 experts.
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests up to 64 experts, leaving the potential benefits and limitations of larger expert counts unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical results comparing models with varying numbers of experts (e.g., 128, 256) on the same multilingual ASR tasks, showing performance gains and computational costs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the GLaM model be further improved for code-switching scenarios, such as the Mandarin example in the paper where over-correction occurred?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that GLaM suffers from over-correction and text normalization issues in code-switching examples, suggesting a need for additional training data or model adjustments.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the issue but does not propose or test solutions for improving code-switching performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results showing improved code-switching accuracy after incorporating code-switching-specific training data or architectural modifications.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the GLaM model perform in low-resource language scenarios, given that some languages in the study have as few as 2K training utterances?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that training data for some languages is limited (e.g., 2K to 66M utterances), but does not analyze GLaM's performance specifically on low-resource languages.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed breakdown of GLaM's effectiveness in low-resource settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative performance metrics of GLaM versus baseline models on low-resource languages, highlighting improvements or challenges.

## Limitations

- Limited statistical significance analysis across languages makes it difficult to assess consistency of improvements
- Inference efficiency claims lack rigorous computational profiling and benchmarking
- Domain adaptation effectiveness not systematically validated across all 84 languages

## Confidence

- Confidence Level: Medium for overall WER improvement claims - reported improvements lack statistical significance testing
- Confidence Level: Low for claimed inference efficiency - computational comparisons not rigorously quantified
- Confidence Level: Medium for domain adaptation mechanism - effectiveness based on performance improvements rather than systematic analysis

## Next Checks
