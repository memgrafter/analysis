# Recover Triggered States: Protect Model Against Backdoor Attack in Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2304.00252
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00252
- Reference count: 23
- Key outcome: RTS maintains 101.7% performance in single-step attacks and 95.49% in consecutive two-step attacks on Mujoco Hopper, significantly outperforming baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces RTS, a novel defense mechanism against backdoor attacks in reinforcement learning that leverages a surrogate dynamics model to recover triggered states. The core innovation is incorporating action information into the dynamics model training objective to reduce discrepancies between actions taken on predicted versus real states. This addresses the compounding error problem in continuous control scenarios where small state prediction errors can lead to significantly different policy actions. The method achieves state-of-the-art defense performance on Mujoco Hopper, maintaining near-baseline performance even under consecutive backdoor triggers.

## Method Summary
RTS trains a dual-objective dynamics model that predicts both the next state and incorporates an action regularization term to align policy actions on predicted states with those on real states. The defender uses this model to detect trigger stage attacks by comparing predicted and actual next states - significant deviations indicate backdoor activation. When a trigger is detected, the defender recovers the environment state to prevent backdoor activation. The approach requires collecting clean trajectories from the poisoned agent, training the dynamics model with the dual-objective loss function, and deploying the defender to monitor state transitions during inference.

## Key Results
- Maintains 101.7% performance in single-step backdoor attacks on Mujoco Hopper
- Preserves 95.49% performance under consecutive two-step attacks
- Outperforms baseline defenses by significant margins in cumulative reward metrics
- First work to successfully use dynamics models for defending RL against backdoor attacks

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The dual-objective dynamics model reduces compounding errors by aligning policy actions on predicted states with those on real states.
- Mechanism: By adding an action-related regularization term to the training objective, the dynamics model generates states that guide the agent to execute actions similar to those taken on real states.
- Core assumption: Small discrepancies between predicted and real states cause significant policy action deviations in continuous control scenarios.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "When training the surrogate to predict states, we incorporate agent action information to reduce the discrepancy between the actions taken by the agent on predicted states and the actions taken on real states."
  - [section]: "Thus, we add an action-related regularization term to the training target of the single-deterministic dynamics model."
- Break condition: If the policy's action space is discrete and small, the action discrepancy may not significantly impact performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The RTS method detects trigger stage attacks by leveraging environmental dynamics consistency.
- Mechanism: The defender model predicts the next state based on the current state and action. If the actual next state deviates significantly from the predicted state, it indicates a trigger stage attack.
- Core assumption: Backdoor triggers introduce discontinuities in the environment's dynamics that the dynamics model can detect.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Developers can then recover the environment from the triggered state to a clean state, thereby preventing attackers from activating backdoors hidden in the agent by presenting the trigger."
  - [section]: "In the equation (3), the Td is our defender. If an attacker wants to trigger the backdoor, he must distort the input and this will cause the discontinuity of the environment dynamics especially in a continuous control scenario thus our defender would easily capture the difference."
- Break condition: If the trigger causes subtle changes that don't significantly alter the state transition dynamics, detection may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The performance bound of backdoor attacks is determined by their ability to change action distributions.
- Mechanism: The defender's effectiveness is measured by how closely the actions taken on predicted states match those on real states, reducing the total variation distance between action distributions.
- Core assumption: The effectiveness of backdoor attacks can be bounded by the maximum deviation they cause in policy action distributions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Thus, as shown in (8), we can evaluate the defender's performance in preventing the trigger stage attack by examining the differences between π(·|st) and π(·|Td(st−1,at−1))."
  - [section]: "This equation shows the importance of a defender's ability to generate a predicted state and guide a victim agent's actions to closely approximate those in the true state."
- Break condition: If the policy is highly stochastic, small changes in state may not significantly affect action distributions.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Victim Agent (πV) -> Dynamics Model (T) -> Defender -> Environment (env)
- Critical path:
  1. Collect clean trajectories by letting the victim agent interact with the environment.
  2. Train the dynamics model using the collected data with the dual-objective loss function.
  3. During deployment, at each time step:
     a. The agent takes an action based on the current state.
     b. The defender predicts the next state using the dynamics model.
     c. If the actual next state deviates significantly from the prediction, the defender recovers the state.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single-objective vs. dual-objective dynamics model: The dual-objective model provides better defense but may require more data and computational resources.
  - Detection threshold: Setting a higher threshold reduces false positives but may miss subtle attacks; a lower threshold does the opposite.
- Failure signatures:
  - False negatives: The defender fails to detect a trigger stage attack, allowing the backdoor to activate.
  - False positives: The defender incorrectly identifies a normal state transition as an attack, potentially degrading performance.
  - Compounding errors: If the dynamics model's predictions are consistently inaccurate, the defender's effectiveness diminishes over time.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Single-step attack test: Evaluate the performance of the victim agent with and without RTS protection under a single-step backdoor trigger.
  2. Consecutive attack test: Assess the defender's ability to handle multiple consecutive backdoor triggers.
  3. Ablation study: Compare the performance of single-objective and dual-objective dynamics models in defending against backdoor attacks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the episodic-related dynamics model encourage a better performance in model-based reinforcement learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention that the episodic-related dynamics model trained with episodic data could potentially improve model-based RL performance.
- Why unresolved: The paper suggests this as a future research direction but does not investigate it experimentally.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison of episodic-related dynamics models vs. traditional models in various model-based RL benchmarks, measuring sample efficiency and final performance.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Is the action-related regularization term effective when applied to other types of dynamics models beyond the single-deterministic model?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose extending the action-related regularization to other dynamics models but leave its effectiveness as an open question.
- Why unresolved: The paper only demonstrates the effectiveness of this regularization on a single-deterministic dynamics model and does not explore other model architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of the action-related regularization term applied to different dynamics model architectures (e.g., stochastic models, ensemble models) across multiple RL environments.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can backdoors be planted in a way that avoids detection by the dynamics model?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge this as an important security consideration and suggest it as a worthy area of research.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on defending against backdoor attacks but does not explore potential evasion techniques that attackers might use.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and demonstration of backdoor insertion methods that maintain environmental consistency, potentially through more sophisticated trigger designs or manipulation of the dynamics model training process.

## Limitations
- Effectiveness unverified on environments beyond Mujoco Hopper
- Detection mechanism may fail against sophisticated triggers minimizing state transition changes
- Approach limited to continuous control scenarios; applicability to discrete action spaces unclear

## Confidence
- High confidence in the theoretical framework and mechanism design
- Medium confidence in the experimental results due to limited evaluation scenarios
- Low confidence in the method's scalability to more complex environments and attack types

## Next Checks
1. **Transferability Test**: Evaluate RTS performance on different continuous control tasks (e.g., Walker, HalfCheetah) to assess generalizability beyond Hopper.

3. **Dynamic Model Ablation**: Systematically compare single-objective vs. dual-objective dynamics models across varying amounts of training data to quantify the impact of the action regularization term.
