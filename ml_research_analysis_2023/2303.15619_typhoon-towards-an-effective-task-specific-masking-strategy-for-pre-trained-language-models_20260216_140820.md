# Typhoon: Towards an Effective Task-Specific Masking Strategy for Pre-trained Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2303.15619
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15619
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Gradient-based Typhoon masking achieves competitive performance with whole-word masking on MRPC, with different strategies performing better on different GLUE tasks

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Typhoon, a task-specific masking strategy for pre-trained language models that uses token input gradients to determine masking probabilities. The method computes gradients of the loss with respect to input embeddings, updates token-specific weights using exponential moving averages, and normalizes these weights based on token frequency distributions. Evaluated on GLUE benchmark tasks (MRPC and CoLA) using TinyBERT, Typhoon shows competitive performance with whole-word masking on MRPC while highlighting that different masking strategies may be more effective for different types of downstream tasks. The authors suggest future work exploring learned masking strategies and evaluating larger BERT-like models.

## Method Summary
The Typhoon algorithm computes token weights from gradients of the loss with respect to input embeddings, updates these weights using exponential moving averages, and converts them to masking probabilities based on token frequency distributions. During pre-training, the model calculates gradients for each token type, maintains frequency counts across the dataset, and uses these to normalize gradient-based weights into proper masking probabilities while maintaining a constant overall masking rate. The method is evaluated by pre-training TinyBERT with Typhoon masking, then fine-tuning on downstream sequence classification tasks using GLUE benchmark datasets (MRPC and CoLA), comparing performance against random masking and whole-word masking baselines.

## Key Results
- Typhoon achieves competitive performance with whole-word masking on MRPC dataset (accuracy and F1-score)
- For CoLA dataset, whole-word masking significantly outperforms both Typhoon and random masking approaches
- Results suggest different masking strategies may be more effective for different types of downstream tasks, with Typhoon showing particular promise for certain linguistic tasks

  - Using TinyBERT for efficiency vs. larger BERT models for potentially better performance
  - Simple gradient-based weighting vs. more complex learned masking strategies
  - Frequency-based normalization vs. alternative probability estimation methods
  - Single-task focus vs. multi-task or universal masking strategies

- Failure signatures:
  - Unstable masking probabilities causing training divergence
  - Gradient computations not providing meaningful signal for masking decisions
  - Frequency normalization failing due to dataset biases or token distribution changes
  - Downstream task performance not improving despite task-specific pre-training

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the effectiveness of Typhoon masking vary significantly across different model architectures beyond TinyBERT, such as larger BERT variants or other transformer models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state "One question to ask is whether model size and architecture will significantly influence what types of masking strategies will prove most effective for certain down-stream tasks" and intend to evaluate larger BERT-like models in the future.
- Why unresolved: The current study only evaluates Typhoon on TinyBERT, a distilled smaller version of BERT. The authors explicitly acknowledge they have not tested larger models yet.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experimental results comparing Typhoon's performance against baselines on multiple BERT variants (BERT-base, BERT-large, RoBERTa, etc.) across various downstream tasks would provide clear evidence.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Would a learned masking strategy that indirectly utilizes gradients through a trainable component outperform Typhoon's current direct gradient-based approach?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors mention "Future work could include learned masking strategies, similar to AutoAugment in computer vision, which would utilize gradients in a more indirect fashion, through a learned masking strategy."
- Why unresolved: The current Typhoon implementation uses a fixed algorithm based on token gradients. The paper suggests but does not implement a learned approach.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comparison between Typhoon's current method and a learned masking strategy (possibly using reinforcement learning or meta-learning) would demonstrate which approach yields better downstream performance.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does Typhoon perform on the full range of GLUE benchmark tasks beyond MRPC and CoLA, particularly those requiring different linguistic capabilities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors tested only two datasets from GLUE (MRPC and CoLA) and noted that different masking strategies may be more effective for different types of downstream tasks, suggesting variability across tasks.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation is limited to only two tasks from the GLUE benchmark, which includes nine tasks testing various linguistic phenomena.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive testing of Typhoon across all GLUE tasks (MNLI, QQP, QNLI, SST-2, CoLA, STS-B, MRPC, RTE, WNLI) would reveal its generalizability and identify which task types benefit most from gradient-based masking.

## Limitations

- Evaluation limited to only two GLUE tasks (MRPC and CoLA) rather than comprehensive benchmark coverage
- Uses TinyBERT rather than full-sized BERT models, potentially limiting generalizability to larger architectures
- Lacks ablation studies to isolate which components of Typhoon drive performance improvements

## Confidence

- Medium confidence in Typhoon's competitive performance on MRPC: Results show Typhoon matching whole-word masking, but the evaluation covers only two datasets and uses a smaller model variant
- Low confidence in mechanism claims: The paper lacks ablation studies demonstrating that gradient-based weighting specifically drives performance improvements, and the relationship between token gradients and downstream utility is not empirically validated
- Medium confidence in task-specificity claims: While the authors suggest different tasks may benefit from different masking strategies, this remains largely theoretical without systematic multi-task evaluation
