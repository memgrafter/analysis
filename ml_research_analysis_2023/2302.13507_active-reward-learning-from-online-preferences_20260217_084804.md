# Active Reward Learning from Online Preferences

## Executive Summary
This work introduces an adaptive online querying framework for reinforcement learning that maintains a posterior distribution over reward functions and uses expected value of information (EVOI) to determine when and what action preference queries to present to humans during policy execution. The method successfully balances task performance with query efficiency, achieving better results than baselines while reducing the number of questions asked. The approach demonstrates strong transfer capabilities from simulation to real robots, validated through experiments with a Fetch robot for block pushing tasks.

## Method Summary
The method maintains a posterior distribution over reward functions and uses EVOI to select informative action preference queries during policy execution. When EVOI for a query exceeds a threshold, the system asks a human expert to choose between action pairs. Responses update the posterior, which guides both action selection and future query decisions. The approach was validated across GridWorld simulations, driving environments, and real robot experiments, showing improved performance with fewer queries compared to baselines.

## Key Results
- Outperforms baseline methods in task performance while reducing query count
- Successfully transfers from simulation to real Fetch robot for block pushing tasks
- Users rate the method as making intelligent, adaptive queries compared to baselines

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The approach improves task performance while reducing the number of queries by selecting action pairs with high expected value of information (EVOI).
- **Mechanism:** EVOI quantifies how much querying a specific action pair will improve the expected Q-value of the current state, marginalizing over the belief distribution of task parameters. Queries are only made when EVOI exceeds a threshold, ensuring questions are asked only when they provide significant information gain.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal policy under the current belief over tasks is well-approximated by the expected Q-value across tasks, and that myopic EVOI captures long-term benefit.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Our approach designs queries and determines when to present them to maximize the expected value derived from the queries' information."
  - [section] "Using the EVOI metric, we propose an approach for selecting when to query a human expert and what queries to make."
  - [corpus] "Batch Active Learning of Reward Functions from Human Preferences" (similar context but no direct EVOI mention—weak anchor).
- **Break condition:** If the task policies disagree substantially on future actions, the Q-value approximation may be overconfident, leading to poor query selection.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The approach adapts to human preferences in real time by maintaining a posterior over reward functions and updating it based on human responses.
- **Mechanism:** Each human response updates the posterior over the latent task vector ω using a Boltzmann-rational response model. This posterior is used to select actions and compute EVOI, enabling the policy to adapt as more information is gathered.
- **Core assumption:** Human responses follow the Boltzmann-rational model with fixed precision β, and responses are independent given the true task.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "At evaluation, we maintain a posterior over the task vector ω... We update this posterior in response to each successive query made to the human expert."
  - [section] "Assuming this response model for pairwise comparisons between action queries, our goal is to find out when to ask what questions to the user."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence—explicitly weak.
- **Break condition:** If human responses are not Boltzmann-rational (e.g., due to misunderstanding or inconsistency), the posterior update will be incorrect.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The approach transfers from simulation to real robots by training policies in simulation and using waypoint tracking to bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- **Mechanism:** Policies are trained in simulation across a distribution of tasks. During deployment, the robot uses the trained policy and EVOI-based querying, with a simulator tracking block positions to provide necessary state information for continuous action spaces.
- **Core assumption:** The simulation accurately captures the relevant dynamics of the real robot and environment, and the learned policy generalizes to the real system.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "We then ran the approach on a real Fetch robot arm, using policies trained in the simulation... we were also able to transfer to the real robot."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence—explicitly weak.
- **Break condition:** Large sim-to-real domain gaps or unmodeled dynamics can cause the policy to fail even if querying logic is correct.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  - Task distribution Ω -> Q-function library -> Posterior updater -> EVOI calculator -> Query selector -> Human interface -> Policy executor

- **Critical path:**
  1. At each step, compute EVOI for sampled action pairs.
  2. If max EVOI > threshold c, query human and update posterior.
  3. Select action using expected Q-value under current posterior.
  4. Execute action and repeat.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Query frequency vs. performance**: Lower threshold c increases queries but may improve performance.
  - **Sample complexity**: Sampling action pairs in continuous spaces trades off EVOI accuracy for computational feasibility.
  - **Human burden vs. policy quality**: Asking too many questions can frustrate users; too few may lead to poor performance.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Too many queries**: Threshold c set too low; check EVOI distribution.
  - **Poor performance despite many queries**: Q-function approximation poor; check task disagreement and policy quality.
  - **No queries made**: Threshold c too high or EVOI always low; check human response model and belief update.
  - **Catastrophic failure in real robot**: Sim-to-real gap; verify policy transfer and state estimation.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **GridWorld validation**: Run in simple GridWorld with known goal distribution; verify EVOI selects queries near goal boundaries and performance improves with queries.
  2. **Driving simulation with simulated expert**: Tune c and β to balance queries and score; verify Pareto frontier dominates baselines.
  3. **User study pilot**: Test human interface and response latency; ensure queries are understandable and posterior updates behave as expected.

## Limitations
- The method assumes human responses follow a Boltzmann-rational model, which may not hold in practice
- The Q-value approximation for policy selection under belief distributions may be overconfident when task policies disagree substantially
- The sim-to-real transfer mechanism lacks detailed validation and corpus evidence
