# Towards exploring adversarial learning for anomaly detection in complex driving scenes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2307.05256
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05256
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: GANomaly fails on complex driving scenes due to high false negatives and similar features between normal and anomaly classes

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates GANomaly, an adversarial learning-based anomaly detection method, on complex driving scene datasets. While the method successfully reproduced results on MNIST, achieving high accuracy, it failed to effectively detect anomalies in the Berkeley DeepDrive dataset. The primary challenges were high scene complexity, similar features between normal and anomaly classes, and small anomaly object sizes. Despite the ability to reconstruct high-resolution driving scenes, GANomaly's anomaly detection performance was poor, with high false negatives indicating that current GAN-based methods are not directly applicable to highly complex driving scenes.

## Method Summary
GANomaly combines a generator (with encoder and decoder) and an encoder to minimize reconstruction errors in latent space for anomaly detection. The method uses three loss functions: adversarial (feature matching), contextual (pixel-wise), and encoder (latent space distance). During training, the generator learns to reconstruct normal images, and during inference, anomalies are detected based on the distance between latent representations of original and reconstructed images.

## Key Results
- GANomaly successfully reproduced results on MNIST with high accuracy
- Failed to detect anomalies effectively in Berkeley DeepDrive dataset
- High false negatives indicated poor anomaly detection performance
- Could reconstruct high-resolution driving scenes despite detection failures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GANomaly uses latent space distance between original and reconstructed images to detect anomalies.
- Mechanism: The encoder maps the original image to latent space Z, the decoder reconstructs the image from Z, and the encoder maps the reconstructed image to latent space Ẑ. The distance ∥Z - Ẑ∥₂ is used as the anomaly score.
- Core assumption: The generator, trained only on normal images, cannot accurately reconstruct images containing anomaly features, so Z and Ẑ will differ significantly for anomalous images.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "GANomaly combines a generator (with encoder and decoder) and an encoder to minimize reconstruction errors in latent space for anomaly detection."
  - [section 5.1] "As a result, the difference between Z and Ẑ would increase, declaring an anomaly detection in the image."
- Break condition: If the generator learns to reconstruct anomalies well (e.g., due to feature similarity between normal and anomalous classes), the latent space distance will not reliably indicate anomalies.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Feature matching in the discriminator helps stabilize training and improve reconstruction quality.
- Mechanism: The discriminator's intermediate layer outputs for real and reconstructed images are compared using L2 distance, and this difference is backpropagated to the generator to improve reconstruction.
- Core assumption: Minimizing the feature distance between real and reconstructed images leads to more realistic reconstructions that preserve normal features.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] "Using the values of the features in the intermediate layer in the Discriminator to reduce the distance between the features representation of the input image X, and the reconstructed image X̂."
- Break condition: If feature matching does not align with perceptual quality, reconstructions may still fail to preserve distinguishing features.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The contextual loss penalizes reconstruction error to ensure visual fidelity.
- Mechanism: L1 distance between the original image X and the reconstructed image X̂ is added to the generator loss.
- Core assumption: Minimizing pixel-wise reconstruction error forces the generator to produce outputs close to the input, preserving normal image structure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.1] "This Loss is meant to improve the quality of the reconstructed image by penalizing the Generator by calculating the distance between the input image X and the reconstructed image X̂."
- Break condition: If anomalies occupy small regions or are visually similar to normal features, pixel-wise error may be small even for anomalous images.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Generator (encoder-decoder): compresses input to latent space Z, reconstructs image X̂ from Z
  - Encoder E: maps reconstructed image X̂ to latent space Ẑ
  - Discriminator D: classifies real vs fake, provides intermediate features for feature matching loss
  - Loss functions: adversarial (feature matching), contextual (pixel-wise), encoder (latent space distance)

- Critical path:
  1. Forward pass: X → GE(X)=Z → GD(Z)=X̂ → E(X̂)=Ẑ
  2. Compute losses: adversarial (f(X) vs f(X̂)), contextual (X vs X̂), encoder (Z vs Ẑ)
  3. Backpropagate combined loss to update generator parameters
  4. During inference: compute anomaly score = ∥Z - Ẑ∥₂

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Using only normal images for training simplifies setup but limits generalization to unseen anomalies
  - Latent space distance is efficient but may miss anomalies that are small or visually similar to normal features
  - High-resolution reconstruction is computationally expensive and may require architectural changes for complex datasets

- Failure signatures:
  - High false negatives: anomalies are reconstructed well, latent space distance is small
  - Poor reconstruction quality: contextual or adversarial loss dominates, model overfits
  - Training instability: discriminator loss dominates, generator fails to learn meaningful latent space

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce MNIST results: Train GANomaly on MNIST with one digit as anomaly, evaluate AUC and reconstruction quality
  2. Test latent space sensitivity: Add small anomalous patches to normal images, measure change in latent space distance
  3. Evaluate on BDD subset: Train on a simple BDD subset (e.g., daytime, clear weather, single object class), compare reconstruction and detection performance to MNIST
