# Does Saliency-Based Training bring Robustness for Deep Neural Networks in Image Classification?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.16581
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16581
- Authors: 
- Reference count: 5
- Primary result: Saliency-guided training produces more interpretable models but reduces adversarial robustness

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether saliency-based training improves adversarial robustness in deep neural networks. The authors train models using a saliency-guided approach that masks low-gradient input pixels during training, forcing the model to focus on salient features. Despite producing more interpretable saliency maps, these models show significantly lower robustness against white-box adversarial attacks (FGSM, BIM, PGD) on MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets. The authors hypothesize that emphasizing high-gradient regions makes these areas more vulnerable to gradient-based attacks.

## Method Summary
The authors train two models per dataset (MNIST and CIFAR-10): a baseline CNN and a saliency-guided model. The saliency-guided training masks 50% of lowest-gradient pixels with random values per batch and adds KL divergence regularization to enforce similar outputs for original and masked inputs. Both models are evaluated using Foolbox to generate adversarial examples under varying perturbation strengths. The study uses a 2-layer CNN for MNIST and ResNet18 for CIFAR-10, training for 100 epochs with Adadelta optimizer.

## Key Results
- Saliency-guided models show lower accuracy against adversarial attacks compared to baseline models
- Despite improved interpretability through clearer saliency maps, robustness decreases significantly
- The vulnerability appears linked to emphasizing high-gradient regions that become targets for gradient-based attacks
- Results hold across multiple attack types (FGSM, BIM, PGD) and perturbation strengths

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Saliency-guided training makes models focus on high-gradient pixels by masking low-gradient areas during training.
- Mechanism: During each training step, input pixels with low gradient values are masked with random values, and the model is trained to produce similar outputs for both original and masked inputs via KL divergence loss.
- Core assumption: Low-gradient pixels are less important for classification and can be safely masked.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "lower-gradient input pixels are masked with random values during training, encouraging the model to focus on salient features"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Saliency-guided training produces more interpretable saliency maps by highlighting high-gradient regions.
- Mechanism: By training the model to ignore low-gradient pixels, high-gradient regions become more prominent in saliency maps, making decision processes more visually explainable.
- Core assumption: High-gradient regions correspond to the main object or feature used for classification.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "by emphasizing high-gradient regions (typically the main object), saliency-based training inadvertently makes these regions more vulnerable to gradient-based adversarial attacks"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Saliency-guided training creates vulnerability to adversarial attacks by making high-gradient regions more attackable.
- Mechanism: Adversarial attacks work by adding perturbations in gradient direction. By training models to focus on high-gradient regions, these areas become more sensitive to gradient-based perturbations.
- Core assumption: Adversarial attacks exploit gradient information, and saliency-guided training amplifies importance of gradient-sensitive regions.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "by emphasizing high-gradient regions (typically the main object), saliency-based training inadvertently makes these regions more vulnerable to gradient-based adversarial attacks"

- Component map: Input → CNN layers → Fully connected layers → Output. Saliency masking applied during training only.
- Critical path: Data preprocessing → Saliency-guided masking → Forward pass → Loss calculation (cross-entropy + KL divergence) → Backward pass → Weight update
- Design tradeoffs: Interpretability (saliency maps) vs. robustness (adversarial defense). Masking low-gradient pixels may improve interpretability but reduce robustness.
- Failure signatures: Lower accuracy on adversarial examples compared to regular models; saliency maps may not align with human intuition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the precise mechanism by which saliency-guided training makes models more vulnerable to adversarial attacks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors observe that saliency-guided models show lower robustness but do not fully explain why emphasizing high-gradient regions increases vulnerability
- Why unresolved: The paper provides only a hypothesis that "highlighting high-value gradients and their pertinent pixels that are considered as the main object, the main object is the center of attention for adversarial examples" but does not provide rigorous mathematical or empirical evidence
- What evidence would resolve it: Detailed analysis of gradient patterns in both regular and saliency-trained models, including gradient magnitude distributions, gradient correlation matrices, and ablation studies showing which specific components of saliency training contribute most to vulnerability

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can saliency-based training be modified to maintain interpretability benefits while improving adversarial robustness?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note a tradeoff between interpretability and robustness but do not explore potential modifications to the training approach that could preserve both qualities
- Why unresolved: The paper only investigates the current saliency training method and its performance, without exploring alternative formulations, regularization techniques, or hybrid approaches
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments testing modified saliency training methods with different hyperparameter settings, alternative loss functions, or combined with adversarial training techniques, showing whether robustness can be improved without sacrificing interpretability

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the vulnerability to adversarial attacks vary across different types of datasets and model architectures in saliency-guided training?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper only tests saliency training on MNIST and CIFAR-10 with specific CNN and ResNet architectures, leaving open whether results generalize
- Why unresolved: The experimental scope is limited to two datasets and a few architectures, making it unclear if the observed vulnerability is a general phenomenon or specific to these conditions
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensive experiments across diverse datasets (natural images, medical imaging, satellite imagery) and architectures (Transformers, Vision Transformers, different CNN variants) to determine if the robustness-interpretability tradeoff is universal or context-dependent

## Limitations

- Limited scope to white-box attacks only, without testing black-box or transfer-based attacks
- Specific hyperparameter values (especially KL divergence weight λ) not reported, affecting reproducibility
- Results limited to two datasets and specific architectures without exploring generalization across domains

## Confidence

- High confidence: Saliency-guided models produce more interpretable saliency maps than baseline models
- Medium confidence: Saliency-guided training reduces robustness against white-box attacks across multiple perturbation strengths
- Low confidence: The mechanism by which saliency-guided training creates vulnerability (emphasizing high-gradient regions) is definitively proven rather than hypothesized

## Next Checks
