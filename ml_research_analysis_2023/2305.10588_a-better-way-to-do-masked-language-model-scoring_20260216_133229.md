# A Better Way to Do Masked Language Model Scoring

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.10588
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10588
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: Proposes PLL-word-l2r metric that better scores masked language models by masking rightward tokens within words

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a significant issue with the pseudo-log-likelihood (PLL) scoring method for masked language models (MLMs), specifically that it inflates scores for out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words split into multiple subword tokens. To address this, the authors propose a modified metric called PLL-word-l2r, which masks not only the target token but also all subsequent subword tokens within the same word during scoring. This adaptation ensures that the score reflects the difficulty of predicting the entire word given only the leftward context. The authors demonstrate that PLL-word-l2r better satisfies theoretical desiderata, including an inverse relationship between sentence length and negative PLL, and a positive correlation between word frequency and PLL scores. It also shows higher correlation with autoregressive model scores. Additionally, the choice of metric affects results on the BLiMP benchmark, with PLL-word-l2r generally outperforming the original PLL metric.

## Method Summary
The authors propose PLL-word-l2r as an improvement to the original PLL scoring method for masked language models. The key modification is that when scoring a token, not only is the target token masked, but all subsequent subword tokens within the same word are also masked. This prevents the model from using rightward context within the word, creating a more realistic scoring scenario similar to left-to-right autoregressive prediction. The method is implemented using the minicons library and tested on BERT and RoBERTa models across multiple datasets including EventsAdapt, Brown corpus, and BLiMP benchmark. The paper compares PLL-word-l2r against the original PLL metric and a simpler whole-word masking approach, evaluating based on theoretical desiderata and correlation with autoregressive model scores.

## Key Results
- PLL-word-l2r eliminates the inflation of scores for out-of-vocabulary words split into multiple subword tokens
- The new metric better satisfies theoretical desiderata: inverse relationship between sentence length and negative PLL, and positive correlation between word frequency and PLL scores
- PLL-word-l2r shows higher correlation with autoregressive model scores compared to the original PLL metric
- Results on the BLiMP benchmark indicate that metric choice affects performance, with PLL-word-l2r generally outperforming the original PLL

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The PLL-original metric inflates scores for out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words because it computes each subword token's score using the full bidirectional context, including other tokens from the same OOV word.
- Mechanism: When a word is split into multiple subword tokens (e.g., "souvenir" → "so" + "##uven" + "##ir"), the original PLL method allows the model to use the other subword tokens of the same word as context when scoring each individual token. This means the model can trivially predict "so" given the context "##uven ##ir", leading to inflated scores.
- Core assumption: The inflated scores occur specifically because the bidirectional context includes within-word tokens that are not actually available in a left-to-right prediction scenario.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "the original PLL method yields inflated scores for out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words and propose an adapted metric, in which we mask not only the target token, but also all within-word tokens to the right of the target"
  - [section] "The PLL-original metric grossly overestimates the probability of OOV lexical items, such as souvenir... Thus, even though the OOV word itself may be surprising given the sentence context, the individual parts of the OOV word are not surprising to a bidirectional model given a sentence context that includes all other subtokens of that word"

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The PLL-word-l2r metric fixes the inflation issue by masking not only the target token but also all subsequent subword tokens within the same word during scoring.
- Mechanism: When scoring a subword token, PLL-word-l2r masks all tokens to the right of the target within the same word. This ensures the model can only use leftward context (tokens before the target in the word) and tokens from other words, making the scoring more similar to left-to-right autoregressive prediction.
- Core assumption: Masking rightward tokens within the same word during inference creates a more realistic scoring scenario that better approximates the true difficulty of predicting that token.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "we propose an adapted metric, in which we mask not only the target token, but also all within-word tokens to the right of the target"
  - [section] "we adjust the PLL sentence scoring algorithm such that the model cannot access future within-word tokens (PLL-word-l2r)"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The PLL-word-l2r metric better satisfies theoretical desiderata, including an inverse relationship between sentence length and negative PLL, and a positive correlation between word frequency and PLL scores.
- Mechanism: By reducing the artificial inflation for multi-token words, PLL-word-l2r creates a more accurate distribution of sentence and word scores that aligns with expected statistical properties of language models.
- Core assumption: The theoretical properties (inverse relationship with sentence length, positive correlation with word frequency) are valid desiderata that any good scoring metric should satisfy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "it better satisfies theoretical desiderata and better correlates with scores from autoregressive models"
  - [section] "it better satisfies theoretical desiderata: (A) an inverse relationship between negative sentence PLL...and sentence length and (B) a positive correlation between word PLL and word log frequency"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Masked language model (BERT/RoBERTa) -> Tokenizer -> PLL scoring algorithm -> Evaluation datasets
- Critical path: For each token in a sentence: 1) Mask the target token and rightward tokens within the same word, 2) Get model predictions using remaining context, 3) Calculate log probability, 4) Sum across all tokens for sentence score
- Design tradeoffs: PLL-word-l2r provides more accurate scoring but requires more computation than PLL-original; PLL-whole-word is simpler but may overly penalize OOV words
- Failure signatures: If PLL-word-l2r still shows inflation for OOV words, or if it fails to restore the expected correlations with sentence length and word frequency, the implementation may have issues
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify that PLL-word-l2r correctly masks rightward tokens within words by checking the masked input for a multi-token word like "souvenir"
  2. Test the sentence length correlation by computing negative PLL scores for sentences of varying lengths and checking for positive correlation
  3. Evaluate word frequency correlation by computing PLL scores for words with known frequencies and checking for positive correlation

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Question: How can we develop a metric that normalizes PLL scores for words with varying numbers of subword tokens, accounting for increased uncertainty in agglutinative languages?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that their approach pre-specifies the number of tokens in a word, which may limit its utility for agglutinative languages and suggest future approaches might investigate normalizing PLL scores over words with varying token counts.
  - Why unresolved: Current PLL metrics don't account for the inherent uncertainty introduced by splitting words into multiple tokens, particularly problematic for agglutinative languages where this occurs frequently.
  - What evidence would resolve it: A study comparing PLL metrics across multiple languages (isolating, agglutinative, fusional) with varying subword tokenization patterns, showing improved cross-linguistic consistency with a normalized metric.

- Question: What would be the impact of estimating the joint probability of all tokens in a word versus predicting them sequentially (as in PLL-word-l2r)?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors suggest it would be interesting to estimate the joint probability of all tokens in a word instead of predicting them left-to-right or without within-word context.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only explores sequential left-to-right masking within words, but doesn't test joint probability estimation which could better capture word-level dependencies.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis of PLL metrics (sequential vs. joint probability) on word prediction accuracy and sentence scoring, demonstrating whether joint probability estimation provides theoretical and practical advantages.

- Question: How would PLL metrics perform on languages with different writing systems (e.g., right-to-left scripts like Arabic or character-based systems like Chinese)?
  - Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge their left-to-right masking approach follows English writing tradition and note this is primarily a denotational difference, but don't test non-left-to-right languages.
  - Why unresolved: The paper only tests on English text corpora and doesn't investigate whether the left-to-right masking assumption holds for languages with different writing directions or character-based systems.
  - What evidence would resolve it: Empirical comparison of PLL metrics (left-to-right vs. right-to-left masking) on multiple languages with different writing systems, showing whether masking direction affects scores and if right-to-left notation would be more intuitive for certain languages.

## Limitations

- The effectiveness of PLL-word-l2r depends on the specific tokenization scheme used, with potential variations in how the inflation problem manifests across different tokenizers
- The evaluation is primarily focused on English language data, leaving the metric's performance on morphologically rich languages and different word formation patterns unexplored
- The paper does not provide quantitative analysis of the computational overhead introduced by PLL-word-l2r compared to the original metric

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The identification of the inflation issue in PLL-original for multi-token words, and the theoretical justification for why PLL-word-l2r should address this issue. The correlation analysis with sentence length and word frequency is also well-established.
- **Medium Confidence**: The empirical demonstration that PLL-word-l2r better correlates with autoregressive model scores and improves BLiMP benchmark results. While the results are convincing, the evaluation could be strengthened with additional datasets and languages.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that PLL-word-l2r is the "better" metric depends on the specific use case. For certain applications like sentence completion or text generation, PLL-whole-word might be preferable despite not satisfying all theoretical desiderata.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-Tokenizer Validation**: Implement and test PLL-word-l2r across multiple tokenization schemes (WordPiece, BPE, SentencePiece) and different vocabulary sizes to quantify how the inflation problem varies with tokenization approach.

2. **Computational Efficiency Analysis**: Conduct a systematic comparison of computational requirements (time, memory) between PLL-original, PLL-word-l2r, and PLL-whole-word across different model sizes and datasets to provide practical guidance on when each metric is appropriate.
