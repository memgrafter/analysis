# Online Learning with Feedback Graphs: The True Shape of Regret

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.02971
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02971
- Authors: N/A
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces problem complexity R* that provides tighter regret bounds than independence number α for small time horizons T < α³ in online learning with feedback graphs

## Executive Summary
This paper resolves a fundamental question in online learning with feedback graphs by characterizing the true regret scaling for all time horizons. While previous work showed minimax regret scales as √(αT) for T ≥ α³, the behavior for smaller T remained open. The authors introduce a refined problem complexity measure R* that captures the difficulty of the problem more accurately than the independence number α. They prove that regret scales with R* for any time horizon T, and provide the EXP3-EX algorithm that achieves this bound up to logarithmic factors.

## Method Summary
The EXP3-EX algorithm modifies the standard EXP3 algorithm by introducing a graph-aware exploration distribution. It partitions actions into groups based on estimated losses and graph connectivity, then uses dominating sets within these partitions to guide exploration. The algorithm maintains weight vectors for loss estimation, constructs partitions {It,k,l} based on exponential weights and neighbor counts, computes dominating sets Dt,k,l within each partition, and constructs the exploration distribution ut that balances loss and informativeness. The key innovation is that this exploration strategy allows the algorithm to exploit hub-like structures in the graph to achieve regret scaling of approximately T^(2/3) for T < α³, rather than the √αT scaling of previous approaches.

## Key Results
- Introduces problem complexity R* that characterizes regret scaling for any time horizon T
- Proves lower bound of R* for any graph and time horizon
- Provides EXP3-EX algorithm matching the lower bound up to logarithmic terms
- Shows R* can be significantly smaller than √αT for graphs with hub structures when T < α³
- First provably optimal algorithm for the online learning with feedback graphs problem

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Regret scales with a refined graph-dependent quantity R* rather than independence number α for small T
- Mechanism: The algorithm partitions actions into groups based on their estimated loss and informativeness, then uses dominating sets within partitions to explore efficiently
- Core assumption: Graph feedback structure can be leveraged to reduce exploration cost below α for certain graphs when T < α³
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "the minimax regret is proportional to R* for any graph and time horizon T"
  - [section]: "the problem complexity R* (Definition 2.7). This definition is much more tractable from a graph perspective"
- Break condition: When T becomes large enough that α³ ≤ T, the advantage disappears and √αT becomes optimal

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The problem complexity R* can be much smaller than √αT for graphs with hub-like structures
- Mechanism: When T < α³, the algorithm can exploit highly connected vertices to efficiently explore many actions simultaneously, reducing regret to approximately T^(2/3)
- Core assumption: Highly connected vertices provide information about many other vertices, allowing more efficient exploration
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Lemma 2.5 provides an example where the graph contains N − 1 independent vertices and one hub vertex connected to all other vertices"
  - [section]: "Corollary 5.2 states that the minimax rate for this graph indeed scales with δ^(1/3)T^(2/3) instead of √αT"
- Break condition: When T grows beyond α³, the benefit of exploiting hubs diminishes and the problem complexity approaches √αT

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The exploration distribution ut in Algorithm 1 adapts to both loss estimates and graph connectivity
- Mechanism: The algorithm constructs partitions based on exponential weights and neighbor counts, then computes dominating sets within partitions to guide exploration
- Core assumption: Actions with similar loss estimates and similar connectivity patterns can be explored together efficiently
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "Definition 4.3. let It,k,l, for (t, k, l) ∈ [T] × [K] × [L] be a partition of V from Definition 4.1"
  - [section]: "we can define, ut,i ≜ 1/KL + 1 [1/N + Σk∈[K] Σl∈[L] uk,l,t,i]"
- Break condition: When the graph becomes dense or the partitions become too fine, the computational overhead may outweigh benefits

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the exact relationship between the problem complexity R* and the independence number α for intermediate values of T (between α and α^3)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that for T ≥ α^3, R* simplifies to √αT, but the behavior for intermediate values is unresolved
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides bounds for the extremes (T < α^3 and T ≥ α^3), leaving a gap in understanding the transition
- What evidence would resolve it: A precise characterization of R* as a function of T/α^3 that bridges the gap between the two known regimes

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the algorithm be modified to achieve the optimal regret bound for all graph structures without relying on computationally expensive dominating set computations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that computing dominating sets can be computationally very expensive, so it uses approximations
- Why unresolved: The current algorithm relies on approximate dominating sets, which may not be optimal for all graphs
- What evidence would resolve it: An algorithm that achieves the same regret bound using only efficient graph operations

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the problem complexity R* behave for graphs with multiple hubs of varying sizes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses graphs with one hub in Example 1, but does not provide a complete characterization for multiple hubs
- Why unresolved: The examples only cover simple cases with one hub or star graphs, not more complex hub structures
- What evidence would resolve it: A general formula for R* that accounts for the distribution and sizes of multiple hubs in a graph

## Limitations
- Computational complexity of dominating set computations may be prohibitive for large graphs
- Logarithmic factors in the upper bound may dominate the improvement for moderate-sized problems
- Theoretical analysis focuses on worst-case graphs, practical performance on real-world graphs remains unverified

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The lower bound scaling with R* for any T is well-established through information-theoretic arguments. The connection between problem complexity and graph structure for small T is also rigorously proven.
- **Medium Confidence**: The EXP3-EX algorithm achieves the claimed upper bound, but the polynomial-time computation of dominating sets is only sketched in Appendix D without full details.
- **Low Confidence**: The practical performance improvements over standard algorithms for real-world graphs, as the paper focuses on theoretical guarantees rather than empirical validation.
