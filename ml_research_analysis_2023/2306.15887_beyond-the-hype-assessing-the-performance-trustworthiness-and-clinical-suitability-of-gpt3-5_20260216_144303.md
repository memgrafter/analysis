# Beyond the Hype: Assessing the Performance, Trustworthiness, and Clinical Suitability of GPT3.5

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.15887
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15887
- Reference count: 24
- Key outcome: GPT3.5 achieved 0.70 F1 score for medical image protocol assignment, significantly lower than BERT (0.89), BioBERT (0.91), and RoBERTa (0.89), but demonstrated better interpretability and alignment with radiologist word importance

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates GPT3.5's performance for medical image protocol assignment compared to fine-tuned BERT models and a radiologist. Using 4,730 physician entries across 11 imaging protocol classes, GPT3.5 achieved a weighted average F1 score of 0.70, significantly lower than BERT (0.89), BioBERT (0.91), and RoBERTa (0.89). However, GPT3.5 demonstrated better alignment with radiologist-selected word importance, superior model calibration, and the ability to provide detailed explanations for its decisions. Error analysis revealed systematic issues with protocol understanding, anatomy, and medical terminology.

## Method Summary
The study compared GPT3.5's zero-shot performance against fine-tuned BERT, BioBERT, and RoBERTa models on medical image protocol assignment. Researchers used 4,730 deidentified order entries with physician notes, age, gender, and assigned protocols. GPT3.5 was evaluated through the OpenAI API with a custom prompt, while BERT variants were fine-tuned using HuggingFace Transformers. Evaluation metrics included weighted average F1 score, word importance comparison with radiologist selections, and model calibration analysis. Explanations from GPT3.5 were analyzed to identify systematic error patterns.

## Key Results
- GPT3.5 achieved 0.70 weighted average F1 score versus 0.89-0.91 for fine-tuned BERT variants
- GPT3.5's word importance rankings aligned better with radiologist selections than BERT's high-frequency word bias
- GPT3.5 demonstrated superior model calibration out-of-the-box compared to BERT requiring temperature scaling
- Systematic errors included incomplete protocol understanding, anatomy confusion, and medical terminology issues

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: GPT3.5 underperforms fine-tuned models in F1 score but aligns better with radiologist word importance rankings.
- Mechanism: While GPT3.5's weighted average F1 score of 0.70 is lower than BERT (0.89), BioBERT (0.91), and RoBERTa (0.89), it selects top words that more closely match radiologist-selected indicators for specific protocols. For example, for "MR brain mass/mets/infection," GPT3.5 prioritized "headache," "dizziness," and "numbness," aligning with radiologist choices, while BERT favored high-frequency but non-specific words like "Rule" and "date."
- Core assumption: Word importance alignment correlates with clinical relevance even when F1 scores differ.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "However, GPT3.5 outperforms BERT in its ability to explain its decision, detect relevant word indicators, and model calibration."
  - [section] "Overall, the top five words selected by GPT3.5 aligned more closely with the radiologist's choices than BERT's, which showed a bias towards non-specific words."
- Break condition: If GPT3.5's word importance alignment with radiologists does not correlate with improved clinical decision quality or safety.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: GPT3.5 provides better interpretability and detailed explanations compared to BERT, aiding trust and safety in clinical settings.
- Mechanism: GPT3.5 can generate detailed explanations for its predictions by prompting, allowing for systematic error analysis. This interpretability helps identify specific failure modes like incomplete understanding of protocols, anatomy, and medical terminology, which are critical for improving safety.
- Core assumption: Detailed explanations enable more effective error analysis and systematic improvement.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "However, GPT3.5 demonstrates better alignment with radiologist-selected word importance, superior model calibration, and the ability to provide detailed explanations for its decisions."
  - [section] "By analyzing the explanations of GPT3.5 for misclassifications, we reveal systematic errors that need to be resolved to enhance its safety and suitability for clinical use."
- Break condition: If GPT3.5's explanations are misleading or lead to overconfidence in incorrect predictions.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: GPT3.5 shows better model calibration out-of-the-box compared to BERT, which requires temperature scaling.
- Mechanism: GPT3.5's log probabilities translate to confidence values that closely match actual accuracy, while BERT's initial calibration is poor and needs adjustment through temperature scaling to align with true accuracy.
- Core assumption: Better calibration directly impacts trustworthiness in high-stakes medical decisions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Furthermore, by analyzing GPT3.5's explanations for misclassifications, we reveal systematic errors that need to be resolved to enhance its safety and suitability for clinical use."
  - [section] "In comparing the performance of GPT-3.5 and BERT, on this task, it was found that GPT-3.5 was surprisingly well calibrated out of the box, while BERT was not."
- Break condition: If calibration does not translate to improved real-world performance or if GPT3.5's calibration is context-specific.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Physician notes -> GPT3.5/BERT/BioBERT/RoBERTa -> Protocol assignment with explanation
- Critical path:
  1. Process physician notes through each model
  2. Compare predicted protocols against ground truth
  3. Extract and compare word importance rankings
  4. Analyze explanations for misclassifications
  5. Calculate model calibration using log probabilities
- Design tradeoffs:
  - GPT3.5: No fine-tuning required but lower F1 score; provides explanations and better word alignment
  - BERT/BioBERT/RoBERTa: Higher F1 scores with fine-tuning; lacks detailed explanations and may favor high-frequency words
  - Radiologist: Ground truth but subject to inter-operator variability
- Failure signatures:
  - GPT3.5: Systematic errors in protocol understanding, anatomy, medical terminology, and acronyms
  - BERT: Over-reliance on high-frequency words, poor calibration without temperature scaling
  - Both: Age-related errors, ambiguous prompt handling, and arbitrary information generation
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate the study with a different institution's dataset to test generalizability
  2. Fine-tune GPT3.5 on protocol-specific information to improve understanding
  3. Implement temperature scaling on GPT3.5 to see if calibration improves further

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How would GPT3.5's performance change if it were fine-tuned on the same dataset used to train BERT, BioBERT, and RoBERTa?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that GPT3.5 underperformed compared to fine-tuned models, suggesting this as a potential area for improvement.
- Why unresolved: The paper only evaluated GPT3.5's zero-shot performance and did not conduct any fine-tuning experiments.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a study where GPT3.5 is fine-tuned on the same dataset and comparing its performance to the other fine-tuned models.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How would GPT3.5's performance change if it were trained on a more diverse dataset representing different institutions, patient demographics, and geographic regions?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions that the dataset was limited to a single institution and may not represent the diversity of other regions.
- Why unresolved: The paper only used a dataset from a single institution, limiting the generalizability of the findings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating GPT3.5 on a diverse dataset from multiple institutions and comparing its performance to the single-institution dataset.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can GPT3.5's systematic errors related to incomplete understanding of protocols, anatomy, and medical terminology be addressed through targeted training or additional resources?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies these categories of errors and suggests that they could be mitigated with more fine-tuning or additional resources.
- Why unresolved: The paper only identified the error categories but did not propose specific solutions or conduct experiments to address them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a study where GPT3.5 is fine-tuned on a dataset with more detailed protocol information, anatomical relationships, and medical terminology, and evaluating its performance on the task.

## Limitations

- Single institution dataset limits generalizability to other clinical settings
- Synthetic dataset reproduction plan faces critical unknowns about exact physician order wording and fine-tuning hyperparameters
- Assumes word importance alignment correlates with clinical decision quality without empirical validation

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: GPT3.5's significantly lower F1 scores compared to fine-tuned models, and its superior model calibration out-of-the-box
- **Medium confidence**: The interpretability advantage of GPT3.5 through detailed explanations and word importance alignment with radiologists
- **Low confidence**: The clinical utility of GPT3.5's word importance alignment as a compensatory mechanism for lower F1 scores

## Next Checks

1. **Dataset Generalization Test**: Replicate the study using a multi-institutional dataset with varied imaging protocols and documentation styles to assess performance stability across different clinical environments.

2. **Fine-tuning Impact Assessment**: Fine-tune GPT3.5 on protocol-specific training data and compare its performance to the original fine-tuned BERT/BioBERT/RoBERTa models, measuring both F1 scores and interpretability metrics.

3. **Clinical Outcome Correlation**: Conduct a blinded radiologist review of GPT3.5's protocol assignments to determine whether its word importance alignment and explanations translate to clinically appropriate recommendations in real-world scenarios.
