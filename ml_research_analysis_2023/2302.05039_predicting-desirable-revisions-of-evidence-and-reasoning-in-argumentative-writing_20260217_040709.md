# Predicting Desirable Revisions of Evidence and Reasoning in Argumentative Writing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.05039
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05039
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Context—especially longer context—significantly improves classification performance of desirable revisions in student argumentative writing.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the task of automatically classifying desirable and undesirable revisions in student argumentative writing. It introduces a new approach for extracting longer context windows around revisions and applies pre-trained BERT models combined with BiLSTM layers to predict revision desirability. The research explores the predictive value of revision context and feedback received by students, finding that context—especially longer context—significantly improves classification performance. When feedback is combined with longer context, the models achieve the best results, particularly for elementary-level reasoning revisions. Extrinsic evaluation confirms that predicted desirable revisions correlate positively with essay improvement scores, validating the approach.

## Method Summary
The method involves training BERT-BiLSTM models on student argumentative writing corpora with revisions labeled as desirable or undesirable. The approach extracts longer context spans around revisions by capturing sentences before and after the target revision until encountering an unchanged sentence. Feedback messages are encoded as embeddings and concatenated with revision and context embeddings. Data augmentation is performed using synonym replacement to expand training coverage. The model architecture consists of BERT embeddings passed through a BiLSTM layer, followed by global max pooling, dense layers, and sigmoid output for binary classification.

## Key Results
- Models utilizing context—either alone or with feedback—are the most successful in identifying desirable revisions.
- Longer context improves performance over simple context extraction, though the difference varies by dataset and revision type.
- Feedback combined with longer context achieves the best results, particularly for elementary-level reasoning revisions.
- Extrinsic evaluation shows predicted desirable revisions correlate positively with essay improvement scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Context windows that capture inter-revision dependencies improve classification of desirable revisions.
- Mechanism: By extracting longer context spans (sentences before and after the target revision until encountering an unchanged sentence), the model encodes not only the immediate sentence pair but also the broader argumentative flow. This reduces ambiguity between revisions that look similar in isolation but differ in intent when seen in context.
- Core assumption: Revision desirability is not determined by the sentence pair alone but by its role within the surrounding argumentative structure.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "models utilizing context - either alone or with feedback - are the most successful in identifying desirable revisions."
  - [section] "Inspired by this work, we propose a new approach to extract longer context information."
  - [corpus] Weak—longer context benefit varies by dataset (elementary vs high-school), suggesting argumentative writing patterns differ by student level.
- Break condition: If the context window is too long, it may exceed BERT's token limit and force truncation, potentially removing discriminative information.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Feedback messages improve classification when combined with context.
- Mechanism: Feedback is encoded as a single embedding and concatenated with the revision and context embeddings. This aligns the model's prediction with the pedagogical intent behind the revision, especially when feedback addresses essay-level argumentative goals rather than sentence-level edits.
- Core assumption: Students revise in response to feedback, so the content and tone of feedback carry predictive signal for whether a revision aligns with desired argumentative improvements.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "When feedback is combined with longer context, the models achieve the best results, particularly for elementary-level reasoning revisions."
  - [section] "We leverage both pre-deﬁned AWE feedback messages and free form peer feedback in identifying desirable revisions."
  - [corpus] Moderate—feedback improved performance more for elementary data than high-school, likely because elementary students received structured, sentence-level guidance.
- Break condition: If feedback is generic or not tied to specific argumentative goals, it may add noise rather than signal.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Data augmentation via synonym replacement expands training coverage without distorting revision categories.
- Mechanism: For each sentence, one word (not a stop word, length >5) is replaced with a synonym, generating a new training instance. This increases dataset size while preserving the semantic intent of the revision, allowing complex models like BERT-BiLSTM to train effectively.
- Core assumption: Synonym replacement preserves the rhetorical and argumentative category of a revision (evidence vs reasoning, desirable vs undesirable).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "We use a customized version of the synonym replacement (SR) data augmentation strategy...We ignored stop words, selected words that are more than length of 5 characters, and used maximum 5 synonyms per word to limit the number of data generated."
  - [corpus] Strong—Table 5 shows the number of revisions roughly 20x larger after augmentation, enabling the use of deep learning models.
- Break condition: If synonyms alter the nuance or register of the text, the revision category may shift, causing label noise.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: BERT embeddings capture bidirectional context and subword information, making them suitable for sentence-pair classification.
  - Why needed here: The revision task requires understanding both the original and revised sentence in context, and BERT's pre-training on large corpora provides rich semantic representations.
  - Quick check question: What is the maximum sequence length BERT can process without truncation, and how does that limit context window size?

- Concept: BiLSTM layers model sequential dependencies in the concatenated embeddings, allowing the network to capture long-range patterns beyond BERT's attention span.
  - Why needed here: Revisions often depend on flow across multiple sentences; BiLSTM can model dependencies in both directions.
  - Quick check question: How does adding a BiLSTM layer after BERT differ from using BERT alone for classification?

- Concept: Data augmentation through synonym replacement increases training examples while preserving category semantics.
  - Why needed here: The original corpus is small; augmentation allows training deep models without overfitting.
  - Quick check question: What constraints are applied to ensure synonym replacement does not alter the revision category?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: revision sentence pair → BERT encoder → embedding → concatenate with context1, context2, feedback embeddings → BiLSTM layer → GlobalMaxPool1D → Dense (64 units, ReLU) → Dropout (0.2) → Output Dense (1 unit, Sigmoid)
- Critical path: revision → BERT → BiLSTM → Dense → prediction
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Longer context improves performance but risks truncation; simple context is safer but less informative.
  - Adding feedback improves performance but only when combined with context; standalone feedback is less effective.
  - Synonym replacement expands data but risks semantic drift if not constrained.
- Failure signatures:
  - If F1 drops after adding context, check for excessive truncation or misaligned context windows.
  - If feedback adds no benefit, verify that feedback is meaningfully tied to argumentative goals and not generic.
  - If model overfits despite augmentation, reduce model complexity or apply stronger regularization.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train baseline BERT-BiLSTM on revision pairs only; evaluate F1 on elementary reasoning.
  2. Add longer context to baseline; compare F1 and check truncation rate.
  3. Add feedback to context model; measure improvement and inspect correlation with essay improvement scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the utility of feedback differ between sentence-level and essay-level feedback for predicting revision desirability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that elementary school students did not receive feedback at sentence-level; instead, the feedback is usually about specific areas of the essay or about the argumentative structure of the essay. They suggest that when combined with context, it helps the model to capture a better picture.
- Why unresolved: The study did not directly compare sentence-level and essay-level feedback, and the feedback provided to students was largely framed at the essay-level.
- What evidence would resolve it: Future work could experimentally compare the performance of models using sentence-level feedback versus essay-level feedback, and investigate the optimal level of feedback granularity for predicting revision desirability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the proposed method of extracting longer context compare to other methods of context extraction in terms of predicting revision desirability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose a new method for extracting longer context information and compare it to simple context extraction. They find that longer context generally improves performance over simple context, but the difference is not always significant.
- Why unresolved: The study only compared two methods of context extraction and did not explore other potential methods. It is possible that other methods of context extraction could be more effective.
- What evidence would resolve it: Future work could compare the proposed method of extracting longer context to other methods of context extraction, such as using a sliding window or a dependency-based approach.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance of the proposed models generalize to other domains of writing beyond argumentative writing?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The study only evaluated the proposed models on argumentative writing corpora. It is unclear whether the models would perform well on other domains of writing, such as creative writing or technical writing.
- Why unresolved: The study did not evaluate the proposed models on other domains of writing. It is possible that the models may not generalize well to other domains due to differences in writing style and purpose.
- What evidence would resolve it: Future work could evaluate the proposed models on other domains of writing and compare their performance to models specifically designed for those domains.

## Limitations

- Context extraction ambiguity: The definition of "longer context" may not be consistently applicable across different writing styles or revision patterns, potentially limiting generalizability.
- Feedback encoding variability: Encoding feedback as a single embedding may not capture the full complexity of free-form peer feedback containing multiple suggestions.
- Cross-dataset consistency: The effectiveness of context and feedback varies significantly between elementary and high-school datasets, suggesting the approach may not generalize uniformly.

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The core finding that context improves classification performance is well-supported by both intrinsic (F1 scores) and extrinsic (correlation with essay improvement) evaluations.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that feedback combined with context provides optimal performance is supported, but the mechanism is less clear—it may work differently for elementary versus high-school students.
- **Low Confidence**: The data augmentation approach's impact is difficult to assess independently since it was applied universally across experiments, making it impossible to isolate its contribution.

## Next Checks

1. **Ablation study**: Train models without data augmentation to isolate the contribution of synonym replacement to performance gains.
2. **Context window sensitivity**: Systematically vary context window sizes to identify the optimal balance between information gain and truncation risk.
3. **Feedback specificity analysis**: Categorize feedback messages by type (sentence-level vs. essay-level) and measure their differential impact on classification accuracy.
