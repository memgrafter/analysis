# DeepFEL: Deep Fastfood Ensemble Learning for Histopathology Image Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2301.09525
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09525
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: DeepFEL achieves comparable or better accuracy than end-to-end CNNs while being significantly faster, especially with limited training data (e.g., 77.80% accuracy in 0.5 minutes vs 70-75% in 1.5-1.7 minutes for 50 samples per class)

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces DeepFEL, a method that combines deep features from pre-trained CNNs with Fastfood random projections to efficiently solve computational pathology tasks. The approach addresses challenges in histopathology image analysis such as multi-gigapixel images, limited annotated data, and computational costs. DeepFEL pools deep features from popular CNN models trained on ImageNet, projects them onto diverse dimensions using Fastfood approximation, and trains simple classifiers on each projection. An ensemble output is formed by consensus of individual classifiers. Experiments on three histopathology datasets show that DeepFEL achieves comparable or better accuracy than end-to-end CNNs while being significantly faster, especially when training data is limited.

## Method Summary
DeepFEL extracts deep features from six pre-trained CNN models (VGG16, VGG19, Xception, ResNet50, MobileNet, DenseNet) using global average pooling layers, concatenates these features, and projects them onto lower-dimensional spaces using Fastfood random projections. Simple Decision Tree classifiers are trained on each projection, and ensemble outputs are generated via consensus (weighted average). The method is evaluated on three histopathology datasets for tasks including colorectal cancer grading, tumor metastasis detection, and tissue type classification, demonstrating comparable or better accuracy than individual CNNs with significantly reduced training time.

## Key Results
- On CRC-TIA with 50 samples per class, DeepFEL achieved 77.80% accuracy in 0.5 minutes versus 70-75% accuracy by individual CNNs in 1.5-1.7 minutes
- DeepFEL maintains competitive accuracy with full training data while offering 3-34x faster training times
- The method effectively handles multi-gigapixel histopathology images through patch-based feature extraction and random projections

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Random projections (via Fastfood approximation) create diverse feature subspaces that enable ensemble diversity.
- Mechanism: Fastfood replaces dense Gaussian matrices with Hadamard and diagonal scaling matrices, enabling O(n log n) projection instead of O(n²). The paper generates N orthogonal random matrices, each projecting concatenated deep features (M dimensions) into D-dimensional subspaces. Simple classifiers trained on these subspaces form the ensemble.
- Core assumption: The projected subspaces capture complementary discriminative information despite being low-dimensional.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "projected onto diverse dimensions using random projections, the so-called Fastfood"
  - [section 2] "N matrices of M×D are generated by Fastfood approximation so that the random matrices are mutually orthogonal"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers focus on histopathology but don't directly validate Fastfood's effectiveness
- Break condition: If the orthogonal constraint is violated or D is too small relative to M, information loss could eliminate ensemble benefits.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Pre-trained CNNs provide transferable features that work well even on histopathology images.
- Mechanism: Six different CNN architectures pre-trained on ImageNet extract deep features from histopathology patches. These features are pooled from global average pooling layers and concatenated, creating a rich representation despite domain shift.
- Core assumption: Features learned from natural images transfer to histopathology despite visual differences.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2] "it uses features obtained from popular deep CNN models pre-trained on completely different problems (source domain) and combining them to solve problems in computational pathology (target domain)"
  - [section 3.2] "Six popular CNN models VGG16[18], VGG19[18], Xception[6], ResNet50[7], MobileNet[8], and DenseNet[9] with pre-trained ImageNet weights are used"
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence - related papers show vision-language models emerging in histopathology
- Break condition: If the domain gap is too large, transferred features may not contain relevant discriminative information.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Simple classifiers on lower-dimensional projections train much faster than end-to-end CNNs.
- Mechanism: After Fastfood projection, simple Decision Tree classifiers are trained instead of fine-tuning entire CNN architectures. The paper shows training times of 0.5-2 minutes versus 1.5-17 minutes for individual CNNs.
- Core assumption: Decision Trees can effectively classify the projected features without needing deep architectures.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2] "Simple Decision Trees[5] classifiers are used for the ensemble"
  - [section 3.2] "When smaller number of training samples is available (n: 50, 100), the Fastfood ensemble outperforms other models both in terms of accuracy and time"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - no direct comparison of Decision Trees vs CNNs in related papers
- Break condition: If the decision boundary is highly complex, simple classifiers may underperform despite faster training.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input patches -> 6 pre-trained CNNs -> Global average pooling -> Concatenated features -> N Fastfood projections -> N Decision Trees -> Ensemble consensus

- Critical path:
  1. Patch extraction from WSIs
  2. CNN forward passes for deep feature pooling
  3. Fastfood projection
  4. Decision Tree training
  5. Ensemble inference

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs speed: End-to-end CNNs slightly better with full data but much slower
  - Ensemble size vs complexity: N and D parameters balance diversity and computational cost
  - CNN selection vs performance: Using 6 diverse architectures provides robustness but increases feature dimension M

- Failure signatures:
  - Accuracy drops when training samples are extremely limited (<10 per class)
  - Performance degrades if orthogonal constraint on Fastfood matrices is violated
  - Slow training indicates inefficient Decision Tree implementation

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate Fastfood projection: Compare classification accuracy using raw concatenated features vs projected features with varying D
  2. Test ensemble diversity: Run with N=1, N=10, N=50 and observe accuracy improvement
  3. Evaluate CNN contribution: Remove one CNN architecture at a time and measure impact on ensemble performance

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of DeepFEL scale with different numbers of ensemble members (N) and projection dimensions (D)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions exploring D: {20, 100, 1K, 10K, 20K} and N: {10, 20, 50} but only reports results for the best configuration.
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide a systematic analysis of how performance varies across the different parameter settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive ablation study showing accuracy and training time for all combinations of N and D values.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of using different combinations of CNN backbones in DeepFEL?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses six different CNN models but doesn't explore whether using all six is optimal or if certain subsets perform better.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses all six CNN models but doesn't analyze the contribution of individual models or explore alternative combinations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing DeepFEL performance using different subsets of the six CNN models.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does DeepFEL perform on other computational pathology tasks beyond the three tested?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed deep fastfood ensemble learning as compared to the state-of-the-art methods for three different tasks in histopathology image analysis."
- Why unresolved: The evaluation is limited to three specific tasks, leaving generalization to other pathology tasks untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing DeepFEL on additional computational pathology datasets covering different organ systems or diagnostic tasks.

## Limitations

- The evaluation focuses primarily on accuracy without reporting precision, recall, or F1-scores that would be more informative for medical applications
- The orthogonal constraint on Fastfood matrices is critical but not specified how it's enforced or validated
- The method's generalization to other computational pathology tasks beyond the three tested remains unproven

## Confidence

- **High confidence**: Computational efficiency improvements (clear timing comparisons showing 3-34x faster training)
- **Medium confidence**: Accuracy claims for limited training data scenarios (focus on accuracy without comprehensive evaluation metrics)
- **Low confidence**: Effectiveness of orthogonal Fastfood projections (critical implementation detail not specified)

## Next Checks

1. **Orthogonality validation**: Verify that the N Fastfood random matrices maintain mutual orthogonality across different dimensions D and ensemble sizes N, and measure the impact of orthogonality violations on classification accuracy.

2. **Transferability analysis**: Conduct ablation studies removing individual CNN architectures to quantify each model's contribution to the ensemble, and test feature extraction on histopathology images that are visually dissimilar from ImageNet training data.

3. **Classifier complexity comparison**: Replace Decision Trees with more complex classifiers (Random Forests, SVMs) on the same projected features to determine if simple classifiers are sufficient or if increased model capacity would improve accuracy without sacrificing computational efficiency.
