# Evaluating statistical language models as pragmatic reasoners

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.01020
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01020
- Reference count: 11
- Large language models can infer human-like distributions over interpretations of gradable adjectives but systematically fail on negation and polarity inversion.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates whether large language models (LLMs) can perform pragmatic inference by mapping natural language utterances to probabilistic programs that represent context-sensitive interpretations. Using a tug-of-war domain with gradable adjectives like "strong," the research frames the task as semantic parsing where LLMs must infer threshold distributions that match human judgments. The LLMs successfully handle most constructions including comparisons with comparison classes, but systematically overestimate thresholds when negation ("not strong") or polarity inversion ("weak") is involved. These results demonstrate both the potential and limitations of LLMs in pragmatic reasoning tasks.

## Method Summary
The study uses a probabilistic programming framework where natural language queries about a fictional player's strength are mapped to discretized threshold distributions (0-100) via an LLM conditioned on a Church program defining the tug-of-war world model. The LLM (OpenAI's code-davinci-002) estimates probabilities for each threshold program, with temperature parameter selected via leave-one-out cross-validation. Human behavioral norms for the same stimuli are collected through point estimates over thresholds. The similarity between LLM-estimated and human distributions is measured using Jensen-Shannon distance, with statistical significance assessed through permutation tests and FDR correction.

## Key Results
- LLMs produce context-sensitive, human-like distributions for simple gradable adjectives like "strong" and "very strong"
- LLMs accurately handle comparison classes and qualification, even inferring group membership from indirect descriptors
- LLMs systematically overestimate thresholds when processing negation ("not strong") or polarity inversion ("weak")

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LLMs can infer context-sensitive probabilistic interpretations of gradable adjectives by amortizing pragmatic reasoning.
- Mechanism: The LLM treats natural language as a conditional distribution over probabilistic programs, using the context to assign probability weights to threshold values.
- Core assumption: The LLM's internal representations encode enough distributional information about how gradable adjectives are used in context to approximate human-like inferences.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states LLMs derive context-grounded, human-like distributions over interpretations of complex pragmatic utterances.
- Break condition: When the input requires explicit recursive reasoning over nested communicative intentions.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LLMs handle composition of pragmatic phenomena by leveraging learned lexical semantics and syntactic patterns.
- Mechanism: By conditioning on context that defines comparison classes, the LLM can jointly infer both the group membership and the relative strength within that group.
- Core assumption: The LLM's pretraining exposed it to enough variation in comparative constructions to generalize to novel but semantically related terms.
- Evidence anchors: Results support lexical semantic robustness of LLMs for parsing conditional mixtures.
- Break condition: When the comparison class is too abstract or requires reasoning beyond lexical semantics.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLMs fail to correctly interpret negation and polarity inversion due to asymmetric treatment of positive and negative concepts in training data.
- Mechanism: The LLM's learned distribution over thresholds for "strong" does not invert cleanly for "weak" or "not strong," leading to systematic overestimation.
- Core assumption: The LLM's pretraining data contains more positive polarity instances than negative ones.
- Evidence anchors: Abstract notes LLMs struggle composing with negation, deviating from human interpretations.
- Break condition: When negation is embedded in more complex syntactic structures or when multiple negations interact.

  - Why needed here: The study uses JSD to compare the LLM's estimated distributions over thresholds with human behavioral data.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Natural language query -> Context encoder (Church program) -> LLM core -> Scoring function (softmax) -> Discrete P(θ) distribution
- Critical path: NL query → LLM conditioning on context → program probability assignment → softmax scoring → discrete P(θ) distribution
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Amortization vs. explicit computation: Using LLMs avoids costly recursive Bayesian inference but may fail on complex negations
  - Discrete vs. continuous θ: Discretizing θ simplifies comparison with human data but loses precision
  - Single LLM vs. hybrid RSA-LLM: A hybrid could handle negations better but increases system complexity
- Failure signatures: Systematic overestimation of θ for negation/polarity inversion; inability to resolve abstract comparison classes
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Evaluate "strong", "very strong", and "not strong" for a player with no comparison class
  2. Test "strong for a beginner", "strong for a professional", and "strong for someone new to the game"
  3. Assess "weak", "very weak", and "not weak" to confirm failure on polarity inversion

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do LLMs handle negation in pragmatic inference beyond the specific gradable adjectives tested in this study?
- Basis in paper: The paper notes that LLMs struggle with negation ("not strong") and polarity inversion ("weak"), systematically overestimating thresholds, and suggests this may reflect broader challenges in resolving negation.
- Why unresolved: The study focuses on a limited set of gradable adjectives and does not test other linguistic phenomena involving negation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing LLMs on a broader range of negated expressions and comparing their interpretations with human judgments across diverse contexts would clarify the generalizability of the observed limitations.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can hybrid models combining LLMs with explicit probabilistic computation (e.g., RSA) improve performance on tasks involving negation and polarity inversion?
- Basis in paper: The paper suggests that hybrid approaches combining amortized and explicit reasoning could address LLM limitations with negation and polarity inversion.
- Why unresolved: The study does not implement or evaluate such hybrid models, leaving their potential effectiveness untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and benchmarking hybrid models on the same tasks used in the study would demonstrate whether explicit computation enhances pragmatic inference.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent do LLMs rely on statistical regularities in training data versus genuine pragmatic reasoning when inferring distributions over interpretations?
- Basis in paper: The paper raises the possibility that LLMs may recover distributions through statistical patterns in text rather than explicit world model reasoning.
- Why unresolved: The study does not distinguish between statistical retrieval and genuine reasoning, nor does it test LLMs in out-of-distribution scenarios.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparing LLM performance on tasks with varying degrees of statistical regularity and testing their ability to generalize to unseen contexts would clarify the balance between retrieval and reasoning.

## Limitations

- The study uses a synthetic tug-of-war domain with limited variation in context and utterance types, limiting generalization to real-world pragmatic phenomena.
- The failure on negation and polarity inversion is demonstrated only for simple constructions, with more complex embeddings untested.
- The use of a single LLM without comparison to other models or ablations limits understanding of whether results are model-specific.

## Confidence

- High confidence: LLMs can produce context-sensitive distributions for simple gradable adjectives and comparisons without negation
- Medium confidence: The failure on negation and polarity inversion is systematic and not due to random variation
- Low confidence: The study's findings generalize to other pragmatic phenomena like metaphor, irony, or real-world domains

## Next Checks

1. Test the same methodology with additional LLM architectures (GPT-4, Claude) and smaller models to determine if the negation failure is model-specific or architectural.

2. Evaluate more complex negation structures (double negation, embedded negation) and other pragmatic phenomena (metaphor, irony) to establish the boundary of LLM capabilities.

3. Conduct human experiments with the exact same interface and instructions used in the study to verify that the "human norms" are reproducible and not artifacts of a particular experimental setup.
