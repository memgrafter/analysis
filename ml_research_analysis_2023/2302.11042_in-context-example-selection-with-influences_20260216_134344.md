# In-context Example Selection with Influences

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.11042
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11042
- Reference count: 26
- Key outcome: This paper introduces in-context influences to analyze how individual in-context examples impact few-shot learning performance in large language models. The authors compute influence scores by comparing model performance across prompts that include or exclude specific training examples, enabling them to identify both helpful and harmful examples. Their influence-based example selection method outperforms several baselines on 9 SuperGLUE tasks, with up to 16.3% performance gap observed between the most positive and negative examples. The framework is also used to quantify recency bias in example ordering. The approach scales well with increasing k-shot and demonstrates strong predictive power through linear datamodels. Results show that example selection is crucial for few-shot performance, and that different models/tasks exhibit varying degrees of sensitivity to in-context examples.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a framework for analyzing and improving in-context learning (ICL) performance by computing influence scores for individual training examples. The authors develop in-context influence as a measure of how much each example affects model performance when included in prompts. Their approach enables identification of both helpful and harmful examples, outperforming several baselines across 9 SuperGLUE tasks. The framework also quantifies phenomena like recency bias in example ordering and scales well with increasing k-shot settings.

## Method Summary
The method computes in-context influences by measuring performance differences between prompts that include or exclude specific training examples. For each example, influence is calculated as the difference between average performance of prompts including that example versus prompts omitting it. The framework collects performance data across random k-sized subsets of training examples, computes influence scores for each example, then selects top-performing examples based on these scores for few-shot inference. The approach is evaluated on 10 SuperGLUE tasks with 400 training and 200 validation examples each, using language models like GPT-Neo and OPT.

## Key Results
- Influence-based example selection outperforms several baselines on 9 SuperGLUE tasks
- Up to 16.3% performance gap observed between most positive and negative examples
- Framework scales well with increasing k-shot settings and demonstrates strong predictive power through linear datamodels
- Successfully quantifies recency bias in example ordering across tasks

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Example selection pipeline: computes influences, ranks examples, selects top performers
  - Influence computation module: calculates performance differences between prompts with/without examples
  - Evaluation framework: tests selected examples against baseline methods
  - Analysis tools: visualizes influence distributions and ordering effects

- Critical path:
  1. Collect performance data across random example subsets
  2. Compute influence scores for each example
  3. Select examples based on influence scores
  4. Evaluate performance on validation tasks

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Many-shot vs few-shot influence estimation: many-shot provides more stable estimates but may not generalize
  - Linear vs non-linear influence models: linear models are simpler but may miss complex relationships
  - Example selection criteria: positive vs negative vs neutral influences each have different applications

- Failure signatures:
  - Random influence distributions suggest the framework isn't capturing meaningful patterns
  - Poor performance on certain tasks may indicate model-task mismatches
  - Unstable influence estimates across different k values suggest scaling issues

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test influence-based selection against random selection on a simple binary classification task
  2. Compare influence-based and KNN-based selection methods on multi-choice tasks
  3. Analyze influence distributions across different model sizes and task types

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the performance of in-context influence-based example selection scale with increasing context window sizes beyond the 2048 character limit?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions that context windows are typically limited to 2048 characters and that k is selected to be the maximal number of examples that can fit in this window.
- Why unresolved: The study does not explore scenarios where context windows are expanded beyond the standard 2048 characters, leaving open questions about how influence-based selection would perform with larger context windows.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing influence-based selection performance across various context window sizes, particularly those exceeding 2048 characters, would provide insights into scalability.

### Open Question 2

- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper suggests that the prompt template may play an important role in determining the influence of an example, as seen in the analysis of low-quality examples and the potential for improved coherence.
- Why unresolved: While the paper discusses the importance of prompt templates, it does not systematically explore how different template designs affect the influence estimates and overall selection performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments testing influence-based selection across multiple prompt template variations would clarify the relationship between template design and influence effectiveness.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do in-context influences vary across different model architectures and training datasets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that different model families (GPT-Neo and OPT) show varying levels of influence agreement, suggesting that model architecture and training data influence the effectiveness of examples.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not delve deeply into how specific architectural differences or training data characteristics affect influence estimates, leaving open questions about the generalizability of findings across diverse models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies examining influence-based selection across a wider range of model architectures and training datasets would elucidate the impact of these factors on influence effectiveness.

## Limitations

- Framework's effectiveness depends heavily on quality and representativeness of random example subsets used to compute influence scores
- Computational cost is significant - collecting performance data across M random subsets requires substantial compute resources
- Method assumes examples with high positive influence scores will generalize to new validation examples, which may not hold for tasks with complex decision boundaries

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The framework can identify both positive and negative examples that affect ICL performance, as demonstrated across 9 SuperGLUE tasks with measurable performance gaps (up to 16.3%) between most positive and negative examples.

**Medium Confidence**: In-context influences generalize across different k-shot settings and remain predictive when fewer examples are used, though this requires more extensive validation across diverse model architectures.

**Low Confidence**: The framework can reliably quantify recency bias in example ordering, as this analysis was presented as a case study rather than systematic evaluation across multiple tasks.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-model validation**: Test influence-based selection across multiple language model architectures (different sizes from different providers) to assess generalizability beyond the original models used.

2. **Influence stability analysis**: Measure how influence scores change when computed on different random subset samplings, establishing confidence intervals for influence estimates.

3. **Long-tail example evaluation**: Systematically examine examples with near-zero influence scores to determine if they represent true neutrality or measurement noise, and test whether these examples contain useful information when combined with high-influence examples.
