# Neighboring Words Affect Human Interpretation of Saliency Explanations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.02679
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02679
- Reference count: 40
- Word-level saliency explanations are influenced by neighboring words, with effects varying by direction and phrase relationships.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how neighboring words influence human interpretation of word-level saliency explanations in text-based models. Through a user study with 64 participants, the authors demonstrate that the perceived importance of a word is significantly affected by its neighboring words, with distinct patterns for left vs. right neighbors and for words that form phrases vs. unrelated words. The findings challenge the common practice of communicating saliency at the word level and suggest that neighboring context can distort the intended message of feature attribution explanations.

## Method Summary
The study collected human ratings of perceived word importance from 64 participants on Amazon Mechanical Turk, using 150 Wikipedia sentences with either randomized or SHAP-based saliency values. Participants rated words on a 1-7 scale while viewing color-coded importance explanations. The analysis employed ordinal generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) to account for participant variability and non-linear relationships. The researchers identified neighboring words using syntactic parsing and collocation measures, then tested for significant effects of neighbor direction and phrase relationships on perceived importance ratings.

## Key Results
- Neighboring words significantly influence perceived importance ratings of target words
- Directional effects exist: left and right neighbors have significantly different influences on perception
- Phrase relationships between neighbors and target words create distinct effects compared to unrelated neighboring words

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Neighboring words influence perceived importance through directional reading bias.
- Mechanism: Sequential left-to-right processing in English creates asymmetric evaluation of left vs. right neighbors, with the brain anchoring judgments based on the immediately preceding word.
- Core assumption: Reading direction creates asymmetric processing of left vs. right neighbors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "influence changes based on neighboring direction (left vs. right)"
  - [section] "influence from left and right word neighbors is significantly different"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers focus on saliency explanations but don't specifically address directional reading bias.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Strong linguistic relationships between words (phrases/collisions) create global processing that leads to assimilation.
- Mechanism: When words form a phrase or collocation, the brain processes them as a unified unit (global processing), causing importance ratings to become more similar between phrase partners.
- Core assumption: Multi-word phrases are processed as semantic units rather than individual words.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "influence changes based on... a-priori linguistic and computational measures of phrases and collocations"
  - [section] "We find qualitative differences... as well as statistically significant differences"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related work mentions saliency estimation but not phrase-level processing effects.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Extreme saliency values inhibit assimilation effects through local processing.
- Mechanism: Very high or low saliency values trigger local rather than global processing, preventing importance "leaking" from neighbors and maintaining individual importance ratings.
- Core assumption: Extreme values trigger local rather than global processing.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "extreme saliencies can inhibit assimilation" and "We identify two general opposing effects: assimilation and contrast"
  - [section] "We refer to Contrast as situations where a word's perceived saliency is perceived as less (or more) important based on whether its neighbor has a higher (or lower) saliency"
  - [corpus] Weak evidence - related papers don't address the interaction between saliency extremity and processing mode.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs)
  - Why needed here: The study uses GAMMs to model ordinal ratings while accounting for random effects (participant variability) and allowing non-linear relationships between variables.
  - Quick check question: What type of model would you use if you needed to account for both participant-level random effects and non-linear relationships between word importance and neighboring word importance?

- Concept: Saliency attribution methods
  - Why needed here: The study uses SHAP values as one source of word importance scores, requiring understanding of how these values represent feature attribution in NLP models.
  - Quick check question: How do SHAP values differ from simple gradient-based saliency scores in terms of accounting for feature interactions?

- Concept: Phrase identification methods
  - Why needed here: The study uses both syntactic (constituents) and statistical (collocation scores) measures to identify multi-word phrases, requiring understanding of different approaches to phrase detection.
  - Quick check question: What are the advantages and disadvantages of using syntactic parsing vs. statistical collocation measures for identifying phrases in text?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data collection interface (Mechanical Turk) -> Text preprocessing pipeline (tokenization, parsing) -> Phrase identification module (constituent extraction + collocation scoring) -> Statistical modeling engine (GAMM fitting) -> Visualization tools (difference plots, p-value heatmaps)

- Critical path: 1. Collect word importance ratings from participants 2. Preprocess text and identify left/right neighbors 3. Classify neighbors as chunk vs. no chunk 4. Fit GAMM with tensor product interactions 5. Generate and analyze difference plots

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Word-level vs. phrase-level explanations: Word-level is more granular but susceptible to context effects; phrase-level is more stable but loses fine-grained attribution
  - Statistical vs. syntactic phrase detection: Statistical methods are corpus-dependent but objective; syntactic methods capture grammatical structure but may miss statistical phrases
  - Random vs. SHAP-based saliency: Random provides baseline; SHAP captures model-specific importance but may introduce dependencies

- Failure signatures:
  - Non-significant tensor product terms: Indicates no interaction between saliency difference and neighbor relationship
  - Unexpected direction of effects: May indicate cultural/linguistic differences in reading patterns
  - High variance in participant ratings: May indicate unclear instructions or interface issues

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test the effect of word length (reproducing Schuff et al.) to verify the experimental setup
  2. Compare left vs. right neighbor effects with random saliency to establish baseline asymmetry
  3. Test chunk vs. no chunk effects at different saliency levels to validate the assimilation/contrast framework

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How generalizable are the observed effects across different languages, text domains, and NLP tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge that effects were observed in English Wikipedia text with English-speaking crowdworkers, and note this study is a "proof of existence" that context can influence interpretation of word-importance explanations.
- Why unresolved: The study was conducted in a specific language domain (English Wikipedia) and with English-speaking participants. The authors explicitly state that generalization to other settings requires further investigation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replicating the study across different languages, text domains (e.g., medical, legal, social media), and NLP tasks (sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, etc.) with appropriate participants would demonstrate generalizability.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific linguistic or cognitive properties cause global versus local processing that leads to assimilation versus contrast effects?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors reference Förster et al. (2008) who demonstrate how global vs. local processing leads to assimilation vs. contrast, and suggest that multi-word phrase neighbors may induce global processing while other neighbors induce local processing.
- Why unresolved: While the authors identify this as a likely explanation, they do not investigate which specific properties of neighbors (syntactic structure, semantic relatedness, frequency, etc.) trigger these different processing modes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic manipulation of neighbor properties (e.g., phrase structure, semantic similarity, collocation frequency) while measuring processing mode and resulting interpretation effects would identify the specific causal properties.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How do different visualization methods for feature-attribution explanations affect the observed neighbor influence effects?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors used color-coded word importance visualizations, which are common in the literature, but acknowledge this as a limitation and note that future work should investigate more effective methods of communicating information.
- Why unresolved: The study only examined one visualization method (color-coded word importance). Other methods like underlining, bolding, heat maps, or alternative layouts might mitigate or eliminate the neighbor influence effects.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting the same user study with different visualization methods for the same underlying importance scores would reveal whether certain visualizations reduce or eliminate neighbor influence effects.

## Limitations
- The study is limited to English text and may not generalize to languages with different reading directions or syntactic structures
- Color-coded visualizations may introduce visual perception confounds beyond purely linguistic processing effects
- The mechanism for when assimilation vs. contrast effects occur, particularly regarding extreme saliency values, remains unclear

## Confidence

- **High Confidence**: The existence of neighboring word effects on perceived importance (supported by statistical significance across multiple measures and visualization methods)
- **Medium Confidence**: The directional asymmetry (left vs. right neighbors) and phrase-based assimilation effects (supported by qualitative patterns but with some variability across different measures)
- **Low Confidence**: The exact boundary conditions for when assimilation vs. contrast effects occur, particularly regarding the interaction with extreme saliency values
