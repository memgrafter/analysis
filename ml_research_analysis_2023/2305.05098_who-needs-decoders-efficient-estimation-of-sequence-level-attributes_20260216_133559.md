# Who Needs Decoders? Efficient Estimation of Sequence-level Attributes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.05098
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05098
- Reference count: 40
- The paper proposes Non-Autoregressive Proxy (NAP) models to efficiently predict sequence-level attributes from transformer encoder outputs, bypassing expensive autoregressive decoding.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Non-Autoregressive Proxy (NAP) models that can efficiently predict sequence-level attributes like predictive uncertainty, BERTScore, and word error rate directly from transformer encoder outputs. By bypassing the expensive autoregressive decoding stage, NAPs achieve 10-138× faster inference while maintaining strong performance on tasks like out-of-distribution detection and data filtering. The approach demonstrates that encoder representations contain sufficient information to estimate metrics that traditionally require full model outputs.

## Method Summary
NAPs are trained to predict scalar-valued sequence-level attributes by using a transformer encoder backbone (typically initialized from a pretrained model like T5) to process input sequences, then pooling the encoder outputs and passing them through a small MLP predictor. The models are trained using differentiable Spearman rank correlation loss to optimize for relative ordering rather than absolute values. The encoder is frozen during training to reduce computational cost, and the approach is validated on machine translation and speech recognition tasks.

## Key Results
- NAPs achieve 10-138× faster inference than autoregressive baselines for predicting sequence-level attributes
- Outperforms deep ensemble methods in OOD detection with better AUROC scores
- Matches or exceeds uncertainty-based baselines in data filtering and resource allocation tasks while being significantly more efficient

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: NAPs can predict sequence-level attributes without autoregressive decoding by training a proxy encoder to directly imitate scalar-valued metrics derived from full model outputs.
- Mechanism: The encoder's learned representation of the input is pooled into a fixed-size vector, then passed through a small MLP trained to regress the target scalar metric (e.g., BERTScore, WER). This bypasses the need for the decoder to generate hypotheses before computing metrics.
- Core assumption: The encoder's intermediate representation contains sufficient information to reconstruct the scalar attribute that normally depends on the decoder's output.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "NAPs predict these metrics directly from the encodings, avoiding the expensive autoregressive decoding stage."
  - [section]: "We propose Non-Autoregressive Proxy (NAP) models that can efficiently predict general scalar-valued sequence-level attributes... NAPs predict these metrics directly from the encodings..."
- Break condition: If the encoder's representation is insufficiently expressive to capture nuances needed for the scalar metric, the proxy will fail to predict accurate rankings or values.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Rank correlation (Spearman) loss is more effective than point-wise regression loss for training NAPs because the downstream tasks care about ordering, not absolute values.
- Mechanism: By optimizing for Spearman rank correlation, the proxy learns to order instances by their predicted metric correctly, which is sufficient for tasks like OOD detection and data filtering.
- Core assumption: Downstream applications only require relative ranking of instances by metric, not precise metric values.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "we seek to learn the relative ordering (rankings) of our scores, as this simplifies the task and is more pertinent for downstream applications such as OOD detection."
  - [section]: "we follow Figure 1a in training a proxy on both single teacher confidence and entropy scores or ensemble mutual information estimates, evaluating its imitation ability and downstream out-of-distribution detection ability."
- Break condition: If an application requires accurate absolute metric values (not just ranking), Spearman-based training will underperform compared to point-wise regression.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Using the encoder backbone from a pretrained model (e.g., T5) as initialization for NAPs improves performance by leveraging learned feature representations.
- Mechanism: The NAP encoder is initialized from the teacher model's encoder weights, then frozen during training of the small predictor head, reducing training cost while retaining useful inductive biases.
- Core assumption: The pretrained encoder has already learned general-purpose features that transfer to the proxy task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "By default, the NAP encoder backbone is initialized from the encoder weights of the main encoder-decoder model."
  - [section]: "the purpose of our investigations are not to create the best possible NAP model (for example, fine-tuning the backbone encoder could improve performance at no cost of inference speed)."
- Break condition: If the proxy task is highly domain-specific or the pretrained features are irrelevant, using a mismatched encoder backbone could hurt performance (as shown in ablation studies with ALBERT).

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Input → Encoder (frozen pretrained) → Pooling layer (average or attention) → Predictor MLP (3-layer with softmax) → Scalar output

- Critical path:
  - Encoder forward pass → Pooling → MLP forward → Loss computation → Backpropagation to MLP only

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Frozen encoder vs fine-tuned: Faster training but potentially lower accuracy
  - Average pooling vs attention pooling: Simpler vs better for variable-length sequences
  - Spearman vs Pearson loss: Ranking vs point-wise accuracy

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor rank correlation indicates encoder representation insufficient or pooling inappropriate
  - Overconfident predictions on OOD data indicate model not capturing uncertainty well
  - Slow inference indicates inefficient pooling or large predictor

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train NAP to predict sequence confidence from T5 encoder on IWSLT-2017, evaluate rank correlation vs teacher
  2. Evaluate NAP uncertainty estimates on OOD detection task (AUROC) vs ensemble
  3. Train NAP to predict BERTScore, test on data filtering task (BERTScore increase when filtering low-score examples)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do different loss functions (e.g., cross-entropy, mean squared error) compare to Spearman rank correlation for training NAPs on sequence-level attributes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "we will mainly opt for the Spearman Rank and Pearson correlation coefficient (SCC & PCC) depending on the specific task considered" but also mentions investigating "alternative loss functions such as the root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE)" in Appendix B.1.
- Why unresolved: The paper only reports results for correlation-based losses and does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other loss functions.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic ablation study comparing NAP performance using different loss functions (e.g., cross-entropy, MSE, MAE, SCC, PCC) on various sequence-level attributes (e.g., uncertainty, BERTScore, WER) would clarify which loss function is optimal.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the choice of pooling method (e.g., average pooling, max pooling, attention-based pooling) impact NAP performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions using "two options, temporal averaging or multi-head attention with a single trainable query" for pooling encoder outputs, and states "early exploratory experiments found that a softmax activation is vital for good performance" in the Predictor Design section.
- Why unresolved: The paper only explores average pooling and attention-based pooling, and does not provide a comprehensive comparison of different pooling methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic ablation study comparing NAP performance using different pooling methods (e.g., average pooling, max pooling, attention-based pooling) on various sequence-level attributes (e.g., uncertainty, BERTScore, WER) would clarify which pooling method is optimal.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the choice of encoder backbone (e.g., T5, RoBERTa, ALBERT) impact NAP performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states "By default, the NAP encoder backbone is initialized from the encoder weights of the main encoder-decoder model" but also mentions investigating "mismatched encoders, e.g. using a RoBERTa NAP to predict the output attributes of a T5 system" in Appendix B.4.
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides a limited comparison of NAP performance using different encoder backbones (e.g., T5, RoBERTa, XLM-RoBERTa, ALBERT) and does not explore the impact of other potential backbones (e.g., BERT, GPT-2).
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic ablation study comparing NAP performance using different encoder backbones (e.g., T5, RoBERTa, ALBERT, BERT, GPT-2) on various sequence-level attributes (e.g., uncertainty, BERTScore, WER) would clarify which encoder backbone is optimal.

## Limitations

- The paper's claims about "state-of-the-art" OOD detection performance should be viewed with caution as evaluation compares against a limited set of baselines without testing against other recent OOD detection approaches.
- The 10-138× speedup claims are based on inference-only comparisons and don't account for the computational cost of generating teacher metrics for training data.
- The effectiveness of Spearman rank correlation loss assumes downstream applications only require relative ordering, but this isn't validated across diverse real-world use cases.

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: Claims about NAPs being faster than autoregressive decoding (10-138× speedup) are well-supported by the methodology and align with known computational differences between encoder-only and encoder-decoder architectures.

**Medium Confidence**: Claims about NAPs achieving strong rank correlation with target metrics and outperforming ensembles in OOD detection are supported by experimental results but rely on specific evaluation protocols that may not generalize.

**Low Confidence**: Claims about NAPs being a general solution for "efficient estimation of sequence-level attributes" are somewhat overstated given the limited scope of tested applications and metrics.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-architecture generalization test**: Evaluate NAP performance when using encoder backbones from different model families (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa) on the same tasks to verify that the approach isn't tightly coupled to T5's architecture.

2. **Absolute value prediction validation**: Design an experiment requiring precise metric values (not just ranking) to test whether Spearman-based training fails when absolute accuracy matters, validating the paper's assumption about downstream needs.

3. **Scaling behavior analysis**: Measure NAP performance and efficiency gains across different model scales (Tiny to XXL) and dataset sizes to determine if the 10-138× speedup claim holds across the full operational range.
