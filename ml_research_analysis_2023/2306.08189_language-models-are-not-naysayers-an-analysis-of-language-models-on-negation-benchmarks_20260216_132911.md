# Language models are not naysayers: An analysis of language models on negation benchmarks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.08189
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08189
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Large language models struggle with negation tasks, showing inverse scaling with size and random performance on lexical semantics

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how well large language models handle negation, a fundamental linguistic phenomenon. Through systematic evaluation of multiple LLMs (GPT-neo, GPT-3, InstructGPT, and FLAN-T5-XXL) on negation-focused benchmarks, the authors find that larger models perform worse on negation tasks, showing inverse scaling behavior. The study reveals that LLMs lack lexical semantic knowledge about negation, yielding almost random performance on synonym/antonym classification tasks. Instruction fine-tuning shows promise in improving negation reasoning, suggesting that new training paradigms beyond scaling are needed for better linguistic competency.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates LLMs on negation benchmarks using zero- and few-shot prompt-based learning. Models tested include GPT-neo, GPT-3, InstructGPT, and FLAN-T5-XXL across various sizes. Benchmarks cover factual statements (MKR-NQ), lexical semantics (SAR), and natural language inference tasks (NegNLI, NaN-NLI, MoNLI). Performance is measured using Weighted Hit Rate for cloze tasks and accuracy for classification tasks. The evaluation examines how different prompt formulations and model sizes affect negation handling.

## Key Results
- Larger LMs show inverse scaling with model size on negation tasks, performing worse than smaller models
- LLMs achieve almost random performance on lexical semantic tasks involving negation (synonym/antonym classification)
- Instruction fine-tuning improves reasoning under negation, though performance remains below random on most NLI datasets

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Larger models are more insensitive to negation due to increased memorization of training data and reduced sensitivity to semantic cues
- Mechanism: As model size increases, the model prioritizes memorization of frequent patterns over understanding of negation semantics. The model learns to associate certain contexts with specific completions, making it less sensitive to negation markers
- Core assumption: Negation is underrepresented in training data, leading models to treat negation as noise rather than meaningful semantic content
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "larger LMs are more insensitive to negation compared to smaller ones"
  - [section]: "This phenomenon reflects the finding that larger models tend to memorize the training data more"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - corpus analysis shows related papers focus on negation robustness rather than scaling effects
- Break condition: When training data includes sufficient negation examples with explicit semantic markers, or when models are fine-tuned with negation-aware objectives

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Models fail to capture synonym/antonym relations because negation lexical semantics are not properly represented in next-token prediction training objectives
- Mechanism: The causal language modeling objective trains models to predict the next token based on statistical co-occurrence patterns rather than semantic relationships. Negation-based lexical relations require understanding of meaning relationships that are not captured by token-level prediction
- Core assumption: Synonym/antonym detection requires explicit semantic understanding beyond surface-level pattern matching
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "LLMs lack lexical semantic knowledge about negation, yielding almost random performance for synonym/antonym classification"
  - [section]: "We argue that this is a specific task that is not captured in the next token prediction training objective of LLMs"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - related papers discuss negation understanding but not specifically lexical relation capture
- Break condition: When models are trained with objectives that explicitly capture semantic relationships, such as contrastive learning or supervised classification tasks

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Instruction fine-tuning improves negation reasoning by providing explicit supervision on task-specific patterns
- Mechanism: Instruction tuning exposes models to explicit examples of negation reasoning through human feedback and task-specific demonstrations. This creates stronger associations between negation contexts and correct reasoning patterns
- Core assumption: Models can learn negation reasoning patterns when provided with explicit examples and feedback, even if they don't learn these patterns through standard pre-training
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "instruction fine-tuning improves reasoning under negation"
  - [section]: "Using this model in combination with explicit instruction about negation (Negation prompt) further improves performance"
  - [corpus]: Weak evidence - corpus analysis shows instruction tuning improves performance but doesn't specifically address negation
- Break condition: When instruction data doesn't include sufficient negation examples, or when fine-tuning focuses on other aspects of task performance

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Negation as a fundamental linguistic phenomenon
  - Why needed here: Understanding negation requires recognizing how negation markers change meaning and require different reasoning patterns
  - Quick check question: How does the presence of "not" change the meaning of "The man owns a dog" versus "The man does not own a dog"?

- Concept: Lexical semantic relations (synonyms/antonyms)
  - Why needed here: Models need to understand that negation can reverse semantic relationships, such as how "not good" relates to "bad"
  - Quick check question: If "hot" and "cold" are antonyms, what is the relationship between "not hot" and "cold"?

- Concept: Natural language inference and entailment
  - Why needed here: Negation reasoning requires understanding how negated statements affect logical relationships between premises and hypotheses
  - Quick check question: Does "The man does not own a dog" entail "The man does not own a mammal"? Why or why not?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the performance gap between OPT and GPT-neo on MWR stem from differences in training data or model architecture?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper notes that OPT and GPT-neo have different pre-training corpora and observes a performance gap on MWR
- Why unresolved: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis comparing the specific training data and model architectures of OPT and GPT-neo
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting controlled experiments with identical training data but different model architectures, or vice versa, would help isolate the cause of the performance gap

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do larger language models like PaLM (up to 540B parameters) perform on the negation benchmarks compared to the models tested in this paper?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions computational constraints prevented testing larger models like PaLM and references Wei et al. (2022b) showing that instruction fine-tuned models like FLAN-PaLM-540B can alleviate inverse scaling trends
- Why unresolved: The paper did not have the computational resources to test the largest available language models on the negation benchmarks
- What evidence would resolve it: Running the negation benchmarks on PaLM and other large language models would provide direct performance comparisons

### Open Question 3
- Question: How well do language models handle negation in languages other than English?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly states it only considers negation in English and suggests it's an open question for other languages
- Why unresolved: The paper focused solely on English negation and did not have native speakers to conduct thorough analysis for other languages
- What evidence would resolve it: Running the negation benchmarks on multilingual models or training language-specific models and evaluating them on negation tasks in various languages would provide insights into cross-linguistic performance

## Limitations

- Data contamination concerns: Instruction-tuned models may have been exposed to negation examples during fine-tuning, potentially inflating performance
- Lack of architectural analysis: Insufficient investigation into why larger models show inverse scaling beyond memorization hypotheses
- Task alignment issues: Random performance on lexical semantic tasks may reflect task design problems rather than genuine negation understanding limitations

## Confidence

**High Confidence**: The observation that larger models show worse negation handling (inverse scaling) is well-supported by empirical results across multiple benchmarks. The methodology for measuring this effect is straightforward and reproducible.

**Medium Confidence**: The claim about instruction tuning improving negation reasoning is partially supported but complicated by potential data contamination. While performance improvements are observed, the source of these improvements remains uncertain.

**Low Confidence**: The assertion that models "lack lexical semantic knowledge about negation" based on random performance on synonym/antonym tasks is questionable. This interpretation doesn't adequately consider alternative explanations related to task design or evaluation methodology.

## Next Checks

1. **Data Contamination Analysis**: Conduct a thorough investigation of the training data for instruction-tuned models to identify whether negation examples were present. This should include both quantitative analysis of negation frequency and qualitative examination of whether the negation examples match the evaluation benchmarks.

2. **Alternative Scaling Hypotheses**: Test whether the inverse scaling relationship persists when controlling for factors beyond model size, such as training data composition, tokenization strategies, or architectural differences. This could involve experiments with models of similar size but different training regimes.

3. **Robustness to Prompt Variations**: Evaluate model performance across a wider range of prompt formulations and negations to determine whether current results reflect genuine negation understanding limitations or specific prompt sensitivities. This should include systematic variations in negation placement, phrasing, and context.
