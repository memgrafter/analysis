# Towards Selection of Text-to-speech Data to Augment ASR Training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.00998
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00998
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Synthetic speech samples with moderate dissimilarity to real speech can reduce TTS data size to below 30% while maintaining ASR accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method to select synthetic speech samples from a large TTS dataset to augment ASR training. The approach uses a GRU model optimized with either cross-entropy or Arcface loss to score the similarity between synthetic and real speech. The key finding is that synthetic samples with moderate dissimilarity to real speech—often due to lexical differences—are most effective for improving ASR performance. On Librispeech test sets, the proposed method can reduce the TTS data size to below 30% while maintaining equivalent ASR accuracy, outperforming several baseline selection strategies.

## Method Summary
The method trains a 2-layer GRU scoring model (256 hidden units per layer) with BCE or Arcface loss to measure similarity between synthetic and real speech. Synthetic speech is generated from TTS models using both Librispeech transcriptions and LM corpus text. The GRU model scores all synthetic samples, and those within a medium similarity range (e.g., 0.2–0.5 for BCE, 0.2–0.8 for Arcface) are selected for ASR training. The selected TTS samples are combined with Librispeech 960h for training an ASR model (Emformer encoder + RNN-T with 5000 sentencepiece tokens), and WER is evaluated on Librispeech test-clean and test-other sets.

## Key Results
- Proposed method reduces TTS data size to below 30% while maintaining ASR accuracy
- Synthetic samples with moderate dissimilarity (scores 0.2–0.5 for BCE, 0.2–0.8 for Arcface) perform best
- Outperforms baseline selection strategies including random sampling and score-based selection
- Inclusion of out-of-vocabulary words from LM text contributes to performance improvement

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Synthetic speech samples with moderate dissimilarity to real speech improve ASR performance more than those that are either very similar or very dissimilar.
- Mechanism: The GRU-based scoring model identifies synthetic samples that lie in a "sweet spot" of similarity. These samples contain lexical or acoustic features that differ enough from the training data to add useful variability but are still close enough to maintain quality.
- Core assumption: The additional information in moderately dissimilar samples outweighs any potential noise or artifacts.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "incorporating synthetic samples with considerable dissimilarity to real speech, owing in part to lexical differences, into ASR training is crucial for boosting recognition performance."
  - [section 3.4] "we discovered that our binary classification-based scoring approaches perform best when employing the TTS data within a medium scoring range."
- Break condition: If the synthetic data introduces too much noise or the dissimilarity is due to synthesis artifacts rather than genuine lexical or acoustic variation.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using a simple GRU model with BCE or Arcface loss for scoring is computationally efficient compared to using a pre-trained ASR model.
- Mechanism: The GRU model directly learns to map speech embeddings to a similarity score with real speech, avoiding the need for a full ASR pipeline.
- Core assumption: The GRU model captures sufficient information about speech similarity for effective data selection.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "our proposed solution can reduce the size of the TTS data down below its 30 %, which is superior to several baseline methods."
  - [section 2] "since our proposed method exploits only a simple neural network, it allows the rapid scoring of synthetic speech samples compared to previous work that typically relies on a pre-trained ASR model."
- Break condition: If the GRU model fails to capture relevant similarity features.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The inclusion of out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words from LM-generated TTS text improves ASR generalization.
- Mechanism: TTS samples generated from LM text contain words not present in the original Librispeech training set. These OOV words force the ASR model to learn new lexical items.
- Core assumption: The ASR model can effectively learn from these OOV words without being overwhelmed by noise or errors.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4] "These results are partially attributable to the incorporation of some extra unseen words given by LM text resources, and as a result, the selected data provide more additional information to benefit ASR training."
  - [section 4] "An utterance containing these unseen words may have a lower similarity to the original training samples, but including them in ASR training can help to improve speech recognition accuracy."
- Break condition: If the TTS synthesis introduces significant errors in pronouncing OOV words.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: TTS model -> GRU scoring model -> ASR model -> Librispeech dataset -> LM text corpus
- Critical path: 1. Generate synthetic speech from TTS model using LM text. 2. Score synthetic speech using GRU model. 3. Select synthetic speech samples within optimal similarity range. 4. Train ASR model on selected synthetic and real speech data. 5. Evaluate ASR performance on Librispeech test sets.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Simplicity vs. accuracy: Using a simple GRU model for scoring is computationally efficient but may not capture all relevant features compared to a full ASR model.
  - Similarity threshold: Selecting samples with moderate dissimilarity improves performance, but the optimal threshold may vary depending on the specific dataset and ASR model.
  - OOV word inclusion: Including TTS samples with OOV words improves generalization but may introduce synthesis errors.
- Failure signatures:
  - ASR performance degrades when using too many highly dissimilar synthetic samples.
  - Scoring model fails to generalize if trained on a limited subset of real speech.
  - WER improvement plateaus or decreases when using too few synthetic samples.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train GRU scoring model with BCE loss on Librispeech data and evaluate its ability to distinguish real from synthetic speech.
  2. Generate synthetic speech from LM text and score using the trained GRU model. Select samples within the optimal similarity range (0.2-0.5) and train an ASR model. Evaluate WER on Librispeech test sets.
  3. Repeat experiment 2 using Arcface loss instead of BCE loss. Compare WER results and analyze the impact of different loss functions on data selection and ASR performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal scoring range threshold for TTS sample selection to maximize ASR performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses finding a "proper scoring range" for TTS data selection and experimentally determines ranges like 0.2-0.5 for BCE loss and 0.2-0.8 for Arcface loss, but notes the determination is experimental and challenging.
- Why unresolved: The optimal threshold appears to depend on multiple factors (dataset characteristics, model architecture) and requires empirical determination rather than theoretical prediction.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying threshold ranges across different datasets, TTS quality levels, and ASR architectures to establish generalizable principles for threshold selection.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the proposed method perform on diverse ASR tasks beyond Librispeech (e.g., conversational speech, multiple languages, noisy environments)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only evaluates on Librispeech test sets (clean and other conditions), leaving generalization to other domains unexplored.
- Why unresolved: ASR performance depends heavily on domain characteristics, and the effectiveness of TTS augmentation may vary significantly across different speech conditions and languages.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensive evaluations on multilingual datasets, conversational speech corpora, and noisy speech databases to assess robustness and cross-domain applicability.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the GRU-based scoring model be replaced with more efficient or effective architectures (e.g., self-supervised speech representations) while maintaining or improving selection quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper uses a "basic recurrent neural network" (GRU) and notes that using simpler models allows rapid scoring compared to pre-trained ASR models, but doesn't explore alternatives.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on a specific architecture without exploring whether other approaches (like HuBERT embeddings, contrastive learning) might offer better efficiency or effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies evaluating different neural architectures (CNNs, transformers, self-supervised models) for speech embedding and their impact on TTS selection quality and computational efficiency.

## Limitations
- The method's effectiveness is primarily demonstrated on Librispeech data, with unclear generalization to other domains or languages.
- The computational efficiency advantage over ASR-based methods is implied but not explicitly quantified with runtime measurements.
- The role of OOV words in improving ASR generalization is hypothesized but not directly validated through controlled experiments.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The core methodology of using a GRU-based scoring model with BCE or Arcface loss for selecting synthetic speech samples is well-defined and reproducible.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that synthetic samples with moderate dissimilarity improve ASR performance more than those with high or low dissimilarity is supported by experimental results but relies on the specific characteristics of the Librispeech dataset and the chosen TTS model.
- **Low Confidence**: The assertion that including OOV words from LM-generated TTS text significantly improves ASR generalization lacks direct experimental evidence.
