# How Many and Which Training Points Would Need to be Removed to Flip this Prediction?

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of identifying minimal subsets of training data that, if removed, would flip a given test prediction. The authors propose two algorithms using influence functions to efficiently approximate the impact of removing training instances on test predictions. They evaluate their methods on binary text classification tasks with both bag-of-words and BERT features. The iterative algorithm often finds small subsets (e.g., median sizes of 67-484) that would flip predictions, with success rates varying by dataset (e.g., 78-99%). The size of these subsets provides complementary information to predicted probabilities, as there are confident predictions that could be flipped by removing relatively few training examples. This approach could enable new mechanisms for contesting model predictions by identifying potentially problematic training instances.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes two algorithms to find minimal subsets of training data whose removal would flip a test prediction. Both methods use influence functions to approximate the effect of removing training instances without full retraining. Algorithm 1 greedily selects training points with the highest influence until the prediction flips. Algorithm 2 iteratively refines this approach by updating model parameters to approximate removing the current subset, then recomputes influences to find a potentially smaller subset. The methods are evaluated on logistic regression models with bag-of-words and BERT features across five binary text classification datasets.

## Key Results
- The iterative algorithm finds smaller subsets than the greedy approach, with median sizes ranging from 67 to 484 depending on the dataset
- Success rates for finding subsets that flip predictions range from 78% to 99% across different datasets
- The cardinality of the minimal subset |St| provides complementary information to predicted probabilities, revealing that some confident predictions can be flipped by removing relatively few training examples
- On the Hate speech dataset with BERT features, the iterative algorithm found subsets of size 67-105 that would flip predictions with 99% success rate

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Influence functions can approximate the effect of removing training instances on test predictions without full retraining.
- Mechanism: Influence functions compute the derivative of the model parameters with respect to the loss of individual training examples, then use these to estimate how predictions change when training examples are removed.
- Core assumption: The model parameters change smoothly with small perturbations to the training data, making the influence approximation valid.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We propose comparatively fast approximation methods to find St based on influence functions"
  - [section]: "We then provide an iterative variant of this method which does a better job of identifying sets St"

- Break condition: The approximation fails when the model is highly non-convex or when removed instances have large influence that causes the optimization landscape to change significantly.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The cardinality of the minimal subset St provides a measure of prediction fragility that complements predicted probabilities.
- Mechanism: Small |St| indicates that a prediction is fragile because minor changes to training data can flip it, while large |St| suggests robustness.
- Core assumption: The relationship between |St| and prediction confidence is monotonic - smaller subsets indicate less confidence in the prediction.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "the cardinality of St provides a measure of robustness"
  - [section]: "We later show that this measure is correlated with, but complementary to, predicted probabilities"
  - [corpus]: "Relabeling Minimal Training Subset to Flip a Prediction" suggests related work on minimal subsets
- Break condition: The relationship breaks down when the model has learned highly robust features that are distributed across many training examples, making |St| large even for fragile predictions.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The iterative algorithm can find smaller subsets than the greedy approach by refining parameter estimates after each iteration.
- Mechanism: After finding an initial candidate set, the algorithm updates the model parameters to approximate removing those points, then recomputes influence to find a potentially smaller subset.
- Core assumption: Each parameter update improves the influence approximation for the remaining points, allowing better identification of minimal subsets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section]: "This variant trades run-time for (hopefully) more accurate identification of minimal St"

- Break condition: The algorithm may get stuck in local minima where further refinement doesn't reduce subset size, or the computational cost becomes prohibitive.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Data preprocessing → Feature extraction (BoW/BERT) → Model training (logistic regression) → Influence computation → Subset identification (Algorithm 1/2) → Validation through retraining

- Critical path:
  1. Compute initial model parameters on full training data
  2. Calculate influence scores for all training points on target test instance
  3. Sort points by influence and greedily select until prediction flips
  4. (For Algorithm 2) Iteratively refine by updating parameters and recomputing influences

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Algorithm 1 vs 2: Speed vs subset minimality
  - Feature choice: BoW (fast, interpretable) vs BERT (rich representations, slower)
  - Dataset size: Larger datasets increase computation but may provide more stable estimates

- Failure signatures:
  - High |St| values consistently: Model may be too robust or features too distributed
  - Algorithm 2 not converging to smaller subsets: Influence approximations may be inaccurate or local minima reached
  - Poor correlation between |St| and prediction confidence: Assumption about fragility measure may be violated

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify influence approximation accuracy by comparing to actual retraining on a small dataset
  2. Test Algorithm 1 vs 2 on a simple dataset to measure trade-off between runtime and subset size
  3. Evaluate correlation between |St| and prediction confidence across different datasets and feature types

## Limitations
- The methods are limited to convex models and binary classification tasks, as stated by the authors
- The computational cost of computing influence functions for all training points can be prohibitive for large datasets
- The relationship between subset size and prediction fragility may not hold consistently across all model architectures and datasets

- **Medium Confidence**: The empirical evaluation showing complementary information between |St| and predicted probabilities
