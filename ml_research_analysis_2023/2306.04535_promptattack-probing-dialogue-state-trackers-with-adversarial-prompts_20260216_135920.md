# PromptAttack: Probing Dialogue State Trackers with Adversarial Prompts

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.04535
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04535
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: PromptAttack generates adversarial examples that reduce DST accuracy by 9.3-31.0% while maintaining fluency

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces PromptAttack, a prompt-based learning approach for generating adversarial examples to probe dialogue state trackers (DSTs). The method learns adversarial prompts in both discrete and continuous settings without requiring access to DST model parameters, using only the DST output. These prompts are then used with a mask-and-filling protocol to generate adversarial examples that maintain natural language fluency while effectively misleading DST models. Experiments on four state-of-the-art DSTs demonstrate significant accuracy reductions and high attack success rates, while adversarial training with these examples substantially improves DST robustness.

## Method Summary
PromptAttack operates in two stages: first, it learns adversarial prompts through either discrete prompt construction (template-based with random value replacement) or continuous prompt tuning (optimizing prompt embeddings to maximize DST loss on ground truth belief states); second, it generates adversarial examples by masking non-slot-value tokens in user utterances, concatenating with adversarial prompts, and using BERT-base-uncased MLM to fill the masked positions. The method requires only the DST output without model parameters, making it a black-box attack approach. Evaluation metrics include Joint Goal Accuracy (JGA), Attack Success Rate (ASR), Perplexity (PPL), and Perturbation Ratio (PER).

## Key Results
- Reduces JGA by 9.3-31.0% across four state-of-the-art DST models (TripPy, CoCo, SimpleTOD, TRADE)
- Achieves high ASR while maintaining good fluency (low PPL) and low perturbation ratio
- Adversarial training with PromptAttack examples increases DST robustness from 61.3 to 67.3 accuracy
- Demonstrates strong transferability of adversarial examples across different DST architectures

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Adversarial prompts force DST models to make incorrect predictions by maximizing loss on ground truth belief states
- Mechanism: Prompt tuning optimizes continuous or discrete prompts to push model output away from correct slot-value pairs while maintaining semantic similarity
- Core assumption: DST confidence inversely relates to distance from ground truth in embedding space
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "maximize the loss for the ground truth belief states... force the model to make the most wrong predictions"; [section 3.3] loss maximization objective
- Break condition: Non-differentiable DST components or poor embedding space semantic representation

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Mask-and-filling protocol generates fluent adversarial examples via contextual understanding
- Mechanism: Randomly masking non-slot-value tokens and using MLM to fill preserves fluency while introducing misleading perturbations
- Core assumption: MLM can generate contextually appropriate tokens that maintain sentence fluency while changing semantic meaning
- Evidence anchors: [section 3.3] "use them to generate adversarial examples via mask-and-filling by pre-trained masked language models"; [section 4.3] BERT-based perturbation generation
- Break condition: Poor MLM contextual understanding or masking critical meaning tokens

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Transferability demonstrates general vulnerability of DST models to prompt-based attacks
- Mechanism: Adversarial examples generated for one DST successfully attack others due to shared vulnerabilities in slot-value processing
- Core assumption: Different DST models share similar weaknesses in handling linguistic perturbations
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "greatest reduction in accuracy" across four DSTs; [section 5.1] examples trained on TripPy attacking other models
- Break condition: Fundamentally different DST architectures or robust training approaches

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Victim DST models (TripPy, CoCo, SimpleTOD, TRADE) -> Prompt generation module (discrete and continuous) -> Mask-and-filling module (BERT-based MLM) -> Evaluation metrics (JGA, ASR, PER, PPL) -> Data pipeline (MultiWOZ 2.1 dataset)

- Critical path: 1. Extract belief states from victim DST 2. Generate adversarial prompts (discrete or continuous) 3. Create adversarial examples via mask-and-filling 4. Evaluate attack effectiveness on victim DST 5. Measure transferability to other DST models

- Design tradeoffs: Discrete vs. continuous prompts (interpretability vs. optimization efficiency), perturbation ratio (attack effectiveness vs. semantic preservation), prompt length (computational cost vs. attack strength)

- Failure signatures: Low ASR despite high perturbation ratio (MLM failing to generate effective perturbations), high PPL scores (examples becoming incoherent), no transferability (victim models too dissimilar or robust)

- First 3 experiments: 1. Generate adversarial examples using discrete prompts and measure JGA drop on TripPy 2. Compare ASR of continuous prompt variants (max vs min loss) on CoCo 3. Test transferability by attacking TRADE with examples generated for SimpleTOD

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the transferability of PromptAttack's adversarial examples compare when attacking newer or larger DST models not included in the original study?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper evaluates transferability across three DSTs and mentions it as a key finding, but doesn't explore performance on larger or newer models
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests on models from 2019-2021, leaving open the question of whether the attack generalizes to more recent or larger architectures
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing PromptAttack on a range of newer DST models (e.g., models using T5, larger transformer variants, or models from 2022-2023) and comparing attack success rates and transferability

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of domain-specific adversarial prompts versus general adversarial prompts on attack success and transferability?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses general adversarial prompts and achieves good results, but doesn't explore domain-specific prompt optimization
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate whether prompts tailored to specific domains (e.g., restaurant vs. hotel) would improve attack effectiveness or transferability compared to general prompts
- What evidence would resolve it: Training separate adversarial prompts for each domain in MultiWOZ and comparing their performance against both domain-specific and general DST models

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the effectiveness of PromptAttack scale with the size and complexity of the dialogue dataset?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper only evaluates on MultiWOZ 2.1, a single dataset, leaving questions about performance on larger or more complex datasets
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't test PromptAttack on datasets with more domains, longer dialogues, or more complex slot structures
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating PromptAttack on multiple dialogue datasets of varying size and complexity (e.g., MultiWOZ 2.2, Schema-Guided Dialogue, or custom datasets) and analyzing performance trends

## Limitations

- The method relies heavily on MLM quality for generating fluent adversarial examples, potentially limiting generalization to domains beyond MultiWOZ
- The discrete prompt construction depends on an unspecified Slot-Value Dictionary, potentially limiting reproducibility
- The study only demonstrates effectiveness on multi-domain dialogues with 7 domains and 30 slot types, leaving open questions about single-domain or more complex multi-domain scenarios

## Confidence

- **High Confidence:** Effectiveness in reducing JGA (9.3-31.0 drop) and achieving high ASR across four state-of-the-art DSTs
- **Medium Confidence:** Adversarial training significantly improves DST robustness (61.3 to 67.3 accuracy), but long-term stability requires further validation
- **Medium Confidence:** Transferability across DST architectures is demonstrated, but the mechanism for successful vs. unsuccessful transfer is not fully explained

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-Domain Transferability Test:** Validate PromptAttack's effectiveness on DST models trained on single-domain datasets (e.g., SGD, M2M) to assess whether the method generalizes beyond the multi-domain MultiWOZ setting

2. **Adversarial Training Robustness Evaluation:** Conduct extended adversarial training with PromptAttack examples over multiple epochs and evaluate performance on a held-out test set with adversarial examples not seen during training to assess long-term robustness

3. **Interpretability Analysis of Continuous Prompts:** Analyze the learned continuous prompt embeddings to understand what linguistic patterns they capture and whether these patterns correspond to known vulnerabilities in DST models, providing insights into why certain prompts are more effective than others
