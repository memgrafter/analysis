# Understanding and Bridging the Modality Gap for Speech Translation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.08706
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08706
- Reference count: 40
- This paper addresses the modality gap problem in end-to-end speech translation by leveraging machine translation data through multi-task learning.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the modality gap problem in end-to-end speech translation (ST) by leveraging machine translation (MT) data through multi-task learning. The authors propose Cross-modal Regularization with Scheduled Sampling (CRESS), which reduces the modality gap by regularizing ST and MT predictions using self-generated words as context and introducing token-level adaptive training to handle difficult cases. Experiments on the MuST-C dataset show that CRESS achieves significant improvements over strong baselines, with 1.8 BLEU gains in the base setting and 1.3 BLEU gains in the expanded setting on average, effectively bridging the modality gap and improving translation quality.

## Method Summary
The authors propose Cross-modal Regularization with Scheduled Sampling (CRESS) to bridge the modality gap in end-to-end speech translation. The method combines scheduled sampling with cross-modal regularization and token-level adaptive training. During training, ST and MT share a transformer encoder-decoder architecture, and their output predictions are regularized using KL divergence when both use similar (self-generated) contexts. Token-level adaptive weights are assigned based on the magnitude of the modality gap at each decoding step. The model is pre-trained on external MT data, then fine-tuned with CRESS on the ST task.

## Key Results
- CRESS achieves 1.8 BLEU gains over strong baselines in the base setting
- CRESS achieves 1.3 BLEU gains over strong baselines in the expanded setting on average
- The method effectively bridges the modality gap between speech and text translation models

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Cross-modal regularization with scheduled sampling reduces the modality gap by aligning ST and MT predictions in output space using self-generated target contexts.
- Mechanism: Scheduled sampling approximates inference mode during training by sampling target contexts between ground truth and predicted tokens. This encourages ST and MT to generate consistent predictions when using similar (self-generated) contexts, minimizing KL divergence between their output distributions.
- Core assumption: The modality gap primarily manifests during inference due to exposure bias, where ST and MT generate different target prefixes that compound over time.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "we regularize the output predictions of ST and MT, whose target-side contexts are derived by sampling between ground truth words and self-generated words with a varying probability."
  - [section 3.2]: "we observe a long-tail problem: there is a large difference between ST and MT representations in some difficult cases."
  - [corpus]: Weak. Related papers discuss bridging modality gaps but don't provide direct evidence for scheduled sampling + regularization effectiveness.
- Break condition: If the cascading effect of exposure bias is not the dominant source of modality gap, or if ST and MT architectures fundamentally prevent alignment despite regularization.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Token-level adaptive training handles difficult cases by assigning higher weights to tokens with large modality gaps during training.
- Mechanism: The modality gap at each decoding step is computed on-the-fly. Tokens with larger gaps receive higher weights (via a linear function of the gap), forcing the model to focus on these challenging cases and reduce their impact.
- Core assumption: A subset of tokens causes most of the modality gap, and emphasizing these during training improves overall alignment.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1]: "we also observe a long-tail problem: there is a large difference between ST and MT representations in some difficult cases."
  - [section 4.2]: "we introduce token-level adaptive training for CRESS, where each target token is given a varying weight during training according to the scale of the modality gap."
  - [corpus]: Weak. Related work mentions adaptive training but doesn't provide direct evidence for its effectiveness on modality gaps.
- Break condition: If the modality gap is uniformly distributed across tokens rather than concentrated in difficult cases, or if overemphasizing difficult cases harms overall performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multi-task learning with shared encoder-decoder reduces the baseline modality gap between ST and MT by learning similar representations from different modalities.
- Mechanism: By sharing all transformer layers between ST and MT tasks, the model learns to map speech and text inputs to similar intermediate representations, reducing initial representation differences before regularization is applied.
- Core assumption: Speech and text can be mapped to a shared semantic space when trained jointly, making cross-modal alignment more feasible.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1]: "With multi-task learning, most of the knowledge of MT can be transferred to ST. However, the performance gap between ST and MT still exists."
  - [section 2.2]: "By sharing encoder and decoder between ST and MT, the model tends to learn similar representations from different modalities."
  - [corpus]: Moderate. Related papers discuss modality gap bridging but don't directly address multi-task learning's role in reducing baseline gaps.
- Break condition: If speech and text modalities are fundamentally incompatible for shared representation learning, or if the modality gap is too large for shared architecture to meaningfully reduce.

## Foundational Learning

- Concept: Exposure bias in sequence generation
  - Why needed here: The paper explicitly links modality gap to exposure bias, showing how different training (teacher forcing) and inference (self-generated context) modes cause ST and MT predictions to diverge over time.
  - Quick check question: Why does the modality gap increase during inference but not during teacher forcing training?

- Concept: Scheduled sampling for bridging train-inference gap
  - Why needed here: The method uses scheduled sampling to approximate inference mode during training, allowing regularization to be applied when both ST and MT use self-generated contexts, making it closer to actual inference conditions.
  - Quick check question: How does scheduled sampling with varying probability help approximate inference mode during training?

- Concept: Regularization in output space (KL divergence)
  - Why needed here: The cross-modal regularization directly minimizes the difference between ST and MT output distributions when using similar contexts, encouraging consistent predictions across modalities.
  - Quick check question: What is the advantage of regularizing predictions in output space rather than hidden representations?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Pre-trained HuBERT acoustic encoder (2 conv layers for downsampling)
  - Shared Transformer encoder-decoder (6 layers each, 512 hidden, 8 heads)
  - Translation model pre-trained on MT data
  - CRESS training with scheduled sampling and cross-modal regularization
  - Token-level adaptive weighting based on modality gap

- Critical path:
  1. Pre-train translation model on MT data
  2. Initialize HuBERT + Transformer architecture
  3. Train with multi-task learning baseline
  4. Apply CRESS: scheduled sampling + cross-modal regularization + token-level adaptive weights
  5. Evaluate on MuST-C benchmark

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Shared architecture vs. modality-specific components: Shared layers reduce parameters but may limit modality-specific optimization
  - Regularization weight λ: Too high may over-constrain model, too low may not bridge gap effectively
  - Token-level adaptive training parameters (B, S): Need careful tuning to avoid overemphasizing difficult cases

- Failure signatures:
  - No improvement over baseline: Check if scheduled sampling probability schedule is appropriate
  - Performance degradation: May indicate over-regularization or poor token-level weight tuning
  - Training instability: Verify Gumbel sampling implementation and gradient flow

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement and validate scheduled sampling with ground truth vs. predicted word selection
  2. Add cross-modal regularization and verify KL divergence computation between ST and MT outputs
  3. Implement token-level adaptive weights and validate modality gap computation during training

- Why unresolved: The proposed method relies on the consistency between the speech and text inputs, and it is unclear how it would handle cases where the quality of these inputs differs.

## Limitations
- Limited analysis of regularization impact on final output quality: While BLEU improvements are shown, detailed analysis of how cross-modal regularization affects different translation phenomena is lacking.
- No ablation of individual CRESS components: The paper presents CRESS as a complete method without separating contributions of scheduled sampling, cross-modal regularization, and token-level adaptive training.
- Training data mismatch handling: The methodology doesn't specify how external MT data with different tokenization/vocabulary is processed alongside ST data.

## Confidence
**High confidence**: The core claim that modality gap exists between speech translation and text translation models, and that this gap can be measured using KL divergence between output distributions when using similar contexts. The experimental results showing BLEU improvements with CRESS are reproducible and the methodology is clearly described.

**Medium confidence**: The mechanism that scheduled sampling combined with cross-modal regularization effectively reduces modality gap during inference. While the experimental results support this, the paper lacks detailed analysis of how different components of CRESS contribute to the observed improvements.

**Low confidence**: The assumption that token-level adaptive training meaningfully improves performance by focusing on difficult cases. The paper mentions a "long-tail problem" but provides limited empirical evidence that the token-level weighting strategy actually addresses this issue rather than potentially overfitting to difficult examples.

## Next Checks
1. **Component ablation study**: Run controlled experiments isolating scheduled sampling, cross-modal regularization, and token-level adaptive training to quantify individual contributions to the 1.8 BLEU improvement. This should include baseline multi-task learning without any CRESS components, with only scheduled sampling, with only regularization, and with only token-level weighting.

2. **Error analysis across translation phenomena**: Conduct detailed error analysis comparing baseline ST, multi-task baseline, and CRESS outputs to determine whether improvements come from better handling of specific translation challenges (e.g., morphology, syntax, lexical choice) or general quality improvements. This should include both automatic metrics and human evaluation.

3. **Generalization across datasets**: Test CRESS on additional speech translation benchmarks beyond MuST-C (such as CoVoST 2 or FLEURS) to verify that improvements generalize beyond the specific dataset used in the paper. This should include evaluation on languages not present in the training data to assess cross-lingual transfer.
