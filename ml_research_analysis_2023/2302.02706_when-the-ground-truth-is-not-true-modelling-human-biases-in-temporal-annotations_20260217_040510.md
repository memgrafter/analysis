# When the Ground Truth is not True: Modelling Human Biases in Temporal Annotations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2302.02706
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02706
- Reference count: 0
- Key outcome: Soft labels generated by modeling human temporal biases outperform hard labels in synthetic experiments when evaluated by mean squared error

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of unreliable temporal annotations in supervised learning, where human annotators' time perceptions and coarse rounding introduce significant uncertainty. The authors propose a method that first estimates annotator time resolution habits using Bayesian inference, then generates soft labels representing probabilities of events occurring at different time points. The approach acknowledges that human annotations are inherently uncertain and models this uncertainty as a latent categorical variable over time resolution categories.

## Method Summary
The method uses Bayesian inference to estimate annotator time resolution habits by modeling annotations as generated from actual event times with added rounding biases. For each annotator, the model computes posterior probabilities over time resolution categories (e.g., 30min, 15min, 10min, 5min, 1min). Using these estimates, soft labels are generated as probability distributions across time intervals, representing the likelihood of events occurring at different time points. The approach assumes annotations follow a uniform distribution within their estimated time resolution category.

## Key Results
- Soft labels provide better approximation of ground truth than hard labels when evaluated by mean squared error in synthetic experiments
- Estimation error for annotator time resolution categories decreases with more annotations per annotator
- The method is demonstrated on a real shower event detection task using humidity sensor data and self-reported annotations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The Bayesian framework accurately estimates annotator time resolution habits by modeling annotation uncertainty as a latent categorical variable.
- Mechanism: The method treats each annotation as generated from a ground truth time with added rounding bias to a discrete time resolution category. By computing posterior probabilities over these categories given observed annotations, it infers the most likely time resolution each annotator uses.
- Core assumption: Annotations follow a uniform distribution within their time resolution category and annotators have consistent rounding habits across annotations.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "The authors propose a method to model human biases in temporal annotations by first estimating annotator time resolution habits using Bayesian inference", [section] "We assume that the i-th annotations (d_i) are generated from C_i and d*_i, and C_i is generated from H" (Bayesian graphical model)

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Soft labels better approximate ground truth than hard labels when evaluated with mean squared error in high-uncertainty annotation scenarios.
- Mechanism: By assigning probabilities across time intervals rather than binary values, soft labels capture the inherent uncertainty in human temporal annotations. The uniform distribution assumption within estimated time resolution creates a probability mass function that reflects this ambiguity.
- Core assumption: The true event timing follows a uniform distribution within the annotation time resolution interval.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Experimental results in synthetic data show that soft labels provide a better approximation of the ground truth for several metrics", [section] "Finally, we compute the soft label (P(label)(t)), which is the probability of the event at time t by multiplying P(d*_s ≤ t) and P(d*_e > t)"

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The model's accuracy improves with more annotations per annotator due to better estimation of time resolution categories.
- Mechanism: As more annotations are collected, the posterior probability distribution over time resolution categories becomes more concentrated, reducing estimation error. The model benefits from statistical power gained through multiple observations of the same annotator's behavior.
- Core assumption: Annotators maintain consistent time resolution habits across multiple annotations.
- Evidence anchors: [section] "Figure 7 shows the error rate of the annotation category estimation... As the plot suggests, the error rate decreases quickly with the more annotation it receives"

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Data preprocessing -> Bayesian inference module -> Soft label generation -> Evaluation module -> Integration with detection models
- Critical path:
  1. Collect annotations and corresponding sensor data
  2. Run Bayesian inference to estimate annotator time resolution habits
  3. Generate soft labels using estimated time resolutions
  4. Evaluate soft vs hard labels on synthetic/real data
  5. Apply soft labels in detection model training or evaluation

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Uniform vs non-uniform distributions for time rounding: Uniform is simpler but may not capture real rounding patterns
  - Number of time resolution categories: More categories provide finer granularity but require more data for accurate estimation
  - Single vs multiple annotator models: Single annotator is simpler but may miss inter-annotator variability

- Failure signatures:
  - High estimation error for time resolution categories (P(H|D) is uniform)
  - Soft labels show minimal improvement over hard labels in MSE
  - Model performance degrades with fewer annotations per annotator

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Generate synthetic data with known time resolution and test the Bayesian inference module's accuracy in recovering the true resolution
  2. Compare MSE of soft vs hard labels on synthetic data with varying annotation resolutions and event durations
  3. Apply the method to a small real-world dataset and qualitatively assess whether the estimated time resolutions match observed annotation patterns

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: What is the optimal evaluation metric for comparing soft and hard labels in temporal annotation tasks?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses how commonly used metrics like F1 score penalize ambiguity in soft labels, while MSE appears more suitable but may not be universally optimal
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges the need for further study to determine which metric is appropriate in which scenario, but does not provide definitive guidance
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments comparing multiple evaluation metrics (including MSE, F1, Brier score, and task-specific metrics) across diverse temporal annotation tasks and ambiguity levels

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the proposed method be extended to handle dependent start and end times in temporal annotations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors acknowledge that their current model assumes statistical independence between start and end timings, which "does not hold in some cases" and should be addressed in future work
- Why unresolved: The paper presents this as a limitation and potential future direction without providing solutions for handling dependent timings
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of a probabilistic model that accounts for the dependency between start and end times, potentially using joint distributions or conditional dependencies

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the minimum number of annotations required for accurate estimation of annotator habits and time resolution categories?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Figure 7 shows error rates decreasing with more annotations, but does not establish a clear threshold for "sufficient" annotations
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the relationship between annotation quantity and accuracy but does not provide specific guidance on sample size requirements for practical applications
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies establishing minimum annotation requirements across different annotation patterns, time resolutions, and task complexities

## Limitations
- Assumes uniform distribution of annotations within time resolution categories, which may not capture complex human rounding behaviors
- Requires multiple annotations per annotator to accurately estimate time resolution habits
- Current implementation focuses on single annotator scenarios, limiting applicability to multi-annotator datasets

## Confidence
- High confidence: The Bayesian framework correctly estimates time resolution categories from multiple annotations
- Medium confidence: Soft labels provide better MSE approximation than hard labels in high-uncertainty scenarios
- Low confidence: The method's applicability to complex multi-annotator scenarios and non-uniform rounding behaviors
