# Scalable Performance Analysis for Vision-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2305.18786
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18786
- Authors:

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a scalable method to analyze the performance of vision-language models by leveraging existing benchmarks without requiring additional annotations. The approach involves extracting a large set of semantic features from vision-language data and measuring their correlation with the model's outputs. The method was tested on CLIP using the SVO-Probes benchmark, confirming that CLIP behaves like a bag-of-words model and performs better with nouns and verbs. Novel findings include CLIP's confusion with concrete words, improved performance with ambiguous terms, and minimal impact from word frequency. The framework, available at https://github.com/MichiganNLP/Scalable-VLM-Probing, can be used to analyze other multimodal models and benchmarks, opening avenues for addressing newly discovered challenges.

## Method Summary
The framework extracts diverse semantic features from existing vision-language benchmarks and measures their correlation with model outputs to identify weaknesses. It processes benchmarks like SVO-Probes to extract features including Levin verb classes, LIWC markers, WordNet hypernyms, concreteness scores, and word frequency. For each instance, it computes CLIP scores (P for positive pairs, N for negative pairs, D for relative alignment) and then calculates Pearson correlations between semantic features and these scores. The framework uses two-sample t-tests for binary features and Pearson correlation for numerical features, filtering significant results at p<0.05. This approach enables scalable analysis without requiring additional annotations or model training.

## Key Results
- CLIP behaves like a bag-of-words model, treating words independently rather than understanding compositional relationships
- CLIP gets confused by concrete words but surprisingly improves performance with more ambiguous terms
- Word frequency has minimal impact on CLIP's performance scores

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Data preprocessing pipeline (SVO-Probes benchmark parsing) -> Feature extraction modules (Levin classes, LIWC, WordNet, concreteness, etc.) -> CLIP model integration for score computation -> Correlation analysis engine (Pearson correlation, t-tests) -> Result visualization components (plots, tables)

- Critical path:
  1. Load benchmark data
  2. Extract semantic features for each instance
  3. Compute CLIP scores (P, N, D) for all instances
  4. Calculate correlations between features and scores
  5. Generate insights and visualizations

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Feature richness vs. computational cost: More semantic features provide better coverage but increase computation time.
  - Statistical significance thresholds: Lower thresholds find more patterns but risk false positives.
  - Dataset specificity: Using a single benchmark provides focused insights but may not generalize to all vision-language tasks.

- Failure signatures:
  - Low statistical significance across all features (p > 0.05)
  - Uniform correlations across all features (no differentiation)
  - High correlation with irrelevant features (noise)
  - Runtime errors in feature extraction for certain word types

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Validate the bag-of-words behavior by testing with simple word replacement scenarios
  2. Analyze concreteness effects by creating controlled test cases with varying levels of abstract vs. concrete words
  3. Test frequency effects by generating synthetic data with controlled word frequencies

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does CLIP's performance vary across different semantic categories when evaluated on diverse vision-language benchmarks beyond SVO-Probes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper analyzes CLIP's performance on SVO-Probes and identifies areas where CLIP excels and struggles, suggesting further exploration with other benchmarks.
- Why unresolved: The analysis is limited to SVO-Probes, and the paper acknowledges the potential for studying CLIP on a wider range of benchmarks to gain a more comprehensive understanding of its limitations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a similar analysis using various vision-language benchmarks and comparing CLIP's performance across different semantic categories would provide insights into its strengths and weaknesses in diverse contexts.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the impact of polysemy on CLIP's performance, and how can this be mitigated in future models?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper mentions the limitation of not considering polysemy when using LIWC or Levin dictionaries, which may lead to incorrect word categorization and influence the error analysis.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not delve into the specific effects of polysemy on CLIP's performance or propose solutions to address this issue.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyzing CLIP's performance on words with multiple meanings and investigating the impact of polysemy on its accuracy would help understand the extent of this problem. Developing methods to disambiguate word senses in vision-language models could potentially mitigate this limitation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the frequency of words in the training data affect CLIP's ability to generalize to less common concepts or rare objects?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper finds that CLIP's performance improves for more frequent words, but the difference in scores is barely affected.
- Why unresolved: While the paper acknowledges the influence of word frequency, it does not explore the implications of this finding for CLIP's ability to handle rare or uncommon concepts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluating CLIP's performance on benchmarks that include rare or uncommon concepts and comparing its accuracy with that of more frequent concepts would provide insights into its generalization capabilities. Additionally, investigating the relationship between word frequency and CLIP's confidence scores could shed light on its ability to handle uncertainty.

## Limitations
- The framework's generalizability across different vision-language models and benchmarks remains untested, as the current evaluation is limited to CLIP and the SVO-Probes benchmark.
- The correlation-based approach assumes linear relationships between semantic features and model performance, potentially missing more complex interactions.
- The semantic feature extraction relies on pre-defined linguistic resources that may not capture all relevant model behaviors, particularly for specialized domains or languages.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The framework's methodology for extracting semantic features and computing correlation with model outputs is technically sound and well-defined.
- Medium confidence: The bag-of-words behavior finding for CLIP is supported by the data but could vary across different model architectures or training paradigms.
- Low confidence: The practical utility of the framework for improving model performance or guiding model development is not demonstrated, only the analysis capability.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-model validation: Apply the framework to at least two additional vision-language models (e.g., Flamingo, BLIP) using the same SVO-Probes benchmark to verify if findings generalize beyond CLIP.
2. Multiple benchmark testing: Evaluate the framework across diverse benchmarks (visual question answering, image captioning, visual reasoning) to assess its applicability across different vision-language tasks.
3. Controlled synthetic testing: Create synthetic datasets with controlled semantic properties to validate that the correlation patterns observed are not artifacts of the SVO-Probes dataset structure or CLIP's specific training data distribution.
