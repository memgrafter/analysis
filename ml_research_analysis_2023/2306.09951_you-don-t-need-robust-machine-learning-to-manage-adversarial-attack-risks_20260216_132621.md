# You Don't Need Robust Machine Learning to Manage Adversarial Attack Risks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2306.09951
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09951
- Reference count: 33
- This paper argues that the costs of robust ML models often outweigh their benefits for most organizations.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the prevailing assumption that robust machine learning models are necessary to defend against adversarial attacks. Through a stylized risk model and analysis of real-world attack cases, the authors demonstrate that the probability and impact of adversarial ML attacks are often overestimated relative to the costs of implementing robust defenses. The paper argues that many organizations can effectively manage adversarial ML risks through non-ML design choices and strategies, rather than investing in expensive robust models.

## Method Summary
The authors employ a stylized risk model to compare the trade-offs between normal and robust ML models, analyzing three main types of adversarial attacks (poisoning, inversion, evasion) across different threat models. They categorize attack viability through a 3×3 grid intersecting attack types with threat models (black, gray, white box). The methodology includes surveying "real-world" adversarial ML cases and evaluating their realism, followed by proposing non-ML mitigation strategies as alternatives to robust models.

## Key Results
- The probability of successful adversarial attacks and their impact is often lower than the cost of developing and maintaining robust models.
- Non-ML design choices like data validation, differential privacy, and restricted model access can effectively mitigate many adversarial ML threats.
- Many so-called "real-world" adversarial ML attacks have issues with their realism, suggesting the threat is often overestimated.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Risk assessment and non-ML mitigations can often be more cost-effective than robust ML models for managing adversarial ML risks.
- Mechanism: The paper argues that for many organizations, the probability of successful adversarial attacks and their impact is lower than the cost of developing and maintaining robust ML models. By focusing on risk assessments, robust ML mitigations, and extrinsic risk reduction strategies, organizations can effectively address adversarial ML threats without the need for expensive robust models.
- Core assumption: The likelihood of adversarial attacks and their impact is accurately estimated, and non-ML design choices can effectively mitigate these risks.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Using a stylized risk model, the authors show that the probability of successful adversarial attacks and their impact is often lower than the cost of developing and maintaining robust models."
  - [section 4]: "Considering the cost trade-offs presented by robust models, we posit that model robustness may be warranted only in a small set of circumstances."
- Break condition: If the actual probability of adversarial attacks or their impact is significantly higher than estimated, or if non-ML mitigations prove ineffective.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Different types of adversarial attacks have varying levels of risk and require different mitigation strategies.
- Mechanism: The paper categorizes adversarial attacks into poisoning, inversion, and evasion, and analyzes their likelihood and potential impact in different threat models. It then recommends specific non-ML design choices to mitigate each type of attack, such as cryptographic signatures for data/label pairs, differential privacy for model inversion, and restricting predictions for evasion attacks.
- Core assumption: The categorization of adversarial attacks and their associated risk levels are accurate, and the recommended non-ML mitigations are effective.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1]: "It is useful to group adversarial attacks into three general types, ordered based on the nature of the risk to the enterprise."
  - [section 5]: "Having elucidated a trade-off between robust and non-robust models, that is mitigated by the likelihood of an attack occurring against the average entity, we now seek to answer how such risks can be managed without relying on robust ML."
- Break condition: If new types of adversarial attacks emerge or if the effectiveness of non-ML mitigations is challenged.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Real-world adversarial ML attacks are less frequent and impactful than often assumed, making robust ML models unnecessary for many organizations.
- Mechanism: The paper surveys published "real-world" cases of adversarial ML attacks and finds that many of them have issues with their realism, such as being purely academic or lacking clear malicious intent. It argues that these cases, combined with the cost and complexity of robust ML models, suggest that robust ML is not necessary for most organizations.
- Core assumption: The analysis of "real-world" adversarial ML attacks is comprehensive and accurate, and the identified issues with these cases are valid.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 6]: "Much literature and documentation exist today on 'real-world' cases of adversarial machine learning. If we take real-world to mean that the model/events under consideration occurred in a non-academic setting... these conditions are often not satisfied."
  - [table 2]: "We categorize the potential issues that would limit the attack's risk as... 1. The work involves a threat model where the adversary must either choose to forgo an easier alternative to achieve the same goals."
- Break condition: If new, more realistic "real-world" cases of adversarial ML attacks emerge or if the analysis of existing cases is found to be incomplete or inaccurate.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  - Risk assessment framework -> Non-ML mitigation strategies -> Real-world attack analysis

- Critical path:
  1. Assess the organization's ML models and their potential vulnerabilities to different types of adversarial attacks.
  2. Estimate the likelihood and impact of these attacks based on the organization's specific context and threat models.
  3. Apply appropriate non-ML mitigation strategies to reduce the risk of adversarial attacks.
  4. Regularly review and update the risk assessment and mitigation strategies as new threats emerge or the organization's context changes.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Cost vs. effectiveness: Non-ML mitigations may be less expensive than robust ML models, but their effectiveness may vary depending on the specific attack and context.
  - Complexity vs. usability: Some non-ML mitigations (e.g., cryptographic signatures) may add complexity to the ML system, potentially impacting its usability or performance.
  - Privacy vs. accuracy: Differential privacy, while effective against model inversion attacks, may reduce the accuracy of the ML model due to the added noise.

- Failure signatures:
  - High false positive rate: If the non-ML mitigations are too restrictive, they may flag legitimate inputs as adversarial, leading to a high false positive rate and reduced usability.
  - Missed attacks: If the risk assessment underestimates the likelihood or impact of certain types of adversarial attacks, the organization may be vulnerable to these attacks despite the non-ML mitigations.
  - Increased complexity: If the non-ML mitigations add too much complexity to the ML system, it may become difficult to maintain, update, or troubleshoot.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Implement a simple risk assessment framework for a sample ML model, evaluating its vulnerabilities to different types of adversarial attacks and estimating their likelihood and impact.
  2. Apply a non-ML mitigation strategy (e.g., prediction restrictions) to the sample ML model and assess its effectiveness in reducing the risk of adversarial attacks.
  3. Analyze a published "real-world" case of an adversarial ML attack and evaluate its realism and relevance to the organization's context.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How do the risks and costs of adversarial attacks evolve over time as more robust ML models become available and attack techniques improve?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses that robustness should be on a long-term roadmap, but does not provide specific timelines or models for how the risk landscape might change.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on current attack scenarios and does not project future trends or provide a framework for evaluating evolving risks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal studies tracking the effectiveness of robust models against new attack techniques, and analysis of how the cost-benefit trade-offs shift as technology advances.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How do the effectiveness and costs of non-ML mitigation strategies vary across different industries and use cases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper discusses general mitigation strategies but does not provide industry-specific analyses or comparisons.
- Why unresolved: The paper focuses on general principles and does not delve into the nuances of how different sectors might implement and benefit from non-ML mitigations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Case studies and empirical analyses of non-ML mitigation strategies in various industries, comparing their effectiveness and costs.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the most effective ways to assess the likelihood and potential impact of adversarial attacks in specific organizational contexts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper mentions the importance of risk assessments but does not provide a detailed framework or methodology for conducting them.
- Why unresolved: The paper emphasizes the need for risk assessments but does not offer concrete guidance on how to perform them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development and validation of a comprehensive risk assessment framework tailored to adversarial ML threats, including case studies and best practices.

## Limitations
- The paper's core argument depends heavily on accurate estimation of attack probabilities and impacts, which remain inherently uncertain in practice.
- The stylized risk model uses simplified assumptions that may not capture real-world complexities.
- The analysis of "real-world" attack cases may suffer from selection bias, as only published cases are considered.
