# Evaluating LLM-Simulated Conversations in Modeling Inconsistent and Uncollaborative Behaviors in Human Social Interaction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17094
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17094
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Under vanilla prompting, LLMs generate far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than humans; prompt engineering fails to reliably control these behaviors and often causes overproduction.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why large language model (LLM)-simulated conversations lack the inconsistent and uncollaborative micro-behaviors (e.g., misunderstandings, interruptions) common in human dialogue. It introduces CoCoEval, a framework using an LLM-as-a-Judge to detect ten specific behavior types at the turn level across 30-turn continuations in multiple domains. Key findings reveal that current LLMs significantly under-produce these behaviors under standard prompting. Explicit instructions to incorporate such behaviors lead to unstable outcomes—either ignoring the instruction or overproducing them—and supervised fine-tuning on human data amplifies a narrow subset like repetition. Generating multiple turns in a single call strongly suppresses these behaviors. The study concludes that coarse-grained conversation-level scores mask substantial fine-grained misalignments between LLM and human interaction styles.

## Method Summary
The CoCoEval framework evaluates LLM-generated 30-turn conversation continuations against human references. It employs o4-mini as an automatic judge to produce both conversation-level scores (1–10 for consistency and collaborativeness) and turn-level counts of ten predefined behavior types. The benchmark consists of 300 continuations from business, academic, governmental, and debate domains. Simulations are run under three conditions: vanilla prompting, taxonomy-guided prompting (explicitly instructing the model to include the behaviors), and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human dialogues. Generation granularity is varied (1, 5, or 30 turns per LLM call). Frequency distributions of behaviors from LLMs are compared to human distributions to quantify misalignment.

## Key Results
- Vanilla-prompted LLMs (GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, Claude Opus 4) produce far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than humans across all tested domains.
- Taxonomy-guided prompting yields unstable control: with 1-turn generation it causes massive overproduction of many behaviors; with 30-turn generation it closely mirrors vanilla prompting (suppression).
- Supervised fine-tuning on human conversations disproportionately increases repetition and redundant information without diversifying other behaviors.
- Turn-level analysis uncovers distributional mismatches that aggregate conversation-level scores (1–10) fail to capture.
- Generating multiple turns in a single LLM call dramatically reduces the occurrence of these behaviors, even when explicit instructions are provided.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Vanilla prompting suppresses inconsistent/uncollaborative behaviors because LLMs default to overly cooperative, conflict-averse generation.
- **Mechanism:** Base LLMs are fine-tuned for helpfulness and harm reduction, which penalizes generating contradictions, misunderstandings, or disagreements that could be perceived as unconstructive. This optimization bias leads to smoother, more consistent dialogues than humans produce.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs' default safety/helpfulness tuning inherently discourages the simulation of socially "negative" micro-behaviors.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] under vanilla prompting, LLM-simulated conversations exhibit far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors; [section 5.1] Table 3 and Figure 4b show near-zero rates for most behavior types under vanilla prompting.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Taxonomy-guided prompt engineering fails to reliably control behavior frequencies because LLMs struggle to balance explicit behavioral instructions with maintaining conversational coherence and persona consistency.
- **Mechanism:** The instruction to "incorporate" specific behaviors at human-like frequencies conflicts with the model's internal objectives to produce fluent, goal-oriented dialogue. This leads to unstable outcomes: either the model ignores the instruction (if generating many turns per call) or overproduces behaviors by treating them as mandatory targets (if generating one turn at a time), disrupting natural flow.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs cannot integratively modulate micro-behavior frequencies without compromising macro-level conversational quality.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] prompt engineering does not provide reliable control; [section 5.2] Figure 4e shows massive overproduction with 1-turn generation; Table 3 shows low consistency/collaborativeness scores.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human conversations causes overproduction of a narrow subset of behaviors (like repetition) because the model overfits to superficial, frequent patterns in the data without learning the underlying distributional diversity.
- **Mechanism:** Human conversations contain rare but diverse "negative" behaviors. SFT on such sparse data can amplify the most statistically common failure modes (e.g., repetitive agreement tokens) while failing to capture rarer behaviors (e.g., interruptions, factual inaccuracies) that require contextual understanding. The model learns to emit high-frequency error tokens rather than simulating realistic error dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** SFT on human conversation logs does not provide a sufficiently diverse or balanced supervision signal for behavior distribution; it amplifies mode-like artifacts.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] SFT can lead LLMs to overproduce a narrow set of behaviors, such as repetition; [section 5.3] Figure 4g shows massive spikes in "Repetition" and "Redundant Information" for SFT models.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Turn-level vs. Conversation-level Evaluation**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper shows coarse-grained scores (1-10 consistency) mask major distributional mismatches in micro-behaviors. Understanding this distinction is key to interpreting CoCoEval's findings.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might a conversation receive a high consistency score even if it contains many subtle misunderstandings or repetitions?

- **Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge Reliability**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire evaluation framework relies on an LLM (o4-mini) to detect behaviors. Low agreement with humans (e.g., kappa ~0.25) means results reflect the judge's biases, not ground truth.
  - **Quick check question:** What are the risks of using a low-agreement LLM-as-a-Judge to claim one model generates "fewer" behaviors than another?

- **Concept: Conversation Simulation Setups (Turn-by-Turn vs. Multi-Turn Generation)**
  - **Why needed here:** A major finding is that generating 30 turns in one call dramatically suppresses behaviors compared to generating 1 turn per call. This is a critical methodological variable.
  - **Quick check question:** How might generating multiple turns in one forward pass affect the model's ability to simulate turn-level "mistakes"?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
COCOEVALBENCH -> Simulation Engine -> LLM-as-a-Judge -> Analysis Pipeline

**Critical path:**  
Load benchmark instance (M, S, x); generate continuation y under simulation setting; run LLM-as-a-Judge on (x, y) to get scores and behavior counts; aggregate across 300 instances; compare LLM distributions to human reference distributions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LLM-as-a-Judge vs. Human Eval: o4-mini scales but introduces low agreement (Cohen’s κ ~0.25); human eval is ground-truth but impossible at scale.  
- Fine-grained vs. Coarse-grained: Turn-level detection provides interpretability but suffers from lower reliability; conversation-level scores are smoother but can mask mismatches.  
- Generation Granularity: 1-turn/call better reflects human turn-taking but is computationally expensive and may overproduce behaviors; 30-turn/call is efficient but suppresses behaviors artificially.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Judge misalignment: o4-mini’s fine-grained counts correlate poorly with human counts (Table 7), making frequency comparisons suspect.  
2. Prompt sensitivity: Taxonomy-guided prompt causes overproduction with 1-turn generation but suppression with 30-turn generation (Figure 4d,e), indicating instability.  
3. SFT collapse: SFT models show massive spikes in "Repetition" (Figure 4g) without corresponding increases in other behaviors, indicating overfitting to superficial patterns.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce vanilla prompting baseline: Generate 30-turn continuations with base GPT-4.1 using vanilla prompt. Verify that fine-grained behavior counts are significantly lower than human references (as in Figure 4b).  
2. Test prompt engineering instability: Run taxonomy-guided prompt with both 1-turn and 30-turn generation settings. Observe that 1-turn yields high counts (overproduction) while 30-turn yields counts similar to vanilla (suppression).  
3. Validate SFT artifact: Fine-tune GPT-4.1 on the provided training set (1,450 conversations). Generate 1-turn continuations. Check for disproportionate increase in "Repetition" and "Redundant Information" versus other categories.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why do vanilla-prompted LLMs generate far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than human conversations?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] RQ1 results show LLMs under vanilla prompting exhibit far fewer behaviors.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not probe underlying causes like training data biases or decoding constraints.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies on model training objectives, analysis of output distributions for atypical dialogue suppression.

- **Open Question 2:** Why does taxonomy-guided prompting often overproduce inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors instead of matching human frequencies?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] RQ2 demonstrates overproduction when explicitly instructing to include these behaviors.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper notes sensitivity but doesn't explain why prompts backfire—possible misinterpretation or overgeneralization.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Prompt variation experiments, calibration of instruction adherence versus naturalness, human evaluation of authenticity.

- **Open Question 3:** Why does SFT on human conversations overproduce repetition and redundancy specifically, without diversifying other behaviors?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] RQ3 and Figure 4g show SFT increases repetition and redundancy.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper hypothesizes unnatural repetition but doesn't investigate why SFT amplifies these patterns—data artifacts or loss function biases?  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Analysis of SFT data for repetitive patterns, comparison of loss gradients for different behaviors, regularization to reduce overfitting.

- **Open Question 4:** How does generating multiple turns per call suppress inconsistent behaviors, and why does this override taxonomy-guided prompting?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] RQ4 finds that generating 30 turns per call suppresses behaviors, even with explicit instructions.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper observes the effect but doesn't mechanistically explain it—context aggregation or reduced per-turn uncertainty?  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments varying context length, measuring turn-level diversity, probing how multi-turn generation affects conversational dynamics.

- **Open Question 5:** To what extent do the 10 behavior types cover all inconsistent behaviors in diverse human conversations, and are domain-specific types missing?  
  *Basis in paper:* [inferred] Limitations note dataset skew toward governmental meetings and no extra behaviors found in studied datasets, but scope is limited.  
  *Why unresolved:* Domain bias may miss behaviors from other contexts (e.g., casual chats), and taxonomy might be incomplete.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Annotation across varied genres, expert elicitation for missing categories, cross-dataset validation of behavior frequencies.

## Limitations
- Reliance on o4-mini as judge with low human agreement (kappa ~0.25), risking measurement bias.
- Benchmark covers only 300 continuations across four domains; may not generalize to other contexts.
- Simulation settings limited to a few models (GPT-4.1/5.1, Claude) and generation granularities; does not explore temperature, top-p, or newer models.

## Confidence
- Vanilla LLMs under-produce inconsistent/uncollaborative behaviors: **High**
- Prompt engineering yields unstable control: **Medium**
- SFT on human dialogues overproduces specific behaviors (e.g., repetition): **Medium**
- Generating multiple turns per call suppresses these behaviors: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Conduct human annotation on a random subset of turns to assess true agreement with o4-mini; adjust scores if necessary.
2. Replicate the granularity experiment with a diverse model set (including open-source LLMs) and varying prompt styles to confirm robustness of the suppression effect.
3. Implement a reweighted SFT objective that penalizes deviation from human behavior distributions and evaluate whether it yields a more realistic mix of micro-behaviors.
