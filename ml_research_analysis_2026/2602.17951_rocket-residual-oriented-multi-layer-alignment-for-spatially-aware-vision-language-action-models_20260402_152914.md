# ROCKET: Residual-Oriented Multi-Layer Alignment for Spatially-Aware Vision-Language-Action Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17951
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17951
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves 98.5% success on LIBERO with ~4% of the training compute of prior SOTA by aligning a 2D-pretrained VLA with a 3D teacher via a shared projector and Matryoshka activation.

## Executive Summary
ROCKET injects 3D spatial reasoning into 2D-pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by aligning multiple intermediate layers of the student VLA with a frozen 3D vision foundation model (teacher). It introduces two key innovations: a **shared projector** that maps all aligned student layers to teacher layers to avoid destructive gradient interference, and a **Matryoshka-style sparse activation** that balances supervision across depths by progressively increasing the active parameters for deeper layers. On the LIBERO robotic manipulation benchmark, ROCKET matches state-of-the-art success rates while drastically reducing training compute. It shows strong robustness on LIBERO-Plus and generalizes across different VLA backbones (OpenVLA, PI0.5) and datasets (RoboTwin).

## Method Summary
ROCKET performs multi-layer representation alignment between a student VLA and a frozen 3D teacher model (e.g., VGGT). It selects a set of corresponding layers from both models (e.g., 10 layers from the student’s LLM backbone and the teacher’s visual backbone). Instead of using independent projectors per layer, a single **shared two-layer MLP projector** transforms all student layer outputs. To prevent shallow layers from dominating training, a **Matryoshka activation** scheme is applied: for each aligned student layer *i*, only a fraction ρᵢ of the projector’s hidden units are activated, with ρ increasing linearly from shallow to deep layers (e.g., ρ_min=0.2, ρ_max=1.0). The alignment loss (1−cosine similarity) is computed between projected student tokens and teacher features, averaged over tokens and layers, and combined with the standard action prediction loss. The total loss L_total = L_action + λ · L_align is used to update the student VLA and the shared projector while keeping the teacher frozen.

## Key Results
- Achieves **98.5%** average success rate on LIBERO, matching prior SOTA while using only **~4%** of the training compute.
- Demonstrates **superior robustness** on LIBERO-Plus, which contains spatial distractors.
- Generalizes effectively across **multiple VLA backbones** (OpenVLA-7B, PI0.5) and **datasets** (RoboTwin), and ablations confirm the critical role of the shared projector and Matryoshka activation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gradient Interference in Naive Multi-Layer Alignment
- **Claim:** Naive multi-layer alignment with independent projectors causes severe gradient interference, leading to performance collapse (~80% on LIBERO vs. ~98% with ROCKET).
- **Mechanism:** In Pre-LN Transformers, gradients at early layers are a superposition of alignment gradients from all deeper aligned layers. Independent projectors produce nearly orthogonal Jacobian interactions across layers, causing destructive interference in this superposition.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and section 3.2 derive gradient superposition; Fig. 1 shows low cross-layer gradient cosine similarity with independent projectors; Table 8 shows dramatic performance drop.

### Mechanism 2: Shared Projector Enforces Gradient Coherence
- **Claim:** A single shared projector across all aligned layers makes cross-layer interference constructive (or less destructive) by introducing a common positive semidefinite structure.
- **Mechanism:** Shared parameters cause the interaction matrix between layer gradients to decompose into a common PSD reference (J J⊤) plus bounded deviations, yielding a lower bound on inner products when teacher errors are correlated.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.3 presents Theorem H.1; Fig. 1 shows higher gradient cosine similarity with shared projector; consistent performance recovery across ablations (Table 8).

### Mechanism 3: Matryoshka Activation Balances Depth Supervision
- **Claim:** Matryoshka-style sparse activation prevents shallow layers from dominating shared projector updates, enabling effective alignment across depths.
- **Mechanism:** Shallow layers align more easily (higher CKA similarity). Matryoshka progressively increases activated projector capacity from shallow to deep layers: shallow layers use fewer parameters, reducing their influence; deep layers use full capacity, receiving stronger gradients.
- **Evidence:** Section 4.3 introduces the linear width schedule; Appendix F shows CKA similarity decreasing with depth; Table 6 shows Matryoshka improves from 98.2% to 98.5%.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Student VLA (OpenVLA) -> Teacher 3D model (VGGT, frozen) -> Shared Projector (2-layer MLP with Matryoshka activation) -> Alignment Loss (1−cos) + Action Loss (L1/CE) -> Total Loss -> Update Student + Projector
- **Critical path:**
  1. Input image/instruction passes through student VLA and teacher 3D model.
  2. Extract visual tokens from selected student LLM layers and teacher layers.
  3. For each student layer, apply shared projector with depth-specific activation mask (Matryoshka).
  4. Compute per-layer alignment loss (1−cosine) and average.
  5. Compute action loss from student’s final output.
  6. Backpropagate total loss (L_action + λ·L_align) through student and projector; teacher frozen.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Shared vs. independent projectors: shared reduces interference but may limit layer-specific adaptation.
  - Number of aligned layers: more layers capture hierarchical cues but increase interference risk; optimal ~8–16.
  - Activation schedule: linear ρ increase is simple; could be learned or non-linear.
  - Layer selection: uniform sampling is robust; similarity-based may adapt but costs extra compute.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Independent projectors cause collapse to ~80% success (Table 8).
  - Without Matryoshka, shallow layers dominate, slightly reducing gains (98.2% vs. 98.5%).
  - Mis-tuned λ: too high harms action learning; too low weakens alignment.
  - Picking only very deep layers may align poorly due to low CKA similarity (Fig. 8).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Ablate independent projectors: replace shared projector with per-layer projectors; expect performance drop (Table 8, "Multi-layer" row).
  2. Ablate Matryoshka: use shared projector with full activation for all layers; expect small decrease (Table 8, "Shared" vs. "Shared+Matryoshka").
  3. Layer selection study: compare uniform, depth-based, and similarity-based strategies (Table 5) to establish default (E2M-Last1).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalizability to other teacher-student pairs:** To what extent can ROCKET generalize to alternative 3D vision foundation models (e.g., Depth Anything, π3) as teachers, and beyond VLA students (e.g., GPT-based VLMs)? Basis: Paper only evaluates VGGT as teacher and OpenVLA/PI0.5 as students on simulated benchmarks. Why unresolved: Different feature spaces may alter gradient interference dynamics. Evidence needed: Experiments with multiple teachers/students, reporting success rates, gradient coherence metrics.
- **Theoretical assumptions under large-scale training:** Does the shared projector’s advantage hold under realistic conditions, or only under restrictive residual-smallness and near-isometry assumptions? Basis: Analysis in Appendix H assumes small residual increments and near-isometry on error-signal subspace. Why unresolved: Assumptions may be violated in practice (large gradients, non-stationary errors). Evidence needed: Empirical validation of Jacobian similarity, gradient cosine similarity, residual norms; perturbation tests that break assumptions.
- **Automatic determination of alignment depth and schedule:** How can optimal number of aligned layers and Matryoshka schedule be determined automatically? Basis: Paper selects N=10 and fixed linear ρ schedule empirically. Why unresolved: Manual tuning is costly and may not transfer. Evidence needed: Adaptive algorithm (e.g., hyperparameter optimization) that dynamically selects N and ρ, demonstrating consistent performance across backbones.
- **Sim-to-real transfer:** Will spatial gains translate to real-world robotics? Basis: All experiments in simulation; no real-robot results. Why unresolved: Real-world domain shifts (sensor noise, dynamics) may degrade policies. Evidence needed: Deployment on physical robots (e.g., WidowX) performing analogous tasks, measuring success rates and failure analysis.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (layer indices, projector dimension *m*, LoRA usage, VGGT variant) are not fully specified, requiring inference from code.
- Theoretical analysis is tailored to Pre-LN Transformers and may not hold for all VLA backbones (e.g., Post-LN).
- Evaluation is confined to simulated robotics tasks; real-world applicability remains unproven.
- Matryoshka activation yields only modest gains (98.2% → 98.5%), suggesting possible sensitivity or overfitting.

## Confidence
- **Gradient interference in naive alignment:** High – Strong theoretical argument, empirical gradient similarity, drastic performance drop.
- **Shared projector enforces gradient coherence:** High – Theorem support, improved gradient similarity, consistent ablation results.
- **Matryoshka activation balances depth supervision:** Medium – Ablation shows small positive effect; assumption (shallow dominance) needs direct validation (e.g., per-layer gradient norms).
- **Overall performance & efficiency:** Medium – Results compelling but rely on specific hyperparameters; compute savings may vary.
- **Generalization across backbones/datasets:** Medium – Tested on two VLAs and one additional dataset; broader validation needed.

## Next Checks
1. **Gradient interference quantification:** For independent projectors, compute pairwise gradient cosine similarity per layer and correlate with final success rate over seeds; expect strong negative correlation.
2. **Shared projector on non-Pre-LN architectures:** Implement ROCKET with a GPT-2-based VLA (Post-LN) and evaluate on LIBERO; if benefits persist, theoretical assumptions require refinement.
3. **Matryoshka schedule robustness:** Systematically vary ρ_min, ρ_max, and schedule type (linear, exponential, constant); track per-layer alignment losses and overall success to determine optimal schedule and sensitivity boundaries.
