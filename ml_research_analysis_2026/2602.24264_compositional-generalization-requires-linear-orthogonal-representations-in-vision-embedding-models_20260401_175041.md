# Compositional Generalization Requires Linear, Orthogonal Representations in Vision Embedding Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24264  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24264  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Vision embedding models achieve compositional generalization when representations are linearly factorized into orthogonal per-concept components, with a minimum embedding dimension \(d \ge k\); empirical results on CLIP, SigLIP, DINO show partial linearity (\(R^2 \approx 0.4–0.6\)) and cross‑concept orthogonality that correlate with accuracy on novel combinations.

## Executive Summary
Compositional generalization—recognizing familiar concepts in novel combinations—remains a key challenge for vision models despite large‑scale training. This paper formalizes three desiderata: divisibility (ability to represent all combinations), transferability (performance on unseen combos), and stability (consistent readouts across any two valid training supports). Using gradient descent with cross‑entropy loss, they prove that satisfying these forces the embedding to be a sum of per‑concept vectors that are orthogonal across concepts, and derive a lower bound \(d \ge k\) on embedding dimension, where \(k\) is the number of concepts.

Empirically, the authors recover per‑concept factors from state‑of‑the‑art vision models (CLIP, SigLIP, DINO) on compositional datasets (dSprites, MPI3D, PUG‑Animal). They measure linearity via whitened projected \(R^2\) and orthogonality via cosine similarities. Results show partial linear factorization (\(R^2 \approx 0.4–0.6\)), near‑orthogonal cross‑concept factors (average absolute cosine ≈ 0.09–0.12), and non‑orthogonal within‑concept factors (≈ 0.53–0.55). The extent of linearity correlates positively with compositional accuracy on held‑out combinations. Factors are low‑dimensional (≤ 4 effective dimensions). The theoretical bound \(d \ge k\) holds, though binary cross‑entropy often requires ≈ 2\(k\) dimensions. These findings suggest modern models approximate the necessary conditions, explaining their partial success on compositional benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The authors combine theoretical analysis with empirical factor recovery. Theoretically, they define divisibility, transferability, and stability and investigate the implicit bias of gradient descent with binary cross‑entropy. For binary concepts, they show that satisfying the desiderata forces a linear additive factorization \(z(x) = \sum_i u_{i,c_i}\), with the difference vectors between concept values orthogonal across different concepts. A minimum embedding dimension \(d \ge k\) is derived from hyperplane‑region counting; for multi‑valued concepts a similar bound follows by reduction to binary subproblems.

Empirically, they analyze pretrained vision models on several compositional datasets. Per‑concept factors \(u_{i,j}\) are obtained by averaging embeddings over all tuples with concept \(i\) equal to \(j\). Linearity is assessed by training linear probes on the full dataset, projecting embeddings onto the probe span, whitening, and computing the \(R^2\) of the additive model against the summed factors. Orthogonality is measured by the mean absolute cosine between centered, normalized difference vectors of different concepts. Factor dimensionality is estimated via effective rank. Finally, they split combinations into training and test sets, train separate linear classifiers per concept, and compute average per‑concept accuracy on held‑out combos; correlating this compositional accuracy with the linearity metric yields a positive relationship across models and checkpoints.

## Key Results
- Partial linear factorization (projected \(R^2 \approx 0.4–0.6\)) and nearly orthogonal cross‑concept factors (average absolute cosine ≈ 0.09–0.12) are observed in CLIP, SigLIP, DINO; within‑concept factors remain non‑orthogonal (≈ 0.53–0.55).
- Factor dimensions are low (effective rank ≤ 4), and the theoretical bound \(d \ge k\) holds, though binary cross‑entropy often requires about \(2k\) dimensions.
- The degree of linearity positively correlates with compositional accuracy on unseen concept combinations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Stability + gradient descent with BCE forces additive linear factorization of embeddings \(z(x) = \sum_i u_{i,c_i}\).  
**Mechanism:** Stability means readouts are identical across any two valid training supports. For binary concepts, consider cross‑datasets that flip only one concept. Under GD+BCE, the solution converges to a max‑margin SVM. The support vectors become pairs differing exactly in that concept, making the difference vector \(u_{i,1} - u_{i,0}\) constant across the support, yielding an additive decomposition.  
**Core assumptions:** Linear readout, binary cross‑entropy loss, exact stability, and a validity class containing at least one counterfactual pair per concept (e.g., \(|T| = k+1\)). Concepts are binary.  
**Evidence anchors:** Proposition 1 and its proof (Section 4.1) establish the SVM geometry and invariance; the abstract mentions linear factorability; related work (e.g., “Linear Spaces of Meanings”) reports additive structures in VLMs.  
**Break condition:** The guarantee fails if stability is violated, if the loss differs from BCE, or if concepts are non‑binary.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Divisibility (ability to represent all \(n^k\) combinations) forces a minimum dimension \(d \ge k\).  
**Mechanism:** Each concept \(i\) requires \(n\) affine hyperplanes to separate its \(n\) values. The total of \(kn\) hyperplanes can carve at most \(\sum_{r=0}^d \binom{kn}{r}\) regions in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (Zaslavsky). To accommodate \(n^k\) distinct combinations, we must have \(d \ge k\). For \(n > 2\), reduction to binary subproblems preserves the bound.  
**Core assumptions:** Linear readout (hyperplanes), hyperplanes in general position, full separation of every combination.  
**Evidence anchors:** Proposition 3 (Section 4.3) proves the bound via region counting; the abstract states \(d \ge k\); related kernel theory work (“When does compositional structure yield compositional generalization?”) discusses packing limits.  
**Break condition:** The bound can be circumvented if the readout is nonlinear, if not all combinations are required (some excluded by validity class), or if hyperplanes are degenerate (e.g., many parallel).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Max‑margin geometry combined with factorization implies orthogonality of cross‑concept difference vectors.  
**Mechanism:** For each concept \(i\), the max‑margin separator’s normal \(w_i\) aligns with the shortest segment joining the positive and negative convex hulls. Stability ensures these segments correspond to pure‑concept flips, i.e., \(u_{i,1} - u_{i,0}\). Orthogonality \(w_i^\top w_j = 0\) for \(i \neq j\) then follows from the additive structure and the fact that the SVM margin‑maximizing solution makes these directions mutually perpendicular.  
**Core assumptions:** Additive factorization, binary concepts, max‑margin solution (arising from BCE), and support vectors that are pure counterfactuals.  
**Evidence anchors:** The orthogonality part of Proposition 1 (Section 4.1); the abstract notes cross‑concept orthogonality; work on reasoning‑augmented representations links failures to lack of orthogonal features.  
**Break condition:** Orthogonality may weaken if data noise obscures pure directions, if stability is only approximate, or if the optimizer does not reach the true max‑margin (e.g., due to strong regularization).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Linear factorization**  
  *Why needed:* Core representation structure; embeddings decompose as sum of per‑concept vectors.  
  *Quick check:* For a two‑concept grid, can you write \(z_{c1,c2} \approx u_{1,c1} + u_{2,c2}\) with small error?  

- **Concept: Max‑margin separator**  
  *Why needed:* GD+BCE converges to it; its geometry (shortest segment between convex hulls) gives additive shifts.  
  *Quick check:* What is the geometric characterization of the maximum‑margin hyperplane for two separable point sets? (Orthogonal to the minimal‑distance segment.)  

- **Concept: Hyperplane arrangements**  
  *Why needed:* Derive \(d \ge k\) from divisibility via region counting.  
  *Quick check:* In \(\mathbb{R}^2\), what’s the maximum number of regions from 3 affine lines? (7; in general \(\sum_{r=0}^d \binom{m}{r}\)).  

- **Concept: Orthogonal subspaces**  
  *Why needed:* Cross‑concept orthogonality prevents interference and is predicted by theory.  
  *Quick check:* If \(v \perp w\) and \(v \perp u\), does that imply \(w \perp u\)? (No; orthogonality is not transitive.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
- Encoder \(f\) → embedding \(z\)  
- Readout \(h\) (linear probes or text encoder weights) → per‑concept logits  
- Training data \(D_T\) from validity class \(T\)  
- Learning \(A\) (e.g., SGD) minimizing BCE per concept  
- Desiderata: Divisibility, Transferability, Stability  

**Critical path**  
1. Use a validity class that contains at least one counterfactual pair per concept (e.g., cross‑dataset size \(k+1\) or random with \(\ge 2^{k-1}+1\) samples).  
2. Train with binary cross‑entropy; GD → max‑margin SVM.  
3. Stability implies the same separator appears for any \(T\); therefore support vectors are pairs differing only in that concept.  
4. These pairs give constant difference vectors → additive decomposition \(z \approx \sum_i u_{i,c_i}\).  
5. Minimal‑segment geometry across concepts yields orthogonal directions.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- BCE vs. CE: BCE typically needs ~2\(k\) dimensions; CE can approach \(d \approx k\).  
- Euclidean vs. spherical: Spherical adds ~1 dimension but preserves same structural constraints.  
- Binary theory only for binary concepts; multi‑valued case relies on approximate factorization; one‑hot encoding inflates effective \(k\).

**Failure signatures**  
- Projected \(R^2 \ll 0.4\) → factorization absent.  
- Cross‑concept cosine \(> 0.3\) → orthogonality missing.  
- Effective rank per concept \(> \min(d, n)\) → inefficient packing; high‑rank within‑concept factors may indicate entangled attributes.  
- \(d < k\) → divisibility impossible; zero‑shot on rare combos fails.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Synthetic validation:** Train from scratch on a binary concept dataset with BCE; check perfect \(R^2=1\) and zero cross‑concept cosine.  
2. **Factor recovery:** For a pretrained model on dSprites, compute per‑concept averages, report whitened projected \(R^2\) and orthogonality heatmap (like Fig. 24).  
3. **Correlation test:** Split concept tuples into 90%/10% train/test; train linear probes, compute projected \(R^2\) on train set and compositional accuracy on test set; verify positive correlation across models/checkpoints.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
How can the stability desideratum be relaxed to approximate stability while preserving the link to compositional generalization?  
*Basis:* The authors state that relaxing worst‑case stability to an average‑case or approximate version is a natural direction for future work.  
*Why unresolved:* The current theory requires identical per‑concept posteriors across any two valid supports, an idealization; practical models only approximate stability, so a weakened theory is needed.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Formalize approximate stability (e.g., small divergence thresholds), prove necessity/sufficiency analogs, and empirically test whether models achieve approximate stability and whether it still predicts compositional accuracy.

**Open Question 2**  
How do different choices of training support (validity class \(T\)) shape the representational geometry predicted by the theory?  
*Basis:* The authors note that characterizing how different training supports change the implied geometry could turn necessary conditions into practical design guides.  
*Why unresolved:* The theory currently abstracts over the set of supports; real‑world data collection strategies (e.g., coverage of marginals, prevalence of certain compositions) may lead to different geometric constraints.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct controlled experiments varying support characteristics (e.g., balance, joint vs. marginal coverage) on synthetic and real datasets; measure linearity and orthogonality; and develop theoretical results linking support distributions to the induced geometry.

**Open Question 3**  
What is the impact on the necessary conditions when the image encoder is trained jointly on a single dataset, as opposed to being fixed while only the readouts are retrained across supports?  
*Basis:* The authors remark that their theory assumes a fixed encoder and retraining across different supports, whereas in practice the encoder is trained once on a single dataset; this gap remains open.  
*Why unresolved:* Joint training may introduce inductive biases that alter the geometry in ways not captured by the fixed‑encoder analysis; the conditions could become more lenient or more stringent.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Compare geometry (linearity, orthogonality, dimension) of encoders trained via joint classification vs. those fine‑tuned under the fixed‑encoder regime; analyze gradient dynamics; attempt to derive joint‑training implicit bias results.

**Open Question 4**  
Do the necessary conditions of linear factorization and cross‑concept orthogonality hold for multi‑valued concept spaces (\(n > 2\)), not just binary?  
*Basis:* The core necessity proof (Proposition 1) is restricted to binary concepts; for multi‑valued datasets the authors simply adopt a natural extension without proof, leaving the generalization open.  
*Why unresolved:* The binary proof exploits symmetry between 0/1 labels; multi‑valued settings involve more than two classes, potentially requiring orthogonality across more difference vectors and additional constraints.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Extend the proof to arbitrary finite concept value sets; verify experimentally that models trained on multi‑valued data satisfy the generalized conditions; adjust the orthogonality definition if needed (e.g., pairwise differences for all value pairs must be orthogonal across concepts).

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (binary concepts, linear readouts, exact stability) are stricter than practical settings, leading to only partial linearity in real models.
- Empirical metrics (projected \(R^2\), orthogonality) are sensitive to implementation choices, and the positive correlation with compositional accuracy may be confounded by model capacity.
- The study is recent (low citation count) and shows moderate similarity to prior work, so independent replication is needed to confirm findings.

2. **Probe training standardization:** Fix train/test split (e.g., 90/10) and learning rate (single LR with early stopping on a validation slice of the training set). Re‑measure the correlation between projected \(R^2\) and compositional accuracy to rule out overfitting artifacts.
3. **Orthogonality robustness:** Recompute cross‑concept cosine under variations: (a) without centering factor vectors, (b) after removing global mean, (c) on raw vs. whitened embeddings. Report sensitivity to ensure the orthogonality finding is not an artifact of processing.
