# Emulating Aggregate Human Choice Behavior and Biases with GPT Conversational Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05597
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05597
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs replicate aggregate human status quo bias with high precision under neutral prompting but overestimate when explicitly instructed to be biased; individual prediction peaks at 68.5% without reliance on personal cues, and interaction with cognitive load is only superficially captured.

## Executive Summary
This study examines whether large language models (LLMs) can emulate human status quo bias in conversational decision-making under cognitive load. Human experiments (N=1100) confirmed the bias but found no significant increase from complex dialogue. LLMs (GPT-4.1, GPT-5) were simulated using participant demographics and dialogue transcripts with three human-likeness prompts. At the sample level, LLMs accurately reproduced aggregate bias (precision 1.00 for GPT-4.1 under minimal prompting) but overestimated bias when explicitly instructed (precision 0.67). Individual decision prediction peaked at 68.5% precision, yet perturbation tests revealed that LLMs do not rely on individual-specific cues, instead using general task patterns. GPT-4.1-mini best aligned with human behavior dynamics (lowest mean absolute difference), while GPT-5 models showed poorer alignment, especially in capturing cognitive load interactions.

## Method Summary
The study combined human experiments and LLM simulations. Human participants engaged in chatbot dialogues that culminated in a decision scenario framed with or without status quo bias, under either simple or complex (cognitively loading) dialogue conditions. For simulation, LLMs were prompted with anonymized participant demographics and verbatim prior dialogue transcripts, using three human-likeness instructions: minimal role assignment, natural response style, and explicit bias susceptibility. Models generated binary choices that were compared to human responses via metrics including Cohen's h for bias strength, sample-level precision (bias reproduction without false positives), mean absolute difference (MD) for interaction dynamics, and individual prediction precision. Ablation tests replaced or removed dialogue components (e.g., arithmetic, memory) to test reliance on contextual cues.

## Key Results
- **Sample-level bias reproduction:** GPT-4.1 achieved perfect precision (1.00) in mirroring aggregate status quo bias under neutral prompting (HL1/HL2), but precision dropped to 0.67 under explicit bias instruction (HL3) due to overestimation across scenarios.
- **Individual prediction limit:** Peak individual decision precision was 68.5% for GPT-4.1; perturbation tests (randomizing dialogue history) showed only minor degradation, indicating models ignored personal cues and relied on general patterns.
- **Interaction effect capture:** Only under HL3 did LLMs show a trend resembling human bias amplification under cognitive load, but this was confounded by overall bias exaggeration. HL1/HL2 failed to replicate the interaction, and GPT-5 models consistently showed higher MD, indicating poorer dynamic alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** LLMs reproduce aggregate human status quo bias by leveraging general task patterns rather than individual-specific cues.  
**Mechanism:** The model ingests the dialogue transcript and demographics, then pattern-matches on the decision scenario structure and status quo framing. High sample-level precision (e.g., 1.00 for GPT-4.1 minimal prompting) indicates reliance on learned statistical regularities from training data about how such scenarios are typically answered.  
**Core assumption:** The decision scenario text and framing contain invariant textual cues that trigger a biased response pattern mirroring human aggregate behavior, independent of specific user utterances.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract (precision 1.00 for GPT-4.1 minimal prompting), section 6.3.1 (perturbation tests show minimal precision drop), neighboring paper (arxiv:2601.11049) finds similar aggregate bias capture.  
**Break condition:** When dialogue history is perturbed (random text replacing human responses), individual prediction precision remains stable. If individual cues were necessary, perturbation would cause a large collapse.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** LLMs faithfully emulate the *magnitude* of human bias at sample level only under neutral/mildly human-like prompts (HL1/HL2), not under explicit bias instruction (HL3).  
**Mechanism:** HL1 ("You are a participant") and HL2 ("answer as naturally") preserve the model's default response distribution, which aligns with human bias magnitudes because it reflects common patterns in training data. HL3 ("act as a human... highly susceptible to biases") overrides this, causing exaggeration.  
**Core assumption:** LLMs possess an internal representation of "normal" human bias strength for classic scenarios, accessible under neutral role-play but superseded by direct bias-instruction.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract (HL3 precision 0.67), section 6.3.2 (HL1/HL2 precision 1.0, HL3 false positives), section 7.2 (overestimation linked to alignment overfitting).  
**Break condition:** HL3 prompts produce significantly inflated effect sizes (Cohen's h) versus human baselines (e.g., Budget Allocation HL3 h≈2.8 vs human h≈0.8), indicating invalid simulation.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** LLMs simulate the *interaction* between status quo bias and cognitive load only under HL3, but this is confounded by bias overestimation; other prompts fail to capture subtle human dynamics.  
**Mechanism:** HL3's emphasis on susceptibility and "avoid overthinking" may prompt the model to amplify bias under complex dialogue, superficially matching the human trend (e.g., College Jobs z-score +0.88 human, +3.32 HL3). HL1/HL2 lack this sensitivity, showing no adjustment.  
**Core assumption:** Capturing context-dependent bias modulation requires simulating reduced cognitive control (System 1 dominance), approximately invoked only by bias-instruction prompts.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 6.3.3 (HL3 MD lower, GPT-4.1-mini best MD), section 7.3 (HL1/HL2 poor match, HL3 alignment due to overestimation), neighboring work (EMNLP 2024) on task-specific bias expression.  
**Break condition:** MD remains high (>1.0) for HL1/HL2; even HL3's lower MD (~0.92) is invalidated by its high overesteration (precision 0.67).

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Status Quo Bias
**Why needed:** It is the target cognitive bias; understanding its definition (preference for the current/default option) and operationalization (framing one alternative as status quo) is essential to grasp what the LLM emulates.  
**Quick check:** In the College Jobs scenario, what textual manipulation creates the status quo condition?

### Concept: Cognitive Load Manipulation via Dialogue Complexity
**Why needed:** The study tests whether bias interacts with contextual cognitive load. The Complex Dialogue (nested referential structure, arithmetic, memory) is the operationalization; knowing this design is key to interpreting "interaction" results.  
**Quick check:** What features of the Complex Dialogue (e.g., in the Music Artist domain) increase working memory demands?

### Concept: Human-Likeness Prompting Levels (HL1–HL3)
**Why needed:** The prompting strategy is the primary independent variable; the levels (minimal role, natural response, explicit bias instruction) directly cause differences in mechanism activation (faithful reproduction vs. overestimation).  
**Quick check:** What is the critical difference between HL2 and HL3 prompts, and what outcome does it produce?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input Parser -> Prompt Constructor -> LLM Agent Wrapper -> Response Parser -> Comparator

**Critical path:** For each human participant simulation: Load participant data → Build HLX-specific prompt → Call LLM API → Parse choice → Compare to human response → Log metrics. A full run iterates this over all participants per model/prompt condition.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Precision vs. recall in individual prediction: High precision (e.g., 68.5%) comes with low recall for idiosyncratic choices; models predict central tendency well but miss personal variation.
- Sample-level accuracy vs. over-alignment: HL1/HL2 yield valid bias magnitudes but may miss context effects; HL3 captures context but via invalid exaggeration.
- Model size vs. fidelity: GPT-4.1-mini often outperformed GPT-5 in aligning with human dynamics, suggesting larger models may overfit pattern-matching or have different bias representations.

**Failure signatures:**
1. High MD in HL1/HL2 (>1.5): Model fails to replicate human interaction pattern (e.g., no bias change under complex dialogue).
2. Precision drop in HL3 (<0.8): Model overestimates bias, producing false positives (e.g., strong bias in Investment scenario where humans show none).
3. GPT-5 family consistently higher MD: Indicates poorer alignment with behavioral dynamics across scenarios.
4. Ablation: Removal of arithmetic/memory components leads to high MD (e.g., GPT-4.1 MD=4.957 with only decision problem), confirming prior dialogue necessity for interaction emulation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate Human Baseline (H1):** For each model/prompt, compute Cohen's h for status quo vs. neutral in Simple Dialogue. Expect LLM h positive and similar to human h (~0.7–0.8) under HL1/HL2.
2. **Test Prompt Sensitivity (H3):** Compare HL1, HL2, HL3 bias precision (Table 6). Expect HL1/HL2 precision=1.00 (no false positives), HL3 precision=0.67 (false positives in Investment).
3. **Ablation on Prior Dialogue Components:** Run conditions with (a) only decision scenario, (b) memory-only, (c) arithmetic-only. Expect high MD in (a) and (b), but lower MD for arithmetic-only (especially GPT-4.1), indicating arithmetic reasoning as key driver for interaction sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** How do cognitive biases beyond status quo bias (e.g., anchoring, framing, confirmation) manifest in conversational decision-making and interact with dialogue-induced cognitive load?  
*Basis:* Conclusion and limitations state further investigation is needed; findings should not generalize beyond status quo without targeted research.  
*Why unresolved:* Only status quo bias was tested; other biases' context interactions remain unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Parallel human and LLM experiments using tasks for anchoring, framing, and confirmation biases across simple/complex dialogues, quantifying bias strength and interactions.

**Open Question 2:** How do different dialogue strategies (e.g., open-ended vs. closed questions, conservative vs. liberal dialog style) influence cognitive load and expression of status quo bias?  
*Basis:* Limitations clarify the work does not characterize dialogue complexity sources; different strategies may contribute but analysis is beyond scope.  
*Why unresolved:* Fixed set of closed yes/no questions and one complex structure was used; broader design choices are untested.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically vary dialogue strategies in human and LLM experiments, measure perceived load (NASA-TLX, recall) and bias effect sizes, compare to baseline.

**Open Question 3:** Can physiological metrics (e.g., eye tracking, EEG, pupil dilation) provide a more real-time and objective assessment of cognitive load during chatbot-mediated decision-making than self-report and behavioral indicators?  
*Basis:* Limitations suggest incorporating physiological measures would offer more precise, dynamic load assessment.  
*Why unresolved:* Current study relied on NASA-TLX and recall/response-time, which are coarse and retrospective; physiological data could capture continuous mental effort.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Run experiments with concurrent physiological recording during simple/complex dialogues and decisions; correlate signals with load and bias susceptibility, validate against existing measures.

**Open Question 4:** Do large language models from other families (e.g., Claude, Gemini, Mistral) replicate the sample-level status quo bias and its interaction with dialogue complexity observed with GPT-4.1?  
*Basis:* Limitations note experiments exclusively used OpenAI models; approach is model-agnostic and extension to others is future work.  
*Why unresolved:* Findings might be GPT-specific; other architectures' alignment with human bias patterns is unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate agent simulation pipeline with state-of-the-art models from Anthropic, Google, Mistral using identical prompts and data; compare precision, MD, and effect-size dynamics to human baseline and GPT results.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt formatting, dialogue transcript structure, and response parsing methods are not fully disclosed, risking implementation drift.
- Model version identifiers ("GPT-4.1", "GPT-5") are ambiguous; reproducibility depends on exact OpenAI model versions.
- Findings are restricted to three specific decision scenarios and a single cognitive load manipulation; generalizability to other biases or tasks is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Aggregate bias reproduction:** High (consistent sample-level precision ~1.0 across scenarios/prompts)
- **Individual prediction limit:** Medium (68.5% peak precision, but perturbation suggests no individual cue use; confidence reduced by unknown parsing accuracy)
- **Prompt-sensitive overestimation:** High (HL3 precision drop to 0.67 and effect size inflation are robust)
- **Interaction effect capture:** Low (only HL3 showed alignment, confounded by overestimation; HL1/HL2 failed, MD remained high)
- **Model comparison (GPT-4.1-mini best):** Medium (based on MD metrics, limited to tested models, may not generalize)

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-model replication:** Run full simulation with at least two non-OpenAI LLMs (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 70B) using identical prompts and dataset to test if findings hold across architectures.
2. **Component-wise ablation:** Systematically remove or simplify each element of the complex dialogue (arithmetic, memory recall, nested references) and measure MD to isolate which cognitive load features are necessary for interaction emulation.
3. **Human validation of parsing:** Randomly sample 200 LLM responses, have human annotators map free-text to binary choices, compute agreement with automated parser; if disagreement >5%, revise parsing logic.
