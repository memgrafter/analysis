# Dissecting Performative Prediction: A Comprehensive Survey

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10176
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10176
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The survey systematically dissects performative prediction, introducing a classification of distribution maps and reviewing optimization methods for stability and optimality, highlighting key open challenges.

## Executive Summary
Performative prediction studies the feedback loop that arises when a deployed model influences the data distribution, causing a shift from the training to operational environment. This phenomenon occurs in high-stakes domains such as lending, policing, and recommendation systems, where human responses to model decisions alter the underlying population. The surveyed paper provides a comprehensive overview of this emerging field, synthesizing theoretical foundations, algorithmic approaches, and open problems.

The authors make several key contributions: they propose a novel taxonomy of distribution maps based on the level of information available (from black-box samples to full parametric models), review optimization algorithms targeting performative stability (e.g., repeated risk minimization) and performative optimality (e.g., performative gradient descent), and draw connections to related areas like adversarial attacks and algorithmic fairness. They also identify pressing challenges, including the lack of standardized benchmarks and the need for methods that work under partial observability of the distribution map. Their analysis offers a structured foundation for future research in performative prediction.

## Method Summary
The survey adopts a thematic and didactic approach to organize the rapidly growing literature on performative prediction. It introduces a classification framework for the distribution map D(θ) according to the degree of structural knowledge: Level 1 provides only black-box sampling access; Level 2 includes a known parametric form; and Level 3 assumes full mathematical specification. Within this framework, the paper reviews two principal optimization targets—performative stability (a fixed point of repeated risk minimization) and performative optimality (global minimum of performative risk)—and the corresponding algorithms (RRM, PerfGD). The authors also articulate the assumptions (strong convexity, smoothness, sensitivity) that underpin convergence guarantees and discuss how these methods relate to external concepts such as strategic classification and distributional robustness. The synthesis is supported by an extensive bibliography of 40 references, positioning the work as a central reference for the field.

## Key Results
- **Taxonomy of Distribution Maps:** A classification based on information access levels (black-box samples to full models) that clarifies the assumptions required by different algorithms.
- **Optimization Landscape:** A clear distinction between performative stability (achievable via repeated risk minimization under standard convexity assumptions) and performative optimality (requiring a differentiable, well-specified model of D(·) and methods like performative gradient descent).
- **Open Challenges:** The survey highlights the absence of standardized benchmarks and the need for methods that handle partial observability of the model's influence as critical gaps hindering progress.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: The Distribution Map as the Causal Engine
- **Claim:** The distribution map D(θ) is the mathematical object that captures how the deployment of a model with parameters θ changes the data distribution, creating the core feedback loop in performative prediction.
- **Mechanism:** After deploying a model with parameters θ, the environment shifts to the distribution D(θ). Subsequent models are then trained on this shifted distribution, potentially leading to suboptimal performance if not accounted for.
- **Core assumption:** The distribution map is static and depends solely on the current model parameters; the environment reaches equilibrium immediately after deployment.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 2.1 introduces D(·) as the mapping that changes the data conditional on the deployed classifier; Section 2.2 defines performative risk PR(θ) = E_{z~D(θ)}[ℓ(z;θ)], coupling the model in both predictor and data-generating processes. Neighboring papers (e.g., "Learning the Distribution Map in Reverse Causal Performative Prediction") study inference of D(·), confirming its centrality.
- **Break condition:** Fails if the environment depends on past deployments (stateful case) or on multiple models (multi-agent). Then D(θ) is insufficient; transition maps or joint distribution maps are required.

### Mechanism 2: Repeated Risk Minimization (RRM) for Stability
- **Claim:** RRM iteratively retrains on the induced distribution and converges to a performatively stable point under strong convexity, smoothness, and sensitivity conditions.
- **Mechanism:** Start with θ_0. For each t, collect samples from D(θ_t) and find θ_{t+1} = argmin_θ Risk(θ, D(θ_t)). This fixed-point iteration approaches a stable point θ_PS where Risk(θ_PS, D(θ_PS)) is minimized over θ.
- **Core assumption:** Loss ℓ is γ-strongly convex and β-jointly smooth; D(·) is ϵ-sensitive (Wasserstein-1 Lipschitz). Cheap sample access (level 1b) to D(θ) is available.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1.2 describes RRM as an iterative procedure that only needs to sample from the new distributions; Theorem 4.1 guarantees linear convergence if ϵ < γ/β; related work such as "Performative Prediction: Past and Future" provides detailed convergence proofs.
- **Break condition:** Divergence or oscillation occurs if ϵ ≥ γ/β or if ℓ is not strongly convex. For non-convex losses, convergence to a stable point is not guaranteed.

### Mechanism 3: Performative Gradient Descent (PerfGD) for Optimality
- **Claim:** PerfGD uses a gradient that accounts for the distribution shift to directly optimize the performative risk, achieving the performatively optimal point when a differentiable model of D(·) is accurate.
- **Mechanism:** Parameterize D(θ) via density p(z; f(θ)). The performative gradient is ∇_θ PR(θ) = E_{z~D(θ)}[ ∂ℓ/∂θ + ℓ ∂f/∂θ ∂log p/∂w |_{w=f(θ)} ]. This gradient is estimated from samples and used in gradient descent.
- **Core assumption:** The distribution map is differentiable in θ via a known function f(θ), and we can estimate f(θ) from data. Requires level 2b access (full probability density model).
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2.2 states the gradient can be used to optimize the performative risk itself; Equation 54 gives the explicit gradient formula; "Nonlinear Performative Prediction" extends gradient-based methods to nonlinear settings, highlighting the need for differentiable D(·).
- **Break condition:** If the model p(z; f(θ)) is misspecified, the gradient may be biased and optimization may not converge to the true optimal point. Also, if D(·) is non-differentiable (e.g., due to discrete decisions), gradient estimates may be unreliable.

## Foundational Learning

### Performative Risk
- **Why needed:** Central objective that quantifies expected loss under the distribution induced by the deployed model.
- **Quick check:** Verify that PR(θ) = E_{z~D(θ)}[ℓ(z;θ)] is well-defined and computable (in expectation) for candidate θ.

### Performative Stability
- **Why needed:** Target of iterative methods like repeated risk minimization; often approximates optimality and is easier to achieve.
- **Quick check:** A point θ_PS is stable if θ_PS = argmin_θ Risk(θ, D(θ_PS)), i.e., it minimizes risk under its own induced distribution.

### Performative Optimality
- **Why needed:** The ideal target that minimizes performative risk over all possible models.
- **Quick check:** Compute θ* = argmin_θ PR(θ). If a stable point coincides with θ*, then stability implies optimality.

### Distribution Map
- **Why needed:** Encapsulates how the deployed model changes the data distribution; the core causal object that creates the feedback loop.
- **Quick check:** Ensure D(θ) is measurable and can be either sampled from (Level 1) or modeled (Level 2/3) for relevant θ.

### Sensitivity Condition
- **Why needed:** Provides a Lipschitz bound on D(θ) in Wasserstein distance, crucial for convergence guarantees of RRM.
- **Quick check:** Estimate ϵ = sup_{θ≠θ'} W_1(D(θ), D(θ')) / ||θ-θ'||; must be finite and ideally satisfy ϵ < γ/β.

### Joint Smoothness
- **Why needed:** The loss's smoothness in both model parameters and data ensures the stability of the risk minimization subroutines.
- **Quick check:** Verify β such that ||∇_θ ℓ(θ,z) - ∇_θ ℓ(θ',z')|| ≤ β ||(θ,z)-(θ',z')|| for all relevant pairs.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Model (θ) -> Distribution Map D(θ) -> Data Distribution D(θ) -> Data Samples -> Loss ℓ(z;θ) -> Risk Estimate -> Optimizer (RRM or PerfGD) -> Updated Model (θ')

**Critical path:**
1. Deploy an initial model θ₀.
2. Collect data drawn from the induced distribution D(θ₀).
3. Solve a risk minimization problem: θ₁ = argmin_θ (1/n) Σ_{i=1}^n ℓ(z_i; θ) (RRM step) or perform a gradient step using the performative gradient (PerfGD).
4. Deploy θ₁ and repeat from step 2 until convergence to a stable point or until performative risk stops improving.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Stability vs Optimality:** Stable points may be suboptimal; achieving optimality requires an accurate, differentiable model of D(·) and gradient-based methods.
- **Sample efficiency vs accuracy:** RRM typically needs many samples per iteration to estimate risk well; PerfGD can use gradient estimates but requires a well-specified D(·).
- **Computational cost:** RRM repeatedly solves an empirical risk minimization, which can be expensive; PerfGD needs to compute gradients that involve the distribution model.
- **Information level:** With only black-box sampling (Level 1), PerfGD is infeasible, leaving RRM as the primary option; with full model access (Level 3), both methods are available.

**Failure signatures:**
- **RRM divergence/oscillation:** sensitivity ϵ too large relative to curvature (ϵ ≥ γ/β) or non-convex loss landscape.
- **PerfGD bias/poor convergence:** misspecification of the parametric model for D(·) or inaccurate gradient estimation.
- **High variance in risk estimates:** insufficient samples from the induced distribution leading to unstable updates.
- **No improvement in performative risk:** may indicate that the distribution shift is negligible or that the iteration scheme is inappropriate (e.g., using RRM when optimality is required but D(·) is smooth enough for PerfGD).

**First experiments:**
1. **Baseline convergence:** Implement RRM on a synthetic linear problem (e.g., linear regression with Gaussian distribution shift that depends linearly on θ). Verify linear convergence under the sensitivity condition and observe violation when ϵ is increased.
2. **Method comparison:** On the same synthetic task, compare RRM and PerfGD in terms of final performative risk, iteration count, and sample complexity. Vary the smoothness of D(·) to illustrate tradeoffs.
3. **Robustness to misspecification:** For PerfGD, use an incorrect functional form for D(·) (e.g., assuming linear shift when the true shift is quadratic) and measure the bias in the final solution relative to the true performatively optimal point.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the scope of performative prediction be formally restricted to scenarios where its optimization methods are genuinely necessary?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Is it possible to define the performative prediction setup such that it covers almost only those scenarios where the methods discussed in this paper are the most appropriate choice?"
- **Why unresolved:** The current formalism is extremely broad (e.g., capturing election forecasts), but many such settings likely have simpler, more appropriate solutions (e.g., pre-deployment auditing). A principled boundary is needed to guide practitioners.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A decision framework that classifies phenomena based on intervention feasibility, adversariality, and equilibrium properties, validated by domain experts across use cases.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can models be optimized efficiently when data collection is expensive (e.g., real-world deployment) or user adaptation spans years?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We know of no solution to this problem." concerning expensive sample access and gradual adaptation.
- **Why unresolved:** All provably convergent methods (e.g., repeated risk minimization) assume cheap, rapid feedback; real settings like lending involve slow human behavior change, making iterations prohibitively slow.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Algorithms with guaranteed suboptimality bounds under costly, limited feedback, demonstrated on semi-synthetic simulations of multi-year adaptation dynamics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does partial observability of the model by users (e.g., seeing only predictions, not parameters) alter the theoretical guarantees and practical design of performative systems?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Another neglected area seems to us the situation where the users only have an imprecise idea of what the deployed classifier is."
- **Why unresolved:** Theory assumes users react to exact θ; in reality they see outputs (e.g., route recommendations), potentially simplifying or complicating the distribution map's functional form.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of output-dependent distribution maps (e.g., via quantiles) yielding new sensitivity conditions and convergence guarantees, with validation on black-box manipulation experiments like traffic routing.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do performative optimization methods implicitly reconstruct base distributions or systematically downweight unstable features? How should robustness be measured?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Do they put less weight on unreliable features? Are they able to internally reconstruct the base features (in a setting belonging to the base-distribution family)?"
- **Why unresolved:** Prior work analyzes convergence rates but not the semantics of learned models; this limits our ability to judge whether algorithms achieve true robustness or mere empirical stability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic feature attribution studies (e.g., SHAP) comparing stabilized models' decision logic against ground-truth base feature importance across distribution map families in semi-synthetic data.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What standardized benchmarks can support reproducible research across the heterogeneity of performative prediction settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "the field suffers from a clear shortage of standardized benchmarks and more realistic datasets."
- **Why unresolved:** Current datasets are ad-hoc, often tied to strategic classification, and rarely provide the full distribution map model required for higher-level optimization methods; this hampers fair method comparison.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Open-source benchmark suites with multiple distribution map families (strategic, outcome performative, stateful transitions), documented access interfaces (samples vs. model), and reference implementations for common baselines adopted community-wide.

## Limitations
- The assumption that D(θ) is static and reaches immediate equilibrium ignores stateful dynamics and multi-agent interactions, limiting realism.
- RRM convergence relies on strong convexity and the sensitivity condition ϵ < γ/β; these are often violated by non-convex models (e.g., deep neural networks), so guarantees may not hold in practice.
- PerfGD requires a differentiable, accurately specified model of D(·); when D(·) is unknown or only accessible via sampling, gradient estimates become biased and optimization may fail.
