# RuCL: Stratified Rubric-Based Curriculum Learning for Multimodal Large Language Model Reasoning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21628  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21628  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** RuCL improves average accuracy by 7.83% over Qwen2.5-VL-7B on seven multimodal reasoning benchmarks, achieving 60.06% average accuracy through stratified rubric-based curriculum learning integrated with GRPO.

## Executive Summary
RuCL tackles reward hacking in multimodal LLM reasoning by replacing outcome-only reinforcement learning with a stratified rubric-based curriculum. It generates generalized rubrics (not per instance), stratifies them into foundational and advanced tiers using initial model pass rates, and dynamically weights them during training: starting with foundational rubrics until performance stabilizes, then gradually incorporating advanced ones. Coupled with GRPO, this curriculum guides the model from basic perception to complex logic. On seven benchmarks (MathVerse, WeMATH, LogicVista, Counting, etc.), RuCL yields a +7.83% average gain over the base Qwen2.5-VL-7B model. Ablations confirm that both stratification and the performance-triggered scheduling are essential to the improvement.

## Method Summary
RuCL consists of an offline rubric generation phase followed by online curriculum learning. First, a teacher LLM produces a set of generalized rubrics that assess reasoning quality (e.g., "are intermediate steps logically consistent?") without being tied to specific instances. These rubrics are then stratified: a small validation set determines the model’s initial pass rate for each rubric; rubrics with high pass rates become the foundational tier (R_easy), others become advanced (R_hard). During training, a scheduler dynamically weights the rubrics: initially only foundational rubrics contribute to the reward; once the model’s performance on them stabilizes (measured by a moving window), advanced rubrics are gradually introduced based on a linear schedule. The weighted sum of rubric scores serves as the reward for GRPO, which updates the policy. This structured curriculum prevents reward hacking by providing dense, multi-faceted feedback aligned with progressive skill acquisition.

## Key Results
- **+7.83%** average improvement over Qwen2.5-VL-7B on seven multimodal reasoning benchmarks, reaching **60.06%** average accuracy.
- **Strongest gains** on MathVerse, WeMATH, LogicVista, and Counting, demonstrating effectiveness on mathematically intensive and logical reasoning tasks.
- **Ablations** confirm that removing stratification or using a static (non-triggered) curriculum significantly degrades performance, underscoring the importance of dynamic, difficulty-aware scheduling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
RuCL mitigates reward hacking by replacing a single outcome-based reward with a multi-rubric reward signal that captures intermediate reasoning quality. The stratified curriculum ensures the model first masters foundational skills (e.g., perceptual grounding, simple arithmetic) before facing advanced challenges (e.g., multi-step deduction, ambiguous context). This smooth progression prevents the policy from exploiting spurious correlations in the outcome reward. The dynamic weighting schedule—triggered by stability in foundational performance—acts as a pacing mechanism, avoiding premature exposure to hard rubrics that could destabilize training. By integrating with GRPO, which efficiently optimizes policies under multiple reward components, RuCL aligns the model’s behavior with rubric-defined reasoning virtues, leading to more robust and generalizable multimodal reasoning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Curriculum Learning**  
  *Why needed:* Structures training from easy to hard, improving sample efficiency and preventing catastrophic forgetting of basic skills.  
  *Quick check:* Does performance on foundational rubrics plateau before advanced ones are introduced?  
- **Rubric-based Dense Reward**  
  *Why needed:* Outcome-only rewards are sparse and prone to hacking; rubrics provide interpretable, stepwise feedback on reasoning quality.  
  *Quick check:* Do rubric scores correlate with human judgments of reasoning correctness on a validation sample?  
- **Stratification by Difficulty**  
  *Why needed:* Separates skills into tiers to enable a smooth curriculum; hard tasks introduced too early can cause optimization instability.  
  *Quick check:* Is the initial pass rate a reasonable proxy for rubric difficulty throughout training?  
- **Dynamic Weight Scheduling**  
  *Why needed:* Controls the pace of curriculum progression, balancing exploration of new skills and exploitation of known ones.  
  *Quick check:* Does the linear ramp of advanced rubric weights correlate with reduced reward variance?  
- **GRPO for Multi-reward Optimization**  
  *Why needed:* GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) handles multiple reward components efficiently, suitable for the weighted rubric sum.  
  *Quick check:* Does GRPO with dynamic rubric weighting outperform a baseline using a simple weighted sum with PPO?  

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Teacher LLM → Generalized Rubrics → Rubric Categorizer (by initial pass rate) → Foundational/Advanced Rubric Sets → Curriculum Scheduler (dynamic weights) → Weighted Rubric Reward → GRPO Trainer → Policy Model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B)

**Critical path:**  
Policy generates a response → each rubric scores the response → scheduler computes weighted sum → GRPO update → next iteration. The scheduler’s weight adjustments are triggered by monitoring the moving average of foundational rubric scores.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Static vs. Dynamic difficulty:* Static stratification (using initial pass rates) simplifies scheduling and ensures stability but may become misaligned as the policy improves.  
- *Offline vs. Online rubric generation:* Offline generation (via teacher LLM) avoids training-time overhead but cannot adapt to the policy’s evolving weaknesses.  
- *Granularity of rubrics:* Too many rubrics increase computational cost; too few may not capture diverse reasoning skills.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Foundational rubric scores do not stabilize within the expected window → advanced rubrics introduced too early → training divergence or plateau.  
- Advanced rubric scores show no improvement after many steps → curriculum too conservative or rubrics poorly categorized.  
- High variance in rubric scores across batches → scheduler may weight unstable signals, causing noisy updates.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Ablate stratification:** Train with all rubrics from step 0 (no curriculum) and compare to full RuCL on stability and final accuracy.  
2. **Vary stability threshold:** Adjust the window size and improvement threshold for triggering advanced rubric inclusion; measure effect on convergence speed.  
3. **Rubric source sensitivity:** Replace the teacher LLM (e.g., GPT-4 → Claude) for rubric generation and evaluate performance consistency; test if rubric quality degrades with a weaker teacher.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can rubric construction be performed online during training to adapt to the model's evolving capabilities, and does this lead to improved efficiency and effectiveness compared to the static generalized rubrics used in RuCL?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Future work will explore online rubric construction and scaling to larger architectures." (Conclusion and Appendix I).
- **Why unresolved:** The current implementation relies on a one-time offline generation of rubric candidates using a teacher LLM, which does not adapt to the model's learning progress and may include irrelevant or miss important rubrics as the policy evolves. It also incurs reliance on a potentially expensive proprietary model.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An extension of RuCL that continuously generates or refines rubrics during RL training (e.g., by sampling failure cases and prompting a teacher or a self-critique mechanism) would need to be implemented. Performance, training stability, and computational cost of such an online system could then be compared against the static baseline across multiple benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does dynamically updating rubric difficulty estimates (e.g., pass rates) throughout training yield more stable and effective curriculum scheduling than the static stratification based on initial pass rates?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Second, to ensure stability, we employ a static stratification based on initial statistics, which simplifies the curriculum by assuming constant rubric difficulty throughout training. Future research could explore developing adaptive mechanisms to dynamically update rubric difficulties during the online phase." (Appendix I).
- **Why unresolved:** The present method uses only an initial assessment to categorize rubrics into foundational (Reasy) and advanced (Rhard) tiers. As the model improves, the true difficulty of a rubric may shift—a currently hard rubric could become easy and vice versa—rendering the static split suboptimal and potentially causing misaligned weighting.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A variant of RuCL that periodically (e.g., every few thousand steps) re-evaluates the model's pass rates on the rubric set and updates the tier assignments and/or the λ_t schedule accordingly would be required. Comparative experiments measuring convergence speed, final accuracy, and reward variance would determine whether adaptivity yields tangible benefits.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the stratified rubric curriculum approach scale effectively to larger model architectures (e.g., >7B parameters), and what modifications (e.g., to hyperparameters, curriculum speed, or integration with model scaling) might be necessary to sustain or improve performance gains?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Future work will explore online rubric construction and scaling to larger architectures." (Conclusion). All reported results are based on Qwen2.5-VL-7B, leaving the scalability of RuCL unexamined.
- **Why unresolved:** Larger models exhibit different optimization dynamics, greater capacity for reasoning, and higher computational demands for RL. The curriculum hyperparameters (α, λ_t growth function, window size w, threshold τth) that worked for the 7B model may not be optimal for larger ones. Additionally, the marginal benefit of fine-grained rubrics might diminish or require adjustment when scaling.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the exact RuCL training pipeline on larger base models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-32B or other open-source models) across the same seven benchmarks would be needed. The experiments should include systematic hyperparameter sweeps and perhaps analyses of training stability to reveal whether the observed +7.83% average improvement generalizes, and what changes are required to maintain effectiveness at scale.

## Limitations
- **Rubric generation opacity:** The process for deriving "generalized rubrics" without per-instance tuning is unclear; it is unknown how well they transfer across tasks or whether they miss critical reasoning patterns.
- **Static difficulty stratification:** The split into foundational/advanced tiers is based on a one-time initial pass-rate assessment, which may not reflect evolving difficulty as the policy improves, and the stability trigger is not quantitatively defined.
- **Benchmark-limited evaluation:** Results are confined to academic reasoning benchmarks; performance on real-world, noisy, or open-ended multimodal tasks (e.g., natural VQA) remains unknown, and reproducibility is unestablished (zero citations, moderate neighbor FMR).

## Confidence
- **Core performance claim (+7.83% avg improvement):** High (directly from paper’s tables, but experimental details not independently verified).  
- **Effectiveness of stratification & performance-triggered scheduling:** Medium (supported by ablations, but ablation scope and statistical significance are not fully reported).  
- **Generalization to "reward hacking" mitigation beyond tested benchmarks:** Low (claim extrapolates from specific benchmarks; no evidence on diverse distributions or model architectures).

## Next Checks
- **Independent reproduction on expanded benchmarks:** Re-run core experiments on additional multimodal reasoning suites (e.g., MMMU, MME-Pro) using the exact rubric generation code and GRPO integration details. Verify if the +7.83% gain replicates and stratify results by rubric tier.
- **Rubric content & coverage analysis:** Manually inspect a sample of the generated foundational vs. advanced rubrics. Quantify their granularity, overlap, and semantic alignment with ground-truth reasoning steps. Test if removing rubric tiering collapses performance.
- **Out-of-distribution robustness test:** Apply the trained RuCL model to held-out task distributions (e.g., natural science diagrams if trained on math/logic) without curriculum adjustment. Measure degradation vs. a non-curriculum GRPO baseline to isolate the benefit of the stratified schedule.
