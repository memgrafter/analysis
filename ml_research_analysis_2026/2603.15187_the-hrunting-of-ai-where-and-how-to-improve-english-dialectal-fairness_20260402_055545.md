# The Hrunting of AI: Where and How to Improve English Dialectal Fairness
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15187
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15187
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLM-as-judge performance is bounded by human-human agreement; low-resource dialects with ambiguous annotation (e.g., Cornish) yield poor judge accuracy and generation quality.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can be adapted fairly for low-resource English dialects (AAVE, Yorkshire, Geordie, Cornish) with West Frisian as a control. Through an end-to-end pipeline—including dialect-specific data generation via transcreation, human annotation with a six-criterion rubric, LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, and fine-tuning (SFT/DPO)—the authors uncover a persistent pattern: LLM-human agreement on generation quality directly mirrors human-human agreement. Dialects with high annotator consensus (e.g., AAVE, AC1=0.98) achieve high LLM judge performance and generative quality (>95%), while those with low consensus (Cornish, AC1=0.51) suffer drastically (40.4% high-quality outputs). Standard fine-tuning improves some metrics but does not break this pattern; DPO often degrades performance. The findings suggest that data scarcity and ambiguous dialectal features pose fundamental challenges to dialectal fairness, though certain LLMs can generate high-quality synthetic data for well-resourced dialects, offering a scalable path forward.

## Method Summary
The authors construct an evaluation pipeline for dialectal fairness. First, they transcreate prompts from existing datasets (WildChat, GSM8K, etc.) into five locales, adapting cultural references (e.g., measurement units). They then generate responses using multiple LLMs (GPT-4.1, Claude Opus 4.1, Qwen3 8B, etc.). Native speakers annotate each output against six binary criteria, yielding a quality label and computing inter-annotator agreement (Gwet’s AC1). To build LLM judges, they test five in-context learning (ICL) prompt strategies and fine-tune Qwen3 8B via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human labels or synthetic data, and via direct preference optimization (DPO). Finally, they correlate judge performance (accuracy, F1, AC1) with human-human agreement, dialect resourceness (speaker population), and linguistic distance (Levenshtein).

## Key Results
- LLM-as-judge performance (F1, AC1) is strongly predicted by human-human agreement on the same dialect; low AC1 (e.g., Cornish 0.51) leads to poor judge metrics.
- Task-specific data generation quality varies dramatically: AAVE and Yorkshire achieve ~96% high-quality outputs, while Cornish (40.4%) and Geordie (61%) lag far behind; West Frisian also low (28%).
- SFT improves LLM judge AC1 for most dialects but does not eliminate the high/low performance gap; DPO on Cornish degrades accuracy by 17.3% and AC1 by 0.27.
- Only dialects where the base LLM already generates high-quality TSD (AAVE, Yorkshire) benefit from synthetic data SFT; for low-quality dialects (Cornish), synthetic data harms performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Mechanism 1: LLM-as-judge performance is bounded by human-human agreement. Human annotations provide the training signal for both ICL and fine-tuning. Low human-human AC1 indicates ambiguous quality standards, producing noisy labels. LLMs learn a decision boundary reflecting this ambiguity, yielding similarly low AC1 with humans and poor discriminative metrics. This pattern persists because the underlying label noise cannot be resolved by simply scaling ICL shots or fine-tuning, unless human consensus reaches near-perfect levels (>0.9). Evidence: Per-locale LLM-human AC1 and F1 rank identically to human-human AC1 (AAVE highest, Cornish lowest); adjacent work on dialectal judge consistency corroborates.

Mechanism 2: Feasibility of generating high-quality TSD depends on dialect resourceness (speaker population, digital presence) and linguistic distinctness. LLMs pretrained on predominantly standard English and well-documented dialects internalize patterns for high-resource dialects (AAVE, Yorkshire), enabling prompt-driven generation of culturally and grammatically appropriate outputs. For low-resource dialects with subtle differences (Cornish, Geordie) or distinct languages (West Frisian), the model lacks clear exposure, leading to errors in locale, grammar, and cultural relevance. Resourceness correlates with generation quality; linguistic distance (Levenshtein) predicts quality only for distinct languages (West Frisian), not for closely-related dialects. Evidence: Generation quality gaps are stark (AAVE ~96% vs. Cornish ~40%); speaker estimates align with these gaps; Levenshtein distance significantly predicts West Frisian quality but not for English dialects.

Mechanism 3: Standard SFT can partially improve LLM-as-judge performance but cannot overcome the agreement pattern; DPO is ineffective and may degrade performance for low-resource dialects. SFT injects patterns from human-labeled demonstrations, effective only when the base model already possesses latent dialect knowledge (e.g., AAVE, Yorkshire). For dialects with low baseline generation quality, SFT’s ceiling is low. DPO optimizes against preference pairs and requires reliable preferences (high human agreement). When human agreement is low, preferences are noisy; DPO amplifies errors, causing performance drops. Synthetic data SFT helps only when the generator LLM already produces high-quality TSD; otherwise it introduces harmful noise. Evidence: Human-based SFT yields AC1 gains but maintains high/low gap; DPO on Cornish causes -17.3% accuracy; synthetic SFT helped Yorkshire and West Frisian but harmed Cornish.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Inter-rater reliability (Gwet’s AC1)  
  **Why needed here:** AC1 quantifies human consensus on TSD quality; the paper’s core finding is that LLM judge performance tracks this human-human agreement. Understanding AC1 is essential to interpret why some dialects are harder and whether LLM judges can be trusted.  
  **Quick check:** "If human annotators achieve only moderate AC1 (e.g., 0.51) on a dialect, what does that imply for training an LLM judge?" → It implies the quality concept is ambiguous, so the LLM will likely learn a noisy signal and achieve similarly low agreement.

- **Concept:** LLM-as-a-judge evaluation  
  **Why needed here:** This paradigm replaces costly human evaluation with automated judges; the paper investigates its reliability across dialects. Knowing its strengths and limitations is crucial for scaling dialectal fairness assessments.  
  **Quick check:** "Why might an LLM judge fail to align with human consensus on a low-resource dialect, even with many few-shot examples?" → Because its pretraining lacks consistent patterns for that dialect, and few-shot examples cannot fully compensate for missing knowledge.

- **Concept:** Task-specific data generation via transcreation  
  **Why needed here:** The pipeline starts by creating dialect-appropriate prompts and responses; transcreation ensures cultural and linguistic relevance, which is key to obtaining meaningful human annotations and model adaptation.  
  **Quick check:** "How does transcreation differ from simple translation, and why is it important for dialectal fairness?" → Translation converts text mechanically; transcreation adapts content to cultural context (e.g., measurement units, names). This yields prompts that feel native to dialect speakers, avoiding out-of-culture artifacts that would skew evaluation.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Transcreation → Response Generator → Human Annotation → Labels (aggregate + AC1) → LLM Judge (ICL or Fine-tuning) → Evaluation Engine (metrics)

Critical path: 1) Sample prompts from source datasets. 2) Transcreate prompts into each dialect. 3) Generate LLM responses. 4) Collect 3 native speaker annotations per output → compute AC1, aggregate label. 5) Build LLM judge: test ICL strategies or fine-tune (SFT/DPO). 6) Evaluate judge on held-out test set (accuracy, F1, AC1). 7) Analyze correlations with resourceness/agreement.

Design tradeoffs: 
- Transcreation vs translation: Transcreation improves cultural fit but requires careful prompt design; incorrect locale instructions yield wrong language output.
- ICL vs fine-tuning: ICL is flexible but token-heavy; fine-tuning yields consistency but needs high-quality labeled data and risks overfitting.
- Per-criterion vs holistic labeling: Decomposition provides error insights but aggregation is fragile; holistic labeling proved more robust.
- Synthetic vs human SFT: Synthetic data scales cheaply but only beneficial when base LLM generation quality is already high; otherwise it degrades performance.
- SFT vs DPO: SFT is safer for injecting knowledge; DPO fails when preference data is noisy (low human agreement).

Failure signatures:
- Human-human AC1 < 0.6 → LLM-judge AC1 low regardless of model size or prompt.
- DPO fine-tuning causes >5% drop in accuracy/F1 (e.g., Cornish -17.3%).
- TSD generation quality (label=1%) < 50% for a dialect indicates unusable synthetic data; common errors: wrong locale, grammar, cultural inaccuracies.
- Strategy 1 AC1 does not improve with more shots → dialect likely suffers from subtle linguistic ambiguity.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproduce TSD generation quality: For all five locales, generate 1000 outputs per source dataset using specified LLMs. Compute human-rated high-quality percentages; verify AAVE/Yorkshire >95%, Cornish/Geordie/Frisian significantly lower.
2. Judge ICL prompt ablation: Evaluate the five prompt strategies with 5-shot examples. Measure AC1 and F1 per locale; confirm strategy 1 (label only) gives highest correlation with human-human AC1 and that per-criterion aggregation (strategy 4) harms performance.
3. Fine-tuning impact: Fine-tune Qwen3 8B with (a) human-labeled SFT; (b) DPO on Cornish preferences; (c) synthetic-data SFT for Yorkshire, Cornish, West Frisian. Evaluate judge AC1/F1 changes; expect SFT to improve AC1 for all but maintain high/low gap, DPO to degrade Cornish, synthetic SFT to help only high-generation dialects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** To what extent can the LLM-human agreement pattern be overcome for low-resource English dialects with inherently low annotator consensus, and what specific methodological innovations are required?  
*Basis:* The core finding is that LLM-human agreement mimics human-human agreement and fine-tuning does not eradicate this pattern. The authors state this raises feasibility questions and that new tools are needed.  
*Why unresolved:* The pattern is linked to data scarcity and ambiguous dialectal features; the path forward ("new tools" or "linguistics-informed tools") is asserted but not defined or evaluated.  
*What evidence would resolve:* A study isolating resourceness from linguistic ambiguity across more dialects, testing novel methods like Bayesian annotation models or multi-teacher distillation from high-agreement dialects.

**Open Question 2:** What specific linguistic properties of English dialects cause the pattern to persist despite fine-tuning, and can these be operationalized into features that predict dialectal LLM brittleness?  
*Basis:* West Frisian is an outlier with significant fine-tuning gains despite low baseline, whereas dialects do not; Levenshtein distance predicts Frisian but not dialects, suggesting ambiguous lexical nature of dialects.  
*Why unresolved:* The explanation is qualitative based on one control language; coarse metrics like Levenshtein are insufficient; nuanced grammatical/phonological features are not measured.  
*What evidence would resolve:* A typological feature analysis using databases like Grambank/WALS, regressing the performance gap against a battery of linguistic features to identify predictive "brittleness" factors.

**Open Question 3:** Can high-quality, large-scale TSD generation for low-resource dialects be reliably automated without human-in-the-loop verification, and if not, what is the minimum viable human supervision required?  
*Basis:* Some LLMs generate high-quality TSD for AAVE/Yorkshire (scalable path), but fail for Cornish/Geordie; synthetic data SFT helps only when base generation quality is already high.  
*Why unresolved:* The scalable path is conditional on already having high human-LLM agreement, which is scarce. For low-agreement dialects (e.g., Cornish, AC1=0.51), there is no consensus on "high-quality," making automated evaluation chicken-and-egg.  
*What evidence would resolve:* A controlled experiment varying human-annotated data volume for a low-agreement dialect, measuring synthetic data utility against a held-out high-agreement human set or downstream task performance.

## Limitations
- Small dialect set (4 English + 1 Frisian) limits generalizability; human-human agreement is low for Cornish (AC1=0.51), possibly due to ambiguous rubric or annotator variability.
- Unknown replication details: exact LLM API versions/system prompts, annotator training protocols, dataset splits, and DPO hyperparameters.
- Transcreation relies on LLMs; potential cultural/linguistic biases in generated prompts may affect TSD quality.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (LLM-as-judge bounded by human-human agreement): High. Robust cross-locale ranking; supported by adjacent literature.
- Mechanism 2 (TSD quality depends on resourceness/linguistic distance): Medium. Strong correlation with speaker population; linguistic distance only predicts distinct languages (Frisian), not subtle dialect differences.
- Mechanism 3 (SFT partial improvement; DPO harmful): Medium. SFT gains observed but high/low gap persists; DPO harms Cornish but needs broader validation.

## Next Checks
1. Generalize to more dialects/languages: Extend pipeline to additional low-resource English dialects (e.g., Scots, Ozark) and distinct low-resource languages (e.g., Welsh, Breton) to test the agreement bound and resource effects.
2. Causal annotation quality experiment: Increase annotator pool (5–7 per item), compute consensus gold labels, filter ambiguous items. Retrain LLM judges to test if raising human-human AC1 lifts LLM-judge performance.
3. Alternative adaptation methods: Evaluate parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, adapters) and data-augmentation (back-translation, code-switching) on low-resource dialects, and re-test DPO using high-consensus preference pairs.
