# LiveMathematicianBench: A Live Benchmark for Mathematician-Level Reasoning with Proof Sketches

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01754
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01754
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Top model Gemini-3.1-pro-preview achieves 43.5% accuracy; under substitution-resistant evaluation, accuracy drops to 17.6% (below 20% random baseline), while proof sketch guidance yields consistent gains (e.g., +13.6 pp for Gemini).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LiveMathematicianBench, a dynamically constructed benchmark designed to evaluate large language models on research-level mathematical reasoning while resisting data contamination. The benchmark builds multiple-choice questions from theorems in recent arXiv papers published after model training cutoffs. It employs a 13-category logical taxonomy and a proof-sketch-guided pipeline to generate plausible but incorrect distractors that target the proof’s logical structure. A substitution-resistant evaluation mechanism further tests genuine reasoning versus answer recognition. Results indicate the benchmark is far from saturated, with top models scoring under 50%, and show that providing proof sketches improves performance, suggesting models can leverage high-level strategies.

## Method Summary
The benchmark construction follows a seven-stage pipeline: (1) retrieve recent arXiv papers post-model cutoff, (2) extract and clean LaTeX sources, (3) extract main theorems, generate proof sketches, and classify theorems into 13 logical categories, (4) generate multiple-choice questions with stems, correct answers, and four distractors—including substitution-resistant options—guided by the proof sketch, (5) filter out items where the stem alone reveals the answer via a judge model, (6) calibrate hardness by overgenerating variants and selecting those a frontier model answers incorrectly, and (7) evaluate models in two modes (with/without proof sketch) and on substitution-resistant subsets. The process prioritizes contamination resistance, adversarial distractor quality, and fine-grained analysis across logical forms.

## Key Results
- Frontier models perform poorly: Gemini-3.1-pro-preview leads with 43.5% accuracy, well below human-level competence.
- Substitution-resistant evaluation causes severe drops (e.g., Gemini to 17.6%, below random), indicating heavy reliance on answer substitution heuristics.
- Providing proof sketches boosts accuracy across models (average +13.6 pp for Gemini), demonstrating that strategic guidance is utilizable.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Post-cutoff arXiv sourcing eliminates data contamination
- **Claim:** Using papers published strictly after each model’s training cutoff ensures test items are never seen during pre-training, preventing memorization-based answers.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline queries arXiv for papers post-cutoff, extracts theorems, and constructs questions from novel statements. This creates a moving target that contamination cannot preempt.
- **Core assumption:** Models’ training corpora exclude these future papers and no retrieval occurs during evaluation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states benchmark is built from “recent arXiv papers published after model training cutoffs.” Section 1 explicitly claims contamination-free by construction via cutoff restriction.

### Mechanism 2: Proof-sketch-guided distractor generation targets proof strategy
- **Claim:** Distractors are crafted by tampering with “load-bearing” constraints from the proof sketch, making them plausible yet false, thus requiring understanding of logical structure to eliminate.
- **Mechanism:** For each theorem, a high-level sketch is extracted. The distractor generator identifies critical constraints (e.g., uniformity, sharp constants) and applies perturbation strategies (controlled weakening/strengthening, property confusion) to create options that exploit specific proof insights.
- **Core assumption:** Sketches reliably capture distinguishing constraints, and LLMs can generate valid, targeted distractors from them.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “proof-sketch-guided distractor pipeline… uses high-level proof strategies.” Section 4b describes how sketches expose load-bearing steps to engineer distractors. Example 6 shows sketch hooks like “counting_estimate” guiding distractor forms.

### Mechanism 3: Substitution-resistant options disrupt shortcut heuristics
- **Claim:** Replacing the correct answer with a meta-option (“One of the remaining options is correct, but a stronger result can be proven.”) forces models to reason about logical strength rather than substitute answers.
- **Mechanism:** Models must first identify which distractor is a true but weaker statement, then independently verify a strictly stronger claim is provable. This cannot be solved by plugging options into the stem or using surface cues.
- **Core assumption:** Models heavily rely on substitution/elimination in standard multiple-choice; the meta-option breaks this without introducing a new shortcut.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract calls it “substitution-resistant mechanism… to distinguish answer recognition from substantive reasoning.” Section 4b details the required two-step reasoning. Figure 5(a) shows accuracy drops sharply under this evaluation, confirming increased difficulty.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Data Contamination in LLM Evaluation
- **Why needed here:** The benchmark’s core motivation is that existing math benchmarks are contaminated, inflating model performance. Understanding contamination—overlap between training and test data—is essential to grasp why dynamic sourcing is necessary.
- **Quick check:** What is data contamination in LLM benchmarks, and why does it inflate performance metrics? How does using post-cutoff papers mitigate it?

### Concept: Logical Taxonomy of Theorem Statements
- **Why needed here:** The 13-category taxonomy (implication, equivalence, existential-universal, etc.) organizes questions, drives category-specific design, and enables fine-grained analysis of model strengths/weaknesses across reasoning forms.
- **Quick check:** Distinguish between an “implication” theorem (A ⇒ B) and an “existential-universal” theorem (∃x ∀y P(x,y)). How would the question stems differ for these two types?

### Concept: Adversarial Distractor Generation
- **Why needed here:** The benchmark’s novelty lies in generating hard negatives via proof sketches. Understanding how to create plausible-but-false options—through controlled perturbation, semantic weakening/strengthening, property confusion—is key to its sensitivity.
- **Quick check:** For a theorem stating “f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x,” give an example of a “semantic weakening” distractor and a “stronger trap” distractor. Which would be harder to rule out without understanding the proof?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Paper Retrieval -> LaTeX Extraction -> Theorem Extraction & Classification -> Proof Sketch -> Stem & Correct Choice -> Distractor Generation -> Quality Rubric -> Stem-Only Triviality Filter -> Hardness Calibration -> Evaluation Protocol

### Critical path
The minimal viable path for one question: Paper retrieval → LaTeX extraction → Theorem extraction → Proof sketch → Stem & correct choice → Distractor generation → Quality rubric → Stem-nontrivial filter → Hardness selection. Extraction (Stage 3) and distractor quality (Stage 4) are high-risk.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Contamination resistance vs. topic coverage:** Post-cutoff sourcing avoids contamination but limits papers to a moving window, potentially under-representing some areas.
- **Automation vs. precision:** LLM-driven extraction/generation scales but risks errors; human validation (Appendix B) mitigates but doesn’t eliminate this.
- **Benchmark difficulty vs. statistical power:** Hardness calibration discards easy items, leaving 177 hard questions—ensures high difficulty but reduces per-category counts.
- **Proof sketch utility vs. leakage:** Sketches improve performance but could leak answers if too detailed; pipeline uses high-level outlines and human checks to avoid direct revelation.

### Failure signatures
- **High stem-trivial rate:** >10% failing Stage 5 indicates stems are too revealing or distractors too weak.
- **Low inter-category consistency:** If models show no category-wise variation (unlike Figure 4a), taxonomy may not be meaningful or distractor generation is uniform.
- **Sketch gain ≈ 0:** No improvement with sketch access suggests sketches are uninformative or models cannot use them.
- **Substitution-resistant accuracy ≈ random:** At/below random on meta-option items may indicate broken mechanism (distractors not weaker-true/false) or pure guessing.
- **Validation failure rate:** >50% of sampled items failing human checks (Appendix B) signals upstream prompt/engineering issues.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Single-paper end-to-end run:** Take one recent arXiv paper (e.g., from November 2025), run full pipeline, manually inspect output MCQ. Verify: theorem extraction accuracy, proof sketch fidelity, category assignment, stem self-containedness, distractor falsity, substitution-resistant option validity.
2. **Category-specific distractor test:** For two contrasting categories (e.g., Implication vs. Equivalence), generate 10 questions without hardness calibration. Evaluate a mid-tier model (e.g., GPT-4o) on standard and substitution-resistant sets. Check: distractors follow category-specific templates, substitution-resistant accuracy is significantly lower, proof sketch access improves accuracy on both.
3. **Hardness calibration sanity check:** Take a source theorem with multiple generated variants (after Stage 4), run a strong model (e.g., Gemini-3.1-pro-preview) in low-reasoning mode. Confirm pipeline selects variant model gets wrong while maintaining quality rubric >5. Verify dropped theorems were all answered correctly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Why do frontier LLMs exhibit systematic performance variations across different logical theorem forms (e.g., implication vs. equivalence vs. universal), and what architectural or training factors determine these strengths? The paper documents differences but provides no analysis of causes. Understanding this could inform model design to balance reasoning capabilities.

### Open Question 2
To what extent can LLMs genuinely perform mathematical conjecturing and comparative reasoning about statement strength, beyond rote recognition, when evaluated under substitution-resistant conditions? The benchmark shows models fail but doesn’t diagnose why—whether it’s lack of comparative reasoning skill, inability to track logical strength, or something else.

### Open Question 3
Do proof sketches provide strategic guidance that models can internalize and apply to novel problems, or are the observed gains merely from surface-level pattern matching with the sketch content? The improvement could be due to sketches revealing answer patterns rather than genuine planning; distinguishing requires transfer or process tracing.

### Open Question 4
How long will the dynamic contamination-resistant construction based on post-cutoff arXiv papers remain effective as models train on more recent data and the benchmark itself becomes part of the literature? The paper assumes dynamic sourcing will perpetually outpace contamination, but models increasingly train on web-crawled data including arXiv; eventual collision is unaddressed.

### Open Question 5
Does the logical taxonomy truly isolate reasoning form from mathematical content, or are observed category differences confounded by domain-specific knowledge distribution in training data? The taxonomy aims to cross domains, but the paper doesn’t verify that category assignment is independent of mathematical area or that low performance isn’t due to sparse training data in certain domains.

## Limitations
- **Pipeline errors:** LLM-driven extraction, classification, and distractor generation may introduce undetected mistakes; human validation is limited.
- **Contamination verification:** Resistance relies on perfect cutoff alignment and no retrieval; impossible to confirm without access to model training data.
- **Statistical sparsity:** Final set contains only 177 hard items, yielding uneven category representation and low per-category counts.

## Confidence
- **Contamination resistance:** Medium (sound by design, but unverifiable).
- **Adversarial distractors + substitution mechanism:** Medium (performance drops support, but shortcut learning remains plausible).
- **Proof sketch utility:** High (consistent +13.6 pp improvement across models).
- **Scalable fine-grained taxonomy:** Medium (automation enables coverage, but quality varies across subfields).

## Next Checks
1. **Corpus overlap audit:** For each evaluated model, search its training data (if accessible) for exact or high‑n-gram matches to benchmark theorems; any hit would contradict the contamination‑free claim.
2. **Expert item review:** Sample a stratified set of MCQs and ask mathematicians to verify (a) all distractors are false, (b) meta‑option items have a weaker true option and a strictly stronger provable result, and (c) proof sketches do not directly reveal the answer; compute inter‑rater reliability.
3. **Sketch quality ablation:** Systematically degrade proof sketches (e.g., remove key constraints, randomize order) and measure changes in model performance with sketch access; a strong correlation between degradation and accuracy drop would confirm that sketches provide genuine strategic information.
