# Transductive Generalization via Optimal Transport and Its Application to Graph Node Classification

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.09257
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09257
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Proposed computable transductive generalization bounds based on 1-Wasserstein distances between encoded feature distributions correlate strongly (often >0.8) with empirical generalization error across multiple GNN architectures, and reveal a depth-dependent trade-off between intra-class concentration and inter-class separation.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the lack of practical, representation-based generalization bounds for transductive graph node classification, where test features are available during training and node representations are dependent. It introduces two new bounds—a global bound and a class-wise bound—expressed in terms of 1-Wasserstein distances between the distributions of encoded training and test features. These bounds are computable from a model's representations and avoid intractable classical complexity measures. Applied to graph neural networks (GNNs), the theory provides depth-dependent upper bounds that explain the empirically observed non-monotonic relationship between model depth and generalization: deeper layers increase intra-class concentration but reduce inter-class separation. Experiments on nine datasets with five GNN architectures show the proposed bounds strongly correlate with actual test error, significantly outperforming prior transductive baselines like PAC-Bayesian and Rademacher complexities.

## Method Summary
The authors derive two main generalization bounds for a transductive setting with a fixed encoder \(\phi\) (e.g., a GNN) and classifier \(f\). The **global bound** (Theorem 4.1) states that the difference between expected test and training losses is at most \((M(f,\phi)/\gamma) \cdot W_1(\phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{train}}, \phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{test}})\), where \(W_1\) is the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical distributions of encoded features, \(\gamma\) is a margin parameter, and \(M(f,\phi)\) is a "change rate" measuring sensitivity of the margin loss. The **class-wise bound** (Theorem 4.2) decomposes the gap into class-conditional Wasserstein distances and a term for class proportion mismatch across splits. Both bounds are computable: \(W_1\) is estimated via optimal transport solvers, and \(M/M_c\) via a robust percentile from pairwise margin differences and distances.

For GNNs, Propositions 6.1 and 6.2 derive depth-dependent upper bounds on these Wasserstein distances. For a linearized GCN, \(W_1 \leq C_1 W_1(\hat{A}^\ell_{\#}\mu_S, \hat{A}^\ell_{\#}\mu_T) + C_2 \rho_\perp(\hat{A})^\ell\), where \(\rho_\perp(\hat{A})<1\) is a spectral decay term. This reveals a trade-off: depth \(\ell\) decays both intra- and inter-class distances, but potentially at different rates, leading to non-monotonic generalization.

## Key Results
*   **Strong Correlation:** The proposed global and class-wise bounds exhibit high Spearman rank correlation (\(>0.8\)) with empirical generalization error across nine graph datasets and five GNN architectures (SGC, GCN, GCNII, GAT, GraphSAGE), significantly outperforming transductive PAC-Bayesian and Rademacher complexity baselines.
*   **Depth Trade-off:** Depth-dependent analysis shows that deeper GNN layers simultaneously decrease intra-class Wasserstein (increasing concentration) and inter-class Wasserstein (decreasing separation). This trade-off explains the non-monotonic test error curves observed in practice.
*   **Practical Utility without Test Labels:** An approximation of the class-wise bound, which does not require test labels, maintains high correlation with empirical error, demonstrating its applicability in realistic transductive settings where test labels are unknown.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Global Optimal Transport Bound
*   **Claim:** The transductive generalization gap is bounded by \((M(f,\phi)/\gamma) \cdot W_1(\phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{train}}, \phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{test}})\), linking it to the distribution shift of encoded features.
*   **Mechanism:** The proof uses the 1/\(\gamma\)-Lipschitz property of the margin loss. By constructing an optimal transport coupling \(\pi\) between the empirical training and test distributions \(\mu_{\text{train}}\) and \(\mu_{\text{test}}\), the expected loss difference is rewritten as an expectation over pairs \((x_i, x_j)\) drawn from \(\pi\). The triangle inequality and Lipschitz property then yield the bound, with \(M(f,\phi)\) quantifying the maximum rate of change of the margin along representation perturbations.
*   **Core Assumptions:** The loss is margin-based and 1/\(\gamma\)-Lipschitz; the data split is random; the encoder \(\phi\) and classifier \(f\) are fixed (or come from a class with bounded change rate \(M\)).
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract states derivation of global/class-wise bounds via OT; Theorem 4.1 formalizes the global bound; the use of Wasserstein distinguishes this from prior transductive bounds based on classical complexity measures.
*   **Break Conditions:** The bound becomes vacuous if \(M(f,\phi)\) is large (high classifier sensitivity) or if \(W_1\) is large due to severe distribution shift. It also assumes access to test features, which may not hold under covariate shift beyond the observed graph.

### Mechanism 2: Class-wise Decomposition
*   **Claim:** The generalization gap decomposes into a sum over classes of \(E_{\pi'}[ (m_c(\pi')/m) \cdot W_1(\phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{train},c}^{\pi'}, \phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{test},c}^{\pi'}) ]\) plus a class-proportion mismatch term, isolating class-conditional distribution shifts.
*   **Mechanism:** By conditioning the global bound's derivation on the random class for a test node and taking an expectation over a random permutation \(\pi'\) that maps training to test nodes within the same class, the bound separates intra-class transport costs. The term \(|u_c/u - m_c/m|\) accounts for differences in class proportions between the unbalanced training set and the balanced test set induced by \(\pi'\).
*   **Core Assumptions:** Class-conditional margin loss is Lipschitz; the expectation over random splits \(\pi'\) is well-defined; each class has sufficient samples to estimate Wasserstein reliably; \(M_c(f,\phi)\) is finite.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract mentions "class-wise bound based on expected class-conditional Wasserstein distances"; Theorem 4.2 gives the formal inequality; Section 5.2 shows high correlation even for an approximation that uses only training labels.
*   **Break Conditions:** If a class is extremely rare, estimating its conditional Wasserstein may be unreliable. The approximation without test labels introduces additional variance.

### Mechanism 3: Depth Trade-off in GNNs
*   **Claim:** Increasing GNN depth simultaneously increases intra-class concentration (reducing class-conditional Wasserstein) but reduces inter-class separation (reducing between-class Wasserstein), creating a non-monotonic depth-generalization relationship.
*   **Mechanism:** Propositions 6.1 (SGC) and 6.2 (GCN) derive depth-$\ell$ upper bounds on Wasserstein distances: \(W_1 \leq C_1 W_1(\hat{A}^\ell_{\#}\mu_S, \hat{A}^\ell_{\#}\mu_T) + C_2 \rho_\perp(\hat{A})^\ell\) (SGC), with an extra factor $\beta^\ell$ for GCN. Since $\rho_\perp(\hat{A})<1$, depth decays all distances, but the rate depends on the initial separation. The class-wise bound involves intra-class distance directly and inter-class distance implicitly via the margin change rate $M_c$. Optimal depth balances these decaying terms.
*   **Core Assumptions:** The normalized adjacency operator $\hat{A}$ has a spectral gap $\rho_\perp(\hat{A}) < 1$; weight matrices have bounded spectral norm ($\|W^{(t)}\|_2 \leq \beta$); initial features have finite norm.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract explicitly states the trade-off; Propositions 6.1 and 6.2 provide the depth-dependent bounds; Figure 3 on Cora shows WG, WC, WS decaying with depth while test error is non-monotonic.
*   **Break Conditions:** If $\rho_\perp(\hat{A})$ is close to 1 (very dense graph) or $\beta$ is large, decay may be slow or the bound may increase with depth (for GCN if $\beta \rho_\perp(\hat{A}) > 1$). The trade-off may be unobservable if classes are already perfectly separated or oversmoothing completely merges them.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Transductive Learning**
    *   **Why needed:** The setting involves learning on a fixed, known test set; test features are available during training and are not i.i.d. from the training distribution. Classical inductive bounds fail.
    *   **Quick Check:** What is the key difference between transductive and inductive generalization bounds? (Answer: Transductive bounds use the specific test features and account for dependence between train and test; inductive bounds assume an i.i.d. test distribution.)
*   **Concept: Optimal Transport / Wasserstein Distance**
    *   **Why needed:** The core bounds are expressed in terms of 1-Wasserstein distances between (class-conditional) feature distributions. It provides a geometrically meaningful, symmetric measure of distributional discrepancy that works for singular measures.
    *   **Quick Check:** How does the 1-Wasserstein distance differ from KL-divergence? (Answer: Wasserstein accounts for the geometry of the underlying space and is finite for singular measures; KL is asymmetric and can be infinite if supports are disjoint.)
*   **Concept: Graph Neural Networks (Message-Passing)**
    *   **Why needed:** The application is node classification on graphs; GNN encoders produce dependent node representations through neighborhood aggregation, which the theory captures via depth-dependent analysis.
    *   **Quick Check:** What is the effect of stacking many GNN layers on node features? (Answer: It can cause oversmoothing, where features of different nodes converge, potentially reducing inter-class separation.)
*   **Concept: Margin-based Loss**
    *   **Why needed:** The derivation relies on the Lipschitz property of margin losses (e.g., hinge loss). The margin $\gamma$ appears directly in the bound's scaling factor.
    *   **Quick Check:** Why is the margin $\gamma$ important in generalization bounds? (Answer: It controls the Lipschitz constant of the loss; larger margins often yield tighter bounds.)
*   **Concept: Class-Conditional Distribution**
    *   **Why needed:** The class-wise bound conditions on node labels to isolate how representations of each class shift between train and test.
    *   **Quick Check:** In transductive node classification, why might class-conditional distributions shift even if overall feature distributions are similar? (Answer: Because train/test splits are non-random with respect to labels—they are fixed nodes—so class proportions can differ, and graph structure may cause different classes to be sampled differently.)

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:** Raw Node Features \(X\) & Adjacency \(A\) -> **Encoder \(\phi\)** (GNN: SGC/GCN/GCNII/GAT/GraphSAGE) -> Node Embeddings \(Z\) -> **Classifier \(f\)** (MLP) -> Class Scores. Margin \(\rho_f(z,y)\) computed from scores. **Bound Calculators** take embeddings to compute \(W_1\) distances and \(M/M_c\) change rates.
*   **Critical Path:**
    1.  Train GNN+MLP on a given transductive split (e.g., 30% train nodes, 70% test nodes) using cross-entropy loss.
    2.  Extract final-layer embeddings for *all* nodes (train+test) from the trained encoder \(\phi\).
    3.  Compute global \(W_1(\phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{train}}, \phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{test}})\) between empirical distributions of train/test embeddings (e.g., using POT library).
    4.  Compute \(M(f,\phi)\): find the 0.9 percentile of \(|\rho_f(\phi(x_i), y_i) - \rho_f(\phi(x_j), y')| / \|\phi(x_i)-\phi(x_j)\|\) over all train \(i\), test \(j\), and classes \(y'\).
    5.  For class-wise: for each class \(c\), compute \(W_1(\phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{train},c}, \phi_{\#}\mu_{\text{test},c})\); approximate the expectation over random splits \(\pi'\) by averaging over \(T=4\) sampled permutations; compute class proportion mismatch term.
    6.  Assemble bound values and compare to empirical test error (computed on held-out test labels).
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   **Global vs Class-wise:** Global is simpler but may mask class-specific shifts; class-wise is more informative but requires handling class imbalance and sampling permutations.
    *   **Test-Label Requirement:** Theoretical class-wise bound needs test labels; the paper's approximation ("Class-wise approx") uses only training labels, trading some accuracy for practicality.
    *   **Percentile for \(M\):** Using the maximum (100th percentile) of pairwise margin/distance ratios is unstable; the 0.9 percentile provides robustness without sacrificing correlation.
    *   **Wasserstein Solver:** Exact OT (linear programming) scales poorly (\(O(n^3)\)) for large graphs; entropy-regularized Sinkhorn is faster but introduces bias. The paper does not specify its choice.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   Bound value is astronomically large (>>1): likely due to unscaled embeddings, very small margin \(\gamma\), or poor \(M\) estimate.
    *   Rank correlation with empirical error is negative or near zero: indicates model violates assumptions (e.g., extremely sensitive classifier, severe class imbalance not captured by the approximation) or bound computation error.
    *   Depth analysis shows monotonic bound but non-monotonic empirical error: suggests the depth-dependent upper bound is too loose or that unmodeled factors (like optimization) dominate.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  **Correlation Across Models & Depths:** For each of 9 datasets and 5 GNN architectures, train at multiple depths (1,2,4,8,16,32). Compute global/class-wise bounds and empirical generalization gap. Calculate Spearman rank correlation. Show proposed bounds >0.8 while PAC-Bayes/Rademacher often weak/negative.
    2.  **Depth Sweep on Cora:** For SGC and GCN on Cora, vary depth. Plot WG (global Wasserstein), WC (avg class-conditional Wasserstein), WS (min inter-class Wassertein), and test error. Show non-monotonic error and link it to competing decays in WC and WS.
    3.  **Approximation Validation:** Compare the exact class-wise bound (using test labels) vs. the class-wise approximation (using only training labels and sampled permutations). Show rank correlation remains high, proving the approximation's utility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** Can we design GNN architectures or training objectives that explicitly optimize the concentration-separation trade-off identified by the proposed bounds to improve generalization, particularly in deep networks where oversmoothing occurs?
    *   **Basis:** The paper establishes the theoretical trade-off but does not propose or evaluate concrete interventions (e.g., regularizers, skip connections) to balance it.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** It remains unknown how to translate the insight about intra- vs. inter-class Wasserstein into a practical optimization objective or architectural change.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Introduce specific interventions (e.g., loss terms penalizing inter-class closeness while encouraging intra-class tightness), test them across depths on benchmarks, and show they shift Wasserstein distances as predicted and improve test accuracy.
*   **Question 2:** Do the depth-dependent Wasserstein bounds and the concentration-separation trade-off hold for other GNN architectures (e.g., GraphSAGE, GAT) that employ different aggregation mechanisms?
    *   **Basis:** The theoretical depth analysis (Propositions 6.1, 6.2) is only provided for SGC and GCN. While experiments include GraphSAGE and GAT, the theory for them is absent.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** Aggregation schemes like max-pooling or attention may lead to different representation dynamics that are not captured by the spectral arguments used for GCN/SGC.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Extend the theoretical analysis to derive analogous depth-dependent bounds for other GNNs by adapting assumptions to their operators. Empirically measure WG, WC, WS for these models at increasing depths to see if similar decay patterns and non-monotonic error occur.
*   **Question 3:** What is the minimal number of permutation samples \(T\) required to reliably estimate the expected class-wise Wasserstein terms, and can variance reduction techniques make the bound computation more efficient for large graphs?
    *   **Basis:** The paper uses \(T=4\) sampled permutations for the expectation, noting that larger \(T\) (16, 64) did not consistently improve correlation. No systematic study of estimator variance vs. \(T\) is provided.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** For graphs with many nodes or classes, generating many permutations is costly. The 4-sample heuristic's universality and efficiency for large-scale graphs are unclear.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Conduct experiments varying \(T\) across diverse graph sizes, measuring bound estimate stability (variance across runs) and its correlation with true generalization. Explore analytical approximations or low-variance estimators to reduce sampling.
*   **Question 4:** Can the optimal transport framework be extended to quantify and bound fairness disparities (e.g., across demographic subgroups) in graph-based transductive learning?
    *   **Basis:** The Impact Statement mentions fairness concerns but the current bounds condition on task labels, not protected attributes. The framework's applicability to subgroup fairness is an open theoretical and empirical question.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** It is unknown whether Wasserstein distances between subgroup feature distributions can meaningfully bound performance equity.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Formulate subgroup-conditioned versions of the class-wise bound. Derive corresponding inequalities and validate empirically that they correlate with observed subgroup performance gaps on graphs with demographic annotations.

## Limitations
*   **Computational Scalability:** Exact Wasserstein distance computation scales cubically with the number of samples. For large graphs (e.g., Amazon-ratings with ~24k nodes), this becomes expensive, though approximations (e.g., Sinkhorn) may be used but are not detailed.
*   **Theoretical Assumptions:** The bound requires a 1/$\gamma$-Lipschitz margin loss and a finite change rate $M(f,\phi)$. In practice, with small margins $\gamma$ or highly sensitive classifiers (e.g., near decision boundaries), $M$ can be large, making the bound vacuous. The spectral analysis for depth assumes $\rho_\perp(\hat{A})<1$, which may not hold for extremely dense graphs.
*   **Approximation Gaps:** The class-wise approximation that avoids test labels introduces an implicit bias. The paper shows it correlates well, but its theoretical properties (e.g., bias, variance) compared to the exact bound are not analyzed.

## Confidence
*   **Global/Class-wise Bound Derivations:** High. The use of optimal transport is sound and aligns with transductive settings; theorems are formally stated and proved.
*   **Empirical Correlation Results:** High. Consistent >0.8 rank correlations across 9 datasets and 5 architectures, with strong baselines for comparison.
*   **Depth Trade-off Mechanism:** Medium. The spectral analysis is rigorous for SGC/GCN but limited to these models. Evidence on Cora is convincing, but generalizability to other architectures and heterophilic graphs is less certain.
*   **Practical Computability without Test Labels:** Medium. The approximation is shown effective empirically, but implementation details (e.g., how exactly test class distributions are proxied) are sparse, making full reproducibility challenging.
