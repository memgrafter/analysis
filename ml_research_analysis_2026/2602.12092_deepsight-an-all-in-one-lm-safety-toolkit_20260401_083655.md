# DeepSight: An All-in-One LM Safety Toolkit

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12092
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12092
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** First open-source toolkit uniting safety evaluation (DeepSafe) and diagnosis (DeepScan) via shared protocols, revealing critical multimodal safety gaps and representation-based failure modes across 14 models.

## Executive Summary
DeepSight integrates two complementary components: DeepSafe for large-scale behavioral evaluation across 20+ benchmarks, and DeepScan for fine-grained representation analysis (X‑Boundary, TELLME, SPIN). Standardizing task schemas and data formats links internal model geometry directly to observed safety outcomes, turning safety assessment from a black‑box to a white‑box paradigm. Evaluations on 14 models uncover that multimodal safety lags text by 10‑14 percentage points, frontier risks show poor transfer, reasoning benefits multimodal but harms text safety, and both insufficient and extreme representation separation degrade robustness.

## Method Summary
DeepSight operates via a configuration‑driven modular pipeline. DeepSafe loads models and datasets, generates responses, and evaluates them using native, rule‑based, or model‑based judges (e.g., ProGuard). DeepScan attaches hooks to extract activations and computes geometric metrics such as separation score, TELLME encoding rate, and SPIN coupling index. Both components share identical model checkpoints, prompt templates, and dataset formats, ensuring alignment between behavioral and representational analyses. The system outputs JSON/Markdown summaries for joint interpretation and is designed for reproducibility, scalability, and low cost.

## Key Results
- Multimodal models are 10‑14pp less safe than text‑only models, with open‑source models lagging 5.5pp; multimodal over‑refusal reaches 54%.
- Frontier risks (e.g., Manipulation, Sandbagging) show negligible transfer: Kimi overall 74.93% but Manipulation 1.11%; DeepSeek Sandbagging 86.42% but AIRD 2.22%.
- Representation separation follows a criticality window: both very low (Mistral: 1.89) and extreme (Gemma‑3: 2998) correlate with degraded safety, while high TELLME encoding predicts strong HarmBench defense (e.g., Qwen2.5‑72B: 951.76 → 86.97%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Unified protocols transform safety evaluation from black‑box to white‑box by directly linking behavioral outcomes to internal representations.
- **Mechanism:** Standardizing task schemas and data formats across DeepSafe and DeepScan enables correlation between metrics like TELLME’s subspace encoding rate and DeepSafe’s HarmBench scores. The core assumption is that internal representation geometry causally influences observed safety when inputs are controlled. [abstract, section 2.1, section 3.4].

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Representation separation exhibits a criticality window—both insufficient and extreme values undermine safety robustness.
- **Mechanism:** DeepScan’s X‑Boundary metric measures centroid separation and boundary alignment. Very low separation yields overlapping safe/harmful distributions, causing high attack success; extremely high separation creates sparse latent space with poor resolution, degrading fine‑grained boundary judgments. A smooth, dense decision boundary is necessary for ambiguous inputs. [section 3.4.1, 3.4.4, Figures 21 & 24].

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Reasoning capabilities introduce a modality‑dependent trade‑off: they enhance safety in multimodal settings but impair it in text‑only scenarios.
- **Mechanism:** In multimodal contexts, reasoning enables cross‑modal consistency checks that mitigate image‑text splitting attacks. In text‑only settings, reasoning’s computational overhead may reduce rapid threat interception, and greater capacity may facilitate deceptive strategies, lowering manipulation resistance. [section 3.2.2, 3.3.2, 3.3.3].

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
DeepSafe Registry Hub -> Models (HFModel, APIModel) -> Datasets (BaseDataset) -> Evaluators (Native/Rule/Model‑based) -> Runner (LocalRunner) -> Summarizer (StandardSummarizer). DeepScan Registry Hub -> Model Runners (expose model/tokenizer for hooks) -> Datasets -> Evaluators (X‑Boundary, TELLME, SPIN, MI‑Peaks) -> Summarizers (per‑evaluator + Combined). Both use YAML configs; DeepScan’s runner preserves raw model access for activation extraction.

### Critical Path
1. User creates YAML specifying models, datasets, evaluators (DeepSafe and/or DeepScan).
2. Runner loads each model once; for DeepSafe: run inference → assessment → summarization. For DeepScan: for each evaluator, load dataset, attach hooks to model, evaluate, compute metrics.
3. Summarizers output JSON/Markdown under `output_dir/run_id/model/evaluator/`.
4. Joint analysis correlates metrics from both toolkits.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Modularity vs. performance: Registry indirection eases extension but adds overhead; DeepScan’s activation hooks can bottleneck inference.
- Unified protocols vs. fidelity: Shared schemas enable correlation but may require adapting benchmark‑specific formats; DeepSafe falls back to native evaluators to preserve originality.
- White‑box vs. accessibility: DeepScan needs open‑source models; closed‑source models limited to DeepSafe’s black‑box evaluation.

### Failure Signatures
- Misaligned inputs: Different prompt templates or dataset versions between DeepSafe and DeepScan cause spurious correlations.
- Registry errors: Misspelled model/evaluator keys in config lead to `KeyError` at runtime.
- Memory exhaustion: Storing activations for large models/layers can OOM; inter‑evaluator CUDA cache cleanup is mandatory.
- Over‑refusal artifacts: Very high safe refusal rates may indicate overly conservative thresholds, not superior safety.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Run DeepSafe on a small model (e.g., InternLM3‑8B) with HarmBench; verify refusal rate aligns with known baselines.
2. Execute DeepScan TELLME diagnosis on the same checkpoint; confirm composite encoding rate matches expected range (e.g., 200–1000 for 7B‑70B models).
3. Perform a joint run with DeepSafe (Flames) and DeepScan (X‑Boundary); if separation score is very low (<5), expect high Flames attack success—validate the negative correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific geometric properties of latent space representations (e.g., optimal separation score, boundary clarity) most reliably predict robust safety across diverse content and frontier risk benchmarks?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.4 shows that both extreme separation (Gemma‑3: 2998) and very low separation (Mistral: 1.89) correlate with degraded safety performance, but the paper does not identify an optimal range or causal mechanism.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis is correlational and based on a limited set of models; it remains unclear whether these geometric metrics are generalizable predictors or if intervening to adjust them improves safety.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments that manipulate representation geometry (e.g., via contrastive fine‑tuning or targeted neuron editing) across multiple model families, followed by re‑evaluation with DeepSafe to measure causal impacts on robustness.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do reasoning capabilities produce opposing effects on safety: improving multimodal defense while increasing vulnerability to manipulation, and can these effects be decoupled in model design?
- **Basis in paper:** Sections 3.2.2 and 3.3.2 report that reasoning models outperform non‑reasoning models in multimodal safety but score drastically lower on Manipulation (mean 11.6% vs. 31.8%), yet the underlying cause is not investigated.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper hypothesizes trade‑offs but does not analyze whether this stems from Chain‑of‑Thought providing more attack surface, misalignment of reasoning objectives, or differences in training data. It is unknown if safer reasoning models are possible.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies comparing reasoning and non‑reasoning variants of the same base model, with fine‑grained analysis of internal reasoning traces during safety evaluations to pinpoint failure modes; then test architectural or training modifications that preserve multimodal reasoning benefits while reducing manipulation susceptibility.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What specific factors explain the widening multimodal safety gap between open‑source and closed‑source models, and can targeted interventions (e.g., data, alignment techniques) close this gap for open‑source models?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.2.3 shows that while open‑ and closed‑source LLMs perform similarly on text safety (0.716 vs. 0.726), the multimodal gap expands significantly (0.545 vs. 0.600). The paper speculates about data and engineering resources but provides no empirical analysis.
- **Why unresolved:** The root causes—whether due to multimodal alignment data scarcity, fusion architecture challenges, or evaluation artifact—are not isolated, leaving open‑source developers without clear improvement pathways.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies dissecting training data composition, multimodal alignment methods, and model architectures between top‑performing closed‑source and open‑source models; then applying identified best practices to open‑source models and measuring convergence in DeepSafe multimodal benchmarks.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does incorporating DeepScan diagnostic feedback (e.g., representation disentanglement, neuron coupling) into the alignment loop yield more robust and interpretable safety improvements than standard SFT/RLHF, without degrading general capabilities?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper proposes an evaluation‑diagnosis integrated paradigm (DeepSight) but does not present any experiments where diagnosis directly informs alignment. Sections 3.4 discuss correlations but not iterative improvement.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unknown whether the diagnostic metrics provide actionable signals for alignment or if they are merely post‑hoc explanations. The proposed closed‑loop engineering paradigm remains unvalidated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a closed‑loop experiment: diagnose a base model with DeepScan, apply targeted alignment interventions (e.g., contrastive learning to improve separation, neuron editing to reduce coupling), then re‑evaluate with DeepSafe across both safety and general capability benchmarks to assess trade‑offs and robustness gains.

## Limitations
- Dataset accessibility: DeepScan diagnostic datasets and frontier risk suites are not clearly documented or publicly hosted; manual download may block reproduction.
- White‑box restriction: DeepScan requires full model access, so correlations are limited to open‑source checkpoints; generalizability to closed‑source models (where frontier risks were observed) is unverified.
- Causal claims: Mechanisms rely on observed correlations; confounding factors like training data or architecture differences may explain patterns.

## Confidence
- Unified white‑box toolkit: Medium
- Multimodal safety decline vs. text: Medium‑High
- Frontier risks non‑transferable: Low
- Over‑safety in multimodal: Medium
- Diagnostic correlations (TELLME ↔ HarmBench, separation ↔ safety): Medium
- Representation separation criticality window: Medium
- Reasoning trade‑off (helps multimodal, harms text): Medium
