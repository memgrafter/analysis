# Toward Guarantees for Clinical Reasoning in Vision Language Models via Formal Verification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24111  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24111  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** A neurosymbolic verification framework using SMT solving to provide reference-free guarantees of diagnostic soundness in VLM-generated radiology reports.

## Executive Summary
Vision‑language models (VLMs) for radiology report generation lack formal guarantees of logical consistency, often producing unsupported diagnoses or missing forced conclusions. Standard lexical metrics fail to detect such deductive failures, especially in reference‑free settings. This paper introduces a neurosymbolic verification framework that autoformalizes free‑text findings into structured propositional evidence and uses an SMT solver (Z3) with a clinician‑audited knowledge base to mathematically verify whether each diagnostic claim is entailed by the evidence. Evaluating seven VLMs across five chest X‑ray benchmarks, the verifier exposes failure modes like conservative observation (high soundness, low completeness) and stochastic hallucination (low precision) that are invisible to lexical metrics. Enforcing solver‑backed entailment as a post‑hoc filter on labeled datasets consistently increased soundness (e.g., MedGemma‑27B on CheXpert: 0.9301→0.9599) and precision while uniformly reducing recall and completeness by a small margin, thereby providing a reference‑free guarantee of diagnostic soundness.

## Method Summary
The pipeline processes a VLM‑generated report (Findings RF and Impression RI). First, a constrained LLM (GPT‑OSS‑20B, temperature 0, schema‑aligned JSON) autoformalizes RF into a binary evidence vector *V* over a fixed predicate set *F*, under a closed‑world assumption (unmentioned findings are false). The RI is parsed via string matching to obtain the claimed diagnosis set *D(RI)*. A clinician‑audited knowledge base *K* encodes clinical guidelines as propositional formulas linking findings to diagnoses. For each claimed diagnosis *d*, the verifier constructs the conjunction Φ*V* (interpreted from *V*) and checks satisfiability of Φ*V* ∧ *K* ∧ ¬*d* using the Z3 SMT solver. Unsatisfiability means *d* is logically supported by the evidence; satisfiability indicates a hallucination. Completeness is measured by exhaustively checking all possible diagnoses to obtain the entailed set *EV* and comparing it to *D(RI)*. Metrics (soundness, completeness, precision, recall, specificity) are then computed. The verifier can be applied as a post‑hoc filter to retain only entailed diagnoses, guaranteeing soundness at the cost of some completeness.

## Key Results
- The verifier uncovers two distinct failure modes undetected by lexical metrics: conservative observers (high soundness, low completeness) and stochastic hallucinators (low precision).
- Enforcing solver‑backed entailment as a post‑hoc filter consistently increases soundness (e.g., MedGemma‑27B on CheXpert: 0.9301→0.9599) and precision while uniformly reducing recall and completeness by a small margin.
- The framework provides a reference‑free guarantee of diagnostic soundness for VLM outputs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Autoformalization via Constrained LLM**  
   - *Claim*: Free‑text findings are reliably mapped to a binary evidence vector *V* over predicate set *F*.  
   - *Core assumption*: The mapping is accurate and the closed‑world assumption (unmentioned findings are absent) is valid.  
   - *Evidence*: GPT‑OSS‑20B with temperature 0 and schema‑aligned JSON ensures deterministic parsing.  
   - *Break condition*: Misclassification or omission of findings due to linguistic variation or negation errors yields an inaccurate *V*, leading to incorrect entailment judgments; uncertainty quantifiable via grammar entropy.

2. **Clinician‑Audited Knowledge Base**  
   - *Claim*: A set of propositional formulas *K* encoded from clinical guidelines provides the inference rules linking findings to diagnoses.  
   - *Core assumption*: *K* correctly and completely captures necessary and sufficient conditions and consistency constraints per expert consensus.  
   - *Evidence*: GPT‑5.2 autoformalizes guidelines into candidate rules; clinicians audit and refine them.  
   - *Break condition*: Missing or incorrect rules cause false hallucinations or missed diagnoses; contradictory rules may make Φ*V* ∧ *K* unsatisfiable, triggering trivial entailments.

3. **Sound and Complete SMT Verification**  
   - *Claim*: Using Z3 to check satisfiability of Φ*V* ∧ *K* ∧ ¬*d* yields a mathematically rigorous entailment test.  
   - *Core assumption*: Z3 is sound and complete for propositional logic and the translation is faithful; the diagnosis set is finite.  
   - *Evidence*: The formulation relies on the solver’s guarantee; unsatisfiability of the conjunction implies *d* is entailed.  
   - *Break condition*: If Φ*V* ∧ *K* itself is unsatisfiable (evidence contradicts knowledge), the entailment checks degenerate; any *d* becomes entailed. Solver performance may degrade with very large *K*.

## Foundational Learning
- **Propositional Logic**  
  - *Why needed*: The framework represents findings and diagnoses as boolean variables and uses logical implication to encode clinical rules; entailment checks rely on it.  
  - *Quick check*: If Φ contains (f₁ ∧ f₂) and *K* contains (f₁ ∧ f₂) → *d*, does Φ ∧ *K* entail *d*? Yes, by modus ponens.

- **Closed‑World Assumption (CWA)**  
  - *Why needed*: It treats any finding not explicitly mentioned in the Findings as absent (false), yielding a complete assignment *V* necessary for constructing the logical context Φ*V*.  
  - *Quick check*: Under CWA, if the Findings section does not mention “cardiomegaly”, what is the truth value of the predicate cardiomegaly in *V*? False.

- **Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) Solving**  
  - *Why needed*: The core operation (checking if Φ*V* ∧ *K* ∧ ¬*d* is satisfiable) is performed by an SMT solver; understanding that it determines the existence of a model explains why “unsat” means entailment.  
  - *Quick check*: What does it mean if the solver returns “unsat” for Φ*V* ∧ *K* ∧ ¬*d*? It means there is no possible world where the evidence and knowledge hold but *d* is false; hence *d* is entailed.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: VLM → Autoformalizer *T* → Knowledge Base *K* → Verifier (Z3) → Metrics calculator  
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Image *I* → VLM generates report *R* = (RF, RI).  
  2. *T*(RF) → *V*; extract *D(RI)* from RI.  
  3. Build Φ*V* = conjunction of literals from *V*.  
  4. For each *d* in *D(RI)*: Z3(Φ*V* ∧ *K* ∧ ¬*d*); if unsat → supported, else → hallucinated.  
  5. For completeness: for each *d* in full diagnosis set *D*, compute entailed set *EV* via Z3; missed = *EV* \ *D(RI)*.  
  6. Aggregate metrics (soundness, completeness, precision, recall, specificity).  

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Deterministic vs flexible autoformalization: temperature 0 ensures reproducibility but may miss legitimate phrasings; flexibility could improve coverage but break determinism.  
  - Closed‑world vs open‑world: CWA simplifies tractability but may incorrectly negate unmentioned normal findings.  
  - Knowledge base curation: Clinician audit ensures quality but may not scale; larger *K* improves coverage but increases solver time and inconsistency risk.  
  - Post‑hoc filtering vs integrated training: Filtering guarantees output consistency without improving VLM reasoning; integrating verification into training could promote consistent generation but is more complex.  

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - High grammar entropy in *T*’s output: low confidence in mapping; trigger human review.  
  - Φ*V* ∧ *K* unsatisfiable: contradictory findings and knowledge; reject or flag report.  
  - All diagnoses labeled hallucinated: *K* too restrictive or autoformalization failure.  
  - Discrepancy between high lexical scores and low soundness/completeness: fluent but logically inconsistent reports.  
  - Solver timeouts or errors: may occur with large *K*; monitor performance.  

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Baseline lexical overlap** – Compute BLEU and ROUGE‑L on paired‑report datasets (MIMIC‑CXR, Indiana‑IU, CheXpert‑Plus). Near‑zero scores show lexical metrics fail to capture clinical reasoning quality.  
  2. **Reference‑free logical auditing** – Apply verifier to compute soundness, completeness, etc., across seven VLMs on five chest X‑ray benchmarks. Results reveal distinct failure modes: conservative observers (high soundness, low completeness) and stochastic hallucinators (low precision).  
  3. **Symbolic filtering impact** – On labeled datasets (CheXpert, NIH‑CXR14), compare raw VLM impression metrics against those after applying the entailment filter. Uniform gains in soundness and precision, with small drops in recall and completeness, confirm the filter’s effectiveness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How accurately does the autoformalization function *T* map diverse free‑text radiographic findings to structured propositional evidence across different hospitals and radiologists?  
2. To what extent does the clinician‑audited knowledge base *K* cover all diagnostically relevant inference rules for chest X‑rays, particularly for rare conditions or complex multi‑finding interactions?  
3. How does the closed‑world assumption (CWA) used in autoformalization affect the soundness and completeness metrics when radiologists omit normal or irrelevant findings?  
4. What is the clinical significance of the uniform decrease in recall/completeness observed when applying symbolic filtering, and can this trade‑off be mitigated?

## Limitations
- Undisclosed knowledge base *K* and autoformalization schema *T* hinder exact replication.  
- Closed‑world assumption may incorrectly negate unmentioned findings, especially in normal or concise reports.  
- Evaluation relies on generated reports; lacking ground‑truth evidence, measured soundness/completeness are only relative to *K* and may not reflect true clinical correctness.

## Confidence
- Autoformalization accuracy: **Medium**  
- Knowledge base adequacy: **Medium**  
- SMT verifier soundness: **High**  
- Post‑hoc filter effectiveness: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Manual formalization study** – Randomly select 200 reports; have clinicians manually convert Findings to binary evidence vectors over predicate set *F*; compare with *T*’s output to compute accuracy and identify failure modes.  
2. **Knowledge base gap analysis** – For diagnoses with low completeness, augment *K* with rules from authoritative guidelines (e.g., Fleischner Society); re‑run verification to measure changes in soundness/completeness, testing coverage.  
3. **Closed‑world vs. open‑world comparison** – Implement an open‑world variant where missing findings are treated as unknown; evaluate on a subset containing explicit normal statements to quantify the impact of CWA on verification outcomes.
