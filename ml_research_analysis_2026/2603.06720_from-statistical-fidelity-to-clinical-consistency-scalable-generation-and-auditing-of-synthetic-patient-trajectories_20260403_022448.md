# From Statistical Fidelity to Clinical Consistency: Scalable Generation and Auditing of Synthetic Patient Trajectories

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06720
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06720
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Coogee generates synthetic EHRs with near-perfect marginal fidelity (R²=0.99) and, after LLM auditing, achieves clinical consistency comparable to real data (Cohen's d ≤ 0.67) while preserving downstream utility and privacy.

## Executive Summary
The authors address the dual challenge of statistical fidelity and clinical consistency in synthetic electronic health record (EHR) generation. Their Coogee pipeline first produces patient trajectories using a knowledge-grounded Transformer with an anatomy-aware tokenization scheme, embedding clinical concepts via PrimeKG and ClinicalBERT to ensure validity. Then a large language model (Qwen3-30B) audits each generated record for demographic alignment, causal reasoning, and temporal plausibility, retaining only high-scoring trajectories. Evaluated on MIMIC-IV, the synthetic data reproduces marginal event distributions almost perfectly (bias ~0, R²=0.99) and, after auditing, shows a dramatically reduced distributional distance from real patient records. Downstream tasks such as phenotyping and readmission prediction yield similar performance when models are trained on synthetic versus real data, and membership inference attacks indicate no substantial privacy leakage.

## Method Summary
Coogee operates in two stages. Generation: A decoder-only Transformer model is trained on tokenized EHR sequences from MIMIC-IV. Each distinct clinical concept (diagnosis, medication, lab) becomes a single token; its embedding combines a structural signal from a relational graph convolutional network over the PrimeKG knowledge graph and a semantic signal from ClinicalBERT. The model generates sequences autoregressively, respecting temporal token intervals and constrained lab-value pairs. Auditing: For each candidate record, a separate LLM (Qwen3-30B) is prompted with a detailed rubric and a "Chief Medical Officer" persona to assign a realism score from 1 to 10 based on demographic coherence, clinical causality, and temporal plausibility. Only records scoring ≥7 are retained. This pipeline produces a synthetic cohort of 18,071 patients from 180,712 real records.

## Key Results
- Synthetic marginal probabilities show near-zero bias (0.00) and an R² of 0.99 compared to real data.
- Clinician review found 45–60% of unaudited records inconsistent; LLM auditing reduced Cohen's d from 0.59–1.60 to 0.18–0.67.
- Downstream TSTR models matched or exceeded real-data performance (phenotyping AUROC 0.91 vs 0.96; readmission AUROC 0.62 vs 0.61, recall 0.66 vs 0.44); privacy risk negligible (membership inference F1=0.51).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Three core mechanisms enable Coogee’s success:

1. **Knowledge‑grounded tokenization and embedding**. Atomic tokens for each clinical concept (ICD, ATC, lab) receive embeddings that fuse structural knowledge from PrimeKG via a relational graph convolution and semantic meaning from ClinicalBERT. This prevents generation of non‑existent codes and ensures related concepts (e.g., a drug and its target disease) occupy nearby latent positions.

2. **LLM‑based auditing**. A separate large language model (Qwen3‑30B) evaluates entire patient trajectories against a structured rubric covering demographic alignment, clinical reasoning (causal chains), and temporal plausibility. Acting as a “Chief Medical Officer”, it assigns a realism score; only records scoring ≥7 are kept. This post‑hoc filter removes semantic hallucinations that the generator’s probability‑driven sampling would otherwise produce.

3. **Synergy of generation and auditing**. The generator ensures statistical fidelity to marginal and co‑occurrence patterns, while the auditor enforces longitudinal clinical consistency. This combination is essential because neither alone suffices: purely statistical generation yields clinically implausible sequences, while a purely rule‑based generator lacks scalability and nuance. The dramatic reduction in Cohen’s d after auditing demonstrates the gap closure.

Break conditions: incomplete PrimeKG mappings, auditor mis‑calibration or outdated knowledge, and over‑/under‑filtering during the synergy stage.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
- Ontology & Knowledge Base (PrimeKG + ClinicalBERT) -> Tokenization Module -> Generator (Coogee‑50M) -> Auditor (Qwen3‑30B) -> Filtering & Post‑processing

Critical path:
- Preprocess MIMIC‑IV: Map codes to atomic tokens, build PrimeKG subgraphs per code, compute frozen knowledge embeddings (RGCN + ClinicalBERT).
- Train Generator on token sequences from training split via causal language modeling objective.
- Generate candidate synthetic records: Initialize with held‑out test demographics. Autoregressive sampling with nucleus sampling. Apply constrained decoding for labs.
- Audit each candidate: Format as CSV, prompt Qwen3‑30B, parse score.
- Filter: Retain only `score ≥ 7`. This yields the final synthetic cohort.

Design tradeoffs:
- Vocabulary size vs. compute: Atomic tokenization (~32k) prevents code hallucination but bloats embedding layer. Mitigated via matrix factorization (embedding dim 100 → 384), reducing trainable parameters.
- Context window vs. trajectory completeness: Fixed 2048‑token window truncates long histories, causing minor divergence in visit/code count tails.
- Auditor sensitivity vs. cohort yield: Threshold `≥7` chosen to balance consistency and sample size; calibrated against human expert distribution.

Failure signatures:
- Generator: High invalid token rate (if mapping fails). Poor R² on unigram/same‑visit/sequential probabilities (<0.90). High validation loss without knowledge embeddings.
- Auditor: Low ICC with human rule‑checker (<0.3). All scores near 1 or 10 indicates rubric failure. Extremely low pass rate (<1%) suggests overly strict threshold.
- Pipeline: Poor TSTR performance (AUROC/F1 significantly below real‑data baseline) indicates over‑aggressive auditing or poor generation.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate statistical fidelity evaluation: compute KS statistics for event gaps, length of stay, and R² for unigram/same‑visit/sequential probabilities; target KS ≤ 0.06, R² ≥ 0.94.
2. Validate auditing impact: re‑run blinded evaluation; submit 20 audited and 20 real records to an LLM or rule‑based script, compute Cohen’s d; expect d < 0.7.
3. Run TSTR benchmark: train a phenotype classifier on audited synthetic data, test on real MIMIC‑IV test set; compare AUROC/F1 to model trained on real data; expect near‑parity (within ~5% absolute).

## Limitations
- Reliance on external knowledge graphs (PrimeKG); missing mappings yield zero structural embeddings and potential concept invalidity.
- LLM auditor shows moderate agreement with human experts (ICC 0.34–0.61), leaving rubric calibration, knowledge currency, and bias as open issues.
- Fixed 2048‑token context truncates long patient histories, possibly distorting longitudinal patterns; validation used limited clinician samples (~20 per condition), risking over‑estimation.
