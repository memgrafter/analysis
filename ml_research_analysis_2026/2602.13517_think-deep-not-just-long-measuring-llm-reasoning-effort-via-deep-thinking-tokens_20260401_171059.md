# Think Deep, Not Just Long: Measuring LLM Reasoning Effort via Deep-Thinking Tokens
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13517
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13517
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Deep-thinking ratio (DTR) strongly correlates with accuracy (average r=0.828) and enables efficient test‑time scaling (Think@n) matching self‑consistency with ~50% cost reduction.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces deep‑thinking ratio (DTR), a metric that quantifies the depth of internal computation during LLM reasoning by measuring how late in the layer stack a token’s prediction stabilizes. Across multiple models and challenging math/science benchmarks, DTR exhibits a robust positive correlation with answer correctness, far surpassing token count. Leveraging DTR, the authors propose Think@n, a test‑time scaling strategy that uses short‑prefix DTR to select high‑quality samples, achieving comparable accuracy to self‑consistency while halving inference cost. The work demonstrates that measuring how deeply models think, rather than how long, provides a more reliable signal for effective and efficient reasoning.

## Method Summary
DTR is computed per token by projecting intermediate hidden states through the unembedding matrix to obtain probability distributions, then measuring Jensen‑Shannon divergence (JSD) between each layer’s distribution and the final layer’s distribution. The settling depth \(c_t\) is the first layer where cumulative JSD falls below a threshold \(g\). Tokens with \(c_t\) in the late regime (e.g., layers \(\ge 0.85L\)) are counted as deep‑thinking; DTR is the proportion of such tokens in the sequence. For test‑time scaling, Think@n samples \(n\) responses, estimates DTR from a short prefix (50 tokens), ranks samples, selects the top \(\eta\)% (e.g., 50%), continues generating only those, and aggregates via majority voting. This early‑stopping approach reduces total token usage while preserving accuracy.

## Key Results
- DTR correlates positively with accuracy (average Pearson \(r = 0.828\)) across four benchmarks, while token count shows a negative correlation (\(r = -0.544\)).
- Think@n matches or outperforms standard self‑consistency (Cons@n) in accuracy while reducing inference cost by approximately 50%.
- The correlation holds across multiple reasoning‑focused models (GPT‑OSS, DeepSeek‑R1, Qwen3) and is robust to hyperparameter variations (threshold \(g\), depth fraction \(\rho\)).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: DTR quantifies internal deliberation depth.
For each token, the model’s intermediate predictions (via unembedding) evolve across layers. The settling depth \(c_t\) is identified when the Jensen‑Shannon divergence from the final distribution drops below a threshold \(g\). Tokens that stabilize only in the late layers (e.g., \(c_t \ge \lceil 0.85L \rceil\)) are tagged as deep‑thinking. The proportion of such tokens (DTR) reflects the amount of sustained computational effort devoted to the reasoning trace. Evidence: The paper’s definition and technical sections; strong correlation with accuracy supports that deeper internal revision is associated with better reasoning.

### Mechanism 2: High DTR sequences are more likely to be correct.
Because DTR aggregates per‑token deep‑thinking signals, a higher DTR indicates that the model engaged in deeper analysis of the problem. Empirically, DTR shows a robust positive correlation with answer correctness across models and benchmarks, while token count often correlates negatively, suggesting that longer outputs can indicate overthinking. This relationship is the cornerstone for using DTR as a quality signal.

### Mechanism 3: Prefix DTR enables efficient sample selection.
Think@n estimates DTR from a short prefix (e.g., 50 tokens) of each sampled response. This early estimate predicts the full‑sequence DTR and thus the answer quality. By ranking samples on prefix DTR and selecting only the top \(\eta\)% for full generation, Think@n reduces total tokens while maintaining accuracy comparable to generating all samples fully (self‑consistency). Evidence: Experiments show ~50% cost reduction with no accuracy drop.

## Foundational Learning
- **Unembedding and vocabulary projection**: Transform an intermediate hidden state into a token distribution by multiplying by the unembedding matrix \(W_U\) and applying softmax. *Why needed*: DTR requires obtaining predictive distributions for each layer to compute divergence. *Quick check*: How do you transform an intermediate hidden state into a token distribution?
- **Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD)**: A symmetric measure of similarity between two probability distributions, computed as \(H((p+q)/2) - 0.5(H(p)+H(q))\). *Why needed*: JSD quantifies how far an intermediate‑layer distribution is from the final distribution, enabling identification of settling depth. *Quick check*: Why is Jensen–Shannon divergence preferred over KL divergence in this context?
- **Test‑time scaling and self‑consistency**: The paradigm of generating multiple reasoning traces and aggregating via majority voting to improve accuracy. *Why needed*: Think@n builds on this baseline to reduce cost. *Quick check*: What is self‑consistency in LLM reasoning?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Autoregressive language model (L layers) -> Unembedding matrix \(W_U\) -> JSD calculator (computes divergence between intermediate and final distributions) -> Settling depth detector (finds first layer where cumulative JSD ≤ threshold \(g\)) -> Deep‑thinking classifier (compares depth to late regime) -> DTR aggregator (computes proportion of deep‑thinking tokens) -> Think@n orchestrator (samples n responses, estimates prefix DTR, ranks, selects top η%, handles early stopping, aggregates answers).

**Critical path**:  
1. Generate sequence token‑by‑token, storing hidden states \(h_{t,l}\) for all layers.  
2. For each token, compute predictive distributions \(p_{t,l} = \text{softmax}(W_U h_{t,l})\) for all layers \(l\).  
3. Compute JSD between \(p_{t,l}\) and final distribution \(p_{t,L}\); track cumulative minima \(\bar{D}_{t,l}\).  
4. Determine settling depth \(c_t = \min\{l : \bar{D}_{t,l} \le g\}\).  
5. Tag token as deep‑thinking if \(c_t \ge \lceil (1-\rho)L \rceil\).  
6. After generation (or prefix), compute DTR = (# deep‑thinking tokens) / total tokens.  
7. For Think@n: repeat for n samples, compute prefix DTR (first ℓprefix tokens), rank, select top η%, continue generating those fully, then majority vote.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Settling threshold \(g\): Higher values require stricter convergence, lowering DTR and potentially reducing discriminative power.  
- Depth fraction \(\rho\): Smaller \(\rho\) expands the late regime, increasing DTR; larger \(\rho\) focuses on deepest layers but may reduce variability.  
- Prefix length ℓprefix: Shorter prefixes reduce overhead but may poorly estimate full DTR; paper found 50 tokens effective.  
- Retention percentage η: Smaller η yields greater cost reduction but risks discarding correct samples if DTR is noisy; η=50% balanced stability.  
- Sample count n: Larger n improves candidate diversity but increases base cost; benefits scale with n.

**Failure signatures**:  
- DTR shows weak or negative correlation with accuracy for some model/benchmark combinations, indicating limited discriminative ability.  
- Prefix DTR correlates poorly with full DTR, causing misranking in Think@n.  
- Overly strict parameters (high \(g\), high \(\rho\)) produce near‑zero DTR values, collapsing the metric.  
- Think@n with very small η (e.g., 25%) may underperform due to insufficient voting robustness.  
- The method assumes DTR is comparable across sequences of different lengths; normalization may be misleading.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Correlation benchmark**: Compute DTR for sampled outputs of eight model variants on AIME 24/25, HMMT 25, and GPQA‑D. Compare Pearson correlation with token count and confidence baselines; DTR achieves average r=0.828, far surpassing others.  
2. **Hyperparameter analysis**: On GPQA‑D using OSS‑120B‑high, sweep \(g \in \{0.25,0.5,0.75\}\) and \(\rho \in \{0.8,0.85,0.9,0.95\}\). Find \(g=0.5, \rho=0.85\) yields robust high correlation; \(g\) impacts correlation more than \(\rho\).  
3. **Think@n efficiency**: For each model and benchmark, set n=48, η=50%, ℓprefix=50. Compare Think@n against Cons@n, Mean@n, Long@n, Short@n, and Self‑Certainty@n. Think@n matches/exceeds Cons@n accuracy while cutting cost ~49–50%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can we train language models to increase their deep‑thinking ratio (DTR), and does increasing DTR improve reasoning accuracy? The paper only measures DTR passively; no interventions were tested. Resolving this would require fine‑tuning with DTR‑rewarding objectives and evaluating accuracy gains.
2. What is a principled method for selecting the settling threshold \(g\) and depth fraction \(\rho\) for DTR across different models and tasks? The paper fixed \(g=0.5, \rho=0.85\) based on limited hyperparameter sweeps; a general calibration method is needed.
3. Does DTR maintain a positive correlation with accuracy on non‑mathematical reasoning tasks, such as commonsense or multi‑modal reasoning? Evaluation was restricted to math/science benchmarks; generalizability is unknown.
4. Can the linear projection (unembedding matrix) used to obtain intermediate predictions in DTR be replaced with more expressive transformations (e.g., learned affine probes or non‑linear decoders) to better identify deep‑thinking tokens? The method assumes the unembedding matrix is sufficient; alternative projections might improve the signal.

## Limitations
- GPT‑OSS checkpoints are private, preventing independent verification.
- DTR hyperparameters (\(g\), \(\rho\)) were tuned on a single benchmark; their generalizability to other models/tasks is uncertain.
- The predictive power of prefix DTR for full‑sequence DTR—and thus for Think@n selection—is not thoroughly established; poor alignment could cause misranking.

## Confidence
- *DTR positively correlates with accuracy*: High – robust average Pearson \(r = 0.828\) across four benchmarks and multiple models, despite isolated negative outliers.
- *Think@n achieves comparable accuracy to self‑consistency at ≈50% lower token cost*: High – consistent results in Table 2 and Figure 5; early‑stopping and prefix‑based selection are well‑supported.
- *Deep‑thinking tokens reflect sustained internal deliberation*: Medium – the mechanism hinges on JSD‑based settling depth; direct evidence (e.g., probing) is absent, and alternative interpretations are possible.
