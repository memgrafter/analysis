# Modular Multi-Task Learning for Chemical Reaction Prediction
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10404
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10404
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LoRA fine-tuning matches full fine-tuning accuracy on C-H reaction prediction while preserving 40-80% accuracy on general tasks versus near 0% for full fine-tuning.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) as a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for chemical reaction prediction with large language models. The authors address catastrophic forgetting—a critical issue when fine-tuning on limited domain-specific datasets like C-H functionalisation reactions. Using ByT5-small models pre-trained on USPTO reactions, they compare LoRA against full fine-tuning across forward prediction, reagent prediction, and multi-task settings. Results demonstrate that LoRA achieves comparable accuracy (e.g., 78.3% vs 69.6% Acc@1 on C-H forward prediction) while drastically reducing forgetting: after adaptation, LoRA retains 40–80% accuracy on general reaction tasks, whereas full fine-tuning nearly obliterates this knowledge (near 0%). In multi-task scenarios, LoRA maintains balanced performance (~75% Acc@1 for both forward and reagent prediction) while full fine-tuning suffers severe interference (56% for both). The modular nature of LoRA—swapping adapters without altering base weights—enables efficient adaptation to new chemical domains with minimal loss of prior expertise.

## Method Summary
The study builds on a pre-trained encoder-decoder language model (ByT5-small) initially fine-tuned on the USPTO dataset for multiple reaction prediction tasks (forward, retrosynthesis, reagent). To adapt this multi-task model to a specialized domain (C-H borylation) with only 557–684 examples, the authors employ Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). LoRA freezes the base model weights and introduces trainable low-rank matrices (A, B) into the attention layers, effectively updating only a small fraction of parameters (e.g., rank r=16). The authors compare LoRA to standard full fine-tuning on metrics including top-1 accuracy (Acc@1) for forward and reagent prediction, and measure catastrophic forgetting by evaluating on the original USPTO test set after adaptation. Hyperparameters such as scaling factor α=32 and dropout 0.0 were selected based on preliminary experiments. The modular design allows different LoRA modules for different tasks or domains, enabling task switching without retraining.

## Key Results
- LoRA achieves 78.3% Acc@1 on C-H forward prediction, comparable to full fine-tuning's 69.6% (ByT5-small).
- After C-H fine-tuning, LoRA retains 40–80% accuracy on general USPTO reaction tasks, while full fine-tuning drops to near 0%.
- In multi-task fine-tuning (forward + reagent) on C-H, LoRA maintains ~75% Acc@1 for both tasks, whereas full fine-tuning performance declines to 56% for both.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LoRA mitigates catastrophic forgetting by isolating task-specific updates within low-rank adapter matrices, leaving the bulk of the base model's weights unchanged.
- Mechanism: During fine-tuning, LoRA freezes the pre-trained weight matrix **W** and learns only the small matrices **A** and **B** (where ∆**W** = **BA**). All new, dataset-specific knowledge is contained within these adapters. When switching tasks, different LoRA modules can be loaded without altering **W**, thereby preserving the general chemical knowledge encoded in the base model.
- Core assumption: The base model's frozen weights retain sufficient general-purpose chemical understanding, and task-specific adaptation can be effectively captured by a low-rank update.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "LoRA achieves comparable accuracy to full fine-tuning... but significantly mitigates forgetting: after C-H fine-tuning, LoRA retains 40–80% accuracy on general reaction tasks versus near 0% for full fine-tuning."
  - [section 2.3.2] "After further full fine-tuning using the C-H dataset, the model’s performance collapses, with Acc@1 falling below 1%... In contrast, the LoRA fine-tuned model retains a substantial amount... accuracy remains above 80% for some classes and between 40%-65% for the remaining classes."
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers discuss catastrophic forgetting in LLMs generally but do not specifically anchor LoRA's mechanism in this chemical context.
- Break condition: If the base model weights (**W**) are inadvertently updated during what is supposed to be LoRA fine-tuning (e.g., incorrect implementation), the isolation is broken and forgetting mitigation fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) can achieve accuracy comparable to full fine-tuning because the low-rank constraint acts as an effective inductive bias, learning the most significant task-specific directions in weight space without overfitting on limited data.
- Mechanism: The rank **r** parameter controls the capacity of the adapter (∆**W** = **BA**). A sufficiently large **r** (e.g., 16 as used) allows the adapter to capture the essential task-specific signal from the small C-H dataset, while the low-rank structure prevents it from memorizing noise, leading to generalizable performance similar to updating all parameters.
- Core assumption: The intrinsic dimensionality of task-specific updates for chemical reaction prediction is low; the most important adaptations can be expressed in a subspace of the full weight matrix.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "LoRA achieves accuracy comparable to full fine-tuning... LoRA achieves 78.3% vs 69.6% Acc@1 for C-H forward prediction with ByT5-small."
  - [section 2.1] "LoRA on average achieves marginally higher average accuracy than full fine-tuning (by 0.3–2%), with larger LoRA ranks (r) generally yielding a further improvement by a few percent."
  - [section 2.3.1] Table 1 shows LoRA (r=16) achieves 78.3% Acc@1, comparable to full fine-tuning's 69.6%.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., on Uni-Mol3) explore multi-molecular foundations but do not directly compare LoRA vs. full fine-tuning performance on reaction prediction.
- Break condition: If the dataset is extremely large or the task requires highly dispersed weight updates that cannot be captured by a low-rank matrix (r too small), LoRA's accuracy would significantly lag behind full fine-tuning.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LoRA's modular architecture enables better multi-task performance preservation because each task (e.g., forward prediction, reagent prediction) can be associated with its own independent adapter, preventing negative interference between tasks during adaptation.
- Mechanism: In a multi-task model, fine-tuning for a new domain (e.g., C-H reactions) with LoRA involves learning a new adapter **A**, **B** for that domain while keeping the base model **W** frozen. The base model already encodes knowledge for other general tasks. Since adapters for different tasks or domains are separate, updating one does not overwrite the parameters (even indirectly via gradient dynamics) that are important for another task.
- Core assumption: The base model's frozen weights provide a stable, shared representation space that is sufficiently rich for multiple related tasks, and task-specific deviations can be additively modeled by independent low-rank updates.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "LoRA... better preserving multi-task performance... LoRA maintains 75% Acc@1 for both tasks, while full fine-tuning drops to 56%."
  - [section 2.3.3] "When task-specific fine-tuned in the multi-task fashion with both forward reaction and reagent prediction, LoRA was able to achieve excellent performance for both tasks at 75 Acc@1, while the full fine-tuning results in poorer performance of both tasks at 56 Acc@1."
  - [section 1, Page 3] "one base model can support multiple LoRA modules - switching tasks/datasets then means switching LoRA modules, not retraining the entire model, thus avoids interference between tasks."
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper "Beyond Chemical QA..." mentions modular chemical operations, but does not directly evidence LoRA's multi-task advantage.
- Break condition: If the base model (**W**) is not sufficiently pre-trained on the broader distribution of tasks, adding adapters cannot recover missing foundational knowledge, and multi-task performance will be poor regardless of the fine-tuning method.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Catastrophic Forgetting
  - Why needed here: The central problem the paper addresses is that standard full fine-tuning causes models to lose previously learned general chemical knowledge when adapted to a small, specialized dataset. Understanding this phenomenon is prerequisite to appreciating why LoRA's parameter isolation helps.
  - Quick check question: What is the typical outcome for a multi-task model's performance on its original tasks after full fine-tuning on a new, narrow dataset?
- Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)
  - Why needed here: LoRA is a specific PEFT method. The paper's entire premise is that updating only a small subset of parameters (adapters) is a viable alternative to full fine-tuning for chemical models. Understanding the goals of PEFT (efficiency, reduced forgetting) is key.
  - Quick check question: How does LoRA technically reduce the number of trainable parameters compared to full fine-tuning?
- Concept: Multi-Task Learning in Chemistry
  - Why needed here: The base models are trained on multiple inter-related tasks (forward, retrosynthesis, reagent prediction). The challenge is to adapt such a model to a new reaction type (C-H) without degrading performance on the existing tasks. This concept frames the "multi-task performance preservation" result.
  - Quick check question: Why might fine-tuning a multi-task model on a single new domain lead to interference and forgetting of other tasks?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: 
  Base Model (pre-trained, frozen) -> LoRA Adapters (trainable low-rank matrices) -> Task-Specific Output (via task prefixes)
- Critical path:
  1. Start with a general multi-task fine-tuned base model (**W**) that already performs well on broad chemistry.
  2. For a new, limited dataset (e.g., C-H), add LoRA adapters to the model's linear layers. Keep **W** frozen.
  3. Train only the adapter parameters on the new dataset, using the same multi-task setup (with prefixes) or single-task.
  4. Inference: Load the base model **W** and the learned adapter(s) for the desired domain/task.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - **Rank (r)**: Lower rank (e.g., 4) = fewer parameters, less risk of overfitting/forgetting, but may underfit complex new patterns. Higher rank (e.g., 16) = more capacity, potentially higher accuracy on new task, but more risk of forgetting if base is not perfectly frozen or if dataset is tiny. The paper found r=16 worked best for C-H.
  - **Alpha (α)**: Scaling factor for adapter output. Larger α increases the magnitude of the LoRA update. Must be balanced with learning rate. Paper used α=32 with r=16 (scale factor=2).
  - **Dropout**: Applied to adapter input to prevent overfitting. Paper found 0.0 dropout was stable for their setting; 0.05 was tested but not superior.
- Failure signatures:
  - **Persistent Catastrophic Forgetting**: If after LoRA fine-tuning, the model still loses >50% accuracy on general USPTO tasks (Section 2.3.2 benchmark), check: (a) base model weights are truly frozen (implementation bug), (b) learning rate is too high causing adapter to "drift" base functionality, (c) LoRA rank is too low to adequately capture new domain.
  - **Poor Task-Specific Accuracy**: If LoRA accuracy on C-H forward prediction is significantly below ~78% (Table 1), likely causes: (a) LoRA rank too low, (b) insufficient training epochs, (c) learning rate not optimal.
  - **Multi-Task Interference**: If in multi-task fine-tuning (e.g., forward + reagent on C-H), performance on one task drops significantly (like full fine-tuning's 56% in Table 2), check: (a) LoRA adapter is being used for *both* tasks but the tasks conflict—consider separate adapters per task, (b) base model's multi-task knowledge was weak initially.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Replicate Forgetting Mitigation Benchmark**: Take a general multi-task ByT5 model. Perform full fine-tuning on a small subset (e.g., one USPTO class). Evaluate Acc@1 on the other 9 classes. Then repeat with LoRA (r=16, α=32). Expect: full fine-tuning drops to ~0%, LoRA retains 40-80%. Validates core claim.
  2. **Multi-Task Adaptation on C-H**: Using the C-H borylation dataset, fine-tune a general model in a multi-task fashion (forward + reagent prediction) using LoRA. Evaluate both tasks on the C-H test set and on the 10 USPTO classes. Expect: ~75% Acc@1 on both C-H tasks and >40% on general tasks. Contrast with full fine-tuning's drop to 56% on C-H tasks and ~0% on general.
  3. **Out-of-Distribution Generalization Probe**: For a fine-tuned model (full or LoRA) on C-H, take a common reagent from training (e.g., THF). Generate top-5 alternative reagent predictions. Manually/chemically evaluate if suggestions are plausible (similar polarity, function) and if any are truly OOD (not in C-H or USPTO train sets). Validates that learning is not mere memorization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How does LoRA's modular adaptation mechanistically preserve general chemical knowledge during task-specific fine-tuning?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper empirically shows LoRA retains 40-80% accuracy on general tasks vs. ~0% for full fine-tuning (Figure 6) but doesn't explain the mechanism, calling for "more systematic interpretation" in future work.
- Why unresolved: Mechanistic insight would enable design of better PEFT methods and predict when LoRA will be effective.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of how base model parameters remain unchanged in LoRA versus full fine-tuning; probing for retained chemical concepts; gradient-based attribution of task-specific vs. general knowledge.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Do LoRA's advantages hold for modern decoder-only LLMs and extremely limited datasets typical of real-world chemistry applications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The study uses T5 encoder-decoder models on datasets with 557-684 examples per reaction type (C-H) but acknowledges "the modular solutions are equally applicable to the latest generation of LLMs" without testing them. Real-world domain-specific datasets are often smaller.
- Why unresolved: Decoder-only architectures dominate current LLMs and may behave differently; extremely small datasets exacerbate forgetting, testing LoRA's limits.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replication with LLaMA/Mistral models on datasets with <100 examples per reaction type, measuring accuracy and forgetting compared to full fine-tuning.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the optimal LoRA hyperparameters for balancing accuracy, forgetting, and multi-task performance in chemical reaction prediction?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Methods state hyperparameter search was "very constrained" due to computational limits, testing only r=4,16 and α=8,32, which may not be optimal across tasks.
- Why unresolved: Hyperparameters control the capacity vs. stability trade-off; optimal settings may differ for forward vs. reagent prediction, or for single- vs. multi-task settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive hyperparameter optimization across wide ranges (r=1-64, α=1-64) for all three prediction tasks, measuring performance on accuracy, forgetting, and multi-task interference metrics.

## Limitations
- Dataset Specificity: Results are confined to USPTO reaction classes and a single C-H functionalisation dataset; generalisability to other reaction types is unknown.
- Mechanistic Evidence: The claim that frozen base weights preserve general knowledge is inferred from performance retention, not directly probed (e.g., via weight analysis or ablation).
- Hyperparameter Sensitivity: Optimal LoRA hyperparameters (r=16, α=32) were determined empirically; breakpoints for other models/tasks are not established.

## Confidence
- Forgetting Mitigation: High
- Accuracy Parity: Medium
- Multi-Task Preservation: Medium

## Next Checks

2. Adapter Interference Probe: Train separate LoRA adapters for forward and reagent prediction on C-H, then evaluate each task with only its dedicated adapter loaded. If multi-task performance drops, interference occurs within a single adapter; if maintained, confirms modular benefit.
3. Chemical Plausibility Audit: Beyond top-1 accuracy, evaluate out-of-distribution reagent suggestions from LoRA vs. full fine-tuning using a chemist-rated plausibility score (e.g., 1–5 scale for solvent polarity, reaction compatibility).
