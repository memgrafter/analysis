# Sharp Capacity Scaling of Spectral Optimizers in Learning Associative Memory

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.26554
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26554
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Muon achieves a storage capacity scaling of d^{1+1/(2α)} versus SGD's d^{1/(2α)} on a linear associative memory task with power-law frequency distributions, and it sustains gains up to a critical batch size of e^{Θ(d^{α+1/2})} compared to e^{Θ(√d)} for SGD.

## Executive Summary
The paper analyzes spectral optimizers, focusing on Muon, for large-scale training using a linear associative memory task. Data consist of Gaussian embeddings with frequencies following a power law p_i ∝ i^{-α} (α>1). The core method analytically compares one-step and multi-step recovery dynamics of Muon and SGD. Primary results show Muon's one-step storage capacity scales as d^{1+1/(2α)}, significantly exceeding SGD's d^{1/(2α)}. Muon also maintains this advantage up to a much larger critical batch size (e^{Θ(d^{α+1/2})} vs. e^{Θ(√d)}). Both optimizers eventually converge to the information-theoretic limit e^{Θ(d²)} with sufficient steps, but Muon achieves a substantially faster initial recovery rate. These scaling laws are confirmed through synthetic experiments and in-context recall tasks with transformers.

## Method Summary
The study considers learning a linear associative memory via multiclass logistic regression on a weight matrix W. Each item i has a pair of d-dimensional embeddings u_i, v_i drawn i.i.d. from N(0, I_d/d). The frequencies p_i follow a power law with exponent α>1. The optimizers are: Muon, which applies a stabilized spectral map h_λ(G) = G(G^T G + λ^2 I)^{-1/2} to the gradient G (approximating the polar decomposition), and SGD, which updates W using the raw gradient. Storage capacity is defined as the number of top-frequency items i for which the model correctly recalls the association, i.e., u_i^T W v_i > max_{j≠i} u_j^T W v_i. The authors derive scaling laws by analyzing the singular value spectrum of the gradient matrix, which concentrates around d^{-α} due to the Gaussian embeddings and power-law frequencies. Theoretical predictions for one-step and multi-step capacity are validated with synthetic experiments varying d, batch size B, and α, as well as with in-context recall experiments using small transformers.

## Key Results
- Muon's one-step storage capacity scales as d^{1+1/(2α)}, while SGD's scales as d^{1/(2α)}.
- Muon's critical batch size scales as e^{Θ(d^{α+1/2})}, far larger than SGD's e^{Θ(√d)}.
- Multi-step Muon recovery follows d_t = e^{Θ(min{d^{2-(1-1/(2α))^t}, B^{1/α}})} and reaches the information-theoretic limit e^{Θ(d²)} faster than SGD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Spectral Amplification via Resolution Tuning
Muon's stabilized spectral map amplifies the gradient's low singular values by a factor ~λ^{-1}. By setting λ ∝ d^{-α} (the bulk eigenvalue scale), the amplification factor becomes d^α, directly boosting the signal for low-frequency items (p_i ≲ λ). This yields a one-step capacity of d^{1+1/(2α)} items, compared to d^{1/(2α)} for SGD. The analysis relies on the concentration of gradient singular values around d^{-α} under isotropic Gaussian embeddings and power-law frequencies. Choosing λ outside Θ(d^{-α}) either over-amplifies noise or under-amplifies signal; the mechanism fails for non-Gaussian embeddings where the bulk spectrum deviates.

### Critical Batch Size Expansion
Muon's one-step capacity is the minimum of d^{1+1/(2α)} and B^{1/α} (with B the batch size). The crosspoint B⋆ ≈ d^{α+1/2} defines a critical batch size: for B ≲ B⋆, capacity grows with B; beyond B⋆, capacity saturates until B becomes exponentially larger. In contrast, SGD's capacity saturates at B ≈ √d. The gap arises because Muon's amplification factor d^α scales with the tail of the power law, allowing it to exploit larger batches to observe rare items. This holds under i.i.d. sampling so that minibatch frequencies approximate the true distribution.

### Deflation-Driven Early Acceleration
In multi-step training, Muon exhibits a deflation effect: after an item is successfully recalled, its predicted probability becomes ~1, effectively removing it from the gradient. The remaining gradient still has a bulk singular value scale d^{-α}. Each subsequent Muon step amplifies this bulk uniformly, increasing the number of recovered items by roughly a factor d^{1/α}. This leads to the recursion d_{t+1} ≈ d^{1/α} d_t^{1-1/(2α)}, driving capacity toward the information-theoretic limit d^2 much faster than SGD, which requires about ⌈2α⌉ steps to recover d items. The analysis approximates the exact dynamics via a deflation model; a full proof for the exact recursion remains open.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Power‑law (Zipf) Distributions  
  Why needed: Determines item frequency tail p_i ∝ i^{-α}; a heavy tail (α>1) creates many low‑frequency items that become a bottleneck for SGD but are solvable by Muon's amplification.  
  Quick check: If α increases, does Muon's capacity gain over SGD increase? (Yes, because SGD's d^{1/(2α)} exponent shrinks.)

- **Concept**: Spectral Optimization (Polar Map)  
  Why needed: Muon updates via h_λ(G) ≈ G(GᵀG)^{-1/2}, directly manipulating the singular spectrum rather than raw gradients.  
  Quick check: Why does h_λ stabilize with λ>0 while exact polar (λ→0) is numerically unstable? (λ avoids division by near-zero singular values; it sets a resolution cutoff.)

- **Concept**: Associative Memory with Gaussian Embeddings  
  Why needed: Provides an information-theoretic capacity Θ(d²) baseline; Gaussian embeddings allow superposition of N ≫ d items and match transformer memorization models.  
  Quick check: Why is orthogonal embedding (prior work) too restrictive? (It forces N ≤ d; here N ~ e^{Θ(d²)}.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Initialize W0 = 0 → Sample minibatch ℬ of size B → Compute gradient G_t = Σ_i q_i u_i v_iᵀ (with empirical freqs q_i) → Apply spectral map h_λ(G_t) (via Newton‑Schulz iterations) → Update W_{t+1} = W_t + η_t h_λ(G_t). Optionally, deflate probabilities for recovered items in analysis.

**Critical path**: The key steps are: gradient computation → singular value decomposition (or approximation) → element‑wise application of h_λ to singular values → update. The resolution parameter λ must be scheduled appropriately: start λ₀ ∝ d^{-α} log^{-2}, and for t≥1 decay λ_t ∝ d^{-α_{t+1}} d^{-1/2} log^{-1} to keep amplification aligned with the bulk spectrum.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- λ vs. noise: Too small λ over‑amplifies noise; too large λ misses low‑frequency signals.
- B vs. capacity: Gains only when B^{1/α} ≳ d^{1+1/(2α)} i.e., B ≳ d^{α+1/2}; very large batches are needed for large α.
- Training stage: Muon excels in early phases due to bulk amplification; later phases converge to SGD‑like rates.

**Failure signatures**:
- No scaling gap when α ≤ 1 or batch size too small (B ≪ √d for SGD, B ≪ d^{α+1/2} for Muon).
- Numerical instability if λ → 0 in exact polar or fixed small λ when bulk eigenvalues drop below λ.
- Mismatched architecture: applying Muon directly to non‑matrix parameters (e.g., embeddings) without adaptation; paper uses AdamW for those.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **One‑step capacity vs. d**: Fixed N=100,000, vary d and α. Fitted exponents ≈ 0.53/α for SGD and ≈ 1.02+0.49/α for Muon, matching theoretical d^{1/(2α)} and d^{1+1/(2α)}.
2. **One‑step capacity vs. B**: Fixed α=1.5, vary B. Muon capacity grows until B ∼ d^{α+1/2} then saturates; SGD plateaus near √d (Figure 3).
3. **Multi‑step capacity**: Fixed N=250,000, run population Muon for T steps. Capacity exponent increases with T, approaching d²; fitted exponents align with Theorem 9 predictions (Figure 4e).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimality of one-step Muon capacity**: Is the d^{1+1/(2α)} scaling the maximum achievable by any bounded spectral optimizer? The paper offers a heuristic argument but lacks a rigorous lower bound.
- **Multi-step capacity without deflation**: Does the exact (non‑deflated) dynamics yield the claimed recursion d_t = e^{Θ(min{d^{2-(1-1/(2α))^t}, B^{1/α}})}? The current theorem assumes a deflation approximation; the precise end‑to‑end guarantee remains conjectural.
- **Batch size scaling with long horizons**: For large number of steps T, does the batch size dependency converge to (T B)^{1/α} as suggested? The analysis only covers constant T.
- **Robustness to anisotropy**: Do the scaling exponents persist under anisotropic or non‑i.i.d. embeddings? The analysis heavily relies on isotropic Gaussian embeddings; real data may violate this assumption.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on idealizations: isotropic Gaussian embeddings, exact power‑law frequencies, and a deflation approximation for multi‑step dynamics.
- Experimental validation is confined to synthetic datasets and small transformer models; scaling to large, diverse architectures is unproven.
- The optimal λ schedule (λ ∝ d^{-α}) may be difficult to implement in practice without exact knowledge of the data spectrum.

## Confidence
