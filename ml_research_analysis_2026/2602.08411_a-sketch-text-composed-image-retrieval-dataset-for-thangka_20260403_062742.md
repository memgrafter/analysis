# A Sketch+Text Composed Image Retrieval Dataset for Thangka

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08411
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08411
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CIRThan introduces a sketch+text composed image retrieval dataset for Thangka, with supervised models achieving 52.03% R@1 and zero-shot methods only 7.96% R@1, highlighting a substantial domain gap.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents CIRThan, the first benchmark for sketch+text composed image retrieval (CIR) focused on culturally specific Thangka art. The dataset contains 2,287 Thangka images, each annotated with a human-drawn sketch and three hierarchical textual descriptions (Level 1: core subject; Level 2: key attributes/relations; Level 3: full contextual detail). This design enables systematic study of fine-grained, domain-specific retrieval requiring structured visual understanding. Experimental results show that supervised CIR models trained on CIRThan reach 52.03% R@1, while zero-shot approaches—including state-of-the-art MLLMs—perform poorly (max 7.96% R@1). Furthermore, retrieval accuracy consistently improves with increased textual granularity, underscoring the value of hierarchical semantics for disambiguating visually similar imagery. The work exposes a significant gap between general-purpose vision‑language models and the demands of knowledge‑specific retrieval, opening directions for research on sketch‑text composition, domain adaptation, and culturally aware representation learning.

## Method Summary
CIRThan comprises 2,227 Thangka paintings, each paired with a fine-grained human-drawn sketch and three hierarchical text descriptions (Level 1: core subject; Level 2: added key attributes and relations; Level 3: full contextual detail). The dataset is split into 1,868 training and 419 test queries. For evaluation, each test image generates three queries, one per text level, combined with its sketch. Standard CIR metrics (Recall@1, @3, @5) are computed by ranking the gallery images against the composed query representation. Supervised models are trained on the training triplets, while zero‑shot methods are applied without in‑domain tuning (some optionally fine‑tuned on CC3M). The primary outcome is the performance disparity: supervised models achieve 52.03% average R@1, whereas zero‑shot methods peak at 7.96% R@1, demonstrating the challenges of transferring general CIR capabilities to fine‑grained cultural domains.

## Key Results
- Supervised CIR models achieve a best average R@1 of 52.03% on CIRThan.
- Zero‑shot methods, including MLLM‑based approaches, top out at only 7.96% R@1.
- Retrieval performance consistently improves with increased textual granularity (Level 1 → Level 2 → Level 3) across all evaluated methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of CIRThan and its benchmarks can be attributed to three key mechanisms:

1. **Fine‑grained visual‑textual alignment via sketch and hierarchical text**. The sketch provides a structural visual anchor that abstracts away irrelevant appearance, focusing on discriminative contours and spatial layouts essential for Thangka composition. The hierarchical text supplies multi‑level semantic constraints (from core subject to full contextual detail). Expert verification ensures cross‑modal consistency, grounding both modalities in the same domain‑specific semantic frame. This synergy enables precise matching between query and target, as evidenced by the high performance of supervised models trained on such data.

2. **Hierarchical granularity systematically improves discrimination**. The three text levels act as controlled increments in semantic richness. Richer descriptions provide additional attributes and relations that disambiguate visually similar Thangka images. The paper reports consistent gains from Level 1 to Level 3 across models, confirming that higher textual detail yields more informative constraints (Section 4.3.3). This indicates that models can effectively exploit incremental semantic information when properly trained.

3. **Substantial domain gap due to fine‑grained alignment deficit**. Zero‑shot methods, even large MLLMs, perform poorly (<8% R@1) compared to supervised models (~52% R@1). This reveals that generic vision‑language pre‑training lacks the aligned representations for sketch abstractions and culturally specific semantics required for Thangka. The failure persists despite scaling model size, pointing to an alignment deficit rather than a capacity limitation (Sections 4.3.2, 4.3.4). Supervised models overcome this by learning domain‑specific feature compositions from in‑domain data.

## Foundational Learning
**Sketch‑based visual abstraction**: Sketches in CIRThan are intentional abstractions preserving structural cues (e.g., deity posture, symbolic object arrangement). A learner must interpret sketch lines as semantically significant contours rather than mere edges.  
*Quick check:* Given two Thangka sketches differing only in the number of arms on a deity, should a model treat this as retrieval‑determining? Why might textual confirmation be needed?

**Hierarchical semantic specification**: The three‑level text design forces models to handle variable detail. A model must use sparse Level 1 queries (“Eleven‑faced Avalokitasvara”) and add constraints from Level 2 (key attributes) and Level 3 (full context) to refine results. This tests compositional reasoning.  
*Quick check:* If a model retrieves correctly with Level 1 but fails with Level 2, what does that suggest about its ability to utilize secondary attributes?

**Domain‑specific visual grammar**: Thangka follows strict canonical rules (mudras, hierarchical placement, color symbolism). Models must implicitly learn these conventions from data to match queries to correct depictions; this knowledge is absent in natural image training.  
*Quick check:* A query mentions “standing on lotus throne with Vajrapani and Manjushri at sides.” Why would a model trained only on natural images likely misuse this spatial‑relational clue?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (data flow):  
Sketch Encoder → Fusion Module ← Text Encoder  
Fusion Module → Composed Query  
Image Encoder → Image Embedding  
Composed Query ↔ Image Embedding → Similarity → Ranking

**Critical path**:  
1. Receive input: sketch `I_s` and text `T` (one of three levels).  
2. Encode sketch via Sketch Encoder → `f_sketch`.  
3. Encode text via Text Encoder → `f_text`.  
4. Fuse: `q = Fusion(f_sketch, f_text)`.  
5. For each candidate image `I_t` in gallery: encode via Image Encoder → `f_img`.  
6. Compute similarity score `s = sim(q, f_img)`.  
7. Rank all candidates by `s`, return top‑K.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Sketch Encoder detail: Higher resolution captures fine lines but increases noise and compute; simplified encoder may miss key structural details.  
- Text granularity handling: Separate heads per level add parameters; a single robust module may overfit to a specific detail level.  
- Fusion simplicity vs. capacity: Early fusion (concatenation + linear layer) is simple but may not model interactions well; attention‑based fusion is powerful but needs more data and risks overfitting on the small dataset.  
- Image Encoder scale: High‑resolution Thangka images (avg 1700×1200) require large ViTs for accuracy but incur significant computational cost for retrieval.

**Failure signatures**:  
1. **Sketch‑only/Text‑only collapse**: Model’s composed‑query performance drops to near the sketch‑only or text‑only baseline, indicating failed fusion.  
2. **Level‑agnostic performance**: No significant gain from Level 1→3, suggesting the model cannot exploit additional textual detail (weak text encoder or non‑hierarchical fusion).  
3. **Zero‑shot transfer failure**: A model pre‑trained on a general CIR dataset (e.g., FashionIQ) performs at zero‑shot levels on CIRThan, confirming domain specificity.  
4. **High error on long‑tailed subjects**: Performance on the “Others” category (34.5% of data) is catastrophically worse than on head subjects, indicating failure to learn fine‑grained distinctions for rare symbolic elements.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Replicate supervised baseline**: Train the top performer (ENCODER) on CIRThan train split; verify reported R@1 ~52% on test.  
2. **Ablation on text levels**: For a fixed trained model (e.g., CLIP4Cir), evaluate test queries using only Level 1, Level 2, Level 3 texts separately; plot R@1 vs. text level.  
3. **Zero‑shot transfer analysis**: Take a model pre‑trained on a large general CIR dataset (e.g., CompoDiff on SynthTriplets18M) and evaluate on CIRThan test without fine‑tuning; measure the performance drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do users realistically formulate sketch+text composed queries for fine‑grained cultural imagery, and how can CIR models effectively integrate these multi‑level semantic intents?**  
   *Basis*: Existing CIR datasets lack realistic user query data for cultural domains; CIRThan provides such data but models are not designed to capture query patterns and intent composition.  
   *What would resolve*: User studies with cultural experts generating queries, analysis of query patterns, and development of models that explicitly model composition intent and user expertise levels.

2. **What architectural innovations are necessary for CIR models to achieve fine‑grained alignment between sketch+text queries and target images in knowledge‑specific domains, particularly regarding instance‑level attributes, symbolic elements, and structured relations?**  
   *Basis*: Current CIR models focus on general‑domain attribute changes and lack mechanisms to handle dense, structured semantics and symbolic knowledge specific to cultural domains.  
   *What would resolve*: New model architectures incorporating structured reasoning (e.g., knowledge graphs, relation networks), evaluated on CIRThan with ablations showing importance of structural components.

3. **Why do multimodal large language models (MLLMs) underperform in zero‑shot sketch+text CIR for cultural domains, and how can they be adapted or augmented to overcome limitations in sketch understanding and domain‑specific knowledge?**  
   *Basis*: MLLMs are pretrained on natural images and text; sketches and cultural semantics are underrepresented; no systematic adaptation techniques exist for this setting.  
   *What would resolve*: Error analysis of MLLM predictions on CIRThan (e.g., sketch misinterpretation, cultural term ignorance), development of domain adaptation methods (e.g., fine‑tuning on cultural data, integrating knowledge bases), evaluation showing improved zero‑shot performance.

## Limitations
- Dataset scale (2.3k images) is small, risking overfitting to Thangka‑specific terms and limiting generalizability.
- Sketch drawing variations (artist styles, abstraction levels) are not quantified, which could affect structural consistency and model robustness.
- The cause of zero‑shot failure (sketch understanding deficit vs. semantic gap) remains ambiguous without controlled ablations.
