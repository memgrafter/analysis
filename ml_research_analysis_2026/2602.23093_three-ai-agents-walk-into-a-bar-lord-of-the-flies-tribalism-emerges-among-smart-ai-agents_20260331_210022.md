# Three AI-agents walk into a bar . . . . `Lord of the Flies' tribalism emerges among smart AI-Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23093
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23093
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** LLM agents in a shared resource congestion task consistently underperform a capacity-matching random baseline, with overload rates up to 72.5% and emergent behavioral clusters (Opportunistic, Aggressive, Conservative) that never achieve steady, baseline-like behavior.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the collective behavior of large language model (LLM) agents in a classic congestion control scenario, the El Farol Bar problem. In this setting, multiple agents independently decide each round whether to request a limited shared resource; if total requests exceed capacity, all requests fail. Counter to expectations, the authors find that LLM agents perform significantly worse than a simple capacity-matching random baseline, with overload rates reaching up to 72.5% for large models compared to 31.25% for the baseline. Through extensive simulations (43 experiments, 154 agents), they observe that agents self-organize into three distinct behavioral clusters: Opportunistic agents frequently request and contribute disproportionately to overloads; Aggressive agents are moderately efficient but still overload; Conservative agents rarely request and suffer prolonged starvation. Notably, no agent approximates the steady, baseline-like strategy that would optimize system safety. The study further reveals that larger reasoning capacity correlates with higher systemic failure, suggesting that increased intelligence can actually degrade collective outcomes in decentralized settings.

The authors attribute these results to three primary mechanisms. First, shared inductive biases in LLM reasoning induce positive correlation in actions, inflating aggregate variance beyond the independent baseline. Second, short history windows encourage agents to overfit to noise, leading to herding and synchronized over-requesting. Third, personality prompts initially bias demand, but multi-agent feedback locks the system into the observed failure clusters without converging to a capacity-matching equilibrium. These findings highlight critical risks in deploying LLM agents for resource allocation and call for interventions—such as explicit randomization or coordination mechanisms—to ensure safety.

## Method Summary
The study implements the El Farol Bar problem: N agents each decide per round whether to request one unit from a shared capacity C. A simulator tracks total requests; overload occurs if requests > C, and all requesting agents receive no payoff. Agents are LLMs (Gemini, GPT, Claude) provided with game rules, values of N and C, a sliding window of the last 5–10 attendance numbers, their own recent payoff history, and a personality prompt (Neutral, Risk‑averse, Contrarian, Trend‑follower, Optimist, Pessimist). The LLM outputs a JSON decision (Go/Stay) along with a brief reasoning. An exploration wrapper (ε‑greedy with ε=0.15 or temperature sampling with T=0.7) may randomly flip the final action to introduce stochasticity. Over 43 simulation runs, the authors vary N∈{3,4}, C∈{1,2}, model size (small vs. large), and exploration method. Each run lasts 30–100 rounds. Performance is compared to the capacity‑matching random baseline, where each agent independently requests with probability p = C/N (yielding a binomial distribution of total demand). The primary metrics are system overload rate, resource utilization, fairness (starvation periods), and effective diversity S_eff. Additionally, per‑agent behavioral metrics (request frequency, success count, max starvation, burst variance, efficiency, overload contribution) are used for clustering analysis.

## Key Results
- LLM agents underperform the random baseline: overload rates reach 72.5% for large models and 53.8% for small models, versus 31.25% for the baseline (N=4, C=2).
- Behavioral clustering of 154 agents yields three persistent types: Opportunistic (48.1%, high request frequency 0.845, contributes 73.7% of overloads); Aggressive (27.3%, highest efficiency 0.624); Conservative (24.7%, suffers severe starvation with mean max 73.5 rounds).
- No agent exhibits steady, baseline-like behavior (request frequency near C/N), and larger model capacity correlates with worse safety, contradicting the intuition that smarter agents yield better collective outcomes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Correlated reasoning**  
- **Claim:** Shared inductive biases in LLM reasoning cause positively correlated actions, inflating aggregate variance and overload probability beyond the independent baseline.  
- **Core assumption:** Agents use similar deterministic logic over short shared histories (5–10 rounds), leading to synchronization.  
- **Evidence:** Overload rates exceed binomial baseline; formal derivation shows Var(A) = Np(1-p)[1+(N-1)ρ]; simulations show high effective diversity S_eff ∼ N indicating correlated actions.  
- **Break condition:** If agents employed independent, calibrated randomisation (p=C/N) after reasoning, correlation would vanish and overload rates would match the baseline.

**Mechanism 2: Overfitting to noise in short histories**  
- **Claim:** Bounded history windows encourage agents to overfit to random fluctuations, producing herding and spurious pattern‑based coordination (e.g., “empty last 2 rounds → will be empty now”).  
- **Core assumption:** Pattern‑matching is over‑applied to short, noisy time series.  
- **Evidence:** Trend‑follower prompt yields 74% Opportunistic agents, the cluster with extreme overload contribution; synchronized spikes in requests are observed when multiple agents infer the same near‑future state from recent attendance.  
- **Break condition:** Provide longer, statistically meaningful histories or dampen pattern‑matching with explicit exploration/noise injection at decision stage.

**Mechanism 3: Feedback‑driven clustering without convergence**  
- **Claim:** Personality prompts set initial demand dispositions, but multi‑agent feedback dynamics sort agents into three failure modes with no steady baseline‑like behaviour emerging.  
- **Core assumption:** Learning from pairwise‑correlated outcomes lacks a convergent dynamics toward the safety‑optimal stochastic policy when agents reason about histories.  
- **Evidence:** Clustering reveals three distinct types, none with request frequency ≈ C/N; even the most cautious (All‑Risk‑Averse) falls slightly below the target but not significantly; repeated interactions reinforce extremes (high requesters see occasional wins, low requesters retreat further).  
- **Break condition:** Introduce an explicit learning target (e.g., penalise deviation of personal p_i from C/N) or enable communication to coordinate p collectively.

## Foundational Learning

**El Farol/Minority Game equilibrium**  
- **Why needed:** Understands why p=C/N is the safety‑oriented baseline, not a Nash equilibrium for individual payoffs. The paper contrasts capacity‑matching (system safety) with payoff‑optimal (individual wins) baselines.  
- **Quick check:** If each agent wants to maximise their own wins, what mixed strategy p maximises expected payoff for N=3, C=1, and how does its overload probability compare to p=C/N?

**Variance inflation under correlation**  
- **Why needed:** Appendix A.9 provides the key formula Var(A) = Np(1-p)[1+(N-1)ρ]. This quantifies why correlated AI behaviour is dangerous for congestion control.  
- **Quick check:** For N=4, p=0.5, independent overload prob=31.25%. What overload probability results if ρ=0.2?

**Behavioural clustering validity**  
- **Why needed:** Paper claims three emergent types. Must understand silhouette scores, Kruskal–Wallis effect sizes (η²), and caveats (features used for clustering are also used for testing; intra‑run dependence reduces effective sample size).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
1. Prompt template (injects rules, N, C, history)  
2. LLM inference engine (generates reasoning and action)  
3. Exploration wrapper (ε‑greedy flip ε=0.15 or temperature T=0.7)  
4. Environment simulator (computes total demand, awards payoffs, updates history)  
5. Metrics logger (records per‑agent and system metrics)

**Critical path:** History buffer → prompt construction → LLM call → action perturbation → environment step → payoff/reward → back to history update. Any latency or non‑determinism in LLM calls can break round timing assumptions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Deterministic reasoning vs. stochastic safety: Pure LLM reasoning (low temperature, no ε‑greedy) minimises decision noise but maximises correlation → unsafe. ε‑greedy adds decision‑level noise but may conflict with model’s “reasoning” output.  
- History length: Longer histories might reduce noise overfitting but increase context length/cost and may introduce spurious long‑term patterns.  
- Prompt simplicity vs. control: Personality prompts are simple but coarse; more structured prompts (e.g., “your p should target C/N”) might improve safety but risk overfitting to this specific experiment.

**Failure signatures:**  
- System-level: Overload rate > binomial baseline for same (N,C); high S_eff (∼N) indicating correlated actions.  
- Agent-level: Emergence of three clusters; absence of “Steady” cluster with request freq ≈ C/N and low variance.  
- Silent failure: Conservative agents with >50% max starvation rounds (e.g., 73.5 mean) indicate systemic exclusion.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline validation: Run pure capacity‑matching random agents (no LLM) for all (N,C) pairs. Verify overload probabilities match binomial formulas (e.g., 31.25% for N=4, C=2). Establishes safety benchmark.  
2. Homogeneous personality sweep: For each personality (Risk‑averse, Contrarian, Trend‑follower, Optimist) run homogeneous LLM populations (large model, N=3, C=1). Observe divergence from baseline (e.g., All‑Optimist → 89% overload). Confirms prompt‑driven demand bias.  
3. Heterogeneous clustering run: Mixed personalities (6 types) with small models, N=4, C=2, 100 rounds. Collect per‑agent metrics and run k‑means (k=3). Validate three clusters and check that no agent falls near p=C/N request frequency. Reproduces core taxonomic finding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does increasing LLM model capacity independently worsen collective safety in congestion control tasks, after controlling for personality prompts, exploration mechanisms, and experimental conditions? *Basis:* Larger models had higher overload (72.5% vs. 53.8%) but comparison not fully controlled (Sec. 6.1). *Unresolved:* No factorial design isolating capacity; confounded by prompt/exploration variations. *Resolution:* Fully matched factorial experiment with identical conditions across model sizes.
- **Open Question 2:** What mechanisms prevent LLM agents from adopting steady, capacity‑matching stochastic strategies, and can targeted interventions (e.g., explicit randomness incentives, modified feedback) induce such behavior? *Basis:* No “Steady” cluster emerged among 154 agents. *Unresolved:* Paper speculates about correlation and short histories but does not test interventions. *Resolution:* Experiments varying prompt instructions (e.g., “randomize with p=C/N”), adjusting history window lengths, or adding rewards for unpredictability.
- **Open Question 3:** How robust are the emergent behavioral clusters (Opportunistic, Aggressive, Conservative) to systematic variations in prompt engineering, such as wording, ordering, and inclusion of quantitative heuristics? *Basis:* Study uses six fixed personality prompts and acknowledges LLM sensitivity to framing; no systematic ablation performed. *Unresolved:* Cluster alignment with personality types might shift under rephrasing. *Resolution:* Large‑scale experiments with multiple rephrasings of each prompt, measuring cluster stability via silhouette scores and consistency.
- **Open Question 4:** Do the findings regarding overload rates and behavioral clusters generalize to larger populations (N > 10) and longer time horizons (T > 1000 rounds), and do new behavioral types emerge at scale? *Basis:* Experiments limited to N∈{3,4} and T ≤ 100; authors note scaling is critical for practical relevance (Sec. 5.3). *Unresolved:* Dynamics may change with more agents—correlation effects could amplify or dampen overload; new strategies might appear. *Resolution:* Simulations with N=50, 100, 1000 agents over thousands of rounds, tracking same metrics and applying clustering.

## Limitations
- Major uncertainties stem from unreported implementation details (exact prompt wording, exploration settings, history window length, round counts, model versions, personality mixes), which block precise replication and may explain variance in reported metrics.
- The reported model‑size effect is confounded by simultaneous variation in prompts, exploration, and other factors across the 43 runs; no factorial design isolates capacity.
- Clustering validity is moderate (silhouette 0.458) and feature selection/standardization choices may influence outcomes, potentially overstating separation significance.

## Confidence
- LLM agents underperform the capacity‑matching random baseline: **High** confidence.
- Correlated reasoning and variance inflation as primary mechanisms: **Medium** confidence.
- Emergence of three distinct behavioral clusters (Opportunistic, Aggressive, Conservative) with no steady agents: **Medium** confidence.

## Next Checks
1. **Baseline replication:** Simulate pure random agents with p = C/N for N=4, C=2 over 10,000 rounds; confirm overload rate ≈ 31.25%.
2. **Homogeneous personality sweep:** For each personality (Neutral, Risk‑averse, Optimist, etc.), run 20 independent 100‑round simulations with a single LLM (e.g., GPT‑4o) and N=3, C=1; record overload rates to verify prompt‑driven demand bias.
3. **Clustering robustness:** Re‑run mixed‑personality experiment (N=4, C=2) using standardized features excluding any metric that may have been used in prompt design (e.g., only request frequency and max starvation).
