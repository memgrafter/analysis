# Learnable Multipliers: Freeing the Scale of Language Model Matrix Layers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04890
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04890
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Learnable multipliers escape weight-decay induced norm traps, yielding consistent downstream gains (+1.21% Adam, +1.10% Muon) and reducing hyperparameter sensitivity, especially on reasoning tasks (BBH +2.33% Adam).

## Executive Summary
Weight decay (WD) in language model matrix layers creates a noise–WD equilibrium that traps weight norms at a scale determined solely by optimization hyperparameters (learning rate η, decay λ), preventing adaptation to data. This paper introduces learnable scalar, rowwise, or columnwise multipliers to reparameterize matrix layers, decoupling the effective scale from the matrix norm. Multipliers are placed architecture‑specifically (e.g., row‑only on Q‑projections, both on down‑projections) and trained with a small WD to prevent symmetry‑driven instabilities. Across AdamW and Muon optimizers, this method improves downstream performance on average by >1%, with larger gains on reasoning benchmarks (e.g., BBH +2.33%), and maintains an increasing performance gap over baselines throughout training.

The approach also reduces the need for meticulous tuning of forward‑pass and WD multipliers that are otherwise required to stabilize training. Empirical analysis shows that without multipliers, matrix norms follow the predicted equilibrium ∥W∥ ∝ √(η/λ); with multipliers, the scale is learned freely from data. However, architectural symmetries (e.g., Q/K multiplicative symmetry) cause unbounded multiplier drift without WD, necessitating a light λ_lrm ≈ 2×10⁻³. The method merges multipliers with weights at inference for zero overhead.

## Method Summary
The core method reparameterizes each matrix layer W as W = r·W·c, where r and c are learnable vectors (row/column multipliers) or a scalar. Placement is architecture‑dependent: for a hybrid attention–SSM model (Falcon‑H1‑0.5B), row multipliers are added to Q‑projection, gate‑projection, and SSM Z/dt/out projections; both row and column are added to attention out‑projection and MLP down‑projection; column multipliers (already present as RMSNorm weights) are kept on residual branches; no multipliers are added to V/K‑projections, conv1d layers, or the logits projector. Training uses a small weight decay (λ_lrm = 2×10⁻³) on multipliers to curb symmetry‑driven divergence and excludes multiplier gradients from global gradient‑clipping norm computation. A learning‑rate sweep is performed for each configuration; multipliers typically share the base learning rate. At inference, multipliers are merged into the base weights, incurring no overhead.

## Key Results
- Average downstream improvement: +1.21% (AdamW), +1.10% (Muon) over baselines without multipliers.
- Reasoning tasks see larger gains: BBH +2.33% (Adam), +3.39% (Muon); MATH/GSM8K also improve.
- Performance gap widens with longer training; multipliers maintain advantage throughout.
- Reduces reliance on hand‑tuned scalar forward‑pass multipliers and separate WD multipliers for stabilization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Weight‑Decay–Noise Equilibrium Traps Matrix Norms
- **Claim:** Stochastic gradient noise induces a Brownian‑like expansion of weight matrices W; WD counteracts this, leading to an equilibrium where the matrix norm ∥W∥ is set by optimization hyperparameters (η, λ) rather than data.
- **Mechanism:** The noise‑to‑signal ratio in matrix gradients drives an effective diffusion process; WD provides a linear restoring force, yielding a steady‑state norm ∥W(η,λ)∥ ∝ √(η/λ).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] describes “stochastic gradient noise induces a Brownian‑like expansion… WD counteracts… equilibrium with a certain weight norm ∥W∥.” [section 2] Equation (1) formalizes S(η,λ) = √(η/λ). No direct measurement of noise in the corpus; related work discusses WD consequences but not this specific equilibrium.
- **Break condition:** Learnable multipliers decouple the effective scale from ∥W∥, allowing the scale to adapt to data.

### Mechanism 2: Multipliers Experience Lower Gradient Noise
- **Claim:** Multiplier gradients (e.g., ∂L/∂rᵢ = ∑ⱼ Wᵢⱼ cⱼ Gᵢⱼ) aggregate across matrix dimensions, reducing stochastic noise variance compared to raw matrix gradients; thus multipliers do not require WD to bound scale.
- **Mechanism:** Summation over many entries averages out noise, making multiplier updates more signal‑driven.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 2] Equation (4) shows multiplier gradients involve sums over rows/columns. [abstract] states multipliers “do not experience noise‑driven Brownian expansion.” No direct noise variance measurements; weight normalization (Salimans & Kingma, 2016) uses similar reparameterization but for different purposes.
- **Break condition:** If alignment between inputs and weight matrices decays with width (observed in width‑scaling experiments), multiplier learning may not fully compensate for suboptimal matrix scale.

### Mechanism 3: Symmetry‑Driven Instability Requires Light WD on Multipliers
- **Claim:** Forward‑pass symmetries (e.g., (r_Q, r_K) ↦ (s·r_Q, s⁻¹·r_K) for Q/K product; residual column multiplier c ↦ s·c with inverse effect after normalization) leave outputs unchanged but allow multiplier norms to diverge without regularization, causing NaNs in low precision.
- **Mechanism:** Symmetry directions are nullspace of the loss gradient; without WD, gradient descent does not constrain them, leading to explosion.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.1] Figure 4 shows unbounded multiplier norm growth without WD. [abstract] mentions “forward‑pass symmetries” as a practical issue.
- **Break condition:** Applying λ_lrm ≈ 2×10⁻³ stabilizes training; too strong WD may hinder learning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Weight decay as regularizer and noise counteractant.**  
  Why needed: To understand why matrix norms are trapped in equilibrium while scalar/vector parameters (e.g., RMSNorm weights) can learn freely.  
  Quick check: If gradient noise drives weight growth, why don’t scalar parameters (e.g., bias) experience the same equilibrium?

- **Concept: Gradient noise and its scaling with parameter tensor shape.**  
  Why needed: The equilibrium norm depends on the noise‑to‑signal ratio; matrix layers have higher noise variance than vector‑like parameters due to less gradient averaging.  
  Quick check: How does the variance of gradient estimates differ between a matrix layer and a vector parameter of similar total size?

- **Concept: Reparameterization and its effect on optimization dynamics.**  
  Why needed: Learnable multipliers change the effective learning rate and WD impact on the scale versus the base matrix.  
  Quick check: If W → s·W, how do η and λ affect s versus W in the parameter update and equilibrium?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Input -> Embedding (column + row multipliers) -> Transformer Block (repeated): 
  - Attention: Q‑projection (row multiplier), V/K (none), Out‑projection (row + column), 
  - MLP: Gate‑projection (row), Up‑projection (none), Down‑projection (row + column), 
  - SSM: Z‑projection (row), dt‑projection (row), Out‑projection (row), Conv1d (none); 
  Residual branch (column multipliers, RMSNorm) -> Projector (column multipliers only).

Critical path:
1. Add scalar multipliers to all matrix layers as a baseline; if instability (NaNs) appears, identify symmetry groups.
2. Apply light WD (λ_lrm = 2×10⁻³) to all multipliers and exclude them from global gradient‑clipping norm.
3. Perform a learning‑rate sweep (log‑scale √2 steps) for the chosen multiplier configuration.

Design tradeoffs:
- Redundancy vs. expressivity: Using both row and column everywhere is maximally flexible but redundant; symmetry‑aware placement (e.g., omitting V/K row multipliers) removes redundancy with minimal loss.
- WD on multipliers: Necessary to stabilize symmetry directions; too strong WD prevents scale learning.
- Gradient clipping: Excluding multipliers prevents aggressive clipping when their gradients are large relative to base weights.

Failure signatures:
- NaNs during early steps: Symmetry drift; add/increase λ_lrm or remove redundant multiplier pairs.
- Initial loss much higher than baseline: Over‑aggressive gradient clipping; ensure multiplier grads excluded from norm computation.
- No improvement over baseline: Incorrect placement (e.g., row multipliers on logits projector harms); follow architecture‑specific recipe.
- Rapid early loss drop but poor final performance: Lazy training from logit row multipliers; avoid row multipliers on final projection.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Projector norm sweep** (Figure 1): Vary S(η_P, λ_P) with/without projector multipliers; confirms matrix norms follow √(η/λ) equilibrium while multipliers adjust to stabilize loss.
2. **MLP scale variation** (Figure 1 bottom right): Fix η, λ for MLP, vary S; shows degradation without multipliers and recovery with scalar multiplier.
3. **Width scaling** (Figure 5): Train Falcon‑H1 at multiple widths with fixed η, λ; matrices keep constant norm (no width scaling), multipliers adjust to keep activations stable, indicating learned µP‑like scaling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What mechanistic property of weight gradients causes matrices to be trapped in the noise–WD equilibrium while scalar/vector parameters can freely learn optimal scales? The paper shows the trap and escape empirically but lacks a theoretical explanation for the shape‑dependent signal‑to‑noise ratio.
- How should learning rate, weight decay, and multiplier initialization scale with model width? Does applying learnable multipliers automatically ensure maximal feature learning in the infinite‑width limit without manual µP scaling? Width‑scaling experiments show multiplier norms scale slower than d⁻¹ and alignment decays with width, contradicting µP.
- Why do learnable multipliers yield larger gains on reasoning benchmarks (BBH, MATH, GSM8K) than on knowledge benchmarks (ARC‑C, MMLU)? Does this reveal enhancement of specific model circuits?
- What causes the decay of alignment between weight matrices and input features with increasing model width when using learnable multipliers? Does this indicate a failure to achieve maximal feature learning in the infinite‑width limit?

## Limitations
- Optimal multiplier learning‑rate schedule is unknown; the paper uses a fixed LR but does not establish whether a tuned LR multiplier is essential across setups.
- Placement rules are derived for the Falcon‑H1 hybrid architecture and may not generalize to transformer‑only or other architectures; a symmetry‑aware theoretical placement guide is open.
- Scaling behavior beyond 0.5B parameters is unclear; how multiplier initialization or LR scaling should change with depth/width remains an open question.

## Confidence
- WD‑noise equilibrium mechanism: Medium (theoretical derivation with equation, weak direct empirical validation).
- Multiplier noise reduction via gradient averaging: Medium (equation and abstract claim, no measured noise variance).
- Symmetry‑induced instability without WD on multipliers: High (Figure 4 shows unbounded growth; clear evidence).
- Downstream performance gains: High (consistent +1‑3% improvements across optimizers and benchmarks).
- Width‑scaling mismatch with µP: High (empirical measurement shows alignment decay).

## Next Checks
1. **Batch size ablation:** Fix η and λ, vary global batch size, and measure matrix norm scaling to test the predicted √(η/λ) equilibrium dependence on gradient noise.
2. **WD on multipliers across precisions:** Ablate λ_lrm (e.g., {0, 2e‑3}) under fp16/bf16/fp32 to isolate symmetry drift from precision‑induced instability.
3. **Direct gradient noise measurement:** Over multiple seeds on a fixed batch, compute variance of multiplier gradients versus matrix gradients per layer to validate the lower‑noise claim.
