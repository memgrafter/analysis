# Benchmarking Egocentric Clinical Intent Understanding Capability for Medical Multimodal Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06750
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06750
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: No evaluated Med-MLLM exceeds 70% average reliability under trap testing, revealing critical safety gaps in perceptual and cognitive hallucinations.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MedGaze-Bench, the first benchmark to evaluate egocentric clinical intent understanding in Medical Multimodal Large Language Models (Med-MLLMs). By leveraging clinician eye-tracking gaze as a "Cognitive Cursor," the benchmark assesses intent across three dimensions: Spatial (target discrimination), Temporal (causal rationale), and Standard (protocol compliance). A "Trap QA" mechanism tests reliability by penalizing hallucinations and sycophancy. Evaluation of nine MLLMs shows current models struggle with intent grounding, over-rely on global features, and exhibit severe reliability deficits, with no model surpassing 70% average trap avoidance. The findings highlight urgent needs for safety-focused architectural improvements before clinical deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors construct MedGaze-Bench from four existing egocentric clinical video datasets (surgery, emergency, radiology) synchronized with clinician gaze data. Using a GPT-4o-driven pipeline, they generate 4,491 multiple-choice questions that require gaze-dependent reasoning across six sub-capabilities (e.g., absolute/relative localization, prospective/retrospective attribution). A subset of 600 samples is augmented with adversarial "Trap QA" items: Type I traps insert non-existent visual distractors; Type II traps embed false procedural premises. Models are evaluated zero-shot with "Dual-Prompting"—a semi-transparent gaze overlay on video frames plus text referencing the circled area—and scored on accuracy per sub-capability and trap reliability (Perceptual and Cognitive).

## Key Results
- Gaze prompting acts as a "Cognitive Cursor": generalist MLLMs improve on Standard Intent (+3.95%), but medical-specific models show negligible gains, indicating uneven binding of text to visual anchors.
- Trap QA reveals critical safety gaps: no model exceeds 70% average reliability; Intern3.5VL 38B achieves 69.82% Perceptual Reliability but only 54.67% Cognitive, showing dissociation between visual grounding and logical vigilance.
- Temporal reasoning is asymmetric: mid-sized models score ~60% on Prospective Anticipation (forecasting next steps) but collapse to ~35% on Retrospective Attribution (inferring prerequisites), exposing a causal reasoning gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Using clinician gaze as a "Cognitive Cursor" forces models to ground intent in fine-grained visual attention rather than global scene statistics.  
**Mechanism**: Gaze coordinates are injected as an explicit attentional prior via dual prompting (semi-transparent overlay + text reference). Questions are designed so correct answers critically depend on the specific region of fixation (e.g., "in the area I'm currently looking at"). This creates a visual-textual coupling that penalizes models relying on saliency bias or textual priors.  
**Core assumption**: Expert clinical intent is manifest in the sequence of fixations; a model that truly understands intent will align its reasoning with this attention pattern.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract]: "...leveraging clinician gaze as a 'Cognitive Cursor' to assess intent..."  
- [section 3.2]: "...a Gaze-Anchored Prompting mechanism... By explicitly injecting fixation coordinates... we force the LLM to derive questions strictly from the clinician’s immediate visual focus."  
- [section 4.2]: Generalist models (Qwen3VL) gain +3.95% on Standard Intent with gaze prompting, while medical-specific models show negligible gains (<1%).  
**Break condition**: If models achieve high accuracy without gaze prompting or if gaze prompting degrades performance across multiple models (e.g., EgoLife on Temporal: -0.66%), the mechanism fails to universally improve intent grounding.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: The "Trap QA" mechanism exposes reliability failures by rewarding model abstention over fabricated confidence.  
**Mechanism**: Two-tier adversarial testing: Type I (Perceptual) injects non-existent objects as plausible distractors; Type II (Cognitive) embeds false procedural premises. A safe model must detect the trap and either select the "error detection" option (Type II) or avoid the hallucinated distractor (Type I). This explicitly penalizes hallucination and sycophancy.  
**Core assumption**: Clinical safety requires not just accuracy, but calibrated uncertainty and resistance to invalid prompts.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract]: "...Trap QA mechanisms to stress-test clinical reliability by penalizing hallucinations and cognitive sycophancy."  
- [section 3.1]: "Type II... evaluates whether the model suffers from logic-level hallucinations. A reliable model must identify the logical fallacy and abstain from answering."  
- [section 4.2]: No model exceeds 70% average reliability; Intern3.5VL 38B has high Perceptual Reliability (69.82%) but low Cognitive (54.67%), showing dissociation.  
**Break condition**: If all models achieve near-perfect Trap QA scores (>95%), the trap is not sufficiently challenging. Current results (max 69.84%) indicate the mechanism successfully reveals gaps.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: The three-dimensional framework (Spatial, Temporal, Standard) reveals asymmetric failure modes, particularly a "causal reasoning gap" where models are forward predictors, not backward reasoners.  
**Mechanism**: By decomposing Temporal Intent into Prospective Anticipation (forecasting next step) and Retrospective Attribution (inferring prerequisite conditions), the benchmark isolates sequential reasoning from causal reasoning. Models score higher on the former (mean ~60%) than the latter (mean ~35%), indicating they predict sequences but cannot reconstruct justifications.  
**Core assumption**: True clinical intent understanding requires bidirectional causal reasoning: understanding why an action is taken now and what it enables.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract]: "...Temporal Intent: inferring causal rationale through retrospective and prospective reasoning..."  
- [section 4.2]: "...mid-sized models achieve ~60% in Prospective Anticipation but only ~35% in Retrospective Attribution, revealing they function as forward sequence predictors rather than true causal reasoners."  
- [section 5]: "Temporal intent shows strong causal asymmetry... failing to infer the prerequisite conditions that justify the current clinical action."  
**Break condition**: If model performance parity is achieved between Prospective and Retrospective sub-tasks (difference <5%), the asymmetry gap is closed. The persistent ~25% gap confirms the mechanism's diagnostic utility.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Egocentric Vision & Gaze as Cognitive Proxy**  
  Why needed: The entire benchmark relies on first-person video and synchronized gaze data. Understanding that gaze is a window into task-oriented attention ("Eye-Mind Hypothesis") is core.  
  Quick check: "How does the 'Cognitive Cursor' concept differ from simply using gaze for saliency mapping?"

- **Concept: Clinical Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) as Latent Logic**  
  Why needed: Standard Intent evaluation depends on inferring the why behind standardized motions, mandated by guidelines for safety.  
  Quick check: "Why is verifying 'Continuous Safety Vigilance' harder than 'Discrete Safety Verification'?"

- **Concept: Multimodal Hallucination Taxonomies (Perceptual vs. Cognitive)**  
  Why needed: Trap QA separates fabrication of visual details (Type I) from acceptance of false logical premises (Type II), crucial for reliability engineering.  
  Quick check: "Give an example of a model error caught by Type II but not Type I trap."

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Data Ingestion Pipeline -> Gaze-Anchored QA Generator -> Trap QA Injector -> Evaluation Harness.

**Critical Path**:  
1. Collect/curate egocentric video + gaze data from clinical scenarios.  
2. Align data with SOP timelines and extract gaze statistics.  
3. Generate base QA set using the 4-stage prompt pipeline (Appendix A).  
4. Apply Trap QA transformation to 600 samples.  
5. Run model inference and parse outputs against JSON schema.  
6. Report metrics: 6-dimension accuracy, Perceptual/Cognitive Reliability.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Gaze Signal Fidelity vs. Generalization: Raw coordinates (high fidelity) vs. abstract references ("area I'm looking at") (better generalization).  
- Benchmark Depth vs. Breadth: Focus on 3 scenarios with deep SOP coverage vs. more specialties with shallow annotation.  
- Multiple-Choice Constraint vs. Open-Ended Reasoning: MCQ enables automated scoring but may oversimplify nuanced clinical reasoning.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Spatial: Model chooses high-textual-prior option (e.g., "Harmonic Scalpel") despite gaze showing forceps.  
- Temporal: Correct Prospective but incorrect Retrospective (cannot justify current step).  
- Standard: Fails to detect "silent" safety rule violation (e.g., not checking tissue color).  
- Trap QA: Selects hallucinated distractor (Type I) or fabricates rationale for false premise (Type II).

**First 3 Experiments**:  
1. Baseline Competency Evaluation: Replicate Table 3 to confirm scale-performance and temporal asymmetry trends on core 4,491 QA set.  
2. Gaze Ablation Study: Compare "w/ Gaze" vs. "w/o Gaze" performance; replicate Table 5 to measure gain/loss per dimension.  
3. Trap QA Stress Test: Run on 600 Trap QA samples; replicate Table 4 to identify models with dangerous sycophancy or hallucination.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can MedGaze-Bench be extended to additional clinical specialties to validate generalizability of the three-dimensional intent framework?
2. What architectural or training modifications are needed for MLLMs to achieve >90% reliability in safety-critical tasks, given all tested models fell below 70%?
3. Why do MLLMs show high prospective anticipation but low retrospective attribution in temporal intent, and how can this causal reasoning asymmetry be corrected?
4. Why does gaze prompting improve generalist models but not medical-specific ones, and what redesigns could enhance gaze utilization in domain-adapted models?
5. Can multiple-choice evaluations be replaced or supplemented with open-ended assessments to better capture nuanced clinical reasoning?

## Limitations
- MedGaze-Bench's reproducibility is hampered by unreleased data/code and unclear specialist annotation protocols; without public release, independent validation is impossible.
- The "Dual-Prompting" gaze integration implementation details (overlay opacity, text templates) are unspecified, creating a major unknown for replication.
- The MCQ format may oversimplify clinical decision-making, potentially allowing pattern matching over genuine intent understanding.

## Confidence
- MedGaze-Bench Validity: Medium (benchmark addresses a gap but lacks public release for validation).
- Gaze-as-Cursor Mechanism: Medium-Low (mixed gains across models; implementation details missing).
- Trap QA Diagnostic Utility: Medium (low reliability scores support difficulty, but MCQ limits assessment of open-ended reasoning).
