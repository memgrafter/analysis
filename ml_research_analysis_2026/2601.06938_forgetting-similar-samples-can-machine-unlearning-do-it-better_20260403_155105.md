# Forgetting Similar Samples: Can Machine Unlearning Do it Better?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06938
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06938
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Existing machine unlearning methods fail to remove influence from similar samples; enhanced robustness techniques achieve near-complete unlearning with minimal utility loss.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies a critical flaw in current machine unlearning: methods focus on removing target samples but ignore their influence via similar training samples, leading to incomplete forgetting that violates the original unlearning definition. To investigate, the authors construct four similarity-entailed datasets—three image datasets (MNIST, FMNIST, CIFAR-10) with perturbed similar samples and one text dataset (PKU) with paraphrased similar samples. Using fine-grained verification (SSIM for images, ROUGE for text), they show that even retraining from scratch retains influence through similar samples, and unlearning target samples often fails to affect similar training or test samples. To address this, they propose enhanced schemes: for images, retraining after removing the target plus top-k similar samples; for text, smoothing the manifold via noise injection and joint unlearning on paraphrased variants with KL regularization. These approaches achieve near-complete unlearning (SSIM/ROUGE near pre‑unlearning baselines) with negligible utility loss, revealing a substantial gap in current practice and offering an effective direction.

## Method Summary
The authors construct controlled datasets where each target sample has associated similar samples: for images, similar samples are created by block‑masking (MNIST: 0.3 fraction, FMNIST: 0.1, CIFAR‑10: 0.05); for text, paraphrases are generated via LLM querying (50 per target). Original models are trained on these datasets, memorizing both targets and similar samples. Baseline unlearning methods (retraining‑from‑scratch, relabel‑FT for images; GA, NPO, TV, GDR ± KLN/GDN for text) are applied to forget only the target samples. Verification uses data reconstruction attacks (images: SSIM between reconstructed and original target/similar; text: ROUGE between model answers and ground‑truth answers). Enhanced unlearning modifies the forgetting set: images – remove target plus top‑k SSIM‑similar training samples; text – inject Gaussian noise into embeddings, jointly unlearn target and its paraphrases, and add KL regularization to preserve utility on retained data.

## Key Results
- Retraining from scratch fails to forget similar samples; SSIM/ROUGE on similar samples remains nearly identical before and after unlearning.
- The presence of similar samples actively hinders unlearning of the target itself; unlearning efficacy is lower when similar samples are included during training.
- Enhanced schemes (top‑k removal for images; noise + paraphrase joint unlearning + KL for text) reduce SSIM/ROUGE on similar samples to pre‑finetuning baseline levels, with negligible utility loss (accuracy drop <1% on held‑out data).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Existing unlearning methods only remove the target sample's direct presence, not its distributed influence via similar samples, leading to incomplete unlearning.
- **Mechanism:** Models learn representations that generalize across similar inputs. Removing one sample (target) does not alter parameters learned from other similar samples, so model behavior on inputs related to the target remains unchanged.
- **Core assumption:** Influence of a sample is shared with similar samples (Theorem 1); unlearning must account for this shared influence.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: "existing methods focus on removing target samples but ignore similar samples... leading to incomplete unlearning."  
  [section 3.3.1]: SSIM values between recovered and similar samples remain nearly identical before and after unlearning (Figures 7, 27).  
  [corpus]: Weak; neighboring papers focus on influence estimation but not similarity-entangled influence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Residual influence retained in similar samples actively hinders unlearning of the target sample itself, causing under‑unlearning even for retraining‑from‑scratch.
- **Mechanism:** Similar samples anchor the model’s representation in the region of the target’s input space. When only the target is removed, the model still learns from nearby similar points, preserving the decision boundary or output behavior associated with the target.
- **Core assumption:** Unlearning a sample requires removing all evidence of its concept; similar samples act as “prototypes” that maintain that concept.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 3.3.2]: With similar samples present, SSIM between recovered and target samples stays high post‑unlearning (Figure 9b vs. 9a). Visual recovery in Figure 10 shows target resemblance persists.  
  [section 3.4.2]: ROUGE scores for target samples are higher when similar samples are included during training (Figure 12).  
  [corpus]: No direct anchor; neighboring work does not study this interaction effect.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Incorporating robustness techniques—retraining with top‑k similar samples (images) or smoothing the manifold via noise + joint unlearning on paraphrases with KL regularization (text)—achieves near‑complete unlearning.
- **Mechanism:** For images: removing a cluster of similar samples (target + top‑k SSIM) excises the entire local region of input space, forcing the model to forget the concept. For text: noise injection smooths the output manifold, making unlearning less sensitive to exact phrasing; joint unlearning on paraphrases covers semantic variants; KL regularization preserves utility on retained data.
- **Core assumption:** Unlearning effectiveness improves by broadening the forgetting set to include input‑space neighbors and regularizing to avoid over‑unlearning.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 4.1]: Equation (2) defines selecting top‑k similar samples via SSIM threshold τ.  
  [section 4.2]: Equations (4)-(7) describe noise injection (ξ∼N(0,σ²)) and joint loss with paraphrases F(x_i) and KL regularization.  
  [section 5.1]: SSIM between recovered and target samples decreases as more similar samples are removed (Figure 15).  
  [section 5.2]: Post‑unlearning ROUGE on similar samples matches pre‑finetuning baseline (Figure 17), with negligible utility drop (Figure 18).  
  [corpus]: "REMIND..." verifies unlearning but not via similarity; "Grokked Models..." studies training dynamics, not similarity‑aware unlearning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Machine Unlearning Definition**  
  *Why needed:* The paper challenges whether current methods adhere to the original definition (complete influence removal). Understanding Definition 1 (Cao & Yang) is essential to see the gap.  
  *Quick check:* Does unlearning require the model to be statistically indistinguishable from one never trained on the target data, including its concept?
- **Concept: Influence Metric (Inf(x_i; M))**  
  *Why needed:* The core problem is about influence, not just sample presence. Theorem 1 uses mutual information analogy to justify shared influence.  
  *Quick check:* If two samples are similar, does removing one reduce the influence of the other on the model?
- **Concept: Similarity Measurement**  
  *Why needed:* Constructing datasets and evaluating effects requires quantifying similarity (cosine similarity, SSIM, clustering, ROUGE).  
  *Quick check:* How do you operationally define “similar samples” for images (pixel/feature space) vs. text (semantic/paraphrase)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Dataset Construction Module -> Model Training Module -> Baseline Unlearning Module -> Verification Module -> Enhanced Unlearning Module -> Evaluation Harness
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Construct similarity‑entailed datasets  
  2. Train original model (pre‑unlearning)  
  3. Apply baseline unlearning (target only)  
  4. Verify on target & similar samples  
  5. Apply enhanced unlearning  
  6. Re‑verify  
  7. Compare to pre‑finetuning baseline
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Completeness vs. Cost:* Retraining with many similar samples is expensive but more complete. Noise/paraphrase is cheaper but may under‑unlearn if γ too small or paraphrase quality low.  
  - *Utility preservation:* KL regularization helps but may not fully prevent utility loss if forgetting set is large.  
  - *Similarity definition:* Cosine/SSIM thresholds (τ) control the forgetting set size; too aggressive removes too much, too conservative leaves residual influence.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High SSIM/ROUGE on similar samples post‑unlearning (indicating residual influence).  
  - Small difference between “with similar samples” and “without similar samples” unlearning results for target samples.  
  - Significant utility drop (accuracy >2% in experiments) after enhanced unlearning.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline gap demonstration:** For each dataset, run retraining‑from‑scratch unlearning on target only. Verify via SSIM/ROUGE that similar samples still have high similarity to target/post‑unlearning outputs.  
  2. **Target unlearning interference:** Train two models: one with only target samples, one with target + similar samples. Unlearn target in both. Compare verification scores (SSIM/ROUGE) on target samples. Show higher scores when similar samples present.  
  3. **Enhanced method validation:** Apply image enhancement (remove top‑5 similar) and text enhancement (noise+paraphrase+KL). Verify that SSIM/ROUGE on similar samples drops to pre‑finetuning levels, and utility loss is minimal (<1%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can machine unlearning methods be designed to automatically identify and mitigate the influence of similar samples without relying on pre‑defined similarity relationships or exhaustive dataset annotations?
- What are the theoretical guarantees for unlearning completeness when similar samples exist, and how do factors like model architecture, data distribution, and similarity strength affect residual influence?
- How can verification of machine unlearning be standardized across data modalities to detect residual influence from both exact samples and their semantic or structural variants, ensuring regulatory compliance?
- What is the trade‑off between unlearning effectiveness (including similar samples) and model utility (e.g., accuracy, fairness), and how can it be optimized for practical deployment?

## Limitations
- **Dataset construction:** Similar samples are artificially perturbed (block masking, LLM paraphrasing) which may not capture the full spectrum of real‑world semantic similarity.
- **Evaluation metrics:** SSIM and ROUGE measure surface similarity but do not directly quantify model influence or prediction changes; they assume reconstruction fidelity correlates with forgetting.
- **Scalability:** Top‑k similar sample removal for images requires computing pairwise SSIM for the whole dataset, which is costly for large datasets. Text method’s noise injection and paraphrase selection involve hyperparameter tuning (γ, number of paraphrases) and may not generalize across model sizes or domains.

## Next Checks
- **Natural similarity validation:** On a real dataset (e.g., CIFAR‑10), automatically find nearest neighbors in a pre‑trained feature space; run unlearning on targets and measure influence on neighbors using a membership inference attack or performance drop. Compare to random non‑neighbors.
- **Alternative similarity metrics:** Replicate key experiments using feature‑space cosine similarity (from a frozen pre‑train model) instead of SSIM/ROUGE for selecting “similar” samples. Assess whether conclusions hold across metrics and thresholds.
- **Scalability and robustness stress test:** For images, increase dataset size (e.g., 10k ImageNet subset) and measure computational overhead of top‑k removal. For text, vary noise scale γ and paraphrase count; evaluate unlearning completeness vs. utility on multiple downstream tasks (e.g., TruthfulQA) to check over‑unlearning.
