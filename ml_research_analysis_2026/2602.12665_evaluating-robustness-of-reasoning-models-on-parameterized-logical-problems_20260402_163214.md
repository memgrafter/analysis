# Evaluating Robustness of Reasoning Models on Parameterized Logical Problems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12665
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12665
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMs exhibit significant brittleness on 2-SAT benchmarks with sharp performance transitions, sensitivity to filler clauses and order, and high SAT bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the robustness of large language models (LLMs) as logical reasoners by constructing a diagnostic 2-SAT benchmark. The benchmark comprises parameterized families of formulas (e.g., contradiction cycles, backbone, monotone split, symmetry) and applies semantics-preserving perturbations (reordering, filler clauses, renaming). Seven decoder-only models ranging from 14B to 120B parameters are evaluated on decision accuracy and assignment validity. Results reveal sharp performance degradation with increasing problem size (e.g., Phi4 drops from 98% to 0% accuracy on ImplicationCycle when clauses increase from 5 to 100), sensitivity to filler clauses and clause ordering, a 25-point accuracy drop when using natural language verbalizations instead of templates, and a strong bias toward predicting SAT (88% of UNSAT instances). These findings highlight that aggregate metrics can mask severe brittleness in LLM reasoning, suggesting limited invariance in current models.

## Method Summary
The authors design a controllable 2-SAT evaluation suite. Four formula families instantiate different logical structures: ImplicationCycle (focus on contradiction cycles), Backbone (critical variable assignments), MonoBridge (monotone split with symmetry), and EquivalenceCore (equivalence relations). Each family is parameterized (e.g., number of clauses, backbone fraction β). Perturbations include shuffling clauses, adding filler clauses that preserve satisfiability, and renaming variables. Models are queried via templated prompts (e.g., “Is the following 2-SAT formula satisfiable? …”) and sometimes natural language verbalizations. Responses are parsed to extract a SAT/UNSAT decision and, when possible, a satisfying assignment. Decision accuracy and assignment validity are measured against ground truth across parameter sweeps.

## Key Results
- Sharp performance transitions: e.g., Phi4 accuracy on ImplicationCycle falls from 98% at 5 clauses to 0% at 100 clauses; backbone fraction β reduces Backbone accuracy by ~10%.
- Filler clauses degrade SAT-generating models when core size is fixed, and clause ordering harms MonoBridge/Backbone but improves EquivalenceCore, demonstrating strong order sensitivity.
- Natural language verbalizations cut accuracy by ~25 points, and Llama-3.3 exhibits a high SAT bias, labeling 88% of UNSAT formulas as SAT.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark isolates specific reasoning subskills by controlling formula structure and semantics. Parameterized families allow systematic exploration of how performance changes with logical complexity, while perturbations test invariance properties essential for robust reasoning. The observed brittleness indicates that LLMs rely on surface heuristics (such as clause count or position) rather than systematic logical inference. Shuffling exposes lack of permutation invariance; filler clauses reveal vulnerability to distraction; the SAT bias suggests an imbalance in decision thresholds learned from training data.

## Foundational Learning
- **2‑SAT**: A restricted satisfiability problem where each clause has at most two literals, solvable in polynomial time. Why needed: Provides a tractable yet meaningful testbed for logical reasoning. Quick check: Verify formula stays within 2‑CNF and test with a standard solver (e.g., MiniSat).
- **Backbone**: The set of variables that have the same assignment across all satisfying assignments. Why needed: Measures a model’s ability to capture necessary logical constraints. Quick check: Compute backbone via exhaustive enumeration or solver for small instances.
- **Clause ordering**: The sequence in which clauses appear in a formula. Why needed: Tests invariance to input permutation, a basic robustness property. Quick check: Shuffle clauses and compare model outputs for consistency.
- **Filler clauses**: Additional clauses that do not affect overall satisfiability. Why needed: Probes distraction resistance and relevance filtering. Quick check: Add random satisfiable filler clauses and check for performance degradation.
- **SAT bias**: Tendency to predict satisfiable even when the formula is unsatisfiable. Why needed: Reveals imbalance in the model’s decision boundary. Quick check: Count SAT vs. UNSAT predictions on a balanced test set; significant skew indicates bias.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: The evaluation framework consists of three main components: (1) Formula Generator (instantiates families and parameters), (2) Perturbation Engine (applies reordering, filler, renaming), (3) Evaluation Pipeline (constructs prompts, queries LLM, parses responses, computes metrics). Data flows: Generator → core formula → Perturbation Engine → perturbed formula → Evaluation Pipeline → results.

**Critical path**: Starting from a chosen family and parameter setting, a core formula is generated; perturbations are applied; the formula is embedded into a prompt; the model generates a response; the response is parsed to extract decision and assignment; these are compared to ground truth to compute accuracy. Latency is dominated by model inference.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Simplicity vs. expressiveness: 2‑SAT is decidable but limited; richer logics may reveal more but are harder to control.
- Template vs. natural language: Templates reduce variability but miss real‑world ambiguity; verbalizations increase realism but introduce confounders.
- Perturbation granularity: Fine‑grained (e.g., variable renaming) vs. coarse (shuffling) affects detection of invariance failures.

**Failure signatures**: Observed patterns include: (1) Sudden accuracy collapse with increasing clause count, indicating inability to generalize problem size; (2) Order sensitivity (accuracy drops when clauses shuffled for some families) shows reliance on surface features; (3) High SAT bias reveals unbalanced decision boundary.

**First experiments**:
1. Sweep clause count for the ImplicationCycle family to map performance transition.
2. Introduce filler clauses while keeping core size constant to quantify distraction effect.
3. Compare shuffled vs. original order for Backbone and MonoBridge families to test order invariance.

## Limitations
- 2‑SAT simplicity: Results may not generalize to NP‑complete SAT variants (e.g., 3‑SAT) or more complex logical structures.
- Model scope: Only decoder‑only LLMs (14B–120B) were tested; other architectures (encoder‑decoder, sparse models) remain unexamined.
- Missing symbolic baseline: No comparison with state‑of‑the‑art SAT solvers under identical perturbations, so the extent of LLM‑specific brittleness is unclear.

## Confidence
- Sharp performance transitions (e.g., Phi4 drop with clause count): High
- Filler clauses degrade SAT generators: Medium
- Order sensitivity (harm to MonoBridge/Backbone, help to EquivalenceCore): Medium
- Verbalizations reduce accuracy: Medium
- High SAT bias in Llama‑3.3: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Scale complexity: Introduce 3‑SAT and 5‑SAT parameterized families to test whether brittleness intensifies with NP‑complete hardness.
2. Diversify architectures: Evaluate encoder‑decoder models (e.g., T5) and sub‑10B parameter models to isolate scale vs. architectural effects.
3. Symbolic solver baseline: Run CDCL solvers (e.g., MiniSat) on identical perturbed benchmarks to disentangle LLM‑inherent vs. general SAT‑solving challenges.
