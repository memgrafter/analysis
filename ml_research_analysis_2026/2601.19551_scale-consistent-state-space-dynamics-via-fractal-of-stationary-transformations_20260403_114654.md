# Scale-Consistent State-Space Dynamics via Fractal of Stationary Transformations

## Quick Facts
* arXiv ID: 2601.19551
* Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19551
* Reference count: 19
* Primary result: FROST enforces scale-consistent latent dynamics via a fractal inductive bias in state-space models, enabling adaptive halving and achieving higher cosine similarity with final states than vanilla or SSM baselines.

## Executive Summary
Deep models often lack structural guarantees, rendering early stopping and adaptive computation ill-posed. This paper introduces FROST, a framework that enforces scale-consistent latent dynamics through a fractal inductive bias in state-space models (SSMs). Under this bias, intermediate latent states correspond to self-similar resolutions of a shared representation, ensuring that each step refines towards the final state in a geometrically consistent manner. Experiments on ImageNet-100 and CIFAR-100 demonstrate that FROST achieves significantly higher cosine similarity between intermediate and final states compared to vanilla models and standard SSMs (e.g., 0.9925 vs. 0.8371 at depth 15). Furthermore, a ranking-based halving strategy naturally emerges: by training halting scores to correlate with the relative task loss within a batch, samples are allocated depth adaptively without extra supervision. On CIFAR-100, low-quantile samples exit after ~4.4 iterations with 45.39% accuracy, while the full depth of 16 reaches 62.0%. Complexity analysis confirms that FROST reduces average depth and GFLOPs while increasing throughput, validating the emergent efficiency from the aligned latent geometry.

## Method Summary
FROST modifies the state-space model (SSM) backbone by imposing a stationary, contractive transformation parameterized by a scaling factor λ and a fixed Hurst exponent H. The discrete update rule is $h_{t+1} = (1-λ)h_t + λ( h_t + A(h_t) + B(x_t) )$, where A and B are linear maps (often realized as 1×1 convolutions) and λ ∈ (0,1] ensures contraction. The fractal bias is enforced by scaling A and B jointly with λ and H (e.g., $B_λ(x) = λ^{1+H} B(x)$) so that the dynamics are invariant under rescaling, making all intermediate states lie on a self-similar manifold that approximates the final state. For adaptive computation, a halting router produces a score $s_i,t = σ(w_s^⊤ h_i,t + b_s)$ for each sample i at step t. The scores are trained to rank-order samples according to their intermediate task losses ℓ_i,t using a combination of margin ranking ($L_{rel}^{rank}$) and binary cross-entropy ($L_{abs}^{rank}$) losses, while the task loss itself is only applied at the final step. At inference, a streaming quantile sketch (KLL) of historical s values provides a threshold $\hat{s}$; samples halt when $s_i,t > \hat{s}$. The backbone (e.g., ResNet50) is adapted by replacing pooling with dilated convolutions to maintain spatial dimensions across iterations, allowing the SSM to process fixed-shape latents. The overall objective is $L = L_{task} + α_{rel} L_{rel}^{rank} + α_{abs} L_{abs}^{rank}$, with $α_{rel}=0.7$ and $α_{abs}=0.3$ found effective.

## Key Results
* FROST achieves near-perfect scale-consistency: average cosine similarity between intermediate and final states reaches 0.9925 at depth 15 on ImageNet-100, far exceeding vanilla (0.8371) and SSM (0.8979) baselines.
* Adaptive halving yields substantial computation savings: on CIFAR-100, the lowest quantile (q=0.2) uses only 4.4 iterations with 45.39% accuracy, while full depth (16) attains 62.0%; throughput increases from 23.33 to 47.18 images/sec.
* Complexity: Average depth drops from 16 to 7.56 (ResNet50), GFLOPs from 82.77 to 39.11, confirming emergent early stopping without sacrificing top accuracy (62.0% vs. 59.05% for a 16-step vanilla model).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** FROST enforces that all intermediate latent states lie on a shared, self-similar manifold where each state is a valid resolution of the final representation.  
**Mechanism:** A stationary, contractive state-space transformation (Eq. 8) is parameterized by a scaling factor λ. This induces fractal dynamics where scaling time and input (by λ and Hurst exponent H) preserves the functional form of the update rules (Eqs. 13-17). This structural equivariance makes intermediate states $h_t$ scale-consistent approximations of the final state $h_T$.  
**Core assumption:** The transition operator A and input map B can be scaled jointly with λ and H to maintain the dynamics' form, which requires the model's functional family to be closed under such scaling.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "enforces a self-similar representation manifold through a fractal inductive bias. Under this geometry, intermediate states correspond to different resolutions of a shared representation."  
- [section 3.3] Lemma 3.3 and Eqs. (13)-(17) derive the scaled operators $A_λ$, $B_λ$, $C_λ$ that preserve the dynamics.  
- [corpus] *Weak evidence.* Neighbor papers discuss fractals and state-spaces (e.g., "Fractal Flow", "GP State-Space Models") but do not address the specific scaling equivariance or iterative refinement mechanism of FROST.  
**Break condition:** If the learned λ > 1.0 (violating contractivity) or if H is learned and diverges (Appendix D), the fractal structure collapses, and intermediate states drift off the shared manifold.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The fractal geometry makes halting an intrinsic property, enabling a ranking-based halting strategy that aligns computational cost with representational fidelity without auxiliary objectives.  
**Mechanism:** Since all states $h_t$ are on the same self-similar manifold, their "quality" can be ranked intrinsically via the task loss ℓ_i,t computed on each intermediate prediction. Halting scores $s_i,t$ are trained to correlate with this rank (Eqs. 19-23), so a sample's final required depth is determined by its inherent difficulty relative to others in the batch.  
**Core assumption:** The task loss evaluated on intermediate predictions $y_i,t$ is a meaningful, monotonic proxy for the representational fidelity of $h_t$ relative to $h_T$. This holds because the fractal bias ensures $h_t$ and $h_T$ are scale-consistent.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "halting naturally admits a ranking-based formulation driven by intrinsic feature quality rather than extrinsic objectives."  
- [section 4] The entire section defines the batch-time ranking (Eq. 18) and the ranking loss (Eqs. 22-23) that supervises the halting signal.  
- [corpus] *Weak evidence.* Neighbors on adaptive computation (ACT, PonderNet, Mixture-of-Depths) use extrinsic signals; none employ a ranking mechanism tied to a fractal latent geometry.  
**Break condition:** If the scale-consistent property fails (Mechanism 1 breaks), the ranking signal becomes misaligned with true representational quality, causing gradient conflict and training collapse (Appendix D).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The contractive nature of the fractal transformation ensures stable convergence of the iterative dynamics and bounded gradient propagation, enabling reliable training over many iterations.  
**Mechanism:** The update rule $h_{t+1} = (1-λ)h_t + λ( h_t + F(h_t, x_t) )$ (Eq. 8) is a convex combination. With λ ∈ (0,1], the Jacobian's spectral norm is bounded by λ (Theorem B.2), making the update a contraction mapping. This guarantees $||h_t - h^*||$ decays exponentially (Theorem B.3) and gradients $\partial h_t/\partial h_0$ vanish as λ^t (Proposition B.4).  
**Core assumption:** The activation functions (e.g., ReLU) are 1-Lipschitz, and the effective transition operator $A_λ$ has spectral norm ≤ λ. This is enforced by the parameterization and λ's range.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 3.2] "λ effectively modulates the eigenvalues of the Jacobian... pulling the spectrum towards unity, improving condition number and gradient flow."  
- [Appendix B] Theorem B.2 (fixed point convergence), Theorem B.3 (exponential error decay), Proposition B.4 (bounded gradients).  
- [section 6.1] Table 1 shows smooth, monotonic increase in cosine similarity with depth, empirically indicating stable convergence.  
**Break condition:** If λ ≥ 1.0 (e.g., due to poor initialization or H divergence as noted in §6.3), the mapping is no longer contractive, leading to exploding activations/gradients and unstable training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Fractal/Statistical Self-Similarity (Hurst Exponent H)**  
  *Why needed here:* H governs how the input scaling affects the dynamics ($B_λ(x) = λ^{1+H} B(x)$), defining the roughness of the latent trajectory. It must be fixed (not learned) to strictly enforce the fractal bias and prevent trivial oversmoothing (§6.3).  
  *Quick check question:* If H is too low (e.g., 0.2), what happens to the effective scaling of $B_λ$ and why might λ then diverge above 1.0?

- **Concept: Contraction Mapping & Spectral Norm**  
  *Why needed here:* The core guarantee of stable, consistent refinement relies on the update being a contraction (λ < 1). Understanding spectral norm as the Jacobian's maximum expansion factor is key to reasoning about convergence and gradient flow (Appendix B).  
  *Quick check question:* Given λ=0.6, what is the worst-case upper bound on the norm of $h_{t+1} - h_t$ relative to $h_t - h_{t-1}$? What does this imply for long-term state differences?

- **Concept: Ranking-based Adaptive Computation**  
  *Why needed here:* FROST's efficiency comes from allocating depth per-sample. The mechanism relies on ranking intermediate losses within a batch to generate a scale-free halting signal. One must understand why absolute thresholds fail and why relative ranking is robust (§4.1).  
  *Quick check question:* Why is using the raw loss value ℓ_i,t as a halting score problematic across different batches or tasks? How does ranking mitigate this?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** The FROST architecture centers on a stationary SSM core with shared functions A(·), B(·), C(·), D(·) (typically 1×1 convolutions). A fractal parameterization adds scalar λ (learned via exp) and fixed Hurst exponent H, which scale A and B to A_λ, B_λ. The iteration uses a convex combination: $h_{t+1} = (1-λ)h_t + λ·(h_t + A_λ(h_t) + B_λ(x_t))$. A halting router (linear layer + sigmoid) produces scores s_i,t. During training, these scores are supervised by ranking losses that align with batch-time task loss ranks. At inference, a quantile sketch (KLL) of historical s values sets a global threshold $\hat{s}$; samples exit when $s_i,t > \hat{s}$. The backbone (e.g., ResNet) is modified to avoid pooling (use dilated convolutions) and interleaves the SSM updates.

- **Critical path:** Input image x_t (presented at each step) → initial latent h_0 (e.g., zero) → iterative SSM update using fractal-scaled operators → at each t compute prediction y_t = C(h_t) + D(x_t) → compute task loss ℓ_t (only on final step) and halting score s_t → if s_t exceeds threshold, halt and output y_t; else continue.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *λ:* Smaller λ improves stability but slows convergence (requires more steps); larger λ speeds up change but risks instability if λ ≥ 1.0. Learned λ balances this.  
  *H:* Fixed H enforces strict fractal structure; making H learnable leads to divergence (extra flexibility exploited).  
  *Router complexity:* A linear router is stable but may be less expressive; more complex routers could interfere with backbone training.  
  *Loss weighting:* The relative ($α_{rel}$) and absolute ($α_{abs}$) ranking losses must be balanced (0.7 and 0.3) to avoid score collapse or compression.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Score collapse: all s_i,t → 0 or 1, causing no discrimination (observed with only $L_{abs}^{rank}$).  
  - Score compression: s_i,t cluster in a narrow range, making quantile thresholding ineffective (observed with only $L_{rel}^{rank}$).  
  - Gradient conflict: when ranking losses are applied to non-fractal architectures, gradients from $L_{task}$ and $L_{rank}$ align negatively, training diverges (Appendix D).  
  - λ divergence: λ climbing above 1.0, breaking contraction and causing instability (Fig. 1).  
  - Non-monotonic cosine similarity: intermediate states failing to consistently approach final state, indicating broken scale-consistency.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Scale-consistency probe:** Train FROST on CIFAR-100 with ResNet50. Compute average cosine similarity between h_t and h_T across many samples for each t. Expect a monotonic increase approaching ≥0.99.  
  2. **H hypersensitivity test:** Fix different H values (0.2, 0.5, 0.8) and monitor λ convergence and cosine similarity trends. Low H should risk λ > 1.0 and degrade consistency.  
  3. **Ranking loss ablation:** Train with ablated objectives: (a) $L_{task}$ only, (b) $L_{task} + L_{rel}^{rank}$, (c) $L_{task} + L_{abs}^{rank}$, (d) full combination. Evaluate accuracy at low quantile (q=0.2) and inspect distribution of s scores. Full objective should yield best adaptive efficiency (high accuracy at low q) and well-separated score distributions; ablations should cause collapse/compression and degraded accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can FROST be extended to support token-level or input-dependent transformations while preserving global scale-consistency?**  
   *Basis in paper:* "FROST currently relies on stationary transformations applied uniformly across tokens, which may limit expressivity for non-uniform or task-specific dynamics" (Conclusion).  
   *Why unresolved:* Uniform stationarity may hinder modeling of spatially heterogeneous features, potentially limiting applicability to tasks requiring localized adaptations.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop a variant with token-conditioned λ and H, then verify scale-consistency via cosine similarity across depths and stability of ranking-based halving on ImageNet.

2. **Does the fractal inductive bias and adaptive efficiency of FROST generalize to larger-scale benchmarks and deeper architectures?**  
   *Basis in paper:* "Applying the framework to larger-scale benchmarks also remains an important direction for evaluating the robustness and generality of the proposed geometric principles" (Conclusion).  
   *Why unresolved:* Current evaluations on ImageNet-100 and CIFAR-100 may not reveal challenges of full ImageNet or larger models (e.g., ViT-Large) where representation dynamics could differ.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Train FROST on full ImageNet with ViT-Large backbone, measure scale-consistency metrics (cosine similarity, Minkowski dimension) and computational savings (reduced depth, throughput) compared to baselines.

3. **Can the Hurst exponent H be learned without divergence, and what constraints are necessary for stable optimization?**  
   *Basis in paper:* From ablation: "treating H as learnable causes it to diverge beyond 1.0" (§6.2), indicating instability when H is free.  
   *Why unresolved:* Fixed H is a critical structural constraint; learning H could improve adaptability but currently fails due to spectral bias exploitation.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Introduce regularization (e.g., KL-divergence to a prior) or reparameterization (e.g., softplus with bounds) for H, and monitor its trajectory and impact on scale-consistency and accuracy.

4. **What minimal geometric conditions enable ranking-based halting to be effective, and can other architectures be modified to satisfy them without full FROST?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Ranking-based halting collapses for non-fractal baselines (Vanilla, SSM) due to gradient conflict (Appendix D).  
   *Why unresolved:* The success of ranking-based halting in FROST relies on scale-consistent latent dynamics; identifying key properties could allow more efficient adaptations to existing models.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically ablate components of FROST (e.g., contraction, self-similarity) in other architectures, measure gradient alignment between task and ranking losses, and correlation with halting accuracy.

5. **How does relaxing the linearity assumption on the readout operators (C, D) affect fractal scaling and adaptive halting performance?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Theoretical scaling laws assume linear D for invariance (§3.3); practical implementation uses linear 1x1 convs but non-linear versions are unexplored.  
   *Why unresolved:* Non-linear readouts might enhance expressivity but could violate the fractal inductive bias, leading to inconsistent intermediate representations.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Replace linear B, C, D with non-linear MLPs in FROST, evaluate scale-consistency (cosine similarity progression) and halting effectiveness (accuracy vs. depth trade-off) on ImageNet-100.

## Limitations
- Implementation details (backbone modifications, channel dimensions) and training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, max depth) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- The fractal bias requires a fixed Hurst exponent (H=0.5); learning H leads to instability, and the choice of H may be task-dependent but lacks sensitivity analysis.
- Evaluation is limited to ImageNet-100 and CIFAR-100; no evidence for dense prediction tasks or full ImageNet, leaving scalability and generality open.

## Confidence
- **Scale-consistent dynamics** (Mechanism 1): Medium. Supported by theoretical derivation and monotonic cosine similarity trends, but assumptions (stationarity, linearity) may not hold for deep nonlinear backbones.
- **Adaptive halting via ranking** (Mechanism 2): Low–Medium. Efficiency gains are clear, but strong dependence on fractal bias is shown by failures on non-fractal baselines; ranking mechanism itself lacks independent validation.
- **Stable convergence via contraction** (Mechanism 3): High. Spectral norm bound, λ ∈ (0,1] enforcement, and smooth empirical trends provide strong evidence.
