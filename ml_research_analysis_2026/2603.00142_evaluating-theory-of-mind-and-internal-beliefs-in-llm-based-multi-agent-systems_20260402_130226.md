# Evaluating Theory of Mind and Internal Beliefs in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.00142
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00142
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The efficacy of Theory of Mind and internal belief mechanisms in LLM-based multi-agent systems varies with the base LLM's reasoning capacity; adding these mechanisms improves coordination for some smaller models but degrades performance for others, while top-tier models like ChatGPT-4o remain robust.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how Theory of Mind (ToM) and internal belief (IB) mechanisms, augmented with symbolic verification, affect coordination in LLM-based multi-agent systems. The authors propose an architecture where agents model peers’ mental states (ToM), maintain BDI-style beliefs, and use Answer Set Programming (ASP) to check logical consistency. Evaluated on a dynamic resource allocation task across five LLMs, results show that performance gains from these cognitive modules are highly model-dependent: mid-tier models benefit from ToM alone but suffer when both ToM and IB are combined, whereas the most capable model (ChatGPT-4o) excels regardless. The study underscores that adding reasoning overhead can overwhelm smaller models, making mechanism design contingent on base LLM capability.

## Method Summary
The study introduces a novel agent architecture integrating three key components: (1) a Theory of Mind module that generates expectations about other agents’ actions; (2) an internal belief module inspired by BDI, where the agent articulates its understanding of the world in natural language; and (3) an Answer Set Programming (ASP) verifier that translates these beliefs into logical rules and checks for consistency, prompting revision up to three times if contradictions are found. Agents operate in a simulated city with four districts, each requiring resources to maintain health. Three cooperative agents (FOOD, MEDICINE, SECURITY) start at a central supply district and take turns moving and supplying resources over seven rounds. Shared and private memories store observations and beliefs. The system is evaluated in four configurations (Base, ToM-only, IB-only, ToM+IB) using five LLMs (ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo, ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-4o-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 8B). Performance is measured by the median final health of the three demand districts, with bootstrapped confidence intervals.

## Key Results
- Performance varied markedly across LLM families; ChatGPT-4o achieved high median health scores in all configurations, while other models showed sensitivity to added mechanisms.
- Enabling ToM alone improved results for some smaller models (ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama 3.1 8B), but combining ToM and IB caused notable degradation, especially for Llama 3.1 8B.
- The interaction suggests that the cognitive overhead of dual mechanisms exceeds the reasoning capacity of mid-tier models, whereas the most capable model remains unaffected.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Theory of Mind for Anticipatory Coordination
- **Claim:** Theory of Mind (ToM) enables agents to anticipate other agents' actions and align their own strategies, improving coordination in multi-agent resource allocation.
- **Mechanism:** The agent’s “Beliefs on Others” module reasons about peers’ roles, observed behavior, and inferred intentions to generate expectations about future actions. These expectations inform the agent’s internal planning.
- **Core assumption:** Agents can reliably infer mental states from limited observations and role-based priors.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “Theory of Mind (ToM) … enhance collaborative intelligence via agents’ capacity for improved prediction and adaptation.”
  - Section 3.2 defines the “Beliefs on Others” component and its integration with the LLM response structure.
  - Corpus: neighboring papers (e.g., *Theory of Mind for Multi-Agent Collaboration via Large Language Models*) explore ToM in LLM‑MAS, indicating the approach’s relevance.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM’s reasoning capacity is insufficient, ToM predictions become inaccurate, leading to miscoordination and performance degradation (observed for Llama 3.1 8B in ToM+IB).

### Mechanism 2: ASP Verification for Consistent Internal Beliefs
- **Claim:** The Internal Beliefs (IB) module with ASP‑based logical verification promotes structured internal reasoning and prevents contradictory plans, enhancing decision reliability.
- **Mechanism:** The agent expresses its understanding of the world (“Internal Beliefs”) in natural language, which is translated into an Answer Set Programming (ASP) model. The Clingo solver checks consistency; inconsistencies generate explanations, prompting the agent to revise beliefs (up to three attempts).
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can produce a sufficiently precise, logic‑friendly representation of the game state, and ASP can accurately detect contradictions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “BDI‑style internal beliefs … integrated … with symbolic solvers for logical verification.”
  - Section 3.3 details the iterative ASP verification pipeline and example translation.
  - Corpus: limited direct support; evidence primarily from the paper’s implementation.
- **Break condition:** Vagueness or errors in the natural language description hinder correct translation to ASP, causing false positives/negatives in verification and limiting the benefit.

### Mechanism 3: Synergy of ToM and IB is Capacity‑Dependent
- **Claim:** The synergistic interplay of ToM and IB (with verification) can yield greater than additive improvements, but only for LLMs with adequate reasoning depth; otherwise the combined cognitive load harms performance.
- **Mechanism:** ToM expectations feed into the generation of Internal Beliefs, which are then verified by ASP. The cycle aims to produce decisions that are both socially informed and logically sound.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can bear the extra reasoning overhead without sacrificing task performance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “The interplay between these mechanisms, particularly in relation to formal logic verification, remains largely underexplored.”
  - Section 4.4 analyzes performance variability: ChatGPT‑4o remains high across configurations, while smaller models show mixed or degraded results with both mechanisms.
  - Corpus: neighboring studies typically isolate ToM or BDI; few examine their combination with logical verification.
- **Break condition:** For models with limited capacity (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B), the dual mechanisms exceed their reasoning budget, causing performance collapse in the ToM+IB condition.

## Foundational Learning
- **Theory of Mind (ToM):** The cognitive ability to attribute mental states to others. *Why needed here:* To enable agents to model other agents’ mental states and predict actions, foundational for coordination in multi‑agent systems. *Quick check:* What distinguishes first‑order from higher‑order Theory of Mind, and how might higher‑order reasoning affect cooperation?
- **Belief‑Desire‑Intention (BDI) Model:** A framework for rational agency where beliefs represent information, desires represent goals, and intentions represent committed plans. *Why needed here:* Provides the theoretical basis for the Internal Beliefs module, allowing agents to maintain and update representations of the world and their plans. *Quick check:* In a BDI agent, how do beliefs, desires, and intentions interact, and what is the role of practical reasoning?
- **Answer Set Programming (ASP):** A declarative logic programming paradigm where solutions are represented as stable models (answer sets). *Why needed here:* ASP serves as the formal logic layer for verifying the consistency of an agent’s internal beliefs before actions are executed. *Quick check:* What is an answer set in ASP, and how does a solver like Clingo determine whether a logic program is consistent?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Observations -> LLM Core (with ToM Module, IB Module) -> ASP Verifier -> Action Executor -> Environment Simulator -> Updated State & Shared Memory.  
  Private memory stores Beliefs on Others and Internal Beliefs; Shared memory holds collective knowledge (Response).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Agent observes current state (district resources, health, own inventory, shared memory).  
  2. If ToM enabled, LLM generates Beliefs on Others.  
  3. LLM drafts Internal Beliefs, optionally influenced by ToM output.  
  4. IB Module translates beliefs into ASP facts/rules.  
  5. ASP Verifier runs; if inconsistent, explanation fed back for revision (≤3 attempts).  
  6. Once consistent, LLM finalizes Response and Action.  
  7. Action Executor applies the action; environment updates; loop repeats.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Cognitive load vs. coordination: Adding ToM/IB helps high‑capacity LLMs but may overload smaller ones.  
  - Expressivity vs. verifiability: Natural‑language beliefs are flexible but error‑prone in translation; strict logical forms limit expressiveness.  
  - Sequential vs. parallel: Turn‑based protocol avoids race conditions but introduces latency; parallel execution could improve throughput but needs conflict resolution.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Low final district health scores relative to baseline.  
  - Repeated ASP inconsistency loops (exceeding three revisions).  
  - High variance across trials with same configuration.  
  - Illegal actions (e.g., moving to non‑adjacent districts) indicating flawed world models.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline:** Run simulation with no ToM and no IB (Base) across all LLMs to establish reference performance.  
  2. **ToM Only:** Enable Beliefs on Others while disabling IB/ASP; compare to Baseline to isolate ToM’s contribution.  
  3. **IB Only:** Enable Internal Beliefs with ASP verification but disable ToM; assess whether logical verification alone improves coherence and health scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can more sophisticated, learned models of other agents' goals and behaviors (beyond prompt-based ToM inference) improve coordination in LLM-based MAS?  
- *Basis:* Future Work: "investigating more complex ToM applications, like learned models of other agents’ goals and behaviors [17,35,19]."  
- *Why unresolved:* Current ToM uses static prompts and observation; learned models could capture dynamic strategies but integration cost, training needs, and capacity impact are unknown.  
- *Resolving evidence:* An architecture with fine-tuned small models or retrieval-augmented prediction, evaluated against prompt-based baseline across multiple LLMs in the resource task and a more complex scenario.

**Open Question 2:** How do alternative communication protocols (e.g., parallel updates, gossip-based, or event-driven) affect the performance and efficiency of ToM/IB-enabled agents compared to the sequential shared-memory protocol?  
- *Basis:* Future Work: "exploring different communication protocols would have a significant effect on information flow." Discussion notes inefficiencies of the sequential protocol.  
- *Why unresolved:* The fixed sequential protocol may create bottlenecks; interplay between communication structure and cognitive mechanism effectiveness is untested.  
- *Resolving evidence:* Implement and compare existing architecture with 2-3 alternative protocols (simultaneous messaging, topic-based channels) in the same task, measuring median health scores, communication rounds per turn, and belief consistency.

**Open Question 3:** What is the precise cognitive overhead threshold where combining ToM and IB mechanisms shifts from beneficial to detrimental for specific LLMs, and can adaptive prompting mitigate this?  
- *Basis:* Inferred: Llama 3.1 8B best with ToM-only but worst with ToM+IB; discussion hypothesizes "cognitive overload."  
- *Why unresolved:* The degradation cause (conflicting representations, prompt complexity, solver cost) is unclear; diminishing returns point per model unknown.  
- *Resolving evidence:* Follow‑up experiment controlling cognitive load (e.g., limiting ToM depth, IB rule complexity) with smaller models, measuring token count, belief errors, performance. Include adaptive prompting that dynamically enables/disables modules based on task phase.

**Open Question 4:** How generalizable are the observed interactions between LLM capacity, ToM, and IB to tasks with different characteristics (e.g., competitive objectives, partial observability, or longer horizons)?  
- *Basis:* Inferred: Discussion states: "the relatively simple nature of this task environment limits the generalizability of these findings." Task is cooperative with full observability.  
- *Why unresolved:* The resource allocation scenario is a specific cooperative finite‑horizon problem; unknown if same pattern holds in adversarial, hidden‑information, or extended planning settings.  
- *Resolving evidence:* Replicate exact model/configurations in at least two new domains: one competitive (e.g., negotiation game) and one with partial observability (e.g., predator‑prey), then compare performance trends against original task.

## Limitations
- Exact simulation parameters (initial district resources, agent carrying capacity) are unreported, hindering precise replication of the environment dynamics.
- Prompt templates and the full ASP translation scheme are omitted, making it difficult to reconstruct the LLM’s reasoning process and verification accuracy.
- Evaluation is confined to a single cooperative resource allocation task; findings may not extend to competitive, partially observable, or longer‑horizon multi‑agent scenarios.

## Confidence
- Model-dependent efficacy of ToM/IB: **Medium confidence** (results show variation but details missing; limited model set).
- ASP verification improves decision reliability: **Low confidence** (implementation details sparse; translation accuracy unknown).
- Synergy of ToM and IB benefits only high-capacity LLMs: **Low confidence** (evidence relies on one model; no direct measure of reasoning capacity; other models' degradation could be due to artifacts).
