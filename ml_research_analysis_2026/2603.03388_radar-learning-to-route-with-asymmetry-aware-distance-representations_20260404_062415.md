# RADAR: Learning to Route with Asymmetry-aware DistAnce Representations
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03388
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03388
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Achieves 0.72% optimality gap on ATSP100, significantly improving over prior SOTA (2.08%), and generalizes zero-shot to ATSP1000 with only 4.13% gap.

## Executive Summary
Neural solvers for vehicle routing problems (VRPs) typically assume symmetric distances, yet real-world routing often involves asymmetric costs due to one-way streets, time-dependent travel, or terrain. RADAR addresses this by introducing a framework with two core innovations: an SVD-based initialization that creates compact node embeddings encoding static asymmetry, and a Sinkhorn-normalized attention mechanism that jointly normalizes rows and columns to model dynamic asymmetry during encoding. Evaluated on 17 synthetic VRP variants and three real-world datasets, RADAR consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, demonstrating not only lower optimality gaps but also strong zero-shot generalization to much larger instance sizes (up to 1000 nodes). The approach shows superior robustness and scalability for asymmetric routing problems.

## Method Summary
RADAR adapts a Transformer-based neural VRP solver to handle asymmetric distance matrices. It initializes node embeddings using a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) of the per-instance asymmetric distance matrix, separating inbound and outbound cost structures. Within the encoder, multi-head attention replaces standard softmax with Sinkhorn normalization, an iterative row-column balancing procedure that yields doubly stochastic attention weights, thereby reflecting bidirectional relationships. The decoder remains autoregressive, constructing routes step by step. Training employs policy gradient (REINFORCE/POMO) with instance augmentations and per-instance z-score normalization of distances to stabilize learning.

## Key Results
- RADAR achieves 0.72% optimality gap on ATSP100, far below the 2.08% gap of previous best neural methods.
- It demonstrates remarkable zero-shot scalability: training on ATSP100 and testing on ATSP1000 yields only 4.13% gap, while other neural solvers show >7–38% gaps or fail completely.
- Across 17 synthetic VRP variants and three real-world datasets, RADAR consistently delivers the lowest objective values and maintains robustness under varying asymmetry levels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### SVD-Based Embeddings Encode Static Asymmetry
RADAR computes a truncated SVD of the asymmetric distance matrix D ≈ UkΣkVk^T. Rows of Uk capture outbound (source) signals, while rows of Vk capture inbound (destination) signals. Concatenating [Uk√Σk | Vk√Σk] produces initial node embeddings that inherently satisfy a linear reconstruction property, meaning there exist projections W1, W2 such that XW1(XW2)^T ≈ D. This compact representation compresses the dominant directional patterns of D, providing a generalizable initialization that transfers across instance sizes. Ablation studies confirm that random initialization fails to capture asymmetry, while SVD initialization yields significant gains, especially on larger instances.

### Sinkhorn Normalization Models Dynamic Asymmetry
Standard Transformer attention uses a row-wise softmax, making the attention score A[i,j] depend only on node i's distance profiles (D[i,:], D[:,i]) and the query-key similarity. This ignores node j's full outbound/inbound context. Sinkhorn normalization replaces softmax with an iterative scaling procedure: start from exponentiated similarity scores, then alternately divide by column sums and row sums for T iterations. The resulting doubly stochastic matrix imposes a joint row-column balance, causing A[i,j] to reflect both nodes' complete distance signatures. This dynamic adjustment allows the model to adapt attention to the specific asymmetric structure of each layer's embeddings. Ablations show that adding Sinkhorn dramatically reduces optimality gaps, particularly on larger and more asymmetric instances.

### Complementary Design Enables Size Generalization
The synergy between SVD initialization and Sinkhorn attention yields strong zero-shot generalization to larger problem sizes. SVD captures the global directional topology of the distance matrix in a low-rank form that is largely independent of the number of nodes. Sinkhorn's doubly stochastic normalization remains stable and balanced even as the graph scale increases, preventing attention collapse when nodes are visited or removed during decoding. Together they avoid the "size-specific embedding" problem that plagues methods relying on k-nearest neighbor graphs or local neighborhood statistics. Empirical results show RADAR's performance degrades gracefully with scale, while baselines deteriorate sharply.

## Foundational Learning

- **Asymmetric Distance Matrices in VRPs**  
  *Why needed:* Real-world routing includes one-way streets, time-dependent speeds, or terrain gradients where the cost from i to j differs from j to i. Symmetric solvers assume D[i,j]=D[j,i] and cannot directly process asymmetric inputs.  
  *Quick check:* "If a VRP has a one-way street from depot to customer A but not back, how does this break the assumption D[i,j] = D[j,i]? What downstream effects does this have on constructive decoding?"

- **Attention as Learnable Message Passing**  
  *Why needed:* Transformers replace static distance-based heuristics with learned aggregation. In VRPs, attention weights should bias message passing toward directionally meaningful interactions. The question is whether attention should remain symmetric (softmax) or learn asymmetry.  
  *Quick check:* "In a standard Transformer, attention is symmetric in the sense that A[i,j] and A[j,i] are computed independently. Why might this be suboptimal for asymmetric cost structures?"

- **Low-Rank Matrix Approximation**  
  *Why needed:* SVD decomposes an n×n distance matrix into O(nk) parameters (Uk, Vk, Σk), creating a compact node embedding that preserves most directional information without over-parameterizing by n².  
  *Quick check:* "Why does SVD of D = UΣV^T naturally separate 'outbound' and 'inbound' signals? How would this differ from eigenvalue decomposition of a symmetric matrix?"

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input (asymmetric distance matrix D) → SVD module (z-score normalize D, compute truncated SVD, form initial embeddings X = [Uk√Σk | Vk√Σk]) → Encoder stack (5 Transformer layers: multi-head attention with Sinkhorn normalization and distance bias MLP) → Decoder (autoregressive node selection with masking) → Training (policy gradient, 3×8 augmentations)

### Critical path
The per-instance pipeline begins with D, which undergoes SVD once to produce X. X is then passed through multiple encoder layers, each applying attention with Sinkhorn to refine embeddings. The final embeddings feed an autoregressive decoder that constructs a route by repeatedly selecting the next node until all are visited. During training, gradients flow back through the SVD (via randomized autograd), Sinkhorn iterations, and the policy loss. The entire process is batched, with SVD computed per instance (or per batch using efficient randomized routines).

### Design tradeoffs
- SVD rank k: Larger k preserves more asymmetry but increases embedding size and compute; the paper finds k=10 optimal for generalization.
- Sinkhorn iterations T: More iterations yield better doubly stochasticity but with diminishing returns; T=10 is used.
- Distance normalization: Z-score normalization of D is crucial; min-max normalization harms performance.
- Attention heads: Using fewer heads (8 vs. 16) reduces memory usage and enables larger batches, aiding training speed.
- Deterministic vs. stochastic initialization: SVD is deterministic; this removes benefits from stochastic augmentations but improves stability and reproducibility.

### Failure signatures
- Attention collapse: Without Sinkhorn, attention concentrates on hub nodes, leaving later steps with nearly uniform scores and random decisions (observed in Figure 8).
- SVD instability: Outliers in D can cause extreme singular values and NaNs during SVD; robust z-score normalization is necessary.
- Memory blow-up: Naive full SVD on large n (e.g., 1000) can exceed GPU memory; randomized truncated SVD must be used.
- Degenerate embeddings: If D is perfectly symmetric, SVD yields non-unique singular vectors and collinear embeddings; small noise or auxiliary features can mitigate.
- Infeasibility in constrained VRPs: If capacity or other constraints are not properly incorporated (e.g., via demand features), the model may produce invalid solutions.

### First 3 experiments
1. Ablation study: train RADAR variants with/without SVD initialization and with/without Sinkhorn on ATSP. Expect full RADAR best; SVD alone helps but Sinkhorn adds most gain for larger sizes.
2. Normalization comparison: train MatNet family with z-score, min-max, and no normalization on ATSP100. Expect z-score to consistently yield lowest gaps (Table 13).
3. Zero-shot generalization: train on ATSP100, test on 200, 500, 1000. Expect RADAR's gap to grow minimally while baselines degrade sharply, demonstrating the benefit of SVD-based scaling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: Generalization to Non-VRP Combinatorial Problems
The paper suggests extending RADAR to broader combinatorial optimization problems where relational edge features are central. Current evaluation is limited to asymmetric VRP variants; transfer to problems like scheduling or assignment is unproven. Evidence: Explicitly stated in conclusion. Resolution would require benchmarking on diverse non-VRP CO problems with standardized datasets and comparison against specialized baselines.

### Open Question 2: Integration with Improvement or Search Paradigms
RADAR is currently a constructive solver. The authors propose integrating it with improvement heuristics or search-based solvers, where RADAR could provide asymmetric, cost-aware priors. Unresolved because such hybrid frameworks have not been explored. Evidence: Explicitly stated. Resolution: develop hybrid methods where RADAR initializes or biases a search procedure and empirically evaluate on asymmetric benchmarks measuring solution quality and trade-offs.

### Open Question 3: Role of Explicit Coordinates in Asymmetric VRPs
Section 5.4 shows RADAR performs strongly without coordinates, even outperforming coordinate-augmented baselines. The precise benefit of coordinates—whether as inductive bias or for augmentation—remains unclear. Evidence: Inferred from experiments. Resolution: systematic studies varying coordinate availability and augmentation factors across controlled asymmetry levels, combined with architectural ablations to isolate coordinate-specific effects.

### Open Question 4: Theoretical Understanding of Sinkhorn's Advantage
The paper provides an intuitive explanation that Sinkhorn balances bidirectional flows, but lacks formal analysis of why doubly stochastic matrices better preserve directional relationships. Evidence: Inferred from the replacement of softmax with Sinkhorn and consistent gains. Resolution: analytical work linking Sinkhorn's scaling to measures of asymmetry preservation, e.g., row-column covariance, plus empirical tracking of attention distributions across layers.

## Limitations
- Data generation fidelity: The exact method for introducing asymmetry (e.g., MatNet's perturbation) is unspecified, risking mismatched training/test distributions and hindering direct reproducibility.
- Decoder specification: While the decoder is said to be unchanged from MatNet, its architecture is not described, creating uncertainty about context vector construction, masking, and search strategies.
- Computational cost: The paper emphasizes scalability in instance size but does not report training time, memory usage, or overhead of SVD and Sinkhorn operations, leaving practical efficiency for large-scale or real-time use unclear.

## Confidence
- SVD-based initialization encodes static asymmetry: **High** (strong theoretical foundation and consistent empirical benefit).
- Sinkhorn normalization models dynamic asymmetry: **Medium-High** (large ablation gains, but relative contribution vs. other components not fully isolated).
- Zero-shot size generalization: **Medium** (empirical results favor RADAR, but training protocol/augmentations may also contribute).
- Superior real-world robustness: **Medium-Low** (promising on three datasets, but broader external validation is limited).
