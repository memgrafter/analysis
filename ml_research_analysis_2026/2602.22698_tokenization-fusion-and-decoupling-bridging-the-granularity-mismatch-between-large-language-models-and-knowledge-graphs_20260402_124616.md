# Tokenization, Fusion and Decoupling: Bridging the Granularity Mismatch Between Large Language Models and Knowledge Graphs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.22698
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22698
- **Reference count**: 36
- **Primary result**: KGT achieves state-of-the-art performance on DB15K, MKG-W, and MKG-Y with 5-18% relative improvements in MRR and Hits@K, while reducing input length and training time compared to in-context learning.

## Executive Summary
KGT addresses the fundamental mismatch between large language models' sub-word tokenization and knowledge graph entities. By registering each entity and relation as a dedicated token, KGT avoids the semantic fragmentation caused by sub-word pooling. It initializes these special tokens by fusing textual embeddings (from a sentence encoder) and structural embeddings (from TuckER) using a relation‑guided gating mechanism. During prediction, KGT uses two decoupled heads that separately consider textual and structural evidence, combining their scores for full‑space ranking. This design yields significant gains on multiple KGC benchmarks and improves efficiency.

## Method Summary
KGT expands the LLM vocabulary with special tokens for every KG entity and relation. Each special token's embedding is initialized by fusing a pre‑trained textual embedding (e.g., from OpenAI's text‑embedding‑3‑small) and a structural embedding from TuckER. The fusion uses a relation‑guided gate that modulates the contributions of each modality based on the relation type. The LLM processes the input sequence containing these tokens, and its final hidden state is projected into two subspaces via separate MLPs. Each subspace scores all entities using LoRA‑augmented linear layers whose base weights are the pre‑trained embeddings. The final entity ranking is a weighted average of the two score distributions. Only the fusion module, projection layers, dual‑view heads, and LoRA adapters are trained; the LLM backbone and pre‑trained embeddings remain frozen.

## Key Results
- New state‑of‑the‑art on DB15K, MKG‑W, and MKG‑Y, with relative improvements of 5–18% in MRR and Hits@K over 19 previous methods.
- Enables efficient full‑space prediction without candidate filtering, reducing input length and training time compared to in‑context learning approaches.
- Ablation studies confirm the importance of both modalities, the relation‑guided gating, and the decoupled prediction heads.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
KGT's success rests on three interconnected mechanisms:

**1. Specialized tokenization**  
Treating each entity and relation as an indivisible token eliminates the granularity mismatch that plagues sub‑word based LLMs. This allows the model to perform full‑space prediction over all entities directly, avoiding candidate filtering or pooling strategies that lose semantics. The approach assumes that entities can be represented as atomic vocabulary units; pre‑trained feature alignment provides sufficient initialization. Evidence: KGT outperforms methods that rely on sub‑word composition and achieves significant gains on standard benchmarks.

**2. Dual‑stream fusion with relation‑guided gating (ReGF)**  
Pre‑trained textual and structural embeddings are projected into the LLM space and combined via learnable gates modulated by the relation type. This preserves the integrity of each modality while allowing the model to adapt their contributions per relation. The gating mechanism uses relation‑aware logits and a temperature parameter to control the interpolation. Ablations show that removing either modality severely degrades performance, confirming the complementary nature of the two streams.

**3. Decoupled dual‑view prediction**  
The LLM's final representation is split into textual and structural subspaces via separate MLPs. Each subspace scores entities using LoRA adapters atop frozen pre‑trained entity embeddings. The two score distributions are combined with learnable scaling factors (λ_t, λ_s). This separation ensures that each modality's reasoning is preserved and then fused linearly, which empirically yields robust performance. Ablations removing one head cause large drops, demonstrating the benefit of decoupling.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Tokenizer & Embedding Lookup → Dual‑Stream Specialized Token Embedding → LLM Backbone → Dual‑View Decoupled Predictor

**Critical path**  
Input triple → tokenize special tokens → fuse pre‑trained features into special token embeddings → LLM forward pass → extract last‑token hidden state → dual‑view projection → LoRA scoring → logit scaling → loss computation.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Efficiency vs. Modality Quality*: Using pre‑trained extractors avoids training from scratch but locks in their biases; stronger extractors improve performance but may increase dimensionality.
- *Parameter Growth*: Adding tokens linearly increases vocabulary size, but LoRA keeps trainable parameters low. Very large KGs may strain memory.
- *Decoupling vs. Joint Learning*: Separate heads enhance interpretability and modality control but assume linear separability; a unified head might capture cross‑modal interactions.

**Failure signatures**
- No loss decrease: may indicate pre‑trained feature extractors not loaded correctly or ReGF gates stuck.
- Degraded performance vs. baselines: suspect projection layer misconfiguration or LoRA underfitting.
- Extreme λ_t/λ_s values: one modality dominates; may need temperature adjustment in ReGF.
- Memory explosion: token sequence remains long; ensure only head and relation are special tokens, not all context entities.

**First 3 experiments**
1. Train on 1% of DB15K for 10 steps; verify loss drops and fused embeddings differ from random init.
2. Ablate ReGF by setting gates to fixed 0.5; expect a performance drop per paper's Table 2.
3. Replace dual‑view heads with a single joint head; expect degradation as shown in Section 4.3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **End‑to‑end structural learning**  
   KGT relies on pre‑computed structural embeddings (e.g., TuckER). Future work could integrate a trainable graph encoder to jointly optimize structure with the LLM, eliminating the dependency on external KGE quality.

2. **Handling missing textual descriptions**  
   Many KGs lack textual entity descriptions. It is unclear how KGT performs when textual features are absent and whether the structural branch alone suffices or requires adaptation.

3. **Beyond linear logit combination**  
   KGT combines textual and structural logits via scalar weighting. Richer fusion mechanisms (e.g., attention‑based) might capture more complex cross‑modal interactions and further improve results.

## Limitations
- **Ambiguous entity strings**: When distinct entities share the same surface name (e.g., "Apple"), indivisible tokens may conflate their semantics, a case not evaluated in the paper.
- **Dependency on pre‑trained extractors**: KGT's performance hinges on the quality of the textual and structural embeddings; noisy or misaligned features degrade results.
- **Linear separability assumption**: The decoupled heads assume that textual and structural reasoning are linearly separable in the LLM's representation space, which may not hold for highly abstract relations.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Specialized tokenization)**: High – Ablations confirm the necessity of full‑space prediction without pooling.
- **Mechanism 2 (Dual‑stream fusion)**: High – Removing either modality severely harms performance; gating is empirically validated.
- **Mechanism 3 (Decoupled prediction)**: Medium – Ablations show benefit, but the linear combination assumption is less theoretically justified.

## Next Checks
1. **Ambiguity stress test**: Construct a dataset fragment with duplicate entity labels (e.g., "Java" as island, language, coffee) and measure KGT's disambiguation capability compared to sub‑word baselines.
2. **Modality initialization ablation**: Replace the textual encoder with a weak random model and TuckER with a noisy variant to quantify performance bounds relative to pre‑trained quality.
3. **Head scaling sensitivity**: For DB15K, systematically vary the λ_t/λ_s ratio during evaluation to verify the optimal range (~1.4–1.6) is stable across random seeds.
