# Hallucinations Live in Variance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07058  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07058  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** SparseKD compression at 32% sparsity improves paraphrase consistency (PC@10) from 23.8% to 55.9% on Qwen3-0.6B, revealing a variance‑centric view of hallucinations and a stability‑accuracy tradeoff.

## Executive Summary
This paper redefines LLM hallucinations as **variance‑driven instability**: when semantically equivalent prompts produce divergent outputs due to internal pathway jitter. The authors introduce **Semantic Stability (SS)**, measured via **Paraphrase Consistency (PC@k)** using greedy decoding (temperature 0) to isolate structural variance. Experiments on Qwen3‑0.6B show that **SparseKD**—a multi‑stage structured pruning with probability‑domain knowledge distillation—boosts SS from 23.8% to 55.9% at 32% sparsity. A phase diagram reveals a non‑monotonic relationship: SS peaks where variance reduction outpaces bias accumulation. Crucially, SS and task accuracy are orthogonal; high stability can coincide with stable but incorrect outputs, demonstrating that reducing variance alone does not improve correctness.

## Method Summary
The method quantifies SS by: (1) generating *k* (with *k*=10 in evaluations) semantically equivalent paraphrases for each test prompt; (2) greedy‑decoding each paraphrase using the target model; (3) computing PC@*k* as the fraction of responses matching the most frequent answer (mode) within that prompt’s set; (4) averaging PC@*k* across prompts to obtain SS. For the compression study, SparseKD—which combines iterative magnitude‑based structured pruning with distillation from a teacher model’s log‑probabilities—is applied at increasing sparsity levels (e.g., R1, R4 [32%], R6, R8). For each compressed checkpoint, both SS and task accuracy are measured to characterize their relationship.

## Key Results
- At 32% sparsity (R4), PC@10 increases from 23.8% (dense) to 55.9% on Qwen3‑0.6B.
- SS vs. sparsity is non‑monotonic, peaking at 32% sparsity (R4) where variance reduction dominates before bias collapse.
- Stability and correctness are orthogonal: models with high SS can produce confident but wrong answers (e.g., “the sun” as the largest planet), as shown on a factual recall probe (Facts dataset).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Variance Suppression via Redundant Pathway Pruning
**Claim:** SparseKD reduces semantic instability by suppressing redundant internal pathways that cause divergent outputs under semantic perturbation.  
**Mechanism:** Multi‑stage structured pruning removes parameters, while probability‑domain knowledge distillation (using teacher log‑probabilities) re‑anchors the student to preserve predictive structure. This reduces representational degrees of freedom, constraining the hypothesis space and minimizing activation of alternative, functionally equivalent pathways during paraphrasing.  
**Core assumption:** The model’s instability is primarily *variance‑dominated* (error stems from pathway jitter, not systematic bias or missing knowledge).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract statement that “hallucinations live in variance”; experiments showing SS increase at 32% sparsity (R4) with SparseKD; Table 1 reporting dense vs. compressed SS.  
**Break condition:** When the error becomes *bias‑dominated* (correct answer not well‑represented in pathways), over‑pruning collapses the model onto a stable but incorrect answer (e.g., “the sun” as largest planet), as seen on the Facts benchmark.

### Mechanism 2: Non‑Monotonic Stability‑Sparsity Curve
**Claim:** There is a peak relationship between sparsity and SS, defining a “sweet spot” where variance reduction outpaces bias accumulation.  
**Mechanism:** Early pruning removes high‑variance, low‑impact pathways (variance‑dominated regime), improving SS. Beyond an optimal sparsity, further pruning eliminates pathways critical for representing correct knowledge, increasing bias and causing SS to collapse onto erroneous but consistent outputs.  
**Core assumption:** Dense models contain internal redundancy that contributes to variance; the distillation signal is sufficient to preserve dominant, low‑bias predictive structures during intermediate compression.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 6 describes the non‑monotonic SS curve peaking at 32% sparsity (R4); Figure 2 visualizes the phase diagram; Section 8’s “largest planet” example illustrates bias‑dominated collapse.  
**Break condition:** The peak’s location is model‑ and task‑dependent; inadequate distillation coverage can shift it left or lower the peak SS.

### Mechanism 3: Orthogonality of Stability and Correctness
**Claim:** Semantic Stability (SS) and task accuracy are orthogonal metrics; optimizing one does not guarantee improvement in the other.  
**Mechanism:** SS measures output consistency across paraphrases via greedy decoding, agnostic to ground truth. Accuracy measures proximity to ground truth. A model can achieve high SS by always giving the same wrong answer (low bias, high stability) or low SS by scattering across both correct and incorrect answers (high variance).  
**Core assumption:** The paper’s definition of “hallucination” is strictly variance‑driven instability, not incorrectness.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract’s orthogonal statement; Table 2 showing R4 improves SS on GSM8k but degrades it on Facts; Section 8’s stable‑but‑wrong example.  
**Break condition:** Orthogonality holds only when SS is computed with greedy decoding and without sampling noise; conflating SS with sampling‑based self‑consistency or factuality checks would blur the distinction.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bias‑Variance Decomposition**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s theoretical framing and phase diagram interpretation rely on decomposing error into bias (systematic deviation) and variance (instability under perturbation). This underpins why compression can sometimes help (reduce variance) and sometimes harm (increase bias).  
  *Quick check:* If a model gives different wrong answers to paraphrased versions of the same question, is this primarily a bias or a variance problem? **Answer:** Variance (instability across semantically equivalent inputs).

- **Concept: Knowledge Distillation (KD)**  
  *Why needed:* SparseKD uses a teacher model’s output distribution (log‑probabilities) as a distillation signal during multi‑stage pruning. Unlike hard‑label distillation, probability‑domain targets preserve uncertainty information, helping to “re‑anchor” the student and maintain predictive structure while removing parameters.  
  *Quick check:* Why does SparseKD use probability‑domain (log‑prob) targets instead of hard labels during compression stages? **Answer:** To match the full distribution and retain necessary uncertainty, which is crucial for preserving correct pathways when pruning.

- **Concept: Greedy Decoding vs. Sampling**  
  *Why needed:* SS validity depends on using greedy decoding (temperature 0) to isolate *structural* variance from *stochastic* sampling noise. This ensures the measured instability stems from the model’s internal representations rather than randomness.  
  *Quick check:* If SS were measured using nucleus sampling (temperature > 0), what type of variance would dominate, and why would that be problematic? **Answer:** Sampling noise would dominate, conflating stochastic variability with structural instability and obscuring the true variance‑driven hallucinations the paper aims to study.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Paraphrase Generator -> Target Model (Student) -> Metric Calculator  
Teacher Model -> Compression Engine (SparseKD) -> Target Model (Student) (offline)

**Critical path:** For a given sparsity level: (1) generate *k* paraphrases per test prompt; (2) greedy‑decode each from the compressed model; (3) compute mode agreement per prompt (PC@*k*); (4) average PC@*k* across prompts to obtain SS. The compression pipeline runs separately to produce checkpoints at target sparsities.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **SS vs. Accuracy:** Maximizing SS via aggressive pruning can degrade accuracy due to bias collapse.  
- **Paraphrase quality vs. cost:** More and higher‑quality paraphrases improve SS reliability but increase computational expense and potential noise.  
- **Compression schedule:** Number and aggressiveness of SparseKD stages shape the SS phase diagram; a steeper pruning schedule may lower the peak.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **High SS + Low Accuracy:** Stable but systematically wrong outputs (e.g., “the sun” as largest planet).  
- **Low SS + Medium/High Accuracy:** Variance‑dominated instability; dense baseline exhibits this.  
- **Task‑specific SS degradation:** e.g., lower SS on a factual recall probe indicates insufficient distillation coverage for that knowledge domain.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline SS sweep:** Measure SS (PC@10, greedy) of the dense model on a dataset like GSM8k.  
2. **SparseKD phase diagram:** Apply SparseKD at increasing sparsity levels (R1, R4, R6, R8), compute SS and accuracy, plot SS vs. sparsity to locate the peak.  
3. **Orthogonality stress test:** Compare SS and accuracy of a model at the SS peak (e.g., R4) against the dense model on a factual QA set (Facts) to demonstrate high SS can coincide with lower accuracy.

## Limitations
- Paraphrase generation methodology is unspecified, making SS results sensitive to paraphraser choice and quality.
- Evaluation is limited to a single model (Qwen3‑0.6B) and a narrow set of tasks, raising questions about generalizability.
- The orthogonality of SS and accuracy is established only under greedy decoding; behavior under stochastic decoding remains unexplored.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑model replication:** Apply SparseKD to multiple LLM families (e.g., Llama, Mistral) and verify if the SS peak and orthogonality hold across architectures.
2. **Paraphrase sensitivity analysis:** Systematically vary paraphrase generation methods (rule‑based, T5, GPT‑4) and quantify their impact on SS to establish measurement robustness.
3. **Decoding mode interaction:** Compare SS under greedy versus nucleus sampling (e.g., temperature 0.7) to determine how orthogonality and the SS‑accuracy relationship change when sampling noise is introduced.
