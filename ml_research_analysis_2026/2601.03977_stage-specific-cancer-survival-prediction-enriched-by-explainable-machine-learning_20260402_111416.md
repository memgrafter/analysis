# Stage-specific cancer survival prediction enriched by explainable machine learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03977
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03977
- **Reference count**: 8
- **Primary result**: Stage-specific ML models (especially CatBoost) deliver accurate and interpretable 5‑year survival predictions for colorectal, stomach, and liver cancers, with distinct prognostic features emerging per stage.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the overestimation and loss of stage-specific insights in traditional cancer survival models by developing separate stage-specific models for colorectal, stomach, and liver cancers using SEER data. Four machine learning models are trained per stage, with CatBoost achieving the highest AUC‑ROC across most stage-cancer combinations (e.g., colorectal: localized 0.821, regional 0.809, distant 0.860; stomach localized 0.956; liver localized 0.868). Explainability analysis via SHAP and LIME reveals that key prognostic features vary by stage: age, surgery, and tumor size dominate early stages, while histologic subtypes and lymph node evaluation codes (e.g., code 98) are critical in advanced stages. The work demonstrates that stage-specific modeling not only improves prediction accuracy but also yields clinically interpretable insights.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses SEER registry data (2004–2015) for colorectal, stomach, and liver cancers. A binary 5‑year survival target is constructed from vital status, survival months, and cause of death, censoring non-cancer deaths within 60 months. For each cancer and SEER-defined stage (localized, regional, distant), the data undergo stratified K‑fold cross‑validation. Four classifiers (Logistic Regression, AdaBoost, Random Forest, CatBoost) are trained with hyperparameter tuning via GridSearchCV and class weighting to address imbalance. Numerical features are standardized within the pipeline to avoid leakage. Model performance is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and primarily AUC‑ROC. The best model (CatBoost) is selected and explained globally with SHAP and locally with LIME to identify stage-specific prognostic drivers.

## Key Results
- CatBoost achieved the highest AUC‑ROC across stage-cancer combinations, notably colorectal distant (0.860), stomach localized (0.956), and liver localized (0.868).
- SHAP/LIME analyses revealed stage‑dependent feature importance: early stages are dominated by age, surgery, and tumor size; advanced stages rely on histologic subtypes and lymph node codes (e.g., code 98 for “not examined”).
- Stage‑specific modeling prevented performance overestimation seen in pooled models and provided clinically interpretable insights.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Training separate models for each cancer stage prevents performance overestimation and captures stage-specific survival heterogeneity.
- **Mechanism**: A single model trained on pooled multi-stage data optimizes an average decision boundary that cannot optimally separate survivors from non‑survivors within any single stage, because each stage has distinct prognostic feature distributions. Pooling inflates metrics because easier‑to‑predict subpopulations (e.g., localized) dominate the error landscape, masking poor performance on harder subpopulations (e.g., distant).
- **Core assumption**: The relationship between predictor variables and survival outcome differs meaningfully across SEER-defined stages.
- **Evidence anchors**: Substantial dataset distribution differences by cancer type (e.g., stomach 43.2% distant); notable AUC variations across stages for the same cancer (e.g., colorectal: 0.821 vs. 0.860).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Post‑hoc explainability (SHAP/LIME) applied to stage‑specific models reveals clinically plausible, stage‑dependent prognostic drivers that are obscured in pooled or non‑explainable models.
- **Mechanism**: Isolating model explanations within a single stage allows the marginal contribution of features (SHAP) or local surrogate fits (LIME) to reflect variable importance specific to that stage’s biology and treatment context. For example, surgical completeness may dominate early stages where resection is curative, while histologic aggressiveness dominates metastatic stages.
- **Core assumption**: The optimal predictive features for survival differ systematically across the disease progression spectrum (localized → regional → distant).
- **Evidence anchors**: SHAP shows “Surgery Code” and “Tumor Size” as top features for localized disease across cancers, while “Histologic Type” codes emerge for distant‑stage stomach and liver cancers. The SEER code `98` for “lymph nodes not examined” appears as a top feature only in the *regional* stage for colorectal and stomach cancers, matching clinical logic for nodal assessment.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Model explainability that aligns with known clinical knowledge builds trust and supports personalized treatment planning, a prerequisite for clinical adoption.
- **Mechanism**: XAI outputs (e.g., SHAP summary plots) that recover established medical associations (older age → higher mortality, more nodes examined → better survival) validate that the ML model’s logic is not based on spurious correlations. This “footprint validation” reduces the black‑box barrier, allowing clinicians to scrutinize predictions with confidence.
- **Core assumption**: Clinicians are more likely to accept and integrate ML-based decision support when the rationale is transparent and consistent with their domain expertise.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper notes alignment with prior evidence (age, surgery, lymph nodes) and positions its value as confirming “clinically sensible reasoning.”

## Foundational Learning

- **Survival Analysis with Censoring & Gold‑Standard Definition**  
  The outcome is not a simple binary label but a time‑to‑event derived from Vital Status, Survival Months, and Cause of Death. The rule‑based 5‑year target introduces specific censoring logic (excluding patients with <60 months non‑cancer death). *Quick check*: How would a patient who died of a heart attack 48 months after a colorectal cancer diagnosis be labeled and why?

- **SEER Staging & Data Schema**  
  The entire analysis hinges on accurate stage assignment (Summary Stage: localized = confined to organ, regional = adjacent spread, distant = remote metastasis). Features like Regional Nodes Examined vs. Positive and coded values (e.g., `98` for “not examined”) are SEER‑specific and must be interpreted correctly. *Quick check*: What does a Regional Nodes Positive code of `98` signify, and why is its appearance as a top feature in the regional stage clinically logical?

- **Post‑Hoc Explainability (SHAP vs. LIME)**  
  The study uses SHAP for global feature importance across the dataset and LIME for local, instance‑specific explanations. These are approximations, not causal truths: SHAP values derive from Shapley values in cooperative game theory applied to model outputs; LIME fits a local linear model. Their outputs can differ and have different stability/computational trade‑offs. *Quick check*: What is a key methodological difference between the explanation provided by a SHAP summary plot and a LIME plot for the same patient?

## Architecture Onboarding

The pipeline flows as: Data Ingestion & Preprocessing → Per‑Stage Model Training Pipeline → Evaluation & Selection → Explainability Module.

**Critical path**: Raw SEER → Stage‑based Split → Stage‑specific GridSearchCV → CatBoost Selection → SHAP/LIME on final models.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Chose CatBoost for performance and native categorical handling, sacrificing intrinsic interpretability; compensated with post‑hoc SHAP/LIME.
- Used class weighting instead of resampling to preserve the original data distribution.
- Explained a single final model per stage rather than an ensemble, risking hyperparameter‑specific biases.

**Failure signatures**:
1. **Stage leakage**: Preprocessing before the stage split contaminates folds. Always split first, then normalize per stage‑fold.
2. **Sparse stage failure**: Very few samples in a stage‑cancer group (e.g., distant stomach) may cause convergence issues or noisy SHAP values.
3. **Explanation instability**: LIME outputs vary significantly with random seeds, indicating unreliable local explanations.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Validate stage separation: After splitting, confirm the survival rate differs by >10% absolute between stages for each cancer.
2. Reproduce primary result: Train colorectal models; verify CatBoost AUC for distant (0.860) exceeds regional (0.809) and localized (0.821).
3. Explainability sanity check: For localized colorectal model, ensure `Age` and `Surgery Code` are top‑5 SHAP features; for a high‑risk patient, check if `Age` and `Extension` are top LIME contributors toward “Dead”.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can SHAP and LIME explanations be reliably validated with clinical experts and through multiple XAI techniques to ensure trustworthy model interpretability in oncology?**  
   *Why open*: The study applied SHAP and LIME but did not involve clinical validation or comparison with alternative XAI methods.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Prospective studies where oncology experts assess the consistency and clinical plausibility of SHAP/LIME outputs against ground truth knowledge, and comparative experiments measuring agreement between different XAI methods.

2. **Can counterfactual explanations for stage‑specific cancer survival models provide actionable, personalized intervention insights that improve clinical decision‑making?**  
   *Why open*: Counterfactual methods were not implemented or evaluated.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Clinical user studies testing whether counterfactual suggestions (e.g., “if tumor size were reduced by X mm, survival probability increases by Y%”) lead to more informed or confident treatment decisions compared to current SHAP/LIME outputs.

3. **Do gradient‑based XAI methods like Integrated Gradients yield more stable and reproducible feature attributions than SHAP or LIME for cancer survival prediction models?**  
   *Why open*: Only SHAP and LIME were used; no comparison with Integrated Gradients or other gradient‑based techniques.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Systematic benchmarking across multiple random seeds, data perturbations, or model retrainings to measure the consistency of feature importance rankings from Integrated Gradients versus SHAP/LIME.

4. **How generalizable are the stage‑specific survival prediction models and their feature importance patterns to independent, diverse patient populations outside the SEER dataset?**  
   *Why open*: Models were trained and evaluated solely on SEER data, which may have selection biases and limited diversity.  
   *Resolving evidence*: External validation studies applying the same pipeline to data from other national cancer registries (e.g., European, Asian) or contemporary datasets, assessing whether feature importance patterns remain consistent and performance metrics hold.

## Limitations
- The analysis depends on unreported methodological details (number of CV folds, final hyperparameters, exact encoding strategy, handling of rare SEER codes), blocking exact replication.
- Relying on a single model architecture (CatBoost) per stage means explanations are tied to one hyperparameter set; ensemble explanations were not considered.
- The claim that explainability builds clinical trust remains untested; without expert validation, the clinical plausibility of SHAP/LIME outputs is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Stage‑specific modeling prevents overestimation & captures heterogeneity**: **High confidence**. Supported by consistent AUC variations across stages and a clear mechanistic argument about decision‑boundary averaging.
- **Explainability reveals stage‑dependent prognostic drivers**: **Medium confidence**. SHAP/LIME results align with clinical expectations, but post‑hoc explanations are approximations and may be sensitive to model instabilities or encoding choices.
- **Explainability builds clinical trust for adoption**: **Low confidence**. This aspirational claim lacks clinician evaluation; potential counter‑intuitive features in edge cases could undermine trust.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce stage performance separation**: Train the colorectal cancer models and verify that CatBoost’s AUC for distant stage (0.860) is higher than for regional (0.809) and localized (0.821), confirming the core performance pattern.
2. **Audit preprocessing for leakage**: Encapsulate all preprocessing (scaling, encoding) in a scikit‑learn pipeline fitted only on training folds. Compare metrics with/without strict pipeline; a significant drop would indicate prior leakage.
3. **Test explanation stability**: For the localized colorectal CatBoost model, compute SHAP values 10 times with different random seeds and measure the variance in top‑10 feature rankings (e.g., Spearman correlation). High instability (variance >0.2) would question the reliability of the reported global importance.
