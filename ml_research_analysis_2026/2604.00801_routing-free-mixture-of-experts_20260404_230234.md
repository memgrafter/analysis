# Routing-Free Mixture-of-Experts

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00801
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00801
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Routing-Free MoE eliminates centralized routers, Softmax, Top-K, and static balancing, achieving lower perplexity (e.g., 19.97 vs 24.58 at scale L) and higher downstream accuracy (+0.77 pp) with improved stability and up to 1.19× throughput.

## Executive Summary

Standard Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models rely on a centralized router to select a sparse subset of experts for each token, incurring computational bottlenecks, fixed sparsity constraints, and rigid load-balancing requirements. This paper introduces Routing-Free MoE, which removes the external router entirely. Instead, each expert independently decides whether to activate based on an internal confidence score derived from a low-rank projection of the input. A unified adaptive loss jointly optimizes token- and expert-level load balancing through a configurable interpolation parameter. Across three model scales (up to 0.8B parameters), Routing-Free MoE reduces perplexity, improves zero-shot accuracy, exhibits better training stability, and delivers higher throughput under expert parallelism by eliminating routing synchronization overhead.

## Method Summary

Routing-Free MoE replaces the standard router with an expert-self-activation mechanism. For each expert, the input token \(x\) is projected by a low-rank matrix \(A_{\text{gate},i} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times r}\) to produce a vector; its squared L2 norm \(s_i = \|x A_{\text{gate},i}\|^2\) is computed. A per-expert bias \(b_i\) is subtracted, followed by a ReLU non-linearity: \(G_i(x) = \text{ReLU}(s_i - b_i)\). A global threshold \(\theta\) then yields a binary activation decision \(f_i(x) = \mathbb{1}\{G_i(x) - \theta \ge 0\}\). Only activated experts compute their feed-forward network (FFN) output, which reuses the projection \(x A_{\text{gate},i}\) within a gated linear unit (GLU) design inherited from Autonomy-of-Experts.

Load balancing is handled by a unified adaptive loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}} = \mu \mathcal{L}_{\text{EB}} + (1-\mu) \mathcal{L}_{\text{TB}}\), where \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{EB}}\) encourages uniform token distribution across experts (expert-balancing) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{TB}}\) encourages a uniform number of activated experts per token (token-balancing). The interpolation parameter \(\mu\) allows flexible trade-offs between the two objectives. An adaptive coefficient \(\lambda_t\) multiplies this loss and is updated per training step to steer the empirical activation density \(\rho_t\) toward a target \(\rho_\infty\) (the desired sparsity ratio). Specifically, \(\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t \cdot (1+\eta)\) if \(\rho_t > \rho_\infty\), else \(\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t / (1+\eta)\). Biases \(b_i\) are initialized to a small value (e.g., \(10^{-6}\)) so all experts are initially active, promoting early specialization before sparsity emerges.

Models are trained from scratch on the OpenWebText corpus for one epoch using AdamW. The three evaluated scales (Small, Medium, Large) differ in depth, hidden dimension, number of experts, and low-rank dimension \(r\). Primary metrics are validation perplexity and zero-shot accuracy on nine benchmarks; throughput is measured under expert parallelism.

## Key Results

- **Perplexity reduction:** Routing-Free MoE achieves significantly lower perplexity than standard MoE; e.g., at the Large scale (0.8B), RFMoE obtains 19.97 vs 24.58.
- **Downstream accuracy:** Improves average zero-shot accuracy by +0.77 percentage points over the MoE baseline (statistically significant).
- **Training stability & throughput:** RFMoE remains stable at learning rates where standard MoE collapses, and yields up to 1.19× higher throughput under expert parallelism by removing the router’s synchronization bottleneck.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Expert self-activation via internal score
Each expert determines its activation independently using a scalar confidence score \(s_i = \|x A_{\text{gate},i}\|^2 - b_i\), passed through ReLU and compared to a global threshold \(\theta\). This eliminates the need for a centralized router. The mechanism assumes the norm of the low-rank projection captures input-specific relevance, while the bias \(b_i\) enables adaptive thresholding. Evidence from the abstract and Section 3.1 confirms that activation is fully encapsulated within experts and optimized via gradient flow. A break condition arises if the projection fails to encode relevance, causing misaligned activations and performance degradation.

### Mechanism 2: Removal of centralized routing bottleneck
Standard MoE’s router introduces a sequential, non-parallelizable computation (matrix multiplication, Softmax, Top-K) that must complete before any expert can start. Routing-Free MoE replaces this with per-expert scoring that can be fully parallelized across devices and pipelined with communication. The router’s limited capacity and the rigid Top-K sparsity constraint are avoided, and magnitude information from Softmax is preserved. Theoretical analysis (Section B.1) and empirical throughput results (Table 5) directly attribute efficiency gains to the elimination of the routing pipeline. A potential break condition is that computing \(x A_{\text{gate}}\) for all experts might outweigh router savings if the expert count is very high or the low-rank dimension \(r\) is not sufficiently small; the paper maintains \(r \ll D\) to mitigate this.

### Mechanism 3: Unified adaptive load-balancing loss
A differentiable loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}} = \mu \mathcal{L}_{\text{EB}} + (1-\mu) \mathcal{L}_{\text{TB}}\) jointly optimizes token- and expert-level balancing. \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{EB}}\) promotes uniform token distribution across experts (preventing some experts from being overloaded), while \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{TB}}\) encourages a consistent number of active experts per token (allowing flexible compute per input). The interpolation \(\mu\) (default 0.5) balances these complementary objectives. An adaptive coefficient \(\lambda_t\) steers the empirical activation density \(\rho_t\) toward the target \(\rho_\infty\). Empirical results (Table 4, Figure 7) show that intermediate \(\mu\) yields lower perplexity and higher throughput than either pure objective, supporting the complementarity claim. A break condition could occur if the optimal load-balancing strategy for a particular task or hardware strongly favors one extreme; the fixed \(\mu\) might then be suboptimal.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input token \(x\) → [per-expert linear projection \(A_{\text{gate},i}\)] → squared L2 norm \(\|x A_{\text{gate},i}\|^2\) → add bias \(b_i\) → ReLU → subtract global threshold \(\theta\) → binary activation \(f_i\). If \(f_i=1\), the projection \(x A_{\text{gate},i}\) is reused in the expert’s GLU-based FFN (Equation 6) to produce output.

### Critical path
Under expert parallelism, each device holds a subset of experts. For a batch of tokens, experts on a device compute their internal scores for all tokens in parallel. The thresholding yields a binary mask; only activated tokens are processed by that expert’s FFN. Activated outputs are asynchronously returned and summed across experts. This pipeline avoids the centralized router’s sequential dependency, enabling better overlap of computation and communication.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Flexibility vs. control:** A single global threshold \(\theta\) provides a simple deployment knob for sparsity but cannot adapt to per-layer or per-expert optimal densities; however, Figure 6 shows beneficial self-organization emerges when per-layer enforcement is lifted.
- **Threshold sensitivity:** While performance is robust to moderate \(\theta\) changes (Table 6, Figure 8), extreme thresholds can cause unusual activation patterns (e.g., SST-2 spike), indicating task-dependent specialization.
- **\(\mu\) interpolation:** The choice of \(\mu\) (default 0.5) balances token- vs. expert-balancing; optimal \(\mu\) may vary with hardware constraints or task characteristics.

### Failure signatures
- **Gradient explosion/instability at high learning rates:** Standard MoE collapses at \(\alpha=2\!\times\!10^{-3}\) (scale S) while RFMoE remains stable, but RFMoE also fails at \(\alpha=5\!\times\!10^{-3}\) (Table 8).
- **Unstable \(\lambda\) adaptation:** If the initial activation density \(\rho\) is far from target \(\rho_\infty\) and the update rate \(\eta\) is poorly set, \(\lambda\) may grow or shrink too aggressively, distorting the language modeling loss.
- **Expert collapse or inactivity:** Improper initialization of biases \(b_i\) or too slow \(\lambda\) adaptation can prevent some experts from activating during warm-up, hindering specialization.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Ablation against AoE and ReMoE:** Table 2 shows that replacing standard MoE’s router with AoE’s internal scoring (still using Top-K/Softmax) or ReMoE’s ReLU gating (still external router) yields smaller gains; RFMoE removes both, demonstrating additive improvements.
2. **Vary low-rank dimension \(r\):** Table 3 shows increasing \(r\) improves perplexity with diminishing returns, confirming \(r\) controls capacity of the internal scoring mechanism.
3. **Sweep load-balancing interpolation \(\mu\):** Table 4 and Figure 7 reveal that \(\mu=0.5\) outperforms pure token- or expert-balancing, and visualizations show more uniform activation patterns across tokens and experts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Scalability beyond 0.8B parameters:** The study is limited to models up to 0.8B; it remains an open question whether findings generalize to larger-scale pretraining (e.g., 10B+).
2. **Multilingual and diverse domain evaluation:** Results are on nine English benchmarks; performance on multilingual tasks and varied domains is unexplored.
3. **Conversion of standard MoE to Routing-Free MoE:** The paper did not explore whether a pretrained standard MoE can be converted via continued pretraining or training-free adaptation.
4. **Optimal \(\mu\) across scales and data:** The default \(\mu=0.5\) is used for all scales, but its optimality may vary with model size or dataset; a systematic sweep is lacking.

## Limitations

- **Unspecified training details:** Exact token count/steps and precise adaptive \(\lambda\) update rule are not fully described, hindering exact replication.
- **Scalability unproven:** The approach has only been tested up to 0.8B parameters and 24 experts; behavior with larger models and more experts is unknown.
- **Single global threshold:** Using one \(\theta\) for all experts and layers may be suboptimal; per-expert or per-layer thresholds could yield better trade-offs.

## Confidence

- **Self-activation mechanism replaces router with no performance loss:** High
- **Unified load-balancing loss (\(\mu\) interpolation) improves efficiency:** Medium
- **Throughput gains from removing router bottleneck:** High
- **Improved training stability:** Medium
