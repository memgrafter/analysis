# How to Train Your Deep Research Agent? Prompt, Reward, and Policy Optimization in Search-R1

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19526
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19526
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Search-R1++ (Fast template + REINFORCE + F1+) raises average EM by ~0.04 on Qwen2.5-7B (0.403→0.442) and by ~0.042 on Qwen2.5-3B (0.289→0.331), and F1+ outperforms EM-based training.

## Executive Summary
Deep research agents solve knowledge-intensive QA by iteratively searching external knowledge and generating answers. This paper systematically studies how to train such agents with reinforcement learning, focusing on three components: prompt template, reward function, and policy optimization algorithm. Through extensive ablations, the authors find that a direct “Fast Thinking” template yields better stability and accuracy than a verbose “Slow Thinking” template; the F1 reward alone leads to answer avoidance, but adding small penalties for missing search or answer tags (F1+) mitigates collapse and actually exceeds exact match performance; and the REINFORCE algorithm outperforms PPO and GRPO in both final accuracy and inference efficiency. The Search-R1++ configuration—Fast Thinking, REINFORCE, and F1+—sets new state-of-the-art results on seven benchmark datasets for Qwen2.5-7B and 3B models.

## Method Summary
The study trains LLM agents via RL in a retrieval-augmented environment. Agents receive a question and a prompt that dictates a structured interaction pattern using special tags: they may think, issue a search command (which retrieves top-3 Wikipedia passages via E5), and finally output an answer. An episode terminates when the <answer> tag is produced or the maximum token limit is reached. The training data merges NQ and HotpotQA train sets, and evaluation occurs across seven multi-hop QA benchmarks. The optimization loop follows standard policy gradient methods: for a batch of questions, the current policy generates one (PPO) or multiple (REINFORCE/GRPO) rollouts, an episodic reward is computed (EM, F1, or F1+), and the policy is updated using gradient descent with a KL penalty towards the reference model. Hyperparameters include a learning rate of 1e-6, batch size 512, 600 training steps, AdamW, Fully Sharded Data Parallel, and a context of 4096 tokens. REINFORCE and GRPO use n_agent=5 samples per prompt and a group mean baseline, with KL coefficient 0.001. The best model, Search-R1++, combines the Fast Thinking prompt, REINFORCE, and F1+ reward.

## Key Results
- Fast Thinking direct decisions yield higher stability and accuracy than Slow Thinking (explicit reasoning) across datasets.
- F1 reward causes training collapse via answer avoidance; adding a 0.1 penalty for missing <search> and 0.1 for missing <answer> (F1+) prevents collapse and surpasses EM-based training.
- REINFORCE achieves better performance and efficiency than PPO and GRPO, using fewer search actions on average.
- Search-R1++ improves average EM over Search-R1 from 0.403 to 0.442 on Qwen2.5-7B and from 0.289 to 0.331 on Qwen2.5-3B; F1+ also outperforms EM reward.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Fast Thinking template avoids the degenerate behavior observed with Slow Thinking, where the policy learns to generate excessive `<think>` blocks without making progress, likely because the reward does not directly penalize verbosity and the generates many tags to game the reward. The F1+ reward explicitly incentivizes both search actions and final answer production by subtracting fixed penalties when these are absent, thus preventing the policy from collapsing into a “do nothing” strategy. REINFORCE’s simplicity and use of a group baseline (from multiple rollouts) appears well-suited to the sparse EP reward landscape, while PPO’s critic may suffer from high variance or misestimation with short, sparse trajectories, leading to instability.

## Foundational Learning
- Reinforcement Learning for Language Models: Policy gradient updates from outcome rewards with KL regularization to maintain model quality. *Quick check*: Compute gradient on a single logprob/reward pair and verify loss decreases.
- Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): Iterative search actions retrieve external passages, which are injected into the context to ground answers. *Quick check*: Run retriever on a known fact query (e.g., “capital of France”) and confirm top passages contain Paris.
- Structured Prompting with Control Tags: Tags like `<think>`, `<search>`, `<answer>` define a deterministic interaction protocol that facilitates credit assignment and environment parsing. *Quick check*: Prompt a pretrained model with a test question and check it emits at least one `<search>` before `<answer>`.
- Reward Design for Sparse Outcomes: EM is a binary final reward; F1 provides soft token overlap; penalties like F1+ shape behavior to avoid degenerate strategies. *Quick check*: Compute EM, F1, and F1+ on a few model outputs; ensure F1+ differs appropriately when tags are missing.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Question -> Fast Thinking Prompt -> LLM Policy -> (emits <search> or <answer>) -> if <search> then Retrieval System -> top-3 passages inserted as `<information>` -> back to LLM -> repeat until <answer>.
**Critical Path:** The core loop consists of policy generation, conditional retrieval, and context update, terminating upon answer.
**Design Tradeoffs:** Fast vs Slow prompts trade explicit reasoning for stability; EM vs F1+ reward trades simplicity for robustness; REINFORCE vs PPO trades algorithmic stability for sample efficiency and lower search usage.
**Failure Signatures:** Sudden spikes in response length or `<think>` count (Slow); drop in answer rate (F1); high variance in training score (PPO/GRPO). If the policy ignores tags, rollout parsing fails.
**First Experiments:**  
1. Compare Fast vs Slow: train two models with PPO+EM for 200 steps and measure accuracy/answer rate.  
2. Reward shaping: train Fast+PPO with EM, F1, and F1+ (small scale) and observe stability and final EM/F1.  
3. Algorithm choice: train Fast+F1+ with REINFORCE, PPO, and GRPO on a subset and compare EM and average search count.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What is the causal mechanism linking explicit reasoning prompts (Slow Thinking) to training collapse in Deep Research agents, and can it be mitigated without abandoning structured reasoning entirely? The paper notes a surge in `<think>` tags before collapse but does not prove causality.
- How sensitive is the F1+ reward to the penalty coefficients (α for no-search, β for no-answer), and can these be optimized automatically rather than set heuristically to 0.1?
- Why does REINFORCE achieve greater sample efficiency and lower search counts than PPO in Deep Research, and does this advantage persist when scaling to longer horizons or more diverse knowledge-intensive tasks?
- Are the isolated component effects (Fast Thinking, F1+, REINFORCE) synergistic, independent, or context-dependent when combined, and how transferable are they to other model architectures and retrieval backends?

## Limitations
- **Retrieval setup fidelity** is uncertain: exact Wikipedia 2018 preprocessing and E5 indexing details are unspecified, potentially affecting results.
- **PPO hyperparameters** (e.g., clip range, value loss coefficient) are not fully reported, which could influence stability comparisons with REINFORCE.
- **Computational cost** is prohibitive (e.g., 600 steps on 8 A100s), limiting reproducibility and increasing sensitivity to infrastructure variations.

## Confidence
- Fast Thinking outperforms Slow Thinking in stability and accuracy: **High**
- F1+ reward stabilizes training and surpasses EM: **Medium**
- REINFORCE is more sample-efficient and yields fewer searches than PPO: **Medium**
- Search-R1++ achieves the reported improvements: **Medium** (due to implementation gaps)
