# Transport and Merge: Cross-Architecture Merging for Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05495
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05495
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Cross-architecture merging via optimal transport improves low-resource target models by 3–6.5 percentage points on language benchmarks (e.g., MalayMMLU) and up to 2 points on domain benchmarks, outperforming SFT and distillation with minimal calibration data.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of transferring knowledge from a large high-resource LLM (source) to a smaller low-resource LLM with a different architecture (target). Standard model merging fails due to weight incompatibility. The proposed method uses optimal transport (OT) to align intermediate activations of both models on a small calibration set, inferring structured neuron correspondences. These correspondences are converted into a direct weight-space fusion operation: selective top-k neuron replacement followed optionally by residual-frozen adaptation. Experiments on low-resource languages (Malay, Indonesian, Cantonese, Thai) and specialized domains (finance, medical) show consistent improvements over the base target model and outperform fine-tuning and distillation baselines. The approach achieves these gains with only a few thousand calibration samples, demonstrating effective cross-architecture knowledge transfer.

## Method Summary
The method proceeds in four stages. First, a small calibration set (∼2000 samples) from the target language or domain is passed through both source and target models to capture intermediate activations (inputs/outputs of linear sublayers). Second, for each pair of layers (target layer ℓ, source layer m), a cost matrix is computed using 1 minus Pearson correlation between activation channels; entropic OT (Sinkhorn) solves for pre- and post-layer transport plans Qℓm_in and Qℓm_out. Third, layer-level costs are aggregated, and a second OT solves for a global mixing matrix P that determines how much each source layer contributes to each target layer; an effective matrix Peff combines pre/post alignments. Fourth, for each target layer, the top-k most activated neurons (based on mean absolute activation on the calibration set) are selected; the source weights are transported via fW = Q_out * W_B * Q_in^T and fused into the target weights using W_A ← W_A + α · mask · sum_m Peff[ℓ,m] · (fW - W_A). Finally, an optional residual-frozen adaptation freezes the fused residuals and fine-tunes the base target parameters on a small domain-specific dataset, then folds the residuals into the base weights.

## Key Results
- MalayMMLU accuracy increases by 3–6.5 percentage points over the base target model.
- Thai MGSM (Mathematical reasoning) rises from 0.50 to 0.72.
- Medical domain benchmarks gain up to 2 percentage points.
- The method consistently outperforms supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and knowledge distillation baselines on low-resource language and domain transfer tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Optimal transport aligns the distributions of intermediate activations between source and target models, providing a structured mapping of features (channels/neurons) across architectures. This correspondence ensures that when source weights are transported and inserted into the target model, they occupy semantically meaningful locations. The top-k selection focuses the transfer on the most relevant neurons, limiting noise from less important units. The small coefficient α and residual-frozen adaptation prevent the transferred knowledge from overwriting the target's existing capabilities while allowing controlled integration. Consequently, the target model inherits specialized knowledge from the larger source model without catastrophic forgetting, even when architectures differ.

## Foundational Learning
- **Optimal Transport**: Provides a geometrically meaningful alignment of feature distributions.  
  *Why needed*: Enables mapping between mismatched neuron spaces.  
  *Quick check*: Verify transport plans are sparse (low entropy) and align with correlation structure.

- **Activation-based Alignment**: Uses actual model responses on real data to infer correspondences.  
  *Why needed*: Weight-space alone is insufficient across architectures; activations reflect functional behavior.  
  *Quick check*: Correlation-based costs should be higher for semantically unrelated layers (e.g., embedding vs. output).

- **Selective Neuron Transfer**: Top-k activation masking restricts fusion to the most active units.  
  *Why needed*: Reduces noise and prevents dilution of important target knowledge.  
  *Quick check*: Sweep k; performance should improve up to an optimal k then decline.

- **Residual-Frozen Adaptation**: Treats transferred weights as residuals that are frozen during fine-tuning.  
  *Why needed*: Preserves the transferred knowledge while allowing the base model to adapt to the new domain.  
  *Quick check*: Compare with fine-tuning all parameters; freezing should yield higher or comparable gains with less forgetting.

- **Cross-architecture Merging**: General paradigm of combining models with different structures.  
  *Why needed*: Extends model merging beyond identical architectures.  
  *Quick check*: Apply to pairs with mismatched layer counts; the method should still find sensible alignments.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Calibration Dataset -> Activation Extraction (source & target) -> Cost Computation (per layer pair) -> Inner OT (pre, post) -> Layer Cost Aggregation -> Outer OT -> Effective Transport Matrix -> Top-k Neuron Selection -> Weight Fusion -> Residual-Frozen Adaptation (optional)

**Critical Path**  
Calibration data must be representative of the target domain. Activation extraction precedes all cost computations. Inner OT must be solved before aggregating layer costs. Outer OT depends on aggregated costs. Weight fusion relies on the transport matrices and neuron masks. Adaptation requires a separate domain dataset and fine-tuning pipeline.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Data size vs. alignment fidelity: more calibration samples improve correlation estimates but increase compute.
- Fusion coefficient α: larger values speed up knowledge transfer but risk overwriting target knowledge.
- Neuron count k: higher k injects more source knowledge but also more noise; lower k is conservative.
- Computational budget: OT solves scale quadratically with layer width; approximations may be needed for very large models.
- Freezing vs. full fine-tuning: freezing protects transferred knowledge but may limit adaptation flexibility.

**Failure Signatures**  
- High entropy in transport matrices (e.g., >0.8 normalized entropy) indicates poor alignment; reconsider calibration data or OT hyperparameters.
- Performance degrades after fusion when α > 0.2 typically; suggests over-injection of source features.
- Layer cost matrix shows uniformly high values; could indicate architectural mismatch or insufficient data.
- No improvement on target benchmarks after adaptation; might need to adjust learning rate, dataset size, or reduce freezing.
- Memory explosion during activation storage for large models; requires gradient checkpointing or subset sampling.

**First Experiments**  
1. Sweep α ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2} on Malay language transfer; record MalayMMLU accuracy to find optimal injection strength.
2. Vary top-k ∈ {64, 128, 256} while keeping α fixed; measure performance on Thai MGSM to identify the sweet spot for neuron selection.
3. Apply the method to three source-target architecture pairs: (Llama-2 7B → Llama-2 13B, Llama-2 7B → GPT-2, Llama-2 7B → T5) and compare layer costs and final gains to understand the method's robustness to architectural divergence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly identify open questions for future research. However, based on the limitations and discussion, potential open questions include: determining optimal sublayer selection for transport, establishing protocols for calibration data size and representativeness, defining default hyperparameters (α, k, OT settings), understanding boundary conditions for architectural divergence (e.g., encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only), and comprehensively evaluating general capability retention post-merge.

## Limitations
- The paper does not specify exactly which transformer sublayers (e.g., Q/K/V/O projections, MLP gates) should be included in the transport; practitioners must infer.
- The calibration set selection protocol is vague; size (2000) and representativeness are crucial but not formally defined, possibly affecting reproducibility.
- Key hyperparameters such as fusion coefficient α and adaptation learning rate lack stated defaults, requiring task-specific tuning.
- The method's applicability to highly dissimilar architectures (e.g., encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only, drastically different layer counts) is not thoroughly evaluated.
- Evaluation focuses on domain-specific benchmarks; preservation of general capabilities post-merge is not comprehensively measured.
