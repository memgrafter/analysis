# Simple Baselines are Competitive with Code Evolution
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16805
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16805
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Simple baselines (IID random sampling, sequential conditioned sampling) match or outperform sophisticated evolution pipelines (ShinkaEvolve, AIDE) across math bounds, agentic scaffolds, and ML competitions under equal resource constraints.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the prevailing complexity in code evolution techniques by demonstrating that two minimalist baselines—IID random sampling and sequential conditioned sampling—are competitive with, and often surpass, sophisticated pipelines like ShinkaEvolve and AIDE. Across three distinct domains (mathematical bounds, agentic scaffolds, and ML competitions), the baselines match or outperform advanced methods when given equal computational budgets. The authors argue that performance is primarily capped by the search space design (verifier) and the domain knowledge embedded in prompts, while the evolution algorithm itself plays a secondary role. In agentic scaffold tasks, small validation sets introduce high variance that misleads automated search, causing significant performance drops from validation to test and favoring simple handcrafted scaffolds like majority vote. The work calls for standardized evaluation protocols and highlights the importance of evaluation cascades to mitigate stochasticity.

## Method Summary
The paper conducts a comparative evaluation of two simple code evolution baselines—independent and identically distributed (IID) random sampling and sequential conditioned sampling (SCS)—against state-of-the-art pipelines (ShinkaEvolve and AIDE). Experiments span three domains: mathematical bound discovery (9 problems), agentic scaffold design (AIME dataset), and machine learning competitions (10 Kaggle tasks). All methods are run under identical resource constraints, whether API budget, number of function evaluations, or wall-clock time, using the Gemini 2.5 Pro language model. Programs are generated from prompts that include problem specifications and optional domain knowledge; a verifier executes each program and returns a numeric score or success signal. The primary metric is the best score found within the budget, or scaffold accuracy on held-out problems. The study also highlights the impact of search space formulation via the verifier and the role of domain knowledge in prompts.

## Key Results
- Mathematical bounds (9 problems): Sequential conditioned sampling (SCS) matches or exceeds ShinkaEvolve on 6/9 problems under equal budgets.
- Agentic scaffolds (AIME): All automated methods (SCS, IID RS, ShinkaEvolve) show >10% accuracy drops from validation to test; simple majority vote scaffolds are most robust.
- Machine learning competitions (10 Kaggle tasks): SCS beats AIDE on 6/10 tasks under identical time/evaluation constraints.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A problem's search space formulation (verifier design) is the primary determinant of the performance ceiling in code evolution for mathematical discovery, outweighing the sophistication of the evolution pipeline.
- **Mechanism:** The verifier defines the objective function's landscape. A well-designed search space (e.g., via expert reformulation) expands the set of feasible, high-quality solutions, allowing even simple samplers (IID RS) to find better bounds. A poor search space constrains all methods to similar suboptimal regions.
- **Core assumption:** Bound improvement is limited by the expressiveness of the program class being searched over, not the search algorithm's ability to navigate a fixed space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "a problem’s search space and domain knowledge in the prompt are chiefly what dictate a search’s performance ceiling"
  - [section 4.1] Reformulating the uncertainty inequality problem led *all* methods to find a new, improved bound (0.3482) under the same budget, a larger gain than pipeline optimization.
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighboring papers focus on scaffold design or specific applications, not comparative analysis of search space impact.
- **Break condition:** If a novel, highly sophisticated search algorithm (e.g., with learned mutation operators) consistently and significantly outperforms all simple baselines *across multiple, expertly designed search spaces* with identical prompts and budgets, the primacy of search space design would be challenged.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The amount and nature of domain knowledge embedded in the prompt (beyond minimal specifications) significantly affect the *efficiency* (speed to good solutions) of code evolution, but not necessarily the ultimate performance ceiling if the search space is adequate.
- **Mechanism:** Prompts act as a prior over the program distribution. Including expert insights (e.g., specific algorithmic tricks, known constructions, or properties of good/bad solutions) biases sampling toward promising regions, reducing the number of evaluations needed. However, if the search space itself is limiting, this efficiency gain cannot overcome the ceiling.
- **Core assumption:** LLM-based code evolution is fundamentally a guided search within a prompt-defined distribution over a fixed program space; better priors yield better sample efficiency.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4] "domain knowledge used... can help boost a method’s performance without changing its pipeline." Example: Adding a helper function (`compute_max_radii`) to the circle packing prompt allowed IID RS to match ShinkaEvolve's best bound.
  - [section 4] Conversely, a domain-knowledge-rich initial program sometimes biased ShinkaEvolve toward suboptimal solutions compared to a minimal initial program.
  - [abstract] "variable domain knowledge in the prompts can change its efficiency."
- **Break condition:** If, across a diverse set of problems with fixed, minimal prompts, a sophisticated pipeline consistently reaches the same performance ceiling as IID RS *but* does so with orders-of-magnitude fewer evaluations, prompt domain knowledge would be shown as secondary to algorithmic search power.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** In domains where scaffold performance is evaluated on small, high-variance validation sets (like AIME), the stochasticity in evaluation overwhelms the signal of true scaffold quality, causing code evolution to select overfitted, suboptimal scaffolds. Simple hand-designed scaffolds (e.g., majority vote) are more robust.
- **Mechanism:** With few validation samples (e.g., ~100 questions), the observed accuracy is a noisy estimate of true performance. The selection pressure in evolution (choosing the "best" scaffold) becomes dominated by random fluctuations. Scaffolds that perform well by chance on the small validation set are propagated, but their performance does not generalize to a larger test set. Hand-designed scaffolds like majority vote have low inherent variance in their performance estimate when aggregated over multiple calls.
- **Core assumption:** The generalization gap for agentic scaffolds is inherently noisy; a small validation set provides an unreliable ranking of scaffold quality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5] All automated methods (ADAS, ShinkaEvolve, IID RS) show >10% accuracy drops from validation (AIME 2024) to test (AIME 2025), while majority vote drops only ~5%.
  - [section 5] Re-evaluating the selected scaffolds 10 times on the validation set reveals their *true* (lower) validation performance, indicating they were selected due to evaluation noise.
  - [section 5] Figure 4 shows even majority vote@5 has a standard deviation >1% when evaluated on 10 repetitions of AIME 2025, implying scaffold ranking noise is substantial with ~100 samples.
  - [abstract] "high variance in the scaffolds coupled with small datasets leads to suboptimal scaffolds being selected."
- **Break condition:** If, using a sufficiently large validation set (e.g., >1000 questions with multiple evaluations each) that reduces accuracy standard deviation to <0.1%, code evolution consistently discovers scaffolds that significantly outperform simple hand-designed ones (like majority vote@k) on an unseen test set, then the core limitation was evaluation size, not the evolution process itself.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Search Space Design**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's central thesis is that designing the *verifier* (the function that checks/scoring a program) is more critical than the search algorithm. An engineer must understand how the problem formulation constrains the solution space.
  - *Quick check question:* If changing the verifier for a math bound problem (e.g., allowing a larger class of functions) yields a bigger improvement than switching from IID RS to ShinkaEvolve, what does that imply about where research effort should be focused?

- **Concept: Evaluation Stochasticity & Statistical Robustness**
  - *Why needed here:* The agentic scaffold results are a case study in how noisy performance estimates from small datasets lead to invalid conclusions. An engineer must learn to design experiments that account for variance.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is reporting a single validation accuracy from 3 evaluations of a scaffold on 100 questions potentially misleading, and what metric (from Appendix K) should be used instead?

- **Concept: Cost-Benefit Tradeoffs in API-Driven Search**
  - *Why needed here:* All domains have a primary constraint (API cost, function evals, wall-clock time). An engineer must map algorithmic design choices (parallelism, sequential conditioning, feedback loops) to these constraints.
  - *Quick check question:* Under a fixed *wall-clock time* budget, why might SCS (sequential conditioned sampling) perform worse than IID RS (IID random sampling), even if it is more sample-efficient under an API budget?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1.  **LLM Sampler:** The core generative model (e.g., Gemini-2.5 Pro). Takes a prompt + (optional) parent programs → outputs new program.
  2.  **Verifier/Executor:** Runs the generated code in a sandbox. Returns a score (bound value, validation accuracy) or failure signal. Defines the search space.
  3.  **Prompt Manager:** Constructs prompts. Injects domain knowledge (hints, helper functions) and, for SCS, includes code from selected parent programs.
  4.  **Budget Tracker:** Monitors the primary constraint (cumulative API cost, # of function evaluations, elapsed wall-clock time).
  5.  **Selection Mechanism (Optional):** For advanced pipelines. Selects parents from an archive based on fitness/diversity. *Baselines have no explicit selection; SCS uses random selection from successful parents.*
  6.  **Evaluation Orchestrator (for scaffolds):** Manages cascaded evaluations. First-level: quick eval on small validation set. Second-level: full eval on larger set for top candidates.

- **Critical path:**
  1.  Define problem → **Design Verifier** (search space) → **Craft Base Prompt** (minimal domain knowledge).
  2.  Choose primary constraint (API $, #evals, time).
  3.  Configure **LLM Sampler** parameters (temp, top-p, thinking budget).
  4.  For SCS: Set # programs/gen, # generations/trial, # trials, # parents to condition on.
  5.  Run search loop: Generate → Execute (Verifier) → Store result → Update budget. For SCS: Update conditioning pool.
  6.  After budget exhausted, **pick best program** by score.
  7.  (For scaffolds) **Final robust evaluation** on a large, representative test set with confidence intervals.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **IID RS vs. SCS:** SCS may be more sample-efficient for iterative improvement problems but loses parallelism (critical for wall-clock limits) and has longer prompts (higher cost per sample).
  - **Prompt Complexity:** More domain knowledge can boost efficiency but risks biasing search or making prompts brittle. Use minimal prompts for fair comparisons.
  - **Evaluation Effort:** For scaffolds, large validation sets reduce stochasticity but are costly. Use a *cascade*: cheap filter (small set) → expensive confirmation (large set). Balance cascade thresholds against total budget.
  - **Model Cost:** Cheaper models (Flash Lite) allow more samples but may lower quality. Use for development; validate final results with high-quality models.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Mathematical Bounds:** All methods converge to the same bound quickly, suggesting a *search space ceiling*. Try reformulating the verifier.
  - **Agentic Scaffolds:** Large (>10%) drop from validation to test accuracy, and the selected scaffold is not a strong majority-vote-style method. Symptom: **evaluation stochasticity** from a small validation set.
  - **General:** No improvement over many generations/budget. Could indicate: (a) search space is exhausted, (b) prompt is inadequate, (c) LLM capability is insufficient for the task.
  - **Cost Overrun:** Budget tracker ignored. For SCS, sequential generations can silently exceed time/API limits.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Replicate Math Bounds Baseline:** Run IID RS on one problem (e.g., "Uncertainty inequality") with the minimal prompt and $5 budget. Verify you can match/exceed the ShinkaEvolve bound reported in Table 1 within the full $20 budget. This validates your setup and the baseline's competitiveness.
  2.  **Demonstrate Scaffold Stochasticity:** With a fixed majority vote@5 scaffold for AIME, evaluate it 100 times on the *same* 100-question subset. Calculate the distribution of accuracies. Observe the standard deviation. This concretely shows why a single validation score is unreliable.
  3.  **Test Search Space Impact:** Take a problem (e.g., Circle Packing). Run IID RS with two prompts: (a) minimal prompt (from Appendix E), (b) minimal prompt + explicit domain knowledge (e.g., the `compute_max_radii` helper function). Show that (b) finds a better bound faster, demonstrating Mechanism 2. Then, take the best bound from (b) and try to improve it by modifying the verifier (e.g., allowing a different packing algorithm), demonstrating Mechanism 1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can code evolution systems automatically reformulate their problem's search space (e.g., by modifying the verifier) during search to discover improved mathematical bounds beyond fixed formulations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state it would be interesting to design systems flexible enough to change problem formulations, and show that reformulating the uncertainty inequality yielded larger gains than pipeline changes.
- Why unresolved: Current systems treat search spaces as static; automating reformulation could overcome expert design bottlenecks but requires meta-level reasoning not yet demonstrated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement a system that iteratively proposes and evaluates new verifiers/objectives on mathematical bounds tasks; compare final bounds against fixed-formulation baselines with equal budgets.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Which specific algorithmic components (e.g., parent selection, diversity maintenance, textual feedback) in complex code evolution pipelines actually contribute to performance beyond simple random or sequential sampling?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Results show baselines match sophisticated methods, implying many features may be redundant, but no component-level ablation has been done.
- Why unresolved: Without isolating contributions, it is unclear which design choices are essential; unnecessary complexity may inflate costs without benefit.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies on ShinkaEvolve and AIDE, removing one feature at a time, measuring sample efficiency across the three benchmark domains.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What evaluation cascade structure (number of stages, sample sizes per stage) optimally balances cost and reliability when selecting agentic scaffolds from small, high-variance validation sets?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose evaluation cascades to reduce stochasticity but do not specify how to configure them.
- Why unresolved: The optimal trade‑off depends on dataset variance and budget; improper design may remain too expensive or still produce overfitted scaffolds.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical grid search over cascade configurations on AIME‑like tasks, measuring selection accuracy versus evaluation cost; derive practical guidelines from the results.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can code evolution methods be standardized and fairly compared across different problem domains and resource constraints (API budget, function evaluations, wall‑clock time)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion urges fair comparisons using identical models, prompts, verifiers, search spaces, and budgets; the paper itself attempts this but notes a lack of community standards.
- Why unresolved: Current literature mixes different setups, conflating algorithmic progress with domain knowledge or budget differences, hindering scientific rigor.
- What evidence would resolve it: Propose a standardized benchmark suite with fixed evaluation protocols; run multiple state‑of‑the‑art methods under these protocols to establish reproducible performance rankings.

## Limitations
- Generalizability limited to three domains and the Gemini 2.5 Pro model; other LLM families and tasks may behave differently.
- Agentic scaffold results are compromised by high evaluation stochasticity due to small validation sets, reducing statistical reliability.
- Replication depends on exact prompts, verifiers, and ShinkaEvolve hyperparameters; missing details may hinder faithful reproduction.
