# A Causal Graph Approach to Oppositional Narrative Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06135
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06135
- **Reference Count**: 20
- **Primary Result**: Achieves macro F1-score of 0.93 and MCC 0.8407 on PAN 2024, with average graph compression of 75.2% and uses only 34% of the parameters of the second-best model.

## Executive Summary
Oppositional narratives, such as conspiracy theories, require structured analysis of how entities interact. Existing black-box text classifiers with fixed ontologies fail to capture these relational dynamics. This paper introduces a framework that converts narrative texts into bipartite hypergraphs: entities are linked to relation hyperedges via span-level BERT embeddings. A transformer enriches the entity representations, and a Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT) classifies the narratives. To enhance interpretability, a causal inference module (HyperSCI) estimates individual treatment effects (ITEs) for each entity, enabling greedy removal of non-essential nodes until the classification flips, thereby distilling a minimal causal subgraph. On the PAN 2024 dataset, the method sets a new performance benchmark (macro F1 0.93, MCC 0.8407) while dramatically reducing the graph size and parameter count.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with domain-adaptive pre‑training: BERT‑base is fine‑tuned on the LOCO corpus (MLM task) and then frozen. For each input narrative, all spans up to width 5 are enumerated. Each span is represented by its `[CLS]` embedding, max‑pooled token embeddings, and a learned width embedding, and then classified into one of four entity types or “none”. Extracted entity embeddings (16‑dimensional) are passed through a one‑layer Transformer (4 heads, FFN dim 32) for contextual enrichment. Next, a MLP maps each enriched embedding to 7 logits; a Gumbel‑Sigmoid layer (temperature annealed 0.5→0) produces binary membership in relation hyperedges, yielding a bipartite incidence matrix. A two‑layer HGT (hidden dim 64, 4 heads) operates on this graph to predict the binary label (conspiracy vs. critical) using BCE loss. After HGT training, the HyperSCI module (adapted from Ma et al. 2022) learns a confounder representation `Z` via an MLP regularized by a Wasserstein distance term, propagates it through a HyperGNN layer to obtain interference representations `P_i`, and predicts counterfactual outcomes with an ITE head. Synthetic LOO labels `Y_i(t)` are generated by masking node `i` and recording the HGT output. HyperSCI is trained to match these labels. Finally, for each test graph, entities are sorted by absolute ITE and greedily removed until the HGT prediction flips; the compression rate is the proportion of nodes removed.

## Key Results
- State‑of‑the‑art classification: macro F1‑score 0.93, MCC 0.8407 on the PAN 2024 English subset.
- High causal compression: on average 75.2% of entities can be discarded without changing the classification decision.
- Parameter efficiency: the full model uses only 34% of the parameters of the second‑best benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method succeeds because it moves beyond bag‑of‑words representations and explicitly encodes the relational structure of a narrative. The bipartite hypergraph naturally captures how entities participate in multiple relations, allowing the HGT to exploit heterogeneous message passing tailored to node and edge types. Enriching entity embeddings with a Transformer injects contextual nuances while keeping the span‑level granularity. Causal attribution via HyperSCI isolates each entity’s contribution to the classification by simulating its removal, thereby identifying a small subset of pivotal entities that drive the narrative label. This combination yields both high accuracy and an interpretable explanation grounded in graph perturbation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Span‑based Entity Extraction**  
  *Why needed*: Entities are the atomic units of the narrative graph; accurate span detection is the first step.  
  *Quick check*: How does the model treat overlapping candidate spans with different widths?

- **Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT)**  
  *Why needed*: Handles bipartite graphs with distinct node types (entities, hyperedges) and multiple relation types.  
  *Quick check*: What are the separate parameterizations for entity→hyperedge and hyperedge→entity attention?

- **Gumbel‑Sigmoid for Discrete Graph Construction**  
  *Why needed*: Enables end‑to‑end learning of a binary adjacency matrix between entities and hyperedges via a straight‑through estimator.  
  *Quick check*: What effect does annealing the temperature τ have on the sparsity of the learned graph?

- **Synthetic Leave‑One‑Out (LOO) Causal Labels**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a self‑supervised objective for ITE estimation when true counterfactuals are unavailable.  
  *Quick check*: How are the potential outcomes `Y_i(0)` and `Y_i(1)` constructed for a given node i?

- **Greedy Causal Distillation**  
  *Why needed*: Finds a minimal subgraph that preserves the original prediction, highlighting the core narrative drivers.  
  *Quick check*: Which stopping condition determines that further node removal would alter the classification?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
BERT (frozen) → Span Extractor → Entity Enricher (1‑layer Transformer) → Graph Constructor (MLP + Gumbel‑Sigmoid) → HGT Classifier (2‑layer) → HyperSCI (confounder MLP + HyperGNN + ITE head). HyperSCI operates on the same bipartite graph produced by the constructor.

**Critical Path**  
Text → BERT embeddings → span extraction → enrichment → adjacency generation → HGT classification (training loop). After HGT is fixed, the graph is fed to HyperSCI to compute ITEs, followed by greedy node removal for compression.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Frozen BERT* avoids spillover but forfeits domain adaptation.  
- *Gumbel‑Sigmoid* allows differentiable discrete choices but requires careful temperature annealing.  
- *Synthetic LOO labels* supply a causal signal but may capture classifier bias rather than true narrative causality.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Very high average entity count per document suggests poor span classification.  
- Nearly all entries in the adjacency matrix are 1, indicating τ did not anneal properly.  
- ITE values clustered near zero for all entities imply HyperSCI failed to learn meaningful effects.  
- Compression rates exceeding 90% on majority‑class samples may reveal reliance on dataset bias.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Span extraction sanity check**: Run the trained span classifier on 50 PAN texts; compute average entity count and type distribution; compare to paper examples (e.g., ~4 entities in Figure 2).  
2. **HyperSCI sensitivity test**: Train with α ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1.0}; measure changes in PEHE and stability of ITE rankings across runs.  
3. **Distillation criterion test**: Re‑run greedy removal using both class‑flip and 0.5‑probability‑threshold triggers; compare resulting compression rates to the reported 75.2%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1: Spillover Mitigation**  
*Basis*: Explicit (Section 3 and Section 7).  
*Why unresolved*: Freezing BERT is a temporary fix; contextual information may still leak into entity embeddings, causing uniform ITE values.  
*Resolution evidence*: Develop architectural constraints or training objectives that strictly isolate node‑specific semantics; validate by reduced correlation between ITEs and graph topology/context length.

**Open Question 2: Alternative Entity Framing**  
*Basis*: Explicit (Section 7).  
*Why unresolved*: The greedy span enumeration (max width 5) may miss complex or overlapping entities; no comparison to advanced span extractors is provided.  
*Resolution evidence*: Replace Algorithm 1 with a learnable span extractor like Deep SpERT; measure entity extraction quality and its impact on F1 and compression.

**Open Question 3: Cross‑Dataset Generalization**  
*Basis*: Inferred (Limitations, Section 8).  
*Why unresolved*: Evaluation is limited to PAN 2024 English; domain adaptation used LOCO (COVID‑19) which shares theme. Performance on other languages or topics is unknown.  
*Resolution evidence*: Systematically test on additional conspiracy/narrative datasets in varied languages and domains (e.g., elections, climate change).

**Open Question 4: Validity of Synthetic LOO Labels**  
*Basis*: Inferred (Limitations, Section 8).  
*Why unresolved*: LOO labels reflect the current classifier’s predictive importance, not ground‑truth ITEs; learned ITEs may be biased toward model artifacts.  
*Resolution evidence*: Obtain a small set of human‑annotated causal relevance or compare against a different causal inference framework; assess correlation.

**Open Question 5: Impact of Class Imbalance on Compression**  
*Basis*: Inferred (Section 4.4 notes 67% non‑conspiratorial class).  
*Why unresolved*: Greedy distillation stops when the prediction flips; majority‑class examples may require few or zero nodes to maintain the majority prediction, inflating average compression.  
*Resolution evidence*: Re‑run compression analysis on a balanced test set and separately for each class; report distributions.

## Limitations
- Span classifier training details (annotation scheme, loss weighting for the “none” class) are omitted, making faithful reproduction difficult.
- HyperSCI’s exact architecture (confounder/interference dimensionalities, regularization weight α) and the greedy distillation trigger (class flip vs. probability threshold) are ambiguous.
- The use of synthetic LOO labels may cause the learned ITE to reflect classifier bias rather than the true causal role of entities.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority (macro F1 0.93, MCC 0.8407)**: Medium (clear metrics but not independently replicated).
- **Causal compression (75.2%)**: Low (relies on synthetic labels and an unspecified distillation criterion).
- **Parameter efficiency (34% of second‑best)**: High (explicit parameter counts provided).
