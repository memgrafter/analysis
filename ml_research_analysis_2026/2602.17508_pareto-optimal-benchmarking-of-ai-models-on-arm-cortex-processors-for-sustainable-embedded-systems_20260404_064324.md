# Pareto Optimal Benchmarking of AI Models on ARM Cortex Processors for Sustainable Embedded Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17508
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17508
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Benchmarking on bare-metal ARM Cortex-M0+, M4, M7 shows FLOPs predict latency (R² ≥ 0.93), and optimal processor selection depends on inference duty cycle (M7 for ≤0.5s, M4 for ≥2.5s), with 8-bit quantization reducing model size by up to 4×.

## Executive Summary
This study presents an automated benchmarking framework to evaluate neural network models on bare-metal ARM Cortex-M microcontrollers (M0+, M4, M7) for energy-efficient embedded deployment. By pruning and quantizing models to 8-bit, the work measures inference latency, energy consumption, and accuracy across a suite of use-case models. A strong linear correlation between FLOPs and inference time (R² ≥ 0.93) enables FLOPs to serve as a reliable early-stage latency predictor. Pareto analysis reveals that the optimal processor for minimal energy per inference cycle is determined by the application's duty cycle: the faster M7 excels for frequent short cycles due to lower active energy, while the M4's ultra-low idle current makes it superior for infrequent long cycles. Quantization drastically reduces model size, though the M0+ is found unsuitable for complex models. The results provide a methodology for navigating the energy-accuracy trade-off in sustainable embedded AI design.

## Method Summary
The authors implement an automated four-stage test bench. First, baseline models (e.g., ResNet, MobileNetV1) are pruned and statically quantized to 8-bit, generating a variant set exported to ONNX. Second, ONNX models are converted into self-contained C libraries and integrated with a C++ benchmarking framework, then cross-compiled for each target Cortex core. Third, binaries are flashed to an ATP carrier board hosting the M0+, M4, and M7; inference execution is marked via GPIO toggle, and current is measured with a Nordic Power Profiler Kit (PPK) to derive active current, inference time, and energy. Fourth, results are plotted in accuracy-energy space per processor, and the Pareto frontier of non-dominated solutions is identified for varying duty cycles. Idle current values from datasheets are combined with measured active energy to compute total energy per inference cycle.

## Key Results
- Near-linear FLOPs-inference time correlation (R² ≥ 0.93) across all three processors, enabling FLOPs as a latency predictor.
- For frequent short inference cycles (≤0.5 s), the M7 minimizes total energy due to fast computation; for infrequent long cycles (≥2.5 s), the M4 excels because of its lower deep-sleep current (0.30 mA vs. M7's 1.60 mA).
- Static 8-bit quantization reduces model size by up to 4×, though some complex models exceed memory on the M0+ and cannot be deployed.
- The optimal processor-model pair is application-specific and must be selected from the Pareto front based on required accuracy and duty cycle.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Floating-point operations (FLOPs) serve as a reliable early-stage predictor of inference latency on bare-metal ARM Cortex processors.
- **Mechanism:** The benchmarking reveals a strong linear relationship (R² ≥ 0.93) between a model's total FLOPs and its measured inference time across the M0+, M4, and M7. Under controlled bare-metal conditions without OS overhead, the dominant factor in execution time is the raw computational count, as the processor's core execution units (ALU/DSP) are the primary bottleneck. The linear scaling allows FLOPs to abstract away specific microarchitectural differences (e.g., M7's faster per-FLOP throughput) into a simple multiplier per processor.
- **Core assumption:** The model's memory access pattern and dataflow do not introduce significant non-computational stalls (e.g., cache misses, bus contention). This is more valid for tightly controlled, small embedded models with predictable memory layouts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Results show a near-linear FLOPs-inference time correlation (R² ≥ 0.93), enabling FLOPs as a reliable latency predictor."
  - [section 4.2] "The analysis of the relationship between FLOPs and inference time... reveals a strong linear correlation (R² ≥ 0.93)."
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers like "Accurate Energy Modelling on the Cortex-M0" and "Benchmarking Deep Learning Convolutions on Energy-constrained CPUs" also investigate compute vs. time/energy, but the provided corpus has very low relevance (avg FMR ~0.46, zero citations), offering no strong contradictory or supporting evidence for this specific linear claim.
- **Break condition:** The correlation would break down for models with highly irregular memory accesses, extensive use of hardware-specific accelerators (not utilized here), or when bus/DRAM latency dominates compute time, which is possible with larger models or different memory hierarchies.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The optimal processor (M4 vs. M7) for energy efficiency is determined by the application's inference duty cycle, due to a fundamental trade-off between active computation energy and idle state power.
- **Mechanism:** The M7's superior computational throughput minimizes active-phase energy for short cycles, but its higher deep-sleep current (1.60 mA) causes it to leak more energy during long idle periods. The M4's slower compute increases active energy, but its ultra-low idle current (0.30 mA) makes it more efficient when idle time dominates the total cycle energy. The Pareto front shifts because the *weight* of idle energy in the total budget changes with cycle duration.
- **Core assumption:** The system spends most of its time in a single, well-defined idle (deep-sleep) state between inferences, and the cost of waking up (latency, transient power) is negligible or constant across processors. The duty cycle is a known, fixed parameter of the target application.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "for frequent short inference cycles (≤0.5s), the M7 minimizes energy via fast computation, while for infrequent long cycles (≥2.5s), the M4 excels with low idle current."
  - [section 4.3] "The M4 processor becomes increasingly efficient at longer cycle times, where its low idle current dominates the energy budget... The M7 processor, benefiting from its high computational speed, achieves the lowest energy consumption during short inference cycles."
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak. Neighbors like "Big-Little Adaptive Neural Networks on Low-Power Near-Subthreshold Processors" discuss voltage/frequency scaling for energy, but do not directly address this specific idle-current-dominant trade-off across fixed architectures.
- **Break condition:** If the application has highly variable or unpredictable inter-inference times, or if the system cannot maintain a single low-power idle state (e.g., frequent peripheral activity, different sleep modes with different wake-up costs), the clean dichotomy between M4 and M7 would fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Static 8-bit quantization reduces model size (memory footprint) by up to 4× with minimal accuracy loss for the evaluated use-cases, enabling deployment on resource-constrained processors.
- **Mechanism:** Quantization maps floating-point weights and activations to 8-bit integers, drastically reducing the ROM required to store the model (from 32-bit floats). This shrinks the binary, easing storage and transfer constraints. The paper implies the models were either robust to this precision loss or the accuracy targets (e.g., ≥80% on CIFAR-10) were still met post-quantization, as the Pareto analysis includes quantized models.
- **Core assumption:** The specific models and datasets used (ResNet on CIFAR-10, MobileNetV1 on COCO) are inherently tolerant to 8-bit quantization, and the quantization scheme (likely post-training or quantization-aware training) was applied correctly without introducing catastrophic accuracy drops.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Quantization reduces model size by up to 4×..."
  - [section 4.2] "Quantization yields a substantial reduction in model size, often to one-quarter of the original... unquantized Visual Wake Words models exceeded the available memory."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows strong relevance to quantization (e.g., "Evaluation of Convolution Primitives...", references [1] on quantization), but no direct evidence contradicts the 4× reduction claim for these specific models.
- **Break condition:** For models or datasets highly sensitive to precision (e.g., some anomaly detection or scientific inference tasks), 8-bit quantization could cause accuracy to fall below the usable threshold, nullifying the memory benefit for deployment.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** **Pareto Optimality / Frontier Analysis**
  - **Why needed here:** The core contribution is navigating trade-offs between conflicting objectives (energy vs. accuracy). A Pareto front visually identifies configurations where improving one metric necessarily worsens the other, guiding the developer to non-dominated solutions. Without this, one might optimize for a single metric (e.g., lowest energy) and miss practically viable, more accurate options.
  - **Quick check question:** If a model is on the Pareto front for a given duty cycle, what does that imply about the possibility of finding another model with both lower energy and higher accuracy?
- **Concept:** **Bare-metal vs. Managed/OS Benchmarking**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's value stems from eliminating OS and runtime (e.g., Linux, Python) overhead to isolate hardware behavior. Understanding this distinction is crucial because results from Raspberry Pi (SBC) benchmarks would not translate directly to these microcontrollers. The "why" of the experiment's setup depends on this foundational knowledge.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might inference latency measured on a Raspberry Pi be systematically higher and less predictable than on the bare-metal setup described here, even with the same neural network model?
- **Concept:** **Duty Cycle in Energy-Aware Computing**
  - **Why needed here:** The primary conclusion hinges on the ratio of active time to idle time. This concept—that total energy is the sum of *P_active × t_active* and *P_idle × t_idle*—is fundamental to understanding why processor choice is application-dependent. It moves beyond "inference energy" alone to "cycle energy."
  - **Quick check question:** For a fixed inference task, if you halve the time between inferences (keep inference time constant), which component of total energy (active or idle) increases more significantly?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Model Generation (pruning + 8-bit quantization -> ONNX set) -> Model Deployment (ONNX -> C library -> C++ framework integration -> target-specific binary) -> System Benchmarking (flash binary, run inferences, toggle GPIO, measure current via PPK, collect latency) -> Pareto Optimal Solution (plot energy vs. accuracy per cycle time, filter non-dominated solutions).
- **Critical path:** Establish the **FLOPs-latency model** (Section 4.2) first. This requires compiling and running a diverse set of models to generate the linear correlation data. Only after this predictor exists can one use FLOPs for rapid estimation without full benchmarking. The Pareto analysis (Section 4.4) then applies this estimator in conjunction with measured idle currents to map the solution space.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  1. **Test depth vs. Time:** Full Pareto analysis requires benchmarking many model variants per processor. Using FLOPs as a predictor reduces this burden but requires an initial investment to build the model.
  2. **Generalization vs. Precision:** The FLOPs-latency model is specific to the bare-metal toolchain, compiler optimizations, and processor family. It may not hold for different compilers or for models utilizing processor-specific DSP extensions differently.
  3. **Accuracy vs. Memory:** Aggressive pruning/quantization saves memory and FLOPs but risks falling below the use-case accuracy target. The Pareto front inherently manages this, but the engineer must define the minimum acceptable accuracy *a priori*.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High variance in repeated inference energy measurements (>5%) indicates an unreliable test bench (Section 4.1 says variance was minimal; high variance suggests power supply noise, GPIO synchronization issues, or thermal throttling).
  - A model failing to compile or run (crash, incorrect output) likely exceeds RAM or ROM limits, as RAM/ROM are hard constraints (Section 4.2).
  - The FLOPs-latency plot shows a poor fit (R² < 0.8) or non-linear trend, suggesting memory-bound kernels or inefficient compiler code generation for certain layer types.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Test-Bench Reliability:** Take one model-processor pair, flash and run the benchmark 5+ times. Compute mean and standard deviation for inference current, time, and calculated energy. Goal: Confirm variance is <1% as reported in Section 4.1. Failure indicates measurement or execution instability.
  2. **Build FLOPs-Latency Baseline:** For a single processor (e.g., M4), deploy and time 10-15 models spanning the FLOPs range from smallest (LeNet-5) to largest (unquantized MobileNetV1). Plot FLOPs (log scale) vs. mean inference time. Calculate R². Goal: Achieve R² ≥ 0.90, confirming the linear model for your specific toolchain. This establishes your predictive baseline.
  3. **Validate Duty Cycle Energy Switch:** For M4 and M7, select one medium-sized model (e.g., quantized ResNet). Run the benchmark with artificially varied "cycle times" (e.g., by inserting controlled `sleep()` or idle loops between inferences). Measure total energy per cycle for cycle times of 0.2s, 1.0s, 2.5s, 5.0s. Goal: Reproduce the crossover where M7 is best at ≤0.5s and M4 at ≥2.5s, as in Figure 7. This confirms the idle current dominance principle.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How generalizable are the energy-accuracy trade-offs and FLOPs-latency correlation to newer or heterogeneous embedded architectures (e.g., RISC-V, ARM with NPUs) beyond the tested Cortex-M series?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] The paper acknowledges limitations in focusing on "a limited set of microcontroller architectures" and future work suggests "extending the framework to additional architectures and accelerator-based systems."
  - **Why unresolved:** Architectural variations (ISA, memory hierarchy, accelerators) could alter power profiles, invalidate the linear FLOPs-latency relationship, and shift Pareto optimal points.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the benchmark on a diverse set of modern MCUs, comparing FLOPs-latency R² values and Pareto fronts across architectures for the same pruned/quantized model suite.
- **Open Question 2:** How robust are the reported processor preferences (M7 for short cycles, M4 for long cycles) under real-world deployment conditions like temperature variations, peripheral activity, and hardware aging?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] Limitations note measurements were in "controlled laboratory conditions" and may not capture "environmental factors, peripheral usage, or long-term deployment effects."
  - **Why unresolved:** Real-world factors affect leakage current, clock scaling, and peripheral power draws, potentially changing idle/active currents and duty cycle thresholds.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal field trials measuring identical setups across temperature ranges, with active peripherals, and over accelerated aging to track energy profile changes and duty cycle crossover points.
- **Open Question 3:** For processors with hardware accelerators (e.g., DSP extensions, NPUs), does FLOPs remain a reliable latency predictor, or do accelerator utilization and memory access patterns dominate?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] The paper notes the FLOPs-latency correlation "may weaken for models with complex memory access patterns or hardware-specific accelerations," but accelerators were not evaluated.
  - **Why unresolved:** Accelerators can reduce effective FLOP count or shift bottlenecks to memory bandwidth; the CPU-derived linear model may not hold.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Deploy models with and without accelerator offloading (where supported) on the same MCU, measure latency vs. FLOPs, and analyze deviations correlated with accelerator usage metrics (e.g., DSP instruction counts, memory stalls).
- **Open Question 4:** What are the precise duty cycle breakpoints where the optimal processor switches from M7 to M4 as a function of model accuracy and size, and how does quantization affect these thresholds?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] The paper identifies M7 for ≤0.5s and M4 for ≥2.5s cycles but does not model the continuous transition or how model characteristics (beyond size) interact with cycle time to influence the Pareto front.
  - **Why unresolved:** The interaction between model accuracy, quantization-induced accuracy loss, and duty cycle is unexplored; in idle-dominated regimes, accuracy may outweigh latency, but the trade-off curve's shape is unknown.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a fine-grained sweep over cycle times (e.g., 0.1s to 10s) for multiple models at various quantization levels, fitting a model to predict total energy and mapping the M4/M7 crossover as a function of model accuracy and cycle time.

## Limitations
- Exact model architectures, pruning schedules, quantization calibration details, GPIO synchronization overhead, and precise voltage/temperature conditions are unreported, blocking full replication.
- Corpus support is weak (average FMR 0.46), offering no external validation for the core claims.
- The FLOPs-latency correlation assumes memory-bound effects are negligible; it may degrade with larger models or different memory hierarchies.

## Confidence
- **FLOPs–latency linearity (R² ≥ 0.93):** High (strong internal data) but assumes memory-bound effects are negligible; may degrade with larger models.
- **Duty-cycle-driven processor choice:** Medium-high (measured idle currents and active times align) but relies on ideal deep-sleep states and fixed cycles.
- **Quantization 4× size reduction:** Medium (standard result) but accuracy retention is task-specific and not fully detailed.

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark stability:** Run a single model-processor pair 5+ times; confirm inference time/energy variance <1% as claimed. Failure indicates power/measurement noise.
2. **FLOPs predictor calibration:** Benchmark 10–15 models across M0+/M4/M7; recompute linear fit. Goal: R² ≥ 0.90. Poor fit suggests memory bottlenecks or compiler inefficiencies.
3. **Duty-cycle crossover test:** For one quantized model, measure total energy per cycle with injected idle periods (0.2s, 1s, 2.5s, 5s). Verify M7 wins at ≤0.5s and M4 at ≥2.5s; deviation points to incorrect idle-current assumptions.
