# Spectral Compact Training: Pre-Training Large Language Models via Permanent Truncated SVD and Stiefel QR Retraction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00733  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00733  
- **Reference Count:** 3  
- **Primary Result:** SCT enables a 70B-parameter training step in 7.2 GB memory (172× reduction vs. dense FP32's 1,245 GB) by permanently storing weights in truncated SVD form.

## Executive Summary
Spectral Compact Training (SCT) is a method to dramatically reduce the memory footprint of large language model training by permanently storing weight matrices in a truncated SVD factorization (W = U diag(s) Vᵀ) instead of dense form. This avoids materializing dense matrices during both training and inference. Gradients flow through the low-rank spectral factors, and orthonormality of U and V is maintained via QR retraction after each optimizer step. Experiments show that SCT can train a 70B-parameter model step in only 7.2 GB of memory—a 172× reduction compared to dense FP32 training (1,245 GB). On the SmolLM2-1.7B model, all tested ranks (32–256) converge to a similar loss floor, indicating that learning rate schedule, not spectral rank, is the primary quality limiter. Rank 128 achieves the best perplexity (65.6) with 11.7× MLP compression, while rank 32 reduces GPU memory by 46% and doubles throughput.

## Method Summary
SCT replaces each dense weight matrix W ∈ ℝ^{m×n} with a rank‑k truncated SVD factorization: W = U diag(s) Vᵀ, where U ∈ ℝ^{m×k}, V ∈ ℝ^{n×k} have orthonormal columns and s ∈ ℝ^k are singular values. The forward pass computes y = ((x @ U) * s) @ Vᵀ, avoiding dense matrix multiplication. Backpropagation through these operations yields gradients for U, s, V without ever forming the dense matrix. After each optimizer step (e.g., AdamW), a QR retraction is applied to U and V to project them back onto the Stiefel manifold (matrices with orthonormal columns): Q, R = QR(U_updated); U ← Q·sign(diag(R)), and similarly for V. In the experiments, only the MLP layers (gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj) were converted; attention, embeddings, and layernorms remained dense. Hyperparameters included a spectral learning rate of 5e‑4 (higher than dense’s 2e‑5) and ranks ranging from 32 to 256.

## Key Results
- **Memory reduction:** A 70B‑parameter training step requires only 7.2 GB (vs. 1,245 GB for dense FP32), a 172× reduction.
- **Compression vs. quality tradeoff:** On SmolLM2‑1.7B, rank 128 achieves the best perplexity (65.6) with 11.7× MLP compression; rank 32 reduces GPU memory by 46% and doubles throughput while still converging to a similar loss floor.
- **Orthonormality:** QR retraction keeps orthogonality error below 2×10⁻⁶ even for 70B‑scale layers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Permanent low‑rank storage eliminates dense materialization.**  
Each dense matrix W is replaced by its truncated SVD factors U, s, V with orthonormal columns. Forward and backward passes operate on the small factors: cost O(bk(m+n)) instead of O(bmn). The full dense matrix is never constructed. This relies on the assumption that the dominant singular subspaces capture the task‑relevant information. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim and Section 3’s cost analysis, as well as the 70B step memory measurement. A break condition would be if singular values decay too slowly, forcing k ≈ min(m,n) and erasing memory benefits.

**Mechanism 2: QR retraction maintains orthonormality.**  
After each optimizer step, U and V are reprojected onto the Stiefel manifold via QR decomposition with sign correction: Q,R = QR(U_updated); U ← Q·sign(diag(R)), similarly for V. This hard constraint is more stable than soft penalties for maintaining orthonormality over many steps. Evidence includes the abstract, Section 3’s algorithm, and the reported orthogonality error <2e‑6. Potential break conditions: infrequent retraction or very high rank may cause orthonormality drift; the O(mk²) cost of QR can become significant.

**Mechanism 3: Gradient flow through spectral factors.**  
Standard autograd differentiates through the sequence (x@U, *s, @Vᵀ), producing gradients ∂L/∂U (m×k), ∂L/∂s (k), ∂L/∂V (n×k). No dense gradient ever exists. This assumes the loss landscape restricted to the spectral manifold contains useful minima reachable by AdamW. Evidence includes the abstract, Section 3’s backward description, and the fact that all tested ranks converge to a similar loss floor, indicating informative gradients.

## Foundational Learning
- **Truncated SVD and low‑rank approximation:** SCT’s parameterization depends on capturing the dominant singular subspaces; the approximation error is bounded by the sum of discarded singular values. Quick check: For a matrix W, what does U_k diag(s_k) V_kᵀ represent, and how is its error related to the discarded singular values?
- **Stiefel manifold and Riemannian optimization:** The orthonormality constraints on U and V place them on the Stiefel manifold; QR retraction is a projection onto this manifold. Quick check: Why is explicit retraction necessary after a gradient step, rather than relying on the optimizer to maintain orthonormality?
- **Memory accounting in deep learning:** To evaluate SCT’s savings, one must know what contributes to training memory: parameters, gradients, and optimizer states (e.g., Adam moments). SCT primarily reduces storage for weight matrices. Quick check: In dense Adam training, how many floats are stored per parameter? How does this change for an m×n layer converted to SCT with rank k?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Model Definition → Conversion to SpectralLinear (replacing MLP layers) → Training Loop (with Retraction hook after each optimizer step).

**Critical path:**  
1. Define model architecture.  
2. Replace specified dense layers (e.g., MLPs) with SpectralLinear, initializing via truncated SVD or random orthonormal factors.  
3. Train: forward pass, loss, backward pass, optimizer step.  
4. After each optimizer step, apply QR retraction to all SpectralLinear layers’ U and V.  
5. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Rank k vs. quality/memory: Lower k gives more compression but risks underfitting; paper found k=128 optimal for SmolLM2‑1.7B.  
- Learning rate: SCT requires higher LR (e.g., 5e‑4) than dense (2e‑5); mismatch can cause instability.  
- Retraction cost: QR is O(mk²); for large m or high k, it can dominate step time (40–50% in 70B test).  
- Layer scope: Converting only MLPs gives good compression; extending to attention may increase savings but risk attention fidelity.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Orthonormality drift: UᵀU and VᵀV deviate from identity (>1e‑5 off‑diagonal) indicates retraction failure.  
2. Sudden loss spikes: Often due to learning rate too high for chosen rank.  
3. No compression benefit: If k is close to min(m,n) for small layers, overhead may exceed dense storage.  
4. Gradient explosion/vanishing in factors: Monitor norms of ∇U, ∇s, ∇V; pathology suggests ill‑conditioning.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **70B Step Validation:** Run a single full training step on a 70B‑architecture model (MLPs as SCT) on a low‑memory device; confirm peak memory ~7.2 GB vs. 1,245 GB dense.  
2. **Rank Sweep & Convergence:** Train SmolLM2‑1.7B with SCT on MLPs at ranks 32, 64, 128, 256 for 2000 steps; plot loss curves; expect similar floors, with k=128 often best.  
3. **Gradient Integrity Check (Fine‑Tuning):** Convert a pretrained dense model (SmolLM2‑135M) to SCT at high energy retention (95%); fine‑tune on a small dataset; compare loss/PPL to dense fine‑tuning baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can per‑component learning rate scheduling close the convergence gap between SCT and dense training?**  
   The authors used uniform LR across components; separate LRs for MLP (SCT) and attention (dense) might improve convergence.

2. **How does applying SCT to attention projections impact model quality and training stability?**  
   Attention layers were left dense; low‑rank approximations may distort attention patterns. Need experiments converting q, k, v, o projections.

3. **Does Cayley retraction reduce computational overhead while maintaining orthonormality as effectively as QR?**  
   QR took 40–50% of step time in 70B benchmark; Cayley could be cheaper but may accumulate orthogonality errors.

4. **Can SCT scale to full pre‑training of large language models (e.g., 70B) on large datasets without quality degradation?**  
   Previous experiments were short fine‑tuning; full pre‑training dynamics may expose instability or capacity limits.

## Limitations
- Uncertainty in the exact “95% energy retention” threshold implementation (norm type and SVD driver).  
- Implementation details of QR retraction (mode, sign correction) that could affect orthogonality and stability.  
- Limited scope: only MLP layers were converted; performance when extending to attention projections is unknown.
