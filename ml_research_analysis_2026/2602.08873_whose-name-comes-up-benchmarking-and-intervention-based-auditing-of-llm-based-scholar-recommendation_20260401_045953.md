# Whose Name Comes Up? Benchmarking and Intervention-Based Auditing of LLM-Based Scholar Recommendation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08873
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08873
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMScholarBench reveals configuration-dependent trade-offs between technical quality and social representation in LLM scholar recommendations, with no single model or intervention dominating all metrics.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LLMScholarBench, a novel benchmark designed to audit LLM-based scholar recommendation systems by jointly evaluating model infrastructure and end-user interventions. Focusing on physics expert tasks, the benchmark assesses 22 LLMs using nine metrics—five for technical quality (validity, refusals, duplicates, consistency, factuality) and four for social representation (connectedness, similarity, diversity, parity). Key findings show that common interventions like temperature adjustment, constrained prompting, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) do not universally enhance performance; instead, they redistribute errors across metrics. For instance, higher temperature harms validity and factuality, RAG boosts factuality but reduces diversity and parity, and representation-constrained prompting increases diversity at the cost of factuality. The study concludes that social representativeness is particularly resistant to steering via scaling or simple prompting, underscoring inherent trade-offs and the need for more sophisticated interventions.

## Method Summary
LLMScholarBench operates by simulating scholar recommendation queries within physics, using the APS corpus as ground truth for author lists and collaboration networks. It evaluates 22 LLMs (both open- and closed-access) on their ability to generate valid JSON-formatted lists of recommended scholars. The audit systematically tests three end-user interventions: temperature sampling, representation-constrained prompting (e.g., prompts emphasizing diversity), and RAG (with retrieval from APS abstracts). Performance is measured across nine metrics: validity (JSON compliance), refusals (inappropriate declines), duplicates (list uniqueness), consistency (output stability across runs), factuality (match with ground-truth authors), connectedness (average network proximity), similarity (network closeness to query author), diversity (demographic/collaborative entropy), and parity (demographic balance). This design enables controlled isolation of intervention effects while maintaining task constancy.

## Key Results
- End-user interventions redistribute rather than eliminate errors, with no intervention simultaneously improving all metrics.
- Increasing temperature degrades validity, consistency, and factuality.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) significantly improves factuality but reduces diversity and parity.
- Representation-constrained prompting increases diversity while harming factuality.
- Deepseek models achieve strong factuality and parity; gemma models excel in validity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s joint evaluation of model infrastructure (e.g., size, reasoning capabilities) and user interventions exposes how configuration choices propagate through technical and social dimensions. By holding tasks constant and systematically varying interventions, the audit reveals trade-offs that would be masked in aggregate evaluations. This mechanistic approach demonstrates that social representativeness metrics (diversity, parity) are particularly resistant to steering via prompting or temperature, likely due to entrenched biases in training data and model objectives. Thus, the benchmark successfully isolates the causal impact of interventions on distinct quality facets, highlighting that optimization for one metric often comes at the expense of another.

## Foundational Learning
1. **LLM Auditing**: Systematic, multi-metric evaluation of model outputs to detect biases, failures, and trade-offs. *Why needed*: Moves beyond aggregate accuracy to nuanced quality dimensions. *Quick check*: Audit refusal rates across demographic groups in recommendation outputs.
2. **Social Representativeness Metrics**: Quantitative fairness measures like diversity (entropy of recommended scholars), parity (demographic balance), and similarity (network closeness). *Why needed*: To assess inclusivity and equity in scholar recommendations. *Quick check*: Compute demographic parity index from output author lists.
3. **Validity Measurement**: Checking output format compliance (e.g., machine-parsable JSON). *Why needed*: Ensures outputs are functionally usable in automated pipelines. *Quick check*: Validate output with a JSON schema parser.
4. **Factuality Verification**: Comparing generated scholar names against ground-truth citations or known experts. *Why needed*: To detect hallucinations and ensure accurate recommendations. *Quick check*: Perform exact or fuzzy matching against a gold-standard author list.
5. **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**: Augmenting LLM prompts with external knowledge (e.g., paper abstracts) to ground outputs. *Why needed*: To improve factual accuracy by providing verified context. *Quick check*: Compare factuality scores with and without retrieval context.
6. **Temperature Sampling**: Adjusting the randomness parameter in LLM decoding. *Why needed*: To explore the trade-off between output diversity and determinism. *Quick check*: Generate multiple outputs at varying temperatures and compute consistency scores.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: User Query → Prompt Template (with/without intervention) → LLM → Output Parser → Metric Calculators (Validity, Refusals, Duplicates, Consistency, Factuality, Connectedness, Similarity, Diversity, Parity) → Score Report
- **Critical path**: Query generation → Prompt construction (intervention applied here) → LLM inference → Output parsing → Metric computation. Interventions are injected at the prompt construction stage.
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - Validity vs. factuality: Constrained prompting may increase diversity but reduce factuality.
  - Diversity vs. parity: Measures can conflict depending on demographic distributions.
  - RAG improves factuality but often harms diversity and parity due to retrieval bias.
- **Failure signatures**: 
  - Malformed JSON (invalidity)
  - High refusal rates (avoidance of complex queries)
  - Many duplicates (lack of list diversity)
  - Low factuality scores (hallucinations)
  - Network similarity scores near 1 (homophily)
  - Parity metrics far from 1 (demographic imbalance)
- **First experiments**:
  1. Fix model and prompt; vary temperature (0.1 to 1.0) and measure changes in validity, consistency, and factuality.
  2. Compare RAG vs. no-RAG settings on factuality, diversity, and parity metrics.
  3. Apply representation-constrained prompting (e.g., “include scholars from underrepresented groups”) and evaluate shifts in diversity and factuality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the validity metric be extended to measure context-dependent utility in scholar recommendation systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state: "A direct extension is to add context-dependent utility measures tied to use, such as extraction time, human acceptance, or downstream decision quality."
- Why unresolved: The current validity metric only checks for machine-parsable JSON lists, but real-world systems might accept malformed yet interpretable outputs; usability depends on application-specific factors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop utility metrics aligned with specific use cases (e.g., time to extract names from responses, expert evaluations of list quality), and incorporate them into LLMScholarBench to validate against traditional validity scores.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What experimental designs can causally isolate the effects of model infrastructure (e.g., size, reasoning capability) from confounding variables like training data and alignment policies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note: "Stronger causal claims require tighter controls, such as within-family comparisons or ablations where only one factor changes."
- Why unresolved: The audit compares heterogeneous models across multiple varying dimensions (size, access, reasoning), making it unclear whether observed effects are due to infrastructure choices or correlated factors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct within-family studies (e.g., same model family with only size or reasoning toggled via controlled interventions) while holding training data and decoding constants, then re-audit with LLMScholarBench.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can model adaptation using scholarly knowledge graphs effectively steer social representativeness (diversity, parity) without degrading technical quality?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion states: "Similarity, diversity, and parity move little across these settings, suggesting social representativeness is not easily steered by prompting or scaling alone." The conclusion adds: "motivating interventions beyond prompting, including model adaptation using explicit scholarly knowledge graphs."
- Why unresolved: End-user interventions (temperature, constrained prompting, RAG) reshape trade-offs but fail to jointly improve technical quality and social parity; representation metrics appear resistant to simple steering.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement and evaluate knowledge-grounded fine-tuning or retrieval mechanisms that inject structured demographic and network data into LLMs, then measure changes in diversity/parity relative to factuality and validity.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How generalizable are LLMScholarBench's findings to academic domains other than physics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors write: "Cross-domain applicability... extending to other domains requires swapping ground-truth modules." They acknowledge physics-specific characteristics may limit generalization.
- Why unresolved: Physics has unique features (e.g., APS corpus, documented gender disparities); other fields may have different data availability, collaboration patterns, and representation gaps that affect model behavior.
- What evidence would resolve it: Port LLMScholarBench to multiple disciplines (e.g., biology via PubMed, social sciences via OpenAlex), using domain-specific ground truth, and compare performance trends to identify universal vs. field-specific patterns in LLM scholar recommendation.

## Limitations
- Domain limited to physics expert tasks; applicability to other academic fields or non-expert queries is unknown.
- Evaluation uses simulated recommendation scenarios, omitting real-world dynamics like iterative querying or contextual awareness.
- Only three end-user interventions are tested; other strategies (e.g., multi-turn dialogue, persona specification) remain unexplored.
- Social representation metrics may not fully align with all scholarly equity frameworks and could be skewed by cultural/geographic biases in training data.
