# Long Chain-of-Thought Compression via Fine-Grained Group Policy Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10048
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10048
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: FGO reduces generated token length by up to 24% on MATH500 while preserving accuracy, achieves 100% valid training samples, and maintains higher policy entropy.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the inefficiency of unnecessarily long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) outputs from large language models, which increase computational costs without proportional gains in accuracy. It introduces Fine-grained Group policy Optimization (FGO), a reinforcement learning method that extends Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). FGO divides model responses into correct and incorrect subgroups and assigns sample-specific rewards based on token length and output entropy. Shorter, more confident correct answers and shorter, more diverse incorrect answers are prioritized, leading to compressed reasoning, better data usage, and mitigation of entropy collapse. Empirically, on math benchmarks MATH500 and AIME24, FGO significantly cuts token counts (e.g., from 578 to 441 for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B on MATH500) while maintaining or improving accuracy. Training stability improves with 100% valid samples, and the method sustains higher entropy throughout, addressing GRPO's key limitations. The Accuracy Contribution per Token (ACT) metric improves, demonstrating more efficient reasoning.

## Method Summary
FGO operates within the reinforcement learning framework for language model alignment. For a batch of model responses, it first separates them into correct and incorrect subgroups based on task accuracy. Within each subgroup, each response receives a weight computed as the inverse of its token length multiplied by an entropy factor: correct responses are scaled by the inverse of their entropy (favoring lower entropy, i.e., more confident answers), while incorrect responses are scaled by their entropy (favoring higher entropy, i.e., more diverse wrong predictions). These weights modulate the group-relative advantages, which are derived from the baseline reward (often correctness itself) relative to the group average. The resulting fine-grained advantages drive the policy gradient update. This design simultaneously encourages concise reasoning (through length penalty) and avoids policy collapse (through entropy modulation), while ensuring numerical stability that eliminates invalid training samples observed in GRPO. The method is model-agnostic and directly applicable to any LLM with CoT capability.

## Key Results
- Token compression: On MATH500 with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, FGO reduces average generation length from 578 to 441 tokens (~24% savings) with comparable or better accuracy.
- Training validity: FGO achieves 100% valid training samples, whereas GRPO suffers from many invalid (NaN) updates.
- Entropy and efficiency: FGO maintains higher policy entropy throughout training and improves Accuracy Contribution per Token (ACT), demonstrating more efficient use of compute.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
FGO’s effectiveness stems from its dual reward modulation tailored to response correctness. For correct answers, rewarding shorter length incentivizes the model to find more concise reasoning paths, compressing CoT without sacrificing correctness. The additional preference for lower entropy ensures that these concise answers are also confident, avoiding meandering explanations. For incorrect answers, the algorithm rewards higher entropy, encouraging diverse exploration of wrong reasoning chains. This diversity prevents the policy from collapsing into over-confident but incorrect modes, a common issue in RL fine-tuning known as entropy collapse. By applying these modulations within each correctness group, FGO leverages group-relative advantages while adding fine-grained control. The inverse length weighting directly reduces computational overhead, and the entropy balancing maintains a healthy exploration-exploitation tradeoff, leading to stable training and improved data efficiency.

## Architecture Onboarding
FGO can be viewed as a pipeline of components:
Prompt -> Policy (LLM) -> Responses -> Evaluation (correctness, token length, entropy) -> Grouping (correct/incorrect) -> Weight Computation (inverse length * entropy factor) -> Advantage Modulation (group-relative advantages * weights) -> Policy Gradient Update -> Updated Policy

Critical path: The essential flow is generating responses, computing subgroup weights, modulating advantages, and updating the policy. The grouping and weighting steps are the novel contributions that differentiate FGO from GRPO.

Design tradeoffs:
- Length weighting encourages brevity but may risk omission of necessary reasoning steps; the entropy modulation for correct responses mitigates by favoring confident, compact answers.
- Using entropy as a factor requires stable estimation (e.g., token-level probabilities); approximate entropy may introduce noise.
- Group-relative advantages reduce variance but introduce bias; fine-grained weighting could amplify variance if weights are extreme, necessitating careful normalization.

Failure signatures:
- Token lengths do not decrease over training, indicating ineffective length penalty.
- Policy entropy drops sharply, especially for incorrect responses, signaling collapse.
- Training produces NaN or extremely large gradients, often due to division by zero in weight calculation.
- Accuracy degrades, particularly on hard problems, suggesting over-compression.

## Limitations
- Scope limited to mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, AIME24); generalizability to other domains (e.g., commonsense, code) is untested.
- Evaluation restricted to a single model family (Qwen2.5-Math); applicability to larger or differently architectures (e.g., decoder-only models like Llama) remains uncertain.
- The paper provides no independent reproduction scripts or detailed hyperparameter settings, which may hinder reproducibility.
