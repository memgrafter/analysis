# All Leaks Count, Some Count More: Interpretable Temporal Contamination Detection in LLM Backtesting

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17234
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17234
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Baseline agents exhibit substantial temporal contamination (Shapley-DCLR up to 0.240); TimeSPEC reduces leakage by 75–99% (to 0.001–0.060) but causes performance drops in leakage-sensitive tasks like stock ranking.

## Executive Summary
Temporal contamination—where LLMs use post‑cutoff knowledge in backtesting—invalidates retrospective predictions. This paper introduces an interpretable framework to detect and prevent such leakage. The method decomposes model rationales into atomic claims, categorizes them by temporal nature, and applies Shapley values to weight each claim’s contribution to the decision, yielding the Shapley‑weighted Decision‑Critical Leakage Rate (Shapley‑DCLR). To prevent contamination, TimeSPEC interleaves generation with claim verification and regeneration, enforcing a closed‑world aggregation. Experiments across legal case prediction, NBA salary estimation, and stock ranking show baseline agents leak significantly; TimeSPEC reduces leakage by up to 99% while preserving performance on tasks where pre‑cutoff reasoning suffices, but degrades accuracy on tasks requiring up‑to‑date information (e.g., stock ranking).

## Method Summary
The evaluation pipeline first extracts atomic claims from a model’s rationale using a seven‑category taxonomy (A1–A5, B1–B2). Claims are automatically labeled as always leaked (A4, A5), never leaked (B1, B2), or requiring external temporal verification (A1–A3). For those requiring verification, a search‑based process determines the claim’s determination date τ(claim) relative to a cutoff. Shapley values are then approximated via Monte Carlo sampling to measure each claim’s marginal contribution to the prediction. Shapley‑DCLR aggregates these weighted leakage indicators. TimeSPEC implements a five‑phase architecture: Generator (produces draft with temporally filtered search), Supervisor (extracts and verifies claims, triggers regeneration if violations exist), Regenerator (re‑predicts using only validated claims and new search), Resupervisor (re‑verifies), and Aggregator (synthesizes final prediction from validated claims only, ensuring no new information enters).

## Key Results
- Baseline agents leak substantially: Shapley‑DCLR ranges up to 0.240 on salary estimation and 0.170 on stock ranking.
- TimeSPEC reduces leakage by 75–99% across tasks, achieving Shapley‑DCLR as low as 0.001–0.060.
- Performance trade‑off: TimeSPEC matches baselines on legal case prediction but degrades stock ranking Spearman ρ from 0.543 to 0.167, reflecting honest inference from limited pre‑cutoff evidence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Taxonomy‑based detection** enables efficient and accurate leakage identification without exhaustive search. Claims are classified into seven mutually exclusive types; A4 (outcome) and A5 (consequential) are always leaked by definition, B1 (background) and B2 (definitional) are never leaked, and only A1–A3 require external temporal verification. This reduces unnecessary searches while maintaining detection accuracy, assuming any factual assertion fits one of these types and its temporal property holds universally.

**Shapley‑weighted leakage** captures the influence of temporal contamination on decisions. Shapley values φ_i are computed via Monte Carlo approximation over random permutations of claims. The Shapley‑DCLR sums |φ_i|·ℓ(claim_i) divided by total |φ_i|, giving higher weight to claims that critically drive the prediction. This assumes the Shapley value accurately reflects decision‑critical influence when the characteristic function (e.g., predicted probability) is well‑defined and correlates with the model’s reasoning process.

**TimeSPEC’s interleaved generation and verification** proactively prevents contamination by forcing the model to reason only from validated pre‑cutoff evidence. The Generator drafts a rationale; the Supervisor extracts and categorizes claims, flagging violations. If violations exist, the Regenerator produces a new prediction using only the validated claim set plus new search results; the Resupervisor re‑checks. Finally, the Aggregator synthesizes the prediction from all validated claims under a closed‑world constraint, ensuring no new information is introduced. This assumes the LLM can successfully override its parametric memory of post‑cutoff facts when provided with clean evidence and explicit instructions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Generator -> Supervisor -> [Regenerator -> Resupervisor] -> Aggregator. If Supervisor finds no violations, its valid claims proceed directly to Aggregator.

**Critical path**: Draft generation → claim extraction/verification → conditional regeneration → re‑verification → final aggregation.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Cost vs. leakage reduction: More LLM calls and searches lower leakage but increase computational expense.  
- Strictness vs. performance: Overly strict temporal filters may discard useful pre‑cutoff evidence, hurting legitimate accuracy.  
- Generality vs. taxonomy coverage: The seven‑category taxonomy covers most factual assertions but may require extension for novel domains; unclassifiable claims default to search, raising cost.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High Shapley‑DCLR after TimeSPEC indicates missed violations or regeneration reintroducing leakage.  
- Large performance drop on tasks allowing pre‑cutoff reasoning suggests over‑filtering or poor evidence retrieval.  
- Persistent top‑K leakage (high‑influence claims remain contaminated) points to Supervisor’s search or date extraction issues.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. *Sanity check on legal case prediction*: Run TimeSPEC on a Supreme Court case with clearly pre‑cutoff evidence; expect near‑zero Shapley‑DCLR and baseline‑comparable performance.  
2. *Leakage injection test*: Manually insert a known post‑cutoff claim (e.g., "The Court ruled 6‑3") into a draft rationale; verify Supervisor flags it as A4 and blocks it.  
3. *Ablation – disable regeneration*: Compare Shapley‑DCLR and performance when Supervisor’s valid claims go straight to Aggregator vs. full regeneration loop; regeneration should further reduce leakage by exploring alternative evidence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can RL‑based post‑training remove temporal leakage from model parameters rather than merely blocking it at inference?**  
  *Basis*: TimeSPEC operates entirely at inference time; leaked information persists in parametric memory and is blocked rather than removed. Training‑time interventions remain unexplored.  
  *Why unresolved*: The paper solely investigates inference‑time mitigation.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Implement RL‑based post‑training with temporal constraints, evaluate on benchmarks, and measure Shapley‑DCLR reduction without capability degradation.

- **How can we improve LLM reasoning from limited pre‑cutoff evidence to maintain performance in leakage‑sensitive tasks?**  
  *Basis*: TimeSPEC causes substantial performance drops in stock ranking; improving reasoning from limited valid evidence is an open challenge.  
  *Why unresolved*: No method is proposed to close the performance gap when temporal leakage is blocked.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Design new prompting, fine‑tuning, or architectural methods that better utilize pre‑cutoff information; test on leakage‑sensitive tasks to achieve both low Shapley‑DCLR and high performance.

- **How can we reduce the computational overhead of the claim‑level temporal evaluation pipeline to enable large‑scale backtesting?**  
  *Basis*: The multi‑phase pipeline incurs substantial cost (numerous LLM calls per instance); more efficient attribution and verification methods are needed.  
  *Why unresolved*: Current cost is prohibitive for widespread adoption.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Develop and implement efficient approximations (e.g., fewer Shapley samples, alternative attribution) and demonstrate comparable accuracy with reduced LLM call count.

## Limitations
- **Taxonomy completeness**: The seven‑category scheme may misclassify novel or ambiguous temporal assertions, leading to missed leakage.
- **Shapley‑DCLR validity**: Assumes Shapley values accurately reflect decision‑critical influence; depends on the LLM reasoning on claim subsets without extraneous knowledge, a fragile condition.
- **Generalization and implementation fidelity**: Results hinge on Claude Sonnet 4 and specific prompts; minor changes may alter outcomes, and domain coverage is limited to three tasks and 350 instances.
