# One Panel Does Not Fit All: Case-Adaptive Multi-Agent Deliberation for Clinical Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.00085
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00085
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: CAMP improves Macro F1 by 1.66–5.67 points over baselines while reducing token usage (~15.5K vs 17–20K), with transparent audit trails.

## Executive Summary
Clinical prediction using large language models suffers from case-level heterogeneity: simple cases yield consistent outputs, but complex ones diverge under minor prompt changes. Existing multi-agent methods rely on fixed specialist roles and discard disagreement signals. This paper introduces CAMP, a case‑adaptive multi‑agent system where an attending‑physician agent dynamically assembles a specialist panel tailored to each case’s diagnostic uncertainty, uses three‑valued voting (KEEP/REFUSE/NEUTRAL) to enable principled abstention, and resolves diagnoses via a hybrid router that applies strong consensus, falls back to the attending’s initial judgment, or triggers evidence‑based arbitration on conflicts. Evaluated on MIMIC‑IV across multiple LLMs, CAMP achieves state‑of‑the‑art diagnosis accuracy and efficient token use, while producing interpretable voting and arbitration trails for audits.

## Method Summary
CAMP is a zero‑shot pipeline without training. For a given masked clinical note and candidate diagnoses, an attending‑physician agent first produces initial judgments and extracts key diagnostic dimensions (e.g., involved organ systems, ambiguous findings). Based on this analysis, it dynamically selects exactly three specialist roles (e.g., cardiologist, neurologist) and assigns each a case‑specific focus directive. Each specialist then independently evaluates all candidates, voting KEEP, REFUSE, or NEUTRAL, and returns evidence quotes, confidence scores, and natural‑language rationales. A hybrid router aggregates votes per diagnosis: strong consensus (all non‑abstaining agree, with or without NEUTRALs) is accepted directly; weak consensus (one vote type but NEUTRALs dominate) falls back to the attending’s initial judgment; conflicts (both KEEP and REFUSE) trigger arbitration where the attending weighs argument quality to render a final ACCEPT/REJECT decision. For brief hospital course generation, accepted diagnoses are synthesized with the original note using the attending. Token usage averages ~15,500 per note, lower than most multi‑agent baselines.

## Key Results
- **Diagnosis accuracy**: For Llama-3.1-70B, Macro F1 reaches 87.50% (best baseline 85.84%) and Perfect Rate 67.10% (66.55%). For GPT-OSS-20B, Macro F1 improves by 5.67 points to 82.20%.
- **Efficiency**: Token consumption averages ~15,500 per note, lower than most multi-agent baselines (17–20K).
- **Transparency**: Voting and arbitration trails provide auditable reasoning for each diagnosis decision.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Case‑adaptive specialist panel assembly**  
*Claim*: Dynamically tailoring the panel to each case’s diagnostic uncertainty aligns agent expertise with relevant clinical perspectives.  
*Mechanism*: The attending extracts key diagnostic dimensions (e.g., organ systems, ambiguous findings) from the note and selects three specialists whose role‑conditioned prompts include case‑specific focus directives (e.g., “pay particular attention to the nonvisualization of the right vertebral artery”).  
*Core assumption*: The attending correctly identifies relevant specialties from unstructured text.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract and Section 4.2 describe dynamic assembly; panel composition varies across cases.  
*Break condition*: Misidentification of relevant specialties omits critical perspectives, harming accuracy.

**Mechanism 2: Three‑valued voting with principled abstention**  
*Claim*: Allowing NEUTRAL votes yields cleaner judgment signals and prevents forced errors when a diagnosis lies outside a specialist’s expertise.  
*Mechanism*: Specialists vote KEEP, REFUSE, or NEUTRAL. NEUTRAL indicates insufficient evidence or domain mismatch. A high NEUTRAL proportion signals collective expertise gaps, triggering fallback to the attending’s initial judgment when non‑abstaining votes are inconclusive.  
*Core assumption*: Specialists accurately recognize their expertise boundaries.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract and Section 4.3 define the scheme; NEUTRAL is an informative signal for router decisions.  
*Break condition*: Overuse of NEUTRAL causes excessive fallback, reducing the benefit of multi‑agent deliberation.

**Mechanism 3: Evidence‑based arbitration**  
*Claim*: Weighing argument quality over vote counts improves accuracy on conflicting cases where majority opinion is unreliable.  
*Mechanism*: When both KEEP and REFUSE votes appear for a diagnosis, the attending examines each specialist’s quoted evidence, confidence score, and rationale, then decides based on the strength of arguments, potentially overriding a majority if the minority presents stronger evidence.  
*Core assumption*: The attending reliably evaluates the quality and relevance of competing rationales.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract, Section 4.4, and Figure 3 show arbitration recovering a correct diagnosis rejected by a 2‑to‑1 majority.  
*Break condition*: Biased or inconsistent arbitration degrades performance relative to majority voting.

## Foundational Learning
- **Case‑level heterogeneity in LLM predictions**: Simple and complex cases exhibit different output stability under prompt changes, motivating adaptive strategies. *Why needed*: Recognising heterogeneity underpins CAMP’s design, which targets varying reasoning demands. *Quick check*: “Why might increasing the number of specialists help more on complex cases than on simple ones?”
- **Hybrid resolution router**: Three‑path logic (strong consensus, fallback, arbitration) balances efficiency and accuracy. *Why needed*: The router determines computational trade‑offs; understanding its paths predicts CAMP’s behaviour. *Quick check*: “For a given diagnosis, what vote pattern triggers arbitration instead of fallback?”
- **Principled abstention in multi‑agent voting**: NEUTRAL votes signal expertise gaps, guiding router decisions. *Why needed*: Grasping abstention prevents misinterpreting disagreement as noise. *Quick check*: “When should a specialist vote NEUTRAL rather than KEEP or REFUSE?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Preprocessor -> Attending‑physician agent (initial assessment & panel assembly) -> Specialist agents (k, parallel evaluation) -> Hybrid router (per‑diagnosis resolution) -> [if BHC task: BHC generator] -> Output.

**Critical path**:  
1. Preprocess note and candidate diagnoses (mask discharge sections, apply phrase masking, shuffle candidates).  
2. Attending generates initial KEEP/REFUSE judgments and selects k specialist roles with case‑specific focus directives.  
3. Each specialist evaluates all candidates, returning structured votes (KEEP/REFUSE/NEUTRAL), evidence quotes, confidence, and rationale.  
4. For each candidate, router counts KEEP, REFUSE, NEUTRAL and applies rules: strong consensus → accept; weak consensus → fallback to attending’s initial judgment; conflict (both KEEP and REFUSE) → arbitration.  
5. For arbitrated cases, attending renders final ACCEPT/REJECT based on argument quality.  
6. Accepted diagnoses are returned; for BHC generation, the attending synthesizes a brief hospital course from the original note and accepted diagnoses.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Panel size (k)*: Larger k increases coverage but yields diminishing returns (Table 2) and higher token cost; default k=3 balances accuracy and efficiency.  
- *Arbitration cost*: Arbitration requires an extra attending call; gated resolution limits this to conflicted diagnoses, keeping average token usage moderate (~15.5K vs up to 20K for other multi‑agent methods).  
- *Dynamic vs. fixed roles*: Adaptive assembly leverages case‑specific expertise but depends on attending reliability; fixed roles are simpler but may mis‑align with case needs.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High NEUTRAL proportion across specialists → panel lacks relevant expertise (excessive fallback).  
- Many conflicts → either poor panel composition or genuinely ambiguous cases; monitor arbitration outcomes.  
- Attending overrides majority frequently → could indicate specialist unreliability or attending bias.  
- Token consumption exceeds expected budget → unbounded panel growth or too many arbitrations.  
- Low alignment between selected specialist roles and actual hospital services (Figure 2) → potential mis‑assembly.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Baseline replication**: Run CAMP and all seven baselines on the MIMIC‑IV diagnostic prediction and BHC generation tasks (using at least two LLM backbones) to reproduce Table 1 results.  
2. **Panel alignment check**: Compare CAMP‑selected specialist roles against patients’ actual admission services (as in Figure 2) to validate case‑adaptive assembly.  
3. **Panel size sensitivity**: Vary k from 1 to 7 on a fixed 200‑case subset; observe performance plateau to justify the default choice of k = 3 (Table 2, Figure 5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: Does CAMP's case‑adaptive panel assembly generalize to diverse healthcare systems and languages beyond MIMIC‑IV's single‑center U.S. English documentation?  
*Basis*: Limitations state all experiments use MIMIC‑IV, a single‑center U.S. dataset, and note that documentation style and coding conventions may not generalize to other institutions, systems, or languages.  
*Why unresolved*: The attending‑physician agent's selection of specialists relies on patterns in English clinical notes; variations in terminology, structure, or cultural context could impair its ability to identify relevant expertise. Without multi‑center or multilingual validation, claims of broad applicability remain unsupported.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Evaluating CAMP on multiple international EHR datasets (e.g., eICU, MIMIC‑III, or non‑English corpora) with consistent preprocessing, and comparing specialist selection plausibility and diagnostic performance against the MIMIC‑IV results.

**Open Question 2**: Do LLM‑generated BHC rankings (e.g., by GPT‑5‑mini) correlate with clinician judgments on dimensions like safety, actionable prioritization, and readability?  
*Basis*: The paper acknowledges that BHC evaluation relies on an LLM judge rather than clinician assessment, identifying this as a key future validation direction.  
*Why unresolved*: LLM judges may not replicate clinical expertise in evaluating summaries—for example, they might overlook subtle errors in medication dosing or fail to recognize poor prioritization of critical issues. This risks optimizing for LLM preferences rather than clinical utility.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: A blinded study where board‑certified clinicians rate the same BHC outputs across validated clinical quality dimensions, then correlating these scores with the LLM‑based ranks to measure agreement and identify systematic biases.

**Open Question 3**: Is the three‑valued voting (KEEP/REFUSE/NEUTRAL) mechanism truly beneficial for diagnostic accuracy, or could a binary system with confidence scores perform equally well?  
*Basis*: CAMP introduces NEUTRAL to allow principled abstention, arguing it signals expertise gaps more cleanly than forced binary choices. However, the paper does not compare three‑valued voting against a binary alternative (KEEP/REFUSE) with optional confidence annotations.  
*Why unresolved*: The design assumes that explicit abstention improves the hybrid router's decisions (e.g., triggering fallback), but it's possible that binary votes with confidence thresholds could achieve similar effects with less complexity. The net impact on Macro F1 and Perfect Rate remains unevaluated.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: An ablation experiment where specialists use only binary votes but report confidence scores, comparing the router's resolution path choices and final accuracy against the three‑valued setup, particularly on cases where NEUTRAL votes are frequent.

**Open Question 4**: How robust is CAMP's hybrid router to variations in consensus thresholds (e.g., what fraction of non‑abstaining votes constitutes "strong consensus")?  
*Basis*: The router uses fixed thresholds (e.g., all non‑abstaining must agree for strong consensus). The paper provides no sensitivity analysis or theoretical justification for these thresholds, implying they are heuristic.  
*Why unresolved*: Different case complexities (e.g., number of plausible diagnoses, specialist confidence distributions) might demand different consensus criteria. A threshold that works for straightforward cases may over‑trigger arbitration in ambiguous ones, increasing cost without benefit, or suppress useful disagreements in others.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: A threshold‑sweep study on a validation set, varying required agreement percentages and abstention limits, measuring diagnostic performance, arbitration call frequency, and token consumption to identify thresholds that optimize the cost–accuracy trade‑off across varied case types.

## Limitations
- **Specialist role definition & selection**: The exact roster of possible specialist identities and the algorithm by which the attending selects three per case are unspecified, blocking full replication and potentially introducing hidden biases.
- **Arbitration mechanism opacity**: The process for weighing argument quality (e.g., use of confidence scores, evidence relevance, rationale coherence) is not operationalized, making it hard to verify or reproduce the conflict‑resolution path.
- **Evaluation inconsistencies**: Reported token counts (~15.5K) are lower than some baselines (17–20K), yet CAMP adds attending arbitration calls—suggesting possible offsets in panel size or call frequency that need clarification.

## Confidence
- Case‑adaptive panel assembly: **Medium confidence** (clear concept and flow, but missing implementation details like role list and selection logic).
- Three‑valued voting & abstention: **High confidence** (explicitly described with clear rules and explicit role in router logic).
- Evidence‑based arbitration: **Low confidence** (claimed to weigh argument quality over vote counts, but no reproducible rubric or criteria provided).
