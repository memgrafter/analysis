# Multi-Stage Music Source Restoration with BandSplit-RoFormer Separation and HiFi++ GAN

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04032
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04032
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Achieves average MMSNR 0.8329, Zimt 0.0189, FAD 0.6223, system-level MOS 3.5510 on MSR Challenge 2025 test set.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses Music Source Restoration (MSR) to recover unprocessed instrument stems from mastered mixtures with production artifacts. It proposes a two-stage system: first, a BandSplit-RoFormer separator trained with a three-stage curriculum that progresses from a 4-stem warm-start (using LoRA fine-tuning) to an 8-stem extension via head expansion; second, eight instrument-specific HiFi++ GAN restoration experts trained on separator-generated inputs to match test-time errors. On the MSR Challenge 2025 test set, the system achieves an average MMSNR of 0.8329, Zimt score of 0.0189, FAD of 0.6223, and a system-level MOS of 3.5510.

## Method Summary
The separation stage employs a BandSplit-RoFormer model trained via a three-stage curriculum: Stage 1 fine-tunes a pre-trained 4-stem separator (vocals, drums, bass, other) on clean mixtures using LoRA; Stage 2 continues training on mastered 4-stem mixtures with online per-stem degradation (production effects like EQ, compression, reverb, saturation, limiting, codec artifacts); Stage 3 expands to 8 stems by adding new mask heads, freezing the backbone, and training only the new heads on 8-stem mixtures. The loss combines weighted masked SI-SNR, multi-resolution STFT, L1, and a low-amplitude penalty. The restoration stage trains a HiFi++ GAN (comprising SpectralUNet, upsampling, WaveUNet refinement, and SpectralMaskNet) first as a generalist on the SonicMasterDataset through stages: content pre-training, GAN with feature matching, perceptual loss (e.g., FAD), and gramophone noise augmentation. Then, eight instrument-specific experts are created by fine-tuning the generalist on separator-generated inputs (outputs of the trained separator on training mixtures) filtered per instrument. At test time, the separator processes the mastered mixture, and each separated stem is routed to its corresponding expert to produce the final dry stem.

## Key Results
- **Separation quality:** MMSNR 0.8329, Zimt score 0.0189  
- **Restoration quality:** FAD 0.6223  
- **Perceptual quality:** System-level MOS 3.5510  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Staged curriculum training (4-stem warm-start → 8-stem extension) prevents catastrophic forgetting and enables stable multi-stem learning.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from a pre-trained 4-stem checkpoint (clean mixtures), the model first adapts to production-degraded 4-stem data, then expands to 8 stems by initializing new mask heads while freezing the backbone. This leverages prior knowledge before introducing new output dimensions.
- **Core assumption:** The shared feature extractor (BandSplit-RoFormer backbone) learns representations generalizable across stem types, so freezing it during head expansion preserves previously learned separation capabilities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1] Describes the three-stage curriculum: "Stage 1 (4 stems, clean mixtures)… Stage 2 (4 stems, mastered mixtures)… Stage 3 (8 stems)… Shared layers… initialized from Stage 2. New heads are randomly initialized and only the new mask heads are trained, while the backbone remains frozen."
  - [abstract] States: "trained with a three-stage curriculum that progresses from 4-stem warm-start fine-tuning (with LoRA) to 8-stem extension via head expansion."
  - [corpus] Neighbors discuss multi-stem separation but do not detail this specific curriculum; evidence is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If the frozen backbone lacks capacity to support 8-stem masking, new heads may fail to converge or degrade original 4-stem performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Training restoration experts on separator-generated inputs aligns the restoration module with realistic test-time errors.
- **Mechanism:** During restoration training, inputs are *not* clean or synthetically degraded stems, but actual outputs from the (already-trained) separator run on its own training mixtures. This exposes restorers to the specific error patterns (e.g., leakage, coloration) of the separation model.
- **Core assumption:** The error distribution of the separator on training data matches its error distribution on unseen test data (i.e., separation errors are consistent and learnable).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2] Explicitly states: "we generate restoration inputs by running the trained separator on our synthetic training mixtures; each expert is then trained to map the resulting separator output to the corresponding clean stem, exposing the restorers to realistic separation errors."
  - [abstract] Mentions: "trained as a generalist and then specialized into eight instrument-specific experts" with inputs produced by the separator.
  - [corpus] Neighbors focus on separation or restoration separately; this joint train-test alignment strategy is not discussed in provided neighbors.
- **Break condition:** If the separator's errors on training mixtures differ significantly from its errors on the challenge test set (e.g., due to domain shift), restoration will be misaligned and underperform.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Instrument-specific restoration experts (after generalist warm-up) better handle stem-dependent artifacts than a single generalist model.
- **Mechanism:** A single HiFi++ GAN is first trained as a generalist across all stems to learn broad restoration capabilities. This is then fine-tuned into eight separate experts, each trained only on data for one instrument class (with separator-generated inputs for that instrument). This allows specializing to the distinct production artifacts and spectral characteristics of each stem.
- **Core assumption:** Optimal artifact suppression and signal reconstruction parameters vary significantly across instrument classes (e.g., vocal de-essing vs. drum transient sharpening), so specialized models outperform a one-size-fits-all approach.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2] Details: "We first train a generalist restoration model and then fine-tune it into eight instrument-specific experts… Stage 5: Fine-tune eight instrument-specific experts using instrument-filtered training pairs."
  - [page 1, bullet] "Second, we apply a HiFi++ GAN waveform restorer trained as a generalist and then specialized into eight instrument-specific experts."
  - [corpus] Neighbors like [9] (Finally) propose universal restoration; this paper's expert specialization contrasts with that. No direct neighbor evidence for this specific MoE-style restoration.
- **Break condition:** If the generalist warm-up stage learns representations that are incompatible with later specialization, or if instrument-specific data is insufficient, experts may overfit or fail to improve over the generalist.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Separation-Restoration Decomposition
- **Why needed here:** MSR requires reversing both mixing *and* production artifacts. A monolithic model would need to learn both demixing and de-mastering simultaneously from limited data, which is ill-posed. Decomposition allows using large-scale synthetic mixtures for separation (where ground-truth is available) and targeted restoration on separator outputs.
- **Quick check question:** If you trained a single model to output clean stems directly from mastered mixtures, what fundamental supervision problem arises compared to the two-stage approach?

### Concept: Curriculum Learning & Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA)
- **Why needed here:** Adapting a 4-stem model to 8 stems risks overwriting weights critical for the original 4 stems. The staged curriculum (clean → mastered → 8 stems) gradually increases task difficulty, and LoRA (applied in Stages 1–2) allows updating low-rank adaptation matrices while preserving the original pre-trained weights, mitigating forgetting.
- **Quick check question:** Why is head expansion (adding new output heads) combined with a frozen backbone a safer strategy than fine-tuning all weights when extending from 4 to 8 stems?

### Concept: Train-Test Alignment via Synthetic Error Injection
- **Why needed here:** Restoration models must robustly handle the specific, non-ideal outputs of the separator. Training restoration on clean or artificially degraded stems would create a distribution mismatch. By feeding separator outputs (with real leakage, smearing, etc.) into restoration training, the restorer learns to correct the actual errors it will face at test time.
- **Quick check question:** If you trained the HiFi++ restorers on *clean* stems instead of separator outputs, what failure mode would likely appear on the challenge test set?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input (single-channel mastered mixture waveform) -> Stage 1: BandSplit-RoFormer (band-split front-end + RoFormer blocks) with 9 mask heads (8 target + "other"). Takes STFT magnitude; outputs complex masks; applies to mixture STFT to get estimated degraded stems -> Stage 2: Eight instrument-specific HiFi++ GAN restoration experts (each: SpectralUNet → upsampling → WaveUNet refinement → SpectralMaskNet). Takes separated stem waveform; outputs restored "dry" stem waveform.

### Critical path
1. Train 4-stem separator on clean mixtures (Stage 1) with LoRA.
2. Continue training same separator on mastered 4-stem mixtures (Stage 2) with LoRA.
3. Freeze backbone, expand to 8+1 heads, train *only new heads* on mastered 8-stem mixtures (Stage 3).
4. Run *final* separator on *all* separation training mixtures to generate "noisy" training pairs for restoration.
5. Train generalist HiFi++ on all generated pairs.
6. Fine-tune 8 instrument-specific experts on filtered subsets (Stage 5).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Separator complexity vs. restoration burden:** A more capable separator reduces error burden on restoration but may be harder to train for 8 stems. The chosen curriculum trades separation performance for training stability.
- **Generalist vs. specialist restorers:** Specialists improve quality per stem but increase memory/inference cost 8×. The warm-up-to-specialist pipeline attempts to balance.
- **Auxiliary "other" stem:** Provides a catch-all for sounds not in the 8 classes, but may contain mixed leakage that confuses restoration if not properly handled.

### Failure signatures
- **Separator:** High crosstalk between similar instruments (e.g., synth vs. keyboard), or complete failure on noisy/live recordings.
- **Restoration:** Residual noise or "watercolor" artifacts if separator errors were not representative of test set; over-smoothing of transients if expert overfits to separator's particular smearing.
- **Pipeline:** Cascading errors—poor separation dooms restoration regardless of its capability.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Ablate curriculum:** Train an 8-stem BandSplit-RoFormer from scratch (no warm-start) vs. the proposed curriculum. Expect significantly lower MMSNR/Zimt due to forgetting/instability.
2. **Input alignment test:** Train instrument-specific restorers on *clean* stems (instead of separator outputs). Evaluate on separator-output-driven test set. Expect large FAD increase and lower MOS due to mismatch.
3. **Specialist vs. generalist:** Compare 8 separate experts against a single generalist restorer (trained on all stems) using the same separator-generated inputs. Expect per-stem improvements but no change in average if some stems benefit less.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How should target stems be defined when they inherently contain production effects (e.g., reverb, chorus) that are ambiguous to remove?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state that "time-varying effects embedded in training ground-truth stems... make a consistently 'dry' target ambiguous, since the model is implicitly asked to remove effects that may be present in the reference."
- **Why unresolved:** No solution is proposed; the ambiguity in target preparation remains unaddressed, affecting restoration training consistency.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of different target definitions (e.g., effect-processed vs. "dry" references) via objective metrics and human studies to establish target preparation guidelines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What modifications can improve the separator's robustness to noisy, live, or historically degraded mixtures?
- **Basis in paper:** The limitations note "sensitivity to noisy mixtures, especially live and historical recordings:... the separator can fail to produce sufficiently faithful stem estimates."
- **Why unresolved:** The current separator architecture and training lack explicit robustness mechanisms; no exploration of noise-invariant techniques is presented.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate separator variants (e.g., noise-aware training, robust feature extraction) on held-out noisy mixtures using separation metrics like SI-SNR.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can dataset misalignments and quality disparities (e.g., in RawStems) be mitigated to prevent biased restoration training?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors attribute failure modes to "dataset mismatches and misalignments... [that] can bias restoration training and yield residual noise artifacts."
- **Why unresolved:** No data quality control or alignment verification is implemented; reliance on large-scale uncurated datasets is acknowledged but not addressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Curated dataset subsets with verified stem-mixture alignment, measuring restoration performance degradation correlated with dataset issues.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters and architectural details for BandSplit-RoFormer and HiFi++ are not fully specified, blocking exact replication.
- The exact online degradation pipeline (effects, parameter distributions, mixing order) and definition of "clean" stems for restoration are ambiguous, risking inconsistent synthetic data generation.
- Break conditions for mechanisms (e.g., backbone capacity limits for head expansion, error distribution shifts between separator training/test) are not empirically validated in the paper.

## Confidence
- **Staged curriculum prevents catastrophic forgetting:** Medium confidence. Evidence from paper's training description, but assumes backbone generalizability; capacity limits could degrade 4-stem performance.
- **Restoration trained on separator outputs aligns with test errors:** Medium confidence. Core assumption of error consistency may fail if separator's test-time errors differ from training (e.g., due to unseen degradation).
- **Instrument-specific experts outperform generalists:** Low confidence. Limited direct evidence; risks overfitting on scarce instrument-specific data or incompatibility from generalist warm-up.

## Next Checks
