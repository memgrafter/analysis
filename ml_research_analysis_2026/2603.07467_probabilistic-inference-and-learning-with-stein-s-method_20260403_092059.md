# Probabilistic Inference and Learning with Stein's Method
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07467
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07467
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Stein discrepancies enable computational probabilistic inference and learning without needing intractable normalizing constants, by leveraging Stein operators and sets to define a divergence computable solely from the approximating distribution.

## Executive Summary
This monograph presents Stein’s method as a unified framework for probabilistic inference and learning when target distributions have intractable normalizing constants. It introduces Stein discrepancies, which measure the difference between an approximating distribution \(Q\) and a target \(P\) by integrating a Stein operator \(T_P\) against functions from a Stein set \(G\). The key innovation is that the discrepancy depends only on \(Q\), thus bypassing the need to evaluate integrals under \(P\). The authors establish rigorous conditions under which Stein discrepancies metrize weak convergence (separation, detection, control) and provide concrete implementations using reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) and graph-based sets. Applications span goodness-of-fit testing, particle-based sampling (Stein variational gradient descent), energy-based model training, and variance reduction, demonstrating the versatility of the approach.

## Method Summary
Stein discrepancies quantify the distance between a target distribution \(P\) and an approximation \(Q\) via the supremum over a Stein set \(G\) of the absolute expectation of a Stein operator \(T_P\) applied to functions in \(G\). The Stein operator is constructed so that \(P(T_P g)=0\) for all \(g\in G\). This formulation allows the discrepancy to be expressed solely in terms of \(Q\), avoiding integration under \(P\). For finite-support \(Q\), if \(G\) is the unit ball in an RKHS, the supremum reduces to a closed-form expression involving the Stein kernel. Alternatively, for graph-constrained Stein sets, the supremum becomes a linear program. These computable forms enable practical algorithms: goodness-of-fit tests via bootstrapping, particle methods that minimize the discrepancy (e.g., Stein points, SVGD), learning in energy-based models by minimizing a Stein-based loss, and variance reduction for gradient estimation using discrete Stein operators. Theoretical guarantees ensure that under appropriate conditions (e.g., characteristic kernels, dissipativity), the Stein discrepancy is positive definite, vanishes only when \(Q=P\), and converges to zero if and only if \(Q\) converges weakly to \(P\).

## Key Results
- Stein discrepancy is computable without normalizing constants: for finite-support \(Q\) and RKHS Stein sets, it admits a closed-form expression via the Stein kernel; for graph Stein sets, it reduces to a linear program.
- Separation: \(S(Q)=0\) iff \(Q=P\) holds when the underlying kernel is characteristic (e.g., \(D_{11}\)-characteristic kernels) and the Stein operator satisfies certain conditions.
- Convergence control: Under tightness of \(Q_n\) and \(P\)-dominating indicator conditions, \(S(Q_n)\rightarrow0\) implies weak convergence \(Q_n\Rightarrow P\); conversely, weak convergence implies \(S(Q_n)\rightarrow0\) under suitable richness of \(G\).
- Applications include non-parametric goodness-of-fit tests, particle-based sampling (SVGD, Stein points), training energy-based models (Stein contrastive divergence), and variance reduction for gradient estimation via discrete Stein operators.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Stein discrepancies are computable without evaluating integrals under the intractable target distribution \(P\) by leveraging the Stein identity.
- **Mechanism**: A Stein operator \(T_P\) maps functions \(g\) in a Stein set \(G\) to functions with zero expectation under \(P\). The Stein discrepancy \(S(Q,T_P,G) = \sup_{g\in G} |\int T_P g \, dQ|\) only requires integration under \(Q\). For finite-support \(Q\), the supremum reduces to a closed-form expression when \(G\) is the unit ball in an RKHS (via reproducing property), or to a convex optimization problem (e.g., linear program) for graph-based sets.
- **Core assumption**: \(T_P\) and \(G\) must be chosen so that (i) the supremum is well-defined and \(T_P g\) is \(Q\)-integrable for all \(g\in G\), and (ii) the optimization (or closed form) is tractable for the given \(Q\). For RKHS-based \(G\), the kernel must yield an existing Stein kernel and satisfy RKHS embedding conditions.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract: "The discrepancy depends only on \(Q\), avoiding integration under \(P\)."
  - Section 4.1 (Definition 4.1); Section 4.4 (Theorem 4.2 closed form; Proposition 4.4 LP for graph).
- **Break condition**: If \(Q\) is continuous or the supremum over \(G\) is intractable (e.g., infinite-dimensional \(G\) without closed form), the discrepancy may not be explicitly computable. Also, if \(T_P g\) is not \(Q\)-integrable, the discrepancy is undefined.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Stein discrepancies can detect and sometimes control the convergence of a sequence \(Q_n\) to \(P\), enabling them to measure sample quality and serve as test statistics.
- **Mechanism**: Under suitable conditions on \(G\) (e.g., RKHS characteristic, graph being a \(t\)-spanner) and \(P\) (smooth density, bounded gradient), \(S(Q_n)\rightarrow0\) iff \(Q_n\) converges weakly to \(P\). Moreover, if \(Q_n\) is tight and \(S(Q_n)\rightarrow0\), then \(Q_n\Rightarrow P\) (convergence control).
- **Core assumption**: The Stein discrepancy must be \(P\)-separating and metrize a topology dominating weak convergence. Requires the kernel (for KSD) to be characteristic and additional smoothness (e.g., \(P\in\mathcal{P}_{H_k}^{p}\)).
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract: "rigorous conditions for separation (S=0 iff Q=P), convergence detection (weak convergence ⇒ S→0), convergence control (tightness + S→0 ⇒ weak convergence)."
  - Section 4.4 (Theorems 4.3, 4.5, 4.7 separation; Proposition 4.9 detection; Theorems 4.8, 4.11, 4.12 control). Section 4.3 on graph Stein and \(t\)-spanners (Proposition 4.5).
- **Break condition**: If \(G\) is not rich enough (kernel not characteristic, graph not a spanner), \(S\) may be zero for \(Q\neq P\) or fail to detect weak convergence. If \(P\) violates regularity (e.g., unbounded score), convergence control may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Stein discrepancies are intimately connected to gradient flows of KL divergence, providing a principled way to construct particle-based algorithms (e.g., SVGD) for sampling and approximation.
- **Mechanism**: The gradient of KL(\(Q\|P\)) w.r.t. a transport perturbation is given by a Stein discrepancy: \(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon} \text{KL}(( \text{Id} + \varepsilon g )_\# Q \| P)\big|_{\varepsilon=0} = Q(T_P g)\). Maximizing this over \(g\in G\) yields the steepest descent direction. When \(G\) is the unit ball of an RKHS, the optimal \(g\) is the normalized Riesz representer of \(Q(T_P k_x)\), leading to deterministic updates for particles (SVGD). Thus minimizing \(S(Q_t)\) follows a KL gradient flow.
- **Core assumption**: \(Q\) must be absolutely continuous with smooth density to allow transport and gradient derivation; the RKHS must contain functions approximating the score of \(P\); the kernel should be characteristic for the limit to be \(P\).
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract: "The connection between Stein operators and Stein variational gradient descent is set out in detail."
  - Section 5.3 (gradient flow); Section 5.5 (Stein variational gradient); equation (6.12); Section 6.3.2 (SVGD).
- **Break condition**: If the RKHS is too small (kernel not characteristic, bandwidth too large), the gradient flow may not converge to \(P\). With few particles, the discrete approximation may be poor; particles may collapse (mode collapse) if kernel bandwidth is poorly chosen.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Stein Operator and Stein Set
- **Why needed here**: Stein operators and sets are the fundamental ingredients of any Stein discrepancy. Understanding how to construct them for a given target \(P\) (e.g., Langevin for smooth densities, discrete operators for combinatorial spaces) is the first step.
- **Quick check question**: "Explain why a Stein operator \(T_P\) must satisfy \(P(T_P g)=0\) for all \(g\) in a Stein set \(G\), and give an example of such an operator for a standard normal distribution."

### Concept: Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
- **Why needed here**: RKHS provides the mathematical structure for kernel Stein discrepancies, enabling closed-form computation and analysis of properties like separation and convergence control.
- **Quick check question**: "State the reproducing property of an RKHS and use it to derive the closed-form expression for the kernel Stein discrepancy when \(Q\) is a finite discrete distribution."

### Concept: Kernel Mean Embedding and MMD
- **Why needed here**: Kernel Stein discrepancy can be viewed as a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) with a Stein kernel. This connection is key to understanding the topology induced by KSD and its relation to weak convergence.
- **Quick check question**: "Define the kernel mean embedding of a distribution and the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). How does the Stein kernel \(k_P\) arise in the representation of KSD as an MMD?"

### Critical path
1. Define the problem: Determine if target \(P\) is known up to normalization and choose appropriate Stein operator (Langevin for continuous, discrete for combinatorial) and Stein set (RKHS for closed form, graph for finite \(Q\)).
2. Compute Stein discrepancy: For KSD, compute Stein kernel \(k_P\) and evaluate \(S\) via matrix operations for finite \(Q\). For graph Stein, build the graph and solve the associated linear program.
3. Apply to task: Goodness-of-fit (compute \(S\), bootstrap null distribution); particle approximation (minimize \(S\) via Stein points or SVGD); importance sampling (solve quadratic program for optimal weights).
4. Validate: Check discrepancy decrease, particle coverage, or test calibration.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Stein set choice**: RKHS offers theoretical guarantees (characteristic, convergence control) but needs careful kernel selection (bandwidth, type). Graph-based is flexible for any finite \(Q\) but requires graph construction (computational cost) and LP solving per iteration.
- **Kernel selection**: Gaussian kernel is smooth and universal but may be overly smooth, reducing power for local differences. Matérn kernels allow tuning smoothness via \(\nu\); IMQ kernels offer better tails. Bandwidth: cross-validation or median heuristic; too small → noisy estimates, too large → loss of sensitivity.
- **Stein operator**: Langevin is simple and widely applicable (requires gradient of log \(p\)). Diffusion operators (e.g., preconditioned) can improve mixing but require specifying matrix coefficients. Gradient-free operators avoid derivatives but are less powerful.
- **Computational cost**: Exact KSD is \(O(n^2)\) for \(n\) particles; random feature approximations reduce to \(O(nM)\) but introduce bias. Graph Stein LP costs \(O(n^3)\) worst-case but can be sped up with sparse graphs.
- **For SVGD**: Tradeoff between number of particles and accuracy; step size vs. stability; kernel bandwidth adaptation crucial for multi-modal targets.

### Failure signatures
- **Non-separation**: KSD zero for clearly different distributions → kernel not characteristic or \(P\) not in required RKHS (check Theorem 4.3 conditions).
- **No convergence detection**: \(S(Q_n)\) does not decrease as \(n\rightarrow\infty\) even though \(Q_n\rightarrow P\) → Stein set too small (graph not a spanner, kernel too smooth). Increase Stein set richness.
- **Numerical instability**: Kernel matrix near-singular (add jitter); LP solver fails (graph too dense or poorly scaled). Check condition number; ensure graph connectivity.
- **SVGD mode collapse**: Particles collapse to a single mode → kernel bandwidth too large (repulsion weak) or too small (particles stuck). Try adaptive bandwidth or more particles.
- **High variance in gradient estimation** (policy gradients): Using Stein control variates (RODEO) and variance remains high → learned \(g\) inadequate; increase neural network capacity or adjust regularization.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Stein identity verification**: For standard normal \(P\), use Langevin operator \(T(g)=\nabla\cdot g + g\cdot x\). Test with low-degree polynomials (e.g., linear, quadratic). Compute \(\mathbb{E}[T(g)(X)]\) via Monte Carlo; should be near zero. Validates \(T_P\) implementation.
2. **KSD computability and separation**: For \(P=\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(Q_n\) = empirical from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) with \(\mu\neq0\), compute KSD using Gaussian kernel. Plot particle positions and track KSD. Expect particles to spread to both modes and KSD to decrease.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What are the necessary conditions on the base kernel \(k\) for the Langevin KSD to separate two distinct probability distributions \(Q \neq P\)?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper provides sufficient conditions for \(P\)-separation in Theorems 4.3, 4.5, and 4.7, often relying on kernels being \(D_{11}(\mathbb{R}^d)\)-characteristic or employing a tilting trick to bound the Stein kernel. It identifies the unboundedness of the Langevin Stein kernel as a primary obstacle to general separation.
- **Why unresolved**: Necessary conditions are not characterized; tilting is a constructive remedy, but it is unclear when tilting is essential or if non‑tilted kernels can still yield separation, especially for heavy‑tailed targets.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A proof of necessary and sufficient conditions on the kernel and target \(P\) guaranteeing \(P\)-separation, or a counterexample showing separation fails for any non‑tilted kernel when \(P\) has certain tails.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Under what minimal assumptions does convergence of the Langevin KSD to zero (\(\text{KSD}_P(Q_n) \rightarrow 0\)) imply weak convergence of \(Q_n\) to \(P\) (i.e., \(\text{BL}(\cdot,\cdot)(P,Q_n) \rightarrow 0\))?
- **Basis in paper**: Theorem 4.8 gives sufficient conditions: tightness of \((Q_n)\) and the RKHS \(H_k\) \(P\)-dominating indicators. The necessity of these assumptions is not explored.
- **Why unresolved**: It is unknown whether tightness is automatically implied by \(\text{KSD}_P(Q_n) \rightarrow 0\) for all kernels and targets; the \(P\)-dominating indicator condition might also be stronger than needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A demonstration that for a wider class of kernels (e.g., all characteristic kernels) and targets, \(\text{KSD}_P(Q_n) \rightarrow 0\) forces tightness and hence weak convergence, or a construction of a sequence \((Q_n)\) with \(\text{KSD}_P(Q_n) \rightarrow 0\) but \(\text{BL}(\cdot,\cdot)(P,Q_n) \not\rightarrow 0\).

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the minimal batch size \(m\) required for stochastic Stein discrepancies (SSDs) to guarantee convergence detection and control for general decomposable Stein operators?
- **Basis in paper**: Theorem 4.19 ensures SSD control under uniform dissipativity and growth bounds on the subsampled operators \(T_\sigma\). The analysis uses uniformity over all \(\sigma\) and does not explicitly optimize over \(m\).
- **Why unresolved**: The uniformity conditions may be conservative, potentially allowing smaller \(m\) than currently justified; no lower bounds on \(m\) are established, especially when \(L\) is large.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Non‑asymptotic bounds showing that \(m\) can be chosen sublinear in \(L\) while still preserving detection/control guarantees, or an example where \(m <\) a certain threshold leads to failure of control.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Does convergence of the Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD) in the kernel Stein discrepancy norm imply convergence in the 1‑Wasserstein metric, and what are the achievable convergence rates?
- **Basis in paper**: SVGD is known to converge in KSD at \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\), but Wasserstein convergence is not directly addressed. Classical Stein discrepancies control \(W_1\) under different conditions (Theorem 4.1), yet SVGD operates via a kernel‑based particle flow whose Wasserstein dynamics are not fully understood.
- **Why unresolved**: No general theoretical link between KSD and \(W_1\) for the SVGD process exists; Wasserstein control is key for downstream tasks like uncertainty quantification.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Prove inequalities of the form \(W_1(P,Q_n) \leq C \cdot \text{KSD}(Q_n)^\alpha\) for explicit \(\alpha > 0\) and constant \(C\) under suitable kernel assumptions, together with an accompanying rate for SVGD updates.

## Limitations
- Kernel choice and bandwidth significantly affect sensitivity and computability; no universal selection criterion.
- Theoretical guarantees (separation, convergence control) require restrictive assumptions: characteristic kernels, target density smoothness, dissipativity. Violations may cause failure.
- Exact KSD computation scales as \(O(n^2)\); large-scale use relies on approximations with uncontrolled bias.
- Discrete Stein operators and graph‑based sets are less mature; separation hinges on graph spanner property, which is nontrivial to ensure.
- Applications to energy‑based models and variance reduction lack rigorous analysis; empirical evidence is sparse.

## Confidence
- **Computability without P integrals**: High (closed form via RKHS, LP for graphs).
- **Separation & convergence detection/control**: High for continuous targets with characteristic kernels; Medium for graph Stein (depends on spanner condition).
- **Gradient flow link (SVGD)**: High (derivation provided).
- **Goodness-of-fit & Stein points**: High; Energy-based model training & variance reduction: Low (theoretical support limited).
