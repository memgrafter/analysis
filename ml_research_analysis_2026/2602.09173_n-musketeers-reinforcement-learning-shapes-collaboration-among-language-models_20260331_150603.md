# $n$-Musketeers: Reinforcement Learning Shapes Collaboration Among Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09173  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09173  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Soft hidden-state collaboration via a Perceiver adapter yields a large improvement on arithmetic reasoning (75.3% vs 52.3% for single‑model RLVR) but provides no benefit on tasks where a single model already saturates.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of combining multiple frozen small language models (SLMs) using their internal representations under reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). The authors propose *soft hidden‑state collaboration*: a Perceiver‑style cross‑attention adapter ingests pooled final‑layer hidden states from heterogeneous frozen experts, producing trainable context tokens that prefix a policy model. Both adapter and policy are optimized end‑to‑end with GRPO. Evaluated on Reasoning Gym and GSM8K, the method achieves large gains on arithmetic tasks (e.g., 75.3% vs 52.3% for single‑model RLVR), modest improvements on logic, and no benefit on algorithmic or GSM8K tasks where single‑model performance is already high. Analysis of training dynamics reveals that RLVR induces structured, selective expert attention (entropy drops, negative reward correlation $\rho \approx -0.48$), indicating emergent specialization.

## Method Summary
The method trains a Perceiver adapter to aggregate final‑layer hidden states from multiple frozen expert language models. Each expert processes the input with its own tokenizer; the hidden state of the last token is pooled and projected to a shared dimension. The adapter uses a set of learnable latent queries to perform cross‑attention over these expert vectors, followed by a feed‑forward network, producing a set of context tokens. These tokens are prepended to the input of a policy model (Qwen‑2.5‑3B‑Instruct with LoRA adapters). The entire system is trained end‑to‑end with reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (GRPO), updating both the adapter and policy parameters while keeping the experts frozen. This allows the policy to learn which expert signals to attend to based solely on reward outcomes, without explicit routing supervision.

## Key Results
- On arithmetic tasks from Reasoning Gym, the method achieves **75.3%** accuracy compared to **52.3%** for single‑model RLVR.
- Modest gains are observed on logic tasks; no improvement on algorithmic tasks.
- On GSM8K, where single‑model RLVR is near saturation, adding the multi‑expert context degrades performance.
- Training dynamics show decreasing attention entropy and a negative correlation ($\rho \approx -0.48$) between entropy and reward, indicating that RLVR drives structured, selective expert utilization.
- Ablations verify the necessity of cross‑attention and last‑token pooling; the optimal number of latent queries is $m=8$.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A Perceiver‑style cross‑attention adapter aggregates heterogeneous frozen SLM hidden states into fixed‑size context tokens that condition the policy.
- **Mechanism:** Each expert’s final‑layer hidden state is pooled (last token), projected to a shared dimension, and then a set of trainable latent queries attends to these representations via cross‑attention. The output is processed by an FFN and projected to the policy’s embedding space, yielding $m$ context tokens prepended to the input.
- **Core assumption:** Frozen experts’ hidden states encode useful reasoning signals that can be extracted and combined via a latent interface.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “soft hidden‑state collaboration…”; Section 3: equations (5–11) and Figure 3; limited corpus support.
- **Break condition:** If expert hidden states lack task‑relevant information (e.g., pooling from shallow layers leads to severe degradation as in Table 2), the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** RLVR optimizes both the adapter and policy end‑to‑end, inducing emergent, structured attention over experts without explicit routing supervision.
- **Mechanism:** Under GRPO, updates maximize verifiable reward. Cross‑attention weights evolve from uniform to concentrated, measured by decreasing entropy $H$ and strong negative correlation ($\rho \approx -0.48$) between entropy and reward.
- **Core assumption:** Outcome‑level rewards alone are sufficient to shape latent routing decisions and reveal expert roles.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “Training dynamics reveal that RLVR induces structured, selective expert attention…”; Section 4.3: Figure 4 and Spearman correlations; no direct corpus evidence.
- **Break condition:** If the reward signal is too sparse or uninformative, attention may remain uniform and fail to specialize.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The effectiveness of soft hidden‑state collaboration is task‑dependent: it yields large gains on challenging arithmetic where single‑model RLVR leaves room for improvement, but can degrade performance on saturated tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Learned context tokens provide an inductive bias that supplements policy learning when the task remains difficult; on already‑solved tasks, the additional latent variables act as structured noise, causing interference.
- **Core assumption:** Multi‑expert latent conditioning is not universally beneficial; its value depends on the residual learning potential of the base policy.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract performance differences; Section 4.2 Table 1; limited corpus support.
- **Break condition:** On tasks where the base policy already achieves high accuracy, adding latent context tokens reduces performance.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Input prompt $x$ → tokenized by each frozen expert (using its own tokenizer) → final‑layer hidden states $H_i$ → last‑token pooling → $h_i$ → expert‑specific projection $W_i$ → $z_i$ → stacked $V = [z_1, …, z_n]$ → Perceiver adapter (set of $m$ latent queries $Q_{\text{lat}}$, cross‑attention over $V$, FFN) → context tokens $C$ → prepended to policy input embeddings → policy $\pi_\theta$ generates response $y$ → reward $r(x,y)$ → GRPO updates adapter parameters $\phi$ and policy LoRA parameters $\theta$.

- **Critical path (training loop):** For each batch:  
  1. Run all frozen experts in parallel to extract $h_i$.  
  2. Project to shared dimension and stack $V$.  
  3. Compute $C$ via adapter.  
  4. Prefix $C$ to the policy’s input embeddings and sample completions $y$ from the policy.  
  5. Evaluate verifiable reward $r(x,y)$.  
  6. Compute GRPO loss and back‑propagate to update adapter and policy parameters while keeping experts and reference policy fixed.  
  At inference, steps (1)–(3) are identical; then generate $y$ with greedy or sampling decode.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Latent size* $m$: too small loses capacity; too large adds noise/variance (optimal $m \approx 8$).  
  - *Pooling strategy*: last‑token works best; shallow or random pooling degrades performance.  
  - *Frozen experts vs end‑to‑end*: freezing avoids costly retraining but forces adapter to work with heterogeneous representations.  
  - *Cross‑attention vs direct FFN*: cross‑attention enables input‑dependent weighting; removing it harms performance.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High run‑to‑run variance, especially with larger expert teams.  
  - Entropy of attention weights remains high (no specialization emerges).  
  - Performance drop on tasks where single‑model RLVR already saturates.  
  - Low‑quality experts (e.g., GPT‑2) fail to be suppressed (high utilization persists).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Main evaluation:** Compare full hidden‑state method against single‑model RLVR, output‑level collaboration, and hard routing on Reasoning Gym (Arithmetic, Logic, Algorithmic) and GSM8K (Table 1).  
  2. **Ablation – Cross‑attention:** Remove cross‑attention (apply FFN directly to stacked expert vectors) to demonstrate its necessity (Table 1 “w/o Cross Attn”).  
  3. **Ablation – Pooling & latent size:** Vary pooling strategies (first‑token, random, shallow) and number of latent queries $m$ (Figure 6, Table 2) to identify optimal settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Task dependence and signal quality:** Under what specific task and model conditions do frozen SLMs’ hidden states provide actionable signals for RLVR‑trained policies, and why do benefits vary across reasoning domains? (Basis: explicit; Why unresolved: paper shows correlations but not factors; Evidence needed: controlled experiments varying difficulty, capacity, expert heterogeneity.)  
2. **Functional specialization vs capacity bias:** How can we disentangle whether expert utilization under RLVR reflects genuine functional specialization or merely capacity‑driven preferences? (Basis: explicit; Why unresolved: larger experts dominate even on arithmetic; Evidence needed: capacity‑matched experts or capacity‑controlled routing.)  
3. **Efficiency via selective execution:** Can hidden‑state collaboration be made more efficient via selective execution or capacity‑aware routing without losing benefits? (Basis: explicit; Why unresolved: method uses all experts; Evidence needed: implement selective execution and compare accuracy vs compute.)  
4. **Necessity of Perceiver bottleneck:** Is the Perceiver‑style cross‑attention bottleneck necessary, or could simpler integration mechanisms achieve similar benefits under RLVR? (Basis: inferred; Why unresolved: only one ablation without cross‑attention; Evidence needed: compare with other pooling/attention designs.)  
5. **Failure on saturated tasks:** Why does hidden‑state collaboration fail to improve performance on tasks where single‑model RLVR already achieves high accuracy (e.g., Logic, GSM8K), and can this be mitigated? (Basis: inferred; Why unresolved: speculation about noise; Evidence needed: analyze context token content and experiment with dynamic scaling/gating.)

## Limitations
- **Reward function ambiguity:** The exact reward computation for Reasoning Gym tasks is not detailed, making faithful replication difficult.  
- **Heterogeneous tokenizer handling:** Strategies for aligning variable‑length sequences across different experts (padding/truncation for last‑token pooling) are underspecified.  
- **Task‑dependent applicability:** The method’s benefits disappear on tasks where a single model already performs well (e.g., GSM8K), limiting its general usefulness.

## Confidence
- **Architectural design** (Perceiver cross‑attention adapter): **Medium**  
- **RLVR‑driven emergent attention structure**: **Low**  
- **Task‑dependent performance gains**: **Medium**
