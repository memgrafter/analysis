# Intrinsic-Energy Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures Induce Quasimetric Spaces

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.12245  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12245  
- **Reference count**: 4  
- **Primary result**: Intrinsic-energy JEPAs based on least-action principles naturally induce quasimetric spaces, formally linking predictive world models to quasimetric reinforcement learning.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a rigorous theoretical connection between Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) that employ intrinsic energy functions derived from least-action principles and the quasimetric value functions used in Quasimetric Reinforcement Learning (QRL). The intrinsic energy between two states is defined as the infimum of accumulated local effort over all admissible trajectories connecting them. Under mild assumptions—path closure (admissible trajectories can be concatenated) and additivity (effort sums along concatenated paths)—this energy satisfies all quasimetric properties, including the triangle inequality (Theorem 1). Consequently, such JEPAs fall squarely within the quasimetric function class targeted by QRL (Corollary 2). Moreover, the work proves that symmetric finite energy functions cannot represent directed reachability, underscoring the necessity of asymmetry for tasks with directional dependencies (Proposition 2). The contributions are purely theoretical; no empirical validation is provided.

## Method Summary
The authors consider a JEPA where states are embedded and future state embeddings are predicted from actions. Instead of a standard predictive loss, they focus on an *intrinsic energy* defined via a path-integral formulation: for any two states \(x\) and \(y\), the energy \(E(x,y)\) is the infimum over all admissible trajectories from \(x\) to \(y\) of the sum of local effort costs along the path. This is inspired by the least-action principle. The core analysis identifies two key assumptions: (1) *path closure*—concatenating two admissible trajectories yields another admissible trajectory, and (2) *additivity*—the effort of a concatenated path equals the sum of the efforts of its segments. Under these conditions, the infimum energy becomes a quasimetric (nonnegative, with \(E(x,x)=0\), triangle inequality, but not necessarily symmetric). The paper then maps this result to the QRL framework, showing that intrinsic-energy JEPAs naturally produce the kind of quasimetric value functions QRL aims to learn.

## Key Results
- **Theorem 1**: The intrinsic energy defined as an infimum of accumulated local effort over admissible trajectories is a quasimetric provided the trajectory set is closed under concatenation and the effort is additive.
- **Corollary 2**: Intrinsic-energy JEPAs generate energy functions that belong to the quasimetric value-function class used in Quasimetric Reinforcement Learning.
- **Proposition 2**: Any finite, symmetric energy function cannot express directed reachability, thereby motivating the use of asymmetric quasimetric energies for directional tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the path-integral definition of energy. Because the energy is an infimum over all admissible paths, the triangle inequality follows from the possibility of concatenating a path from \(x\) to \(z\) with one from \(z\) to \(y\): the total effort of the combined path is at least the minimal effort from \(x\) to \(y\), i.e., \(E(x,y) \le E(x,z) + E(z,y)\). Path closure guarantees that the concatenated path is admissible, and additivity ensures the effort adds up exactly. Non-negativity comes from non-negative local efforts. Asymmetry is possible because the minimal effort from \(x\) to \(y\) may differ from that of \(y\) to \(x\) if the local cost landscape is not symmetric (e.g., one-way doors). These properties collectively ensure that the resulting energy function is a quasimetric.

## Foundational Learning
- **Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)**: Embeds states and predicts embeddings of future states given actions. *Why needed*: Decouples representation learning from raw inputs and provides a high-level predictive model. *Quick check*: Does the loss operate on embeddings, not raw pixels? Yes.
- **Intrinsic Energy**: The paper’s central quantity—defined as the infimum of accumulated local effort over trajectories. *Why needed*: Replaces per-step prediction errors with a geometrically meaningful notion of difficulty or cost. *Quick check*: Is it computed by minimizing a sum of local costs? Yes, in the limit.
- **Least-Action Principle**: A variational principle stating that the actual path taken minimizes an action functional. *Why needed*: Provides a physical analogy and justifies the infimum definition. *Quick check*: Does it assume deterministic dynamics? In this context it is used as a definition, not a physical law.
- **Quasimetric**: A distance-like function that may be asymmetric but satisfies the triangle inequality. *Why needed*: Captures directed reachability, crucial for planning in environments with asymmetries. *Quick check*: Does it require symmetry? No.
- **Quasimetric Reinforcement Learning (QRL)**: An RL paradigm that learns quasimetric value functions to improve sample efficiency. *Why needed*: Offers a more expressive value function class for asymmetric problems. *Quick check*: Are value functions trained to approximate a quas metric? Yes.
- **Path Closure & Additivity**: Assumptions about the set of admissible trajectories and effort composition. *Why needed*: They are sufficient (and possibly necessary) for the infimum energy to be a quasimetric. *Quick check*: Can any two admissible paths be concatenated? Does effort along concatenation equal sum? These must hold.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**:  
  Local Effort Module (assigns cost to state-action pairs) → Admissible Trajectory Set (all paths satisfying closure) → Infimum Operator (computes minimal accumulated effort) → Energy Function \(E(x,y)\)
- **Critical Path**: Input states \(x\) and \(y\) are processed by the effort module to obtain local costs. The system considers all admissible trajectories (e.g., all paths of finite length under a policy constraint). The total effort of a trajectory is the sum of local costs along it. The infimum over all such sums yields \(E(x,y)\). The triangle inequality is automatically satisfied because concatenating an optimal path from \(x\) to \(z\) with any path from \(z\) to \(y\) produces a valid \(x\to y\) path whose total effort upper‑bounds \(E(x,y)\).
- **Design Tradeoffs**:  
  *Asymmetry vs. symmetry*: Asymmetry is essential for directed tasks but may complicate representation learning and optimization.  
  *Path closure vs. tractability*: Enforcing closure often requires considering an exponential number of paths; relaxing it risks breaking the quasimetric guarantee.  
  *Infimum approximation*: Practical implementations must estimate the infimum with a finite set of trajectories; this can yield only an upper bound on the true energy and may violate the triangle inequality if the sample is insufficient.
- **Failure Signatures**: Triangle inequality violations when path closure fails (e.g., if certain transitions are prohibited in concatenated paths) or when effort is non-additive (e.g., if time‑dependent costs break linear accumulation). Symmetric energies in environments with one‑way constraints fail to model reachability correctly. Large discrepancies between true and approximated infima indicate poor coverage of admissible trajectories.
- **First Experiments**:  
  1. Compute the intrinsic energy on a small deterministic MDP with known one‑way doors; verify that \(E\) is asymmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality.  
  2. Train a neural network to approximate the intrinsic energy from sampled trajectories; test whether the learned \(E_\theta\) approximately satisfies quasimetric properties on held‑out state pairs.  
  3. Compare a JEPA agent that uses the intrinsic energy as a planning heuristic against a standard model‑based RL agent on a directional goal‑reaching task, measuring sample efficiency and success rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What minimal assumptions guarantee an intrinsic energy is a quasimetric?
- **Basis in paper**: Theorem 1 assumes closure and additivity but does not prove necessity.
- **Why unresolved**: The tightness of these assumptions is unknown; weaker conditions might still yield quasimetrics.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Prove necessity by showing that dropping either assumption can lead to a violation of the triangle inequality, or construct a counterexample.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: For general JEPAs, what conditions make the induced energy a quasimetric?
- **Basis in paper**: The analysis is restricted to intrinsic energies based on least‑action principles; it is unclear how to extend to arbitrary predictive targets.
- **Why unresolved**: No known theoretical criteria link general JEPA training objectives to quasimetric properties; standard JEPAs do not enforce such structure.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive necessary and/or sufficient conditions on the JEPA loss function or architecture parameters that guarantee the learned energy is a quasimetric, or design a regularizer that explicitly enforces the property.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How well do intrinsic‑energy JEPAs perform empirically on goal‑reaching tasks compared to QRL?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper explicitly states that it provides no empirical metrics.
- **Why unresolved**: It is unknown whether the theoretical equivalence translates into practical benefits such as improved sample efficiency or generalization.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct systematic benchmarks comparing intrinsic‑energy JEPA agents with state‑of‑the‑art QRL methods on standard RL environments with directional challenges.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the impact of asymmetry in intrinsic energies on learned representations and planning?
- **Basis in paper**: Proposition 2 highlights the need for asymmetry but does not investigate its effects on the representation space or planning algorithms.
- **Why unresolved**: Asymmetry may influence the geometry of embeddings, optimization dynamics, and robustness; these trade‑offs are unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Perform controlled experiments comparing symmetric and asymmetric JEPA variants on tasks requiring directionality, analyzing both representation quality (e.g., via probing) and planning performance.

## Limitations
- The guarantees rely on exact computation of the infimum over *all* admissible trajectories, which is combinatorially infeasible in large or continuous spaces; real implementations use approximations that can break quasimetric properties.
- The assumptions of path closure and additivity may not hold for stochastic policies, environments with non‑Markovian constraints, or when admissible trajectories are restricted by sampling.
- The results apply only to intrinsic‑energy JEPAs based on a least‑action formulation; standard JEPAs with other predictive losses are not covered and may not induce quasimetrics.

## Confidence
- **Theorem 1**: High – the proof is rigorous and the assumptions are clearly stated.
- **Corollary 2**: Medium – it follows from Theorem 1 but depends on an exact alignment with the QRL value‑function class; minor interpretive issues could arise.
- **Proposition 2**: High – the argument that a symmetric finite energy cannot encode directed reachability is straightforward and sound.

## Next Checks
1. **Empirical sanity check**: Implement an intrinsic‑energy JEPA on a deterministic MDP with known directional costs; compute pairwise energies and verify triangle inequality and asymmetry on a grid of state pairs.
2. **Approximation robustness test**: Train a neural JEPA using a limited number of sampled trajectories; quantify violations of the triangle inequality and asymmetry as sample size increases.
3. **Assumption necessity analysis**: In a toy domain, deliberately break path closure (e.g., by forbidding concatenation of paths) or additivity (e.g., by using a non‑linear accumulation rule) and demonstrate that the resulting energy fails to be a quasimetric.
