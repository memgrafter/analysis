# SHAPE: Structure-aware Hierarchical Unsupervised Domain Adaptation with Plausibility Evaluation for Medical Image Segmentation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21904
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21904
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art average Dice scores on cardiac (90.08% MRI→CT, 78.51% CT→MRI) and abdominal (87.48% MRI→CT, 86.89% CT→MRI) segmentation.

## Executive Summary
SHAPE tackles unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for medical image segmentation by addressing two fundamental limitations: semantically unaware feature alignment that discards discriminative structure, and pseudo-label validation that ignores global anatomical plausibility. Its three-part pipeline—Hierarchical Feature Modulation (HFM) for class-aware, spatially-differentiated feature alignment; Hypergraph Plausibility Estimation (HPE) to enforce global anatomical coherence via hypergraphs; and Structural Anomaly Pruning (SAP) to eliminate unstable class-level predictions—produces anatomically consistent segmentations. On standard cross-modality benchmarks, SHAPE significantly outperforms prior methods, achieving state-of-the-art Dice scores such as 90.08% on cardiac MRI→CT and 87.48% on abdominal MRI→CT.

## Method Summary
SHAPE builds on a frozen DINOv3 ViT-S/16 encoder and a trainable UNet decoder. The Hierarchical Feature Modulation (HFM) module receives features from both source and target images and generates modulated feature sets through class-aware, spatially-differentiated operations (pure core tokens vs. boundary tokens). The decoder processes these features to produce segmentation maps. To generate reliable pseudo-labels on the target domain, an ensemble of teacher predictions (EMA of the decoder) is created using multiple HFM-modulated feature variants. Hypergraph Plausibility Estimation (HPE) scores each pseudo-label based on global anatomical coherence, while Structural Anomaly Pruning (SAP) removes class-level artifacts identified by size instability across the ensemble. Only high-fidelity pseudo-labels contribute to the unsupervised loss, which is combined with a supervised source loss. Training uses AdamW with a cosine schedule for 200 epochs and includes EMA updates for the teacher. The method is evaluated on cardiac (MMWHS) and abdominal (CHAOS, MICCAI 2015) datasets under cross-modality adaptation (MRI↔CT).

## Key Results
- Cardiac: average Dice of 90.08% for MRI→CT and 78.51% for CT→MRI.
- Abdominal: average Dice of 87.48% for MRI→CT and 86.89% for CT→MRI.
- These scores significantly outperform prior state-of-the-art methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Feature Modulation (HFM)
**Claim:** HFM generates features with high distributional fidelity while preserving class discriminability by performing class‑aware, spatially‑differentiated alignment.  
**Mechanism:** Given frozen DINOv3 features, HFM first applies a global AdaIN to match textural statistics. Then it up‑samples the features, unfolds them into patch tokens, and classifies each token’s content via a purity score. Tokens are split into pure semantic cores (high purity) and impure structural boundaries (low purity). For pure tokens, it performs class‑consistent Mixup with a representative target token of the same class. For boundary tokens, it applies a separate statistical mixing based on boundary‑specific statistics. The modulated tokens are refolded to produce the adapted feature map.  
**Core assumption:** Semantic purity can be reliably estimated from patch‑level label histograms, and mixing pure tokens with representative target Exemplars preserves class identity while adapting style.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “Hierarchical Feature Modulation (HFM) performs class‑aware, spatially‑differentiated feature alignment.”  
- [section 3.1] describes the purity threshold τp, the unfolding operation, and the two distinct mixing strategies.  
- [corpus] Neighboring work (e.g., SA²GFM, Structure‑aware Hybrid‑order Similarity Learning) explores structure‑aware augmentations but does not address spatially‑differentiated feature modulation; thus direct corpus validation is weak.  
**Break condition:** If the purity threshold τp is poorly chosen, the core/boundary split becomes unreliable, leading to either over‑smoothing of boundaries or misalignment of core features.

### Mechanism 2: Hypergraph Plausibility Estimation (HPE)
**Claim:** HPE ensures pseudo‑labels are globally anatomically plausible by modeling intra‑class shape and inter‑class layout via hypergraphs, rejecting predictions that violate typical anatomical configurations.  
**Mechanism:** Each predicted segmentation map is converted into a hypergraph: vertices are foreground pixels; class‑specific hyperedges capture intra‑class shape; a single layout hyperedge captures inter‑class spatial arrangement. Shape plausibility is scored using the isoperimetric ratio of each class mask; layout plausibility uses direction cosines between class centroids. Both are normalized by batch statistics and combined with a pixel‑level certainty score (entropy + JSD). The final score gates whether the sample enters self‑training.  
**Assumption:** Anatomical plausibility can be quantified by compactness (isoperimetric ratio) and relative positioning (centroid directions); hypergraphs can represent these higher‑order relationships better than pairwise graphs.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “Hypergraph Plausibility Estimation (HPE) leverages hypergraphs to assess the global anatomical plausibility that standard graphs cannot capture.”  
- [section 3.2] details the hypergraph construction, vertex reliability, intra‑ and inter‑class scoring, and the final gating.  
- [corpus] The neighbor “Hypergraph tversky‑aware domain incremental learning for brain tumor segmentation” demonstrates hypergraph use in medical segmentation, but does not evaluate global plausibility; evidence is indirect.  
**Break condition:** Batch statistics become unrepresentative when the batch contains only atypical anatomies, causing normal structures to be penalized or abnormal ones to be accepted.

### Mechanism 3: Structural Anomaly Pruning (SAP)
**Claim:** SAP removes spurious class‑level artifacts (e.g., hallucinated regions) by exploiting instability of class sizes across an ensemble of teacher predictions under different feature modulations.  
**Mechanism:** An ensemble of teacher predictions (from EMA decoder) on the same target image but with different HFM‑modulated features is gathered. For each foreground class k, the vector of pixel counts across predictions is computed. The coefficient of variation (std/mean) is the instability score Υ(k). Classes with Υ(k) above a dynamic percentile‑based threshold θA are masked as “ignore” in the consensus pseudo‑label.  
**Assumption:** Valid anatomical structures exhibit stable sizes across feature perturbations; unstable size estimates indicate model hallucination or noise.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “Structural Anomaly Pruning (SAP) purges remaining artifacts via cross‑view stability.”  
- [section 3.3] defines Υ(k) and the thresholding rule.  
- [corpus] No direct neighbor evidence; existing UDA works like UPL‑SFDA and IPLC use uncertainty but not cross‑view size stability for pruning.  
**Break condition:** If the ensemble diversity is too low (e.g., features are nearly identical), instability scores may not discriminate; also valid small structures may be pruned if their size naturally fluctuates due to image noise.

## Foundational Learning

### Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA)
**Why needed here:** SHAPE assumes a labeled source domain and an unlabeled target domain; UDA is the overarching problem setting that the method addresses.  
**Quick check:** What are the two main families of UDA methods discussed in the introduction, and what limitation does SHAPE point out in each?  
*(Answer: likelihood-based alignment discards structure; entropy minimization disregards global plausibility.)*

### Foundation Model (DINOv3)
**Why needed here:** SHAPE uses a frozen DINOv3 ViT encoder as a feature extractor. Understanding that this provides rich semantic priors is key to why HFM can modulate features rather than learning from scratch.  
**Quick check:** Why might fine‑tuning the entire DINOv3 encoder be undesirable in low‑resource UDA, and how does using a frozen encoder change the adaptation focus?  
*(Answer: Fine‑tuning is computationally expensive and may overwrite useful pretrained features; a frozen encoder forces adaptation into the decoder and feature modulation, reducing compute.)*

### Hypergraph Representation
**Why needed here:** HPE relies on hypergraphs to model relationships among anatomical structures beyond pairwise connections. Knowing the difference between graphs and hypergraphs clarifies the novelty.  
**Quick check:** How does a hyperedge differ from a graph edge, and why is this useful for capturing “global anatomical plausibility”?  
*(Answer: A hyperedge can connect more than two vertices, representing group relationships; this allows simultaneous modeling of multiple structures, e.g., all pixels of a class, to capture shape and layout holistically.)*

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** The pipeline consists of a frozen DINOv3 ViT-S/16 encoder, an HFM module that modulates source and target features, a trainable UNet decoder, a teacher model maintained by exponential moving average (EMA) of the decoder, an HPE module that builds hypergraphs from teacher ensemble outputs to compute plausibility scores, an SAP module that prunes unstable classes based on size variation across ensemble predictions, and a loss function combining supervised (source) and unsupervised (target) terms.  

**Critical path:** For each training batch: (1) Extract features from source and target images using the frozen DINOv3 encoder. (2) Apply HFM to source features to produce Fs and Fs→t, and to target features to produce Ft and Ft→s (and possibly cross variants). (3) Forward Fs through the student decoder to compute supervised loss L_sup. (4) Forward each modulated target feature set (e.g., Ft, Ft→s) through the teacher EMA decoder to obtain an ensemble of predictions. (5) Combine predictions into a consensus pseudo-label. (6) Run HPE on the consensus to compute plausibility score S_final; select samples that exceed the dynamic threshold ρ. (7) Run SAP on the selected predictions to compute class instability Υ(k) and mask anomalous classes. (8) Compute unsupervised loss L_unsup on refined pseudo-labels, weighted by pixel certainty (entropy + JSD). (9) Combine losses: L_total = L_sup + γ_unsup · L_unsup. (10) Backpropagate to update decoder and HFM parameters. (11) Update teacher decoder via EMA.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Frozen encoder vs. fine‑tuning: reduces compute but limits adaptability of low‑level features.  
- Hypergraph scoring uses batch statistics; requires sufficiently large batch sizes for stable estimates.  
- Dynamic thresholds (ρ for HPE, θA for SAP) balance precision/recall but need per‑task tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Poor feature alignment: t‑SNE shows mixed source‑target class centroids after HFM; Dice on source drops.  
- HPE/SAP thresholds mis‑specified: either no target samples selected or many implausible labels pass; monitor S_final distribution.  
- Training instability/collapse due to poor initial pseudo‑labels; monitor L_unsup and target Dice.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline sanity check: Train without adaptation (source only on target) to establish the domain gap.  
2. Replication of SHAPE full model on Cardiac MRI→CT; compare Dice against reported 90.08%.  
3. Ablation study: Disable each component (HFM, HPE, SAP) individually to verify contributions, following Tab. 3 setup.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** How transferable are SHAPE's carefully tuned hyperparameters (e.g., purity threshold τₚ, selection percentile ρ, anomaly threshold θₐ) to diverse medical imaging scenarios beyond the two tested datasets?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The hyperparameter sensitivity analysis (Fig. 5) reveals significant performance variations for parameters like α and θₐ across the four adaptation tasks, indicating task‑specific optima. The paper states these were manually set for the reported experiments.  
**Why unresolved:** The optimal values were determined empirically on a limited set of cardiac/abdominal, cross‑modality tasks. Their generalizability to other organs (e.g., brain, prostate), imaging modalities (e.g., ultrasound, PET), or different disease states (where anatomy may be pathological) is unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic evaluation across a broader, more heterogeneous collection of UDA benchmarks, investigating correlations between dataset characteristics (e.g., modality gap, anatomical variability) and optimal parameter ranges. This could inform the development of adaptive or automated parameter selection strategies.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** To what extent does SHAPE's performance depend on its specific choice of a DINOv3 foundation model, and would alternative self‑supervised pre‑trained backbones (e.g., MAE, SAM, CLIP) yield similar gains in feature fidelity and pseudo‑label quality?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method is explicitly "Built on a DINOv3 foundation" (Sec. 3.1, Abstract). The related work cites other foundation models, but no experimental comparison or ablation study investigates the impact of swapping the backbone encoder.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates SHAPE's efficacy but does not isolate the contribution of the DINOv3 prior versus the proposed HFM/HPE/SAP modules. The observed benefits might be synergistic with DINOv3's specific feature space, and other foundation models may require different modulation or validation strategies.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled study where the DINOv3 encoder is replaced with other state‑of‑the‑art self‑supervised ViTs (e.g., MAE, I‑JEPA), keeping all SHAPE modules identical. Performance would be compared across the same UDA tasks to measure backbone sensitivity and identify which pre‑training paradigms are most compatible with structure‑aware UDA.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Are the hand‑crafted shape and layout descriptors (isoperimetric ratio, centroid direction cosines) used in Hypergraph Plausibility Estimation (HPE) universally effective for scoring anatomical plausibility, or could learned or adaptive descriptors better capture structural anomalies across different organs?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] HPE defines S_ϕ,k and S_ψ,ij using fixed mathematical descriptors (Eq. 5 & 6). The paper argues hypergraphs capture holistic relations but does not justify why these specific scalar descriptors are optimal or explore alternatives.  
**Why unresolved:** The choice is motivated by geometric properties but is not validated against a ground truth of "plausible anatomy." These descriptors may not fully capture complex, organ‑specific topological constraints (e.g., branching in vasculature, curved surfaces) or be robust to all types of structural errors (e.g., disconnected components, topology violations).  
**What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study comparing the proposed descriptors with learned representations (e.g., a small network extracting features from the mask) or other medical image shape metrics (e.g., spherical harmonics, skeleton‑based measures). Correlation analysis with expert radiologist assessments of pseudo‑label plausibility on a held‑out set would provide direct validation.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Does the Structural Anomaly Pruning (SAP) instability score Υ(k), based on the coefficient of variation of class pixel counts across ensemble predictions, reliably identify *anatomically* anomalous class predictions, or does it primarily flag statistically unstable but structurally valid predictions?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] SAP defines Υ(k) = std(cₖ)/mean(cₖ) + ε (Eq. 8) and thresholds classes as anomalous based on batch statistics. The paper assumes high volatility implies a "spurious, hallucinated" class, but this is not explicitly verified against anatomical correctness.  
**Why unresolved:** A class could have a stable size (low Υ) but a completely wrong spatial location (e.g., liver pixel predicted in the chest), or a high Υ could arise from valid but highly variable boundaries (e.g., in ambiguous slices near organ borders). The metric is a proxy for stability, not a direct measure of anatomical error.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** An error analysis correlating Υ(k) values with specific, clinically relevant structural error types (e.g., shape distortion, wrong position, fragmentation). A controlled experiment where artificial noise is added to specific class regions of pseudo‑labels to measure if SAP consistently removes those classes, regardless of their stable size.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How effective is SHAPE's hypergraph‑based plausibility evaluation (HPE) and SAP pipeline for segmenting pathologically altered anatomies (e.g., organs with tumors, hypertrophy, or post‑surgical changes) where "plausible" structure deviates significantly from healthy priors?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] All experiments use datasets of healthy abdominal and cardiac organs (MMWHS, CHAOS, MICCAI 2015). The method's core premise is enforcing "global anatomical plausibility" based on expected spatial arrangements and shapes.  
**Why unresolved:** The learned plausibility scores (S_ϕ, S_ψ) are estimated from batch statistics during training. On pathological data, the batch distribution would include abnormal shapes/arrangements, potentially causing the model to deem valid pathologies as implausible (false pruning) or to accept severe errors as plausible (false retention). The framework assumes a static anatomical prior.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Testing SHAPE on publicly available datasets with annotated pathologies (e.g., liver tumors from LiTS, cardiac remodeling from ACDC). Key metrics would include: (1) performance drop compared to healthy cases, (2) analysis of pseudo‑label retention/pruning rates for tumor classes, and (3) qualitative inspection of whether HPE scores penalize or accept known pathological morphologies.

## Limitations
- HFM's purity threshold τₚ sensitivity and lack of ablation.
- HPE's batch‑statistic dependence untested on batches with atypical anatomies, risking biased plausibility scores.
- SAP's instability score dependency on ensemble diversity and potential for over‑pruning valid small structures.
