# MARCH: Multi-Agent Reinforced Self-Check for LLM Hallucination

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24579
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24579
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** MARCH reduces hallucination in RAG, raising Llama-8B factual accuracy by 19.73pp (55.20% → 75.23%) and surpassing GPT-4o on multi-hop QA (e.g., HotpotQA 71.2% vs 64.0%).

## Executive Summary
LLM hallucinations in retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) remain a critical reliability issue, exacerbated by confirmation bias when a single LLM both generates and verifies its output. MARCH introduces a multi‑agent reinforced self‑check framework that orchestrates three roles—Solver, Proposer, Checker—under a shared policy. The Solver generates a response; the Proposer decomposes it into claim‑level QA pairs; the Checker independently verifies each claim using only the retrieved documents, thereby breaking the self‑confirmation cycle. This information‑asymmetric pipeline is optimized with a zero‑tolerance reward: any mismatch between a claim and its verified answer penalizes the entire trajectory. Experiments show drastic hallucination reduction: an 8B Llama model’s average factual accuracy rises to 75.23%, and the system rivals or exceeds Gemini/GPT‑4o on several benchmarks.

## Method Summary
MARCH uses a single base policy \(\pi_\theta\) to play three roles via system prompts. For a given query \(x\) and retrieved documents \(D\):  
1. **Solver** generates a response \(y\) conditioned on \((x, D, s_{solve})\).  
2. **Proposer** decomposes \(y\) into propositional QA pairs \(\tau = \{(q_i, a_i)\}\), where \(a_i\) are answers extracted from \(y\).  
3. **Checker** re‑answers each \(q_i\) using only \(D\) (blinded to \(y\) and \(a_i\)), producing an audit \(\lambda = \{(\hat{a}_i)\}\).  
A **Zero‑Tolerance Reward** (ZTR) is computed: \(R = 0\) if all \(\hat{a}_i\) exactly match \(a_i\), otherwise \(R = -1\). Training employs multi‑agent PPO: both the Solver’s \(y\) and Checker’s \(\lambda\) trajectories are stored, advantages estimated via GAE, and the sum of their token‑level gradients (with KL regularization) updates \(\pi_\theta\). No ground‑truth answers are needed; training uses RAG datasets (BioASQ, 2WikiMultiHopQA, MuSiQue) with high irrelevant‑document ratios. Checkpoints are selected by average benchmark scores.

## Key Results
- On RAGTruth and FaithBench, Llama‑8B accuracy improves from 55.20% to 75.23% (+19.73pp).
- Facts Grounding: MARCH‑8B reaches 85.23% (STEM) and 80.12% (General), rivaling Gemini/GPT‑4o.
- On multi‑hop QA (HotpotQA, MuSiQue, 2WikiMHA), MARCH‑8B exceeds GPT‑4o RAG (e.g., HotpotQA 71.2% vs 64.0%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Information Asymmetry Breaks Confirmation Bias
- **Claim:** Information asymmetry between generation and verification breaks confirmation bias cycles.
- **Mechanism:** The Checker validates propositions against retrieved evidence in isolation, deprived of the Solver’s original output, forcing independent grounding rather than re‑validating the Solver’s narrative.
- **Core assumption:** Confirmation bias arises from simultaneous exposure to generated text and evidence; blinding the verifier prevents circular validation.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  • Abstract: “the Checker validates these propositions against retrieved evidence in isolation, deprived of the Solver’s original output. This well‑crafted information asymmetry scheme breaks the cycle of self‑confirmation bias.”  
  • Section 2.1: “the Checker … is strictly blinded to the Solver’s response … to avoid cognitive contamination.”  
  • Corpus: Neighbors maintain symmetric access; MARCH’s explicit blinding is unique.

### Mechanism 2: Zero‑Tolerance Reward Enforces Factual Alignment
- **Claim:** A binary, all‑or‑nothing reward forces strict factual alignment by penalizing any single hallucination.
- **Mechanism:** ZTR assigns \(R = 0\) only if every claim exactly matches its verification; else \(R = -1\). This prevents optimization for partial correctness and demands every claim be grounded.
- **Core assumption:** In data‑intensive RAG, a single unsupported claim invalidates the entire response’s utility.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  • Section 2.3: “we define a binary Zero‑Tolerance Reward function … a trajectory is assigned a successful score only if every claim … achieves an identity match.”  
  • Section 3.4, Table 6: ZTR (\(-1/0\)) outperforms proportional error‑rate reward.  
  • Corpus: Weak direct evidence; neighboring works use soft rewards.

### Mechanism 3: Joint PPO Training Co‑Evolves Generator and Auditor
- **Claim:** Joint optimization of Solver and Checker trajectories under a shared policy aligns generative and evaluative capabilities.
- **Mechanism:** The policy \(\pi_\theta\) generates both response \(y\) and audit \(\lambda\). Advantages are estimated separately, gradients are summed, and a KL penalty keeps \(\pi_\theta\) close to a reference policy. This allows the model to internalize verification logic while generating.
- **Core assumption:** A single policy can play functionally distinct roles if conditioned appropriately; optimizing both simultaneously aligns their internal representations.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  • Section 2.2: “MARCH defines a unified asymmetric cooperative … objective … the policy \(\pi_\theta\) concurrently refines its narrative generation and evidentiary precision.”  
  • Section 2.3, Eq. 8: joint gradient summation over \(y\) and \(\lambda\).  
  • Section 3.4, Table 5: joint (Solver+Checker) update consistently beats Solver‑only.

## Foundational Learning

### Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
- **Why needed here:** MARCH uses PPO with a binary, automatically computable reward (claim match) instead of human feedback or external reward models. Understanding RLVR’s sample efficiency and granularity challenges is key.
- **Quick check question:** Why might outcome‑level RL rewards (e.g., based on final answer correctness) be insufficient for mitigating intermediate hallucinations in RAG?

### Retrieval‑Augmented Generation (RAG)
- **Why needed here:** The problem setting is RAG; hallucinations manifest as context‑conflicting claims. Prerequisite: knowing how retrieved documents are integrated and why noise (irrelevant docs) is prevalent (Table 1).
- **Quick check question:** How does the high irrelevant‑document ratio in MuSiQue (88.35%) make factual grounding harder than in a clean QA setting?

### Multi‑Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) with Role Specialization
- **Why needed here:** MARCH instantiates three roles from one policy but treats them as cooperative agents. Need to understand decentralized execution, credit assignment, and shared vs. separate policies.
- **Quick check question:** What are the trade‑offs between using a single shared policy (MARCH) versus three separate fine‑tuned models for Solver/Proposer/Checker?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Base Policy \(\pi_\theta\) powers Solver, Proposer, and Checker. Pipeline: Solver generates response \(y\) from \((x, D)\); Proposer decomposes \(y\) into QA pairs \(\tau = \{(q_i, a_i)\}\); Checker answers \(\{q_i\}\) using only \(D\) to produce audit \(\lambda\); Reward Calculator compares \(a_i\) and \(\hat{a}_i\) to assign ZTR; PPO Trainer aggregates both \(y\) and \(\lambda\) trajectories and updates \(\pi_\theta\).

**Critical path:**  
1. Rollout: For each \((x, D)\), sample \(y \sim \pi_\theta(x, D, s_{solve})\); sample \(\tau \sim \pi_\theta(y, s_{propose})\) and parse QA pairs; sample \(\lambda \sim \pi_\theta(\{q_i\}, D, s_{check})\) and parse \(\hat{a}_i\); compute \(R\).  
2. Storage: Store \((y, R)\) and \((\lambda, R)\) as two trajectories in batch.  
3. Update: For each token, estimate advantage with GAE, compute PPO loss + KL, backpropagate sum of both losses.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **ZTR vs. ERR:** ZTR is stricter but risks sparse gradients; ERR provides gradient but may tolerate some hallucinations.  
- **Shared vs. Separate Policies:** Shared policy enables co‑adaptation but risks role interference; separate policies avoid interference but lose mutual reinforcement.  
- **Question Count:** Unconstrained Proposer may reduce question count to minimize penalty exposure; mitigation: add a minimum‑question constraint in \(s_{propose}\).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Checker laziness: “Cannot answer” outputs for many questions → artificially high reward but low informativeness.  
- Question shrinkage: steady decline in average number of QA pairs over training steps.  
- Gradient conflict: joint loss increases while Solver and Checker accuracies diverge.  
- Over‑penalization: early training yields near‑zero rewards → vanishing gradients.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablate joint vs. solver‑only:** Train with only Solver trajectory gradient vs. both trajectories; expect joint to improve factual accuracy more (Table 5).  
2. **Test ZTR robustness:** Compare ZTR (\(-1/0\)) vs. incentive (\(0/1\)) vs. error‑rate reward (ERR); hypothesis: \(-1/0\) yields strongest factual grounding (Table 6).  
3. **Measure question density over steps:** Plot average number of Proposer‑generated questions per training step; expect decline without constraints, stability with minimum‑question prompt (Fig 3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does MARCH's factual accuracy scale with increasing proportions of irrelevant documents in the retrieval corpus beyond the 88% tested?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) The training datasets contain high irrelevant document ratios (up to 88.35% in MuSiQue), but evaluation lacks a controlled study on noise sensitivity.
- **Why unresolved:** Real‑world RAG systems encounter varying retrieval quality; knowing the upper noise tolerance limit is essential for reliability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary irrelevant document percentages in validation sets and measure factuality metrics to identify the breaking point of the framework.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the claim decomposition and verification process be generalized to non‑numerical factual assertions (e.g., causal relationships, events) while maintaining zero‑tolerance reward effectiveness?
- **Basis in paper:** (explicit) The implementation “specifically prioritizes numerical and quantitative verification” (Section 2.3), limiting scope.
- **Why unresolved:** General RAG tasks involve diverse claim types; the current design may not capture broader factuality without performance loss.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend prompts to extract all factual propositions, adapt the reward to use entailment checks, and evaluate on mixed‑type benchmarks like Facts Grounding.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the shared policy architecture for Solver, Proposer, and Checker hinder potential gains from heterogeneous or specialized agent designs?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) MARCH uses a single policy \(\pi_\theta\) for all roles; no ablation explores separate policies or model sizes.
- **Why unresolved:** Different roles have distinct epistemic needs; parameter sharing may cause interference or underfitting.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train independent policies (or with different model scales) for each agent, compare factuality and training efficiency against the shared policy baseline.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How robust is MARCH against adversarial retrieval documents that introduce subtle factual inconsistencies?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) The framework assumes retrieved documents are generally accurate; no adversarial evaluation is conducted.
- **Why unresolved:** Adversarial or low‑quality retrievals are common in practice; blind verification may fail on poisoned contexts.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Craft adversarial document modifications (e.g., altered numbers, negated facts) and measure factuality drop rates compared to clean retrieval.

## Limitations
- Parsing rules for claim extraction and answer parsing are underspecified, risking inconsistent implementations.
- The number of Checker resamples per question is unreported, leading to uncertainty in reward reliability.
- The sparse binary reward may cause early gradient starvation; Proposer may shrink question count to game the reward; Checker may become lazy.
- Information leakage could compromise the designed asymmetry if prompts inadvertently include the Solver’s response.

## Confidence
- Hallucination reduction across benchmarks: **High**
- Information asymmetry mechanism: **Medium**
- Zero‑tolerance reward superiority: **High**
- Joint PPO training synergy: **High**
- Cross‑model generalization (to Qwen3‑8B): **Medium**
