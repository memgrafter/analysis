# ScaleBITS: Scalable Bitwidth Search for Hardware-Aligned Mixed-Precision LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17698
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17698
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ScaleBITS achieves up to 36% improvement over uniform quantization and outperforms sensitivity-aware baselines by up to 13% in the 2–3 bit regime with zero runtime overhead, while reducing quantization time 3–6×.

## Executive Summary
ScaleBITS introduces a scalable framework for fine-grained mixed-precision quantization of large language models (LLMs) under strict memory constraints. It combines progressive sensitivity analysis using gradients from quantized models, a bi-directional channel reordering scheme to cluster sensitive weights, and a scalable greedy algorithm for global bitwidth allocation. This approach yields significant accuracy gains at ultra-low bitwidths (e.g., 2.1 bits on Llama3-70B) while maintaining hardware efficiency, and provides a continuous accuracy–compression trade-off curve.

## Method Summary
ScaleBITS automates mixed-precision weight quantization through three core steps. First, it estimates weight sensitivity not from the full-precision model but from progressively quantized checkpoints, using first-order Taylor approximations to capture marginal loss impact under further quantization. Second, it applies bi-directional channel reordering to weight matrices: output and input channels are independently sorted by aggregated sensitivity, clustering high-sensitivity weights into contiguous top-left blocks that align with hardware GEMM tiles. Third, it solves the global bitwidth allocation problem via a scalable greedy search that uses the sensitivity estimates to rank blocks for precision increases/decreases, applies batched updates with an acceptance criterion to control loss, and gradually reduces batch size for convergence. The method starts from a warm state (⌊B⌋ bits per block) and stops when the batch size falls below a threshold, yielding a Pareto-optimal accuracy-compression curve.

## Key Results
- On Llama3-70B at 2.1 bits, zero-shot accuracy increases from 35.61% to 64.72% and MMLU from 23.02% to 58.82%.
- Quantization time is reduced 3–6× versus prior mixed-precision methods, with zero runtime overhead during inference.
- The approach achieves a continuous accuracy–compression trade-off curve and improves over uniform quantization by up to 36% and over sensitivity-aware baselines by up to 13% in the 2–3 bit regime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Progressive Sensitivity Estimation
- **Claim:** Weight sensitivity is dynamic; estimating it from progressively quantized models better predicts true loss impact under low-bit quantization.
- **Mechanism:** Gradients are computed on the current quantized model during greedy search. The first-order term `|g(wQ)^T * Δw|` serves as sensitivity proxy, where `g(wQ)` is evaluated on quantized weights.
- **Core assumption:** The local loss landscape around the quantized model more accurately reflects marginal precision changes than the full-precision landscape.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "guided by a new sensitivity analysis... progressively quantized models"; [section 3.1] "we propose to estimate sensitivity with respect to a quantized reference point"; [section 3.1, Figure 3] quantized-model gradients correctly rank layer sensitivity while full-precision gradients fail.
- **Break condition:** In extremely low-precision regimes (e.g., widespread 1-bit/0-bit), activations may collapse and gradients become unstable, invalidating the linear approximation.

### Mechanism 2: Bi‑directional Channel Reordering
- **Claim:** Sensitive weights exhibit a bidirectional (row-wise and column-wise) clustering pattern; reordering aligns them into contiguous top-left regions, making coarse hardware-aligned blocks expressive enough for mixed-precision.
- **Mechanism:** For each weight matrix, output channels (rows) and input channels (columns) are independently reordered by aggregating element-wise sensitivities (L1 norm) along each channel. Blocks formed from this reordered matrix naturally capture the dense region of sensitive weights.
- **Core assumption:** The spatial sensitivity pattern `sij ≈ |g(y)_i| * |x_j| * |ΔW_ij|` implies sensitive elements concentrate along a subset of both row and column channels.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "bi-directional channel reordering scheme to cluster sensitive weights"; [section 3.2] derivation of `sij` factorization and Figure 2 showing concentration; [section 4.1] "After reordering, sensitive weights concentrate toward the top-left region".
- **Break condition:** If a layer has uniformly distributed sensitivity or architectural constraints (e.g., RoPE in QK projections) prevent full bidirectional reordering, the scheme loses effectiveness.

### Mechanism 3: Scalable Greedy Search
- **Claim:** The classic greedy mixed-precision allocation is made scalable via first-order sensitivity approximations and batched updates, reducing search complexity from O(N²) to O(N) iterations.
- **Mechanism:** At each iteration, sensitivity scores (computed from current quantized gradients) rank marginal gains. Instead of one block per step, a batch of `k` most-sensitive blocks are increased (and optionally `k/2` least-sensitive decreased). An acceptance check on a calibration batch rejects noisy updates and halves `k` if loss increases.
- **Core assumption:** The relative ordering of marginal gains is preserved by the first-order estimate `s_up,i ≈ g_i^T * (w_i - w_i^Q)` and `s_down,i ≈ ϵ_{b_i} * ||g_i ⊙ w_i^Q||₁`; batched updates converge under diminishing returns.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "scalable approximation to the greedy algorithm"; [section 4.2] "greedy decisions are driven primarily by the relative ordering... approximated using a lightweight sensitivity estimate"; [section 5.3, Table 3] quantization time reduced 3–6× vs. SlimLLM; search iterations 16–36 vs. ~3e6 for classic greedy.
- **Break condition:** Highly noisy sensitivity estimates (e.g., small calibration set) cause frequent loss increases and premature termination; warm-start (`b(0)=⌊B⌋`) is needed to avoid unstable regimes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Post‑Training Quantization (PTQ)**
  - *Why needed:* ScaleBITS is a PTQ method; understanding that quantization is applied after pre-training (no retraining) explains constraints like reliance on calibration data and avoidance of training-time adaptation.
  - *Quick check:* What is the key trade‑off in PTQ vs. Quantization‑Aware Training (QAT) regarding accuracy and computational cost?

- **Submodular Optimization and Greedy Approximation**
  - *Why needed:* The bitwidth allocation problem (Eq. 1) is framed as maximizing a monotone DR‑submodular function under a knapsack constraint; the greedy algorithm’s (1−1/e) approximation guarantee justifies the search strategy.
  - *Quick check:* What does the “diminishing returns” property imply for adding precision bits to a quantized LLM?

- **Hardware‑Aligned Partitioning**
  - *Why needed:* To avoid runtime overhead, precision must be assigned at a granularity that matches fixed tile sizes of matrix multiplication kernels (e.g., 64×64 blocks). Unstructured per‑element mixed‑precision breaks hardware efficiency.
  - *Quick check:* Why would per‑weight mixed‑precision typically be infeasible for production LLM inference despite potentially higher accuracy?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Calibration & Sensitivity Estimator → Bi‑directional Channel Reorderer → Block Partitioner → Scalable Greedy Searcher

**Critical path:**
1. Load full‑precision checkpoint.
2. Apply bi‑directional reordering to all linear layers (respecting architectural constraints).
3. Partition weights into fixed‑size blocks (e.g., 64×128).
4. Initialize all blocks to ⌊B⌋ bits.
5. Run scalable greedy loop until `k` drops below γ_T · N.
6. Save block‑wise bitwidths and reordered weights.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Block size:** Must match target hardware’s GEMM tile size; smaller blocks increase expressivity but increase group quantization overhead.
- **Search space B:** Higher max bits (e.g., 8) lets critical weights get more precision but increases search time; lower min bits (0/1) allows aggressive compression but risks unstable gradients.
- **Batch size γ:** Larger γ speeds convergence but may introduce noise; smaller γ is precise but slower.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Noisy sensitivity estimates → oscillation:** Loss does not steadily improve; increase calibration batch size or raise γ_T.
- **Poor accuracy without proper reordering:** Visualize sensitivity heatmap; top‑left region should contain high‑sensitivity elements.
- **Under‑utilized or overflowed budget:** Final average bits deviate significantly from B; adjust γ₀ or acceptance threshold.

**First experiments:**
1. **Sensitivity validation:** Compute per‑layer sensitivity using quantized‑model gradients and correlate with true loss change from layer‑wise ablation; expect Spearman ρ > 0.8.
2. **Reordering ablation:** Quantize with/without bi‑directional reordering at same average bits; expect >5% absolute drop in MMLU without reordering.
3. **Scalability stress test:** Implement classic greedy and scalable greedy on Llama3‑8B; compare wall‑clock time and accuracy while varying calibration batch size; expect ≤2% accuracy difference and 3–6× speedup.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How can ScaleBITS be extended to jointly optimize weight and activation precisions under a unified memory/compute budget?
  - **Basis:** Explicitly stated in the conclusion.
  - **Why unresolved:** Current method only considers weight‑only quantization; activations are dynamic and require separate sensitivity analysis and hardware constraints.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop activation sensitivity metric, integrate with activation quantizers, and show improved end‑to‑end accuracy‑latency trade‑offs.

- **Question:** Can incorporating lightweight fine‑tuning or training‑time adaptation improve robustness at extreme compression (<2 bits)?
  - **Basis:** Explicitly stated in the conclusion.
  - **Why unresolved:** Pure PTQ may lose accuracy severely at sub‑2‑bit budgets; fine‑tuning could recover performance but adds cost.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments with few‑step adaptation (e.g., QAT, LoRA) after quantization, measuring zero‑shot accuracy and training overhead at very low bits.

- **Question:** Does the scalable greedy approximation retain any formal approximation guarantee (e.g., (1−1/e)) compared to the classic greedy algorithm?
  - **Basis:** Inferred; formulation invokes submodular maximization but batched updates deviate without analysis.
  - **Why unresolved:** Without theoretical backing, algorithm optimality and worst‑case behavior are unclear; empirical success might not generalize.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Prove constant‑factor guarantee under monotonicity and submodularity, or provide counterexamples; optionally evaluate approximation ratios on synthetic problems.

## Limitations
- Progressive sensitivity estimation becomes unstable under extreme low‑bit regimes (<2 bits), where gradients may diverge.
- Bi‑directional reordering assumes clear channel‑wise clustering of sensitivities; this may not hold for all layers or architectures, limiting its benefit.
- The scalable greedy algorithm relies on low‑noise sensitivity estimates; with small calibration sets, batched updates may frequently reject changes, leading to premature termination or suboptimal allocation.

## Confidence
- **Progressive sensitivity estimation:** High for moderate bitwidths (2–4 bits) due to strong empirical validation; Medium for sub‑2‑bit where gradients may diverge.
- **Bi‑directional reordering:** Medium; clear gains shown but effectiveness is architecture‑dependent and could vanish with uniformly distributed sensitivity.
- **Scalable greedy search:** Medium; speedups demonstrated, yet first‑order approximation and batched updates might degrade allocation under noisy gradients.
