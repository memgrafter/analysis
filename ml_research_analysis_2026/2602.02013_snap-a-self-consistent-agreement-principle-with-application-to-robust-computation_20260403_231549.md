# SNAP: A Self-Consistent Agreement Principle with Application to Robust Computation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02013
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02013
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SNAP is a parameter-free method for robust computation that weights data points based on mutual agreement, significantly outperforming traditional robust estimators (e.g., Weiszfeld, median-of-means) in vector averaging and PCA with up to 40% outliers.

## Executive Summary
SNAP (Self-consistent Agreement Principle) introduces a novel approach to robust computation by weighting data points according to their mutual agreement with the rest of the dataset. The method computes normalized disagreement scores from pairwise distances, applies a decreasing kernel (Laplacian or Gaussian) with bandwidth set by robust scale estimators (MAD or median), and normalizes to produce weights. Outliers receive exponentially decaying weights, making their influence negligible under mild assumptions. Experiments on synthetic data demonstrate SNAP's effectiveness: for vector averaging in R^2 and R^50, SNAP with a Gaussian kernel achieves lower mean absolute error than iterative Weiszfeld and multivariate median-of-means; for robust PCA, it reduces angle error by up to an order of magnitude even with 40% outliers. The method is parameter-free and scales quadratically with sample size, remaining practical for moderate datasets.

## Method Summary
SNAP operates in two stages: weight computation and task-specific aggregation. Given a dataset of n points, it first computes all pairwise distances (using a problem-specific metric). For each point, the row sum of distances to all other points is calculated, and normalized by the total sum of all row sums to obtain a normalized disagreement score Δ_i. The minimum Δ is subtracted to center the scores, yielding Δ*_i. A robust scale estimator (MAD or median) is applied to Δ* to determine the kernel bandwidth. Then a decreasing kernel (Laplacian: exp(-Δ*/b) or Gaussian: exp(-(Δ*)^2/(2σ^2))) is evaluated, and weights are normalized to sum to one. These weights are subsequently used in a weighted computation, such as a weighted mean for squared-loss tasks or a weighted geometric median for absolute-loss tasks, or a weighted covariance for PCA. The entire process requires no parameter tuning beyond the choice of kernel and robust estimator.

## Key Results
- SNAP (Gaussian kernel) achieves lower mean absolute error than iterative Weiszfeld and multivariate median-of-means in vector averaging experiments across R^2 and R^50 with 10%-30% outliers.
- For principal component analysis with up to 40% outliers, SNAP reduces the angle error of the estimated principal component by up to an order of magnitude compared to standard PCA and maintains low projection error.
- The method is parameter-free, with bandwidths automatically set via MAD or median on normalized disagreements, and its runtime scales quadratically with the number of samples, remaining feasible for moderate-sized datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Normalized disagreement scores reflect mutual agreement
- **Claim:** SNAP weights quantitatively reflect mutual agreement via normalized pairwise distances relative to the dataset, following the Agreement–Reliability Hypothesis (ARH).
- **Mechanism:** For each point o_i, compute a normalized disagreement score Δ_i = D_i / D_a where D_i = Σ_{j≠i} d(o_i, o_j) and D_a = Σ_{p=1}^n D_p. Apply a decreasing kernel κ to these scores and normalize: w_i = κ(Δ_i) / Σ_j κ(Δ_j). This ensures weights depend on relative disagreement.
- **Core assumption:** Inliers form a concentrated cluster, while outliers are dispersed and do not form a competing cluster (ARH).
- **Evidence:** Formal definition in Section IV-A; empirical results show inliers receive high weights.
- **Break condition:** If outliers form a tight cluster that agrees internally but disagrees with inliers (violating ARH dispersion), SNAP may incorrectly upweight the outlier cluster.

### Mechanism 2: Exponential decay of outlier weights
- **Claim:** Outlier weights decay exponentially (or super-exponentially) with sample size n, making their contribution negligible.
- **Mechanism:** Under the Linear Disagreement Gap Assumption (IV.10), the cumulative disagreement difference between an outlier and an inlier is Ω(n). This gap is translated by the exponential or Gaussian kernel into exp(-Ω(n)) or exp(-Ω(n^2)) suppression (Theorem IV.11).
- **Core assumption:** There exists constant c_0 > 0 such that for every inlier i and outlier k: Σ_j d(o_k, o_j) - Σ_j d(o_i, o_j) ≥ c_0 n. This requires outliers to be consistently far from inliers.
- **Evidence:** Theorem IV.11 provides explicit bounds: w_k ≤ (1/(αn)) exp(-n/(σ c_1)) for Laplacian, super-exponential for Gaussian.
- **Break condition:** The linear gap assumption fails if outliers are not uniformly far from inliers, weakening suppression.

### Mechanism 3: Parameter-free bandwidth via robust scale estimation
- **Claim:** SNAP is parameter-free; bandwidths are set via robust scale estimators (MAD or median) applied to normalized disagreement scores Δ*.
- **Mechanism:** Compute Δ_sm = min_i Δ_i. Then Δ*_i = Δ_i - Δ_sm. Use half-distribution robust estimators: b_MAD = 1.4427·MAD(Δ*), σ_MAD = 2.2631·MAD(Δ*) or alternatives based on median. This adapts the kernel width to the dataset's internal scale without manual tuning.
- **Core assumption:** The distribution of Δ* for inliers is concentrated around zero, with outliers producing large Δ*. Robust estimators (MAD/median) resist contamination from these outliers.
- **Evidence:** Section IV-B gives explicit formulas; Section IV-F notes no cross-validation needed.
- **Break condition:** If inlier Δ* distribution is heavy-tailed or multimodal, MAD/median may misestimate scale, leading to poor weight separation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Normalized disagreement scores (Δ_i)**
  - Why needed here: This is the core innovation that makes SNAP scale-invariant and globally informed. Absolute distances are meaningless without context; normalization relative to D_a allows outliers to "stick out" consistently regardless of absolute magnitude.
  - Quick check question: Why would using Δ_i = D_i / D_a instead of raw D_i prevent a dataset with larger overall distances from producing different weights?
- **Concept: Kernel density interpretation of weights**
  - Why needed here: Theorem VI.1 shows that for Gaussian data and ℓ_2^2 loss, SNAP weights with Laplacian kernel exactly reproduce the true mean. This links the weighting scheme to a density estimation perspective: w_i ∝ κ(Δ_i) acts like a data density estimate centered on inliers.
  - Quick check question: In Theorem VI.1's proof, why does the Laplacian kernel κ_l(Δ_x) become proportional to a Gaussian density N(x; μ, aI_m)?
- **Concept: Non-monotonic weight evolution under point motion**
  - Why needed here: Proposition IV.5 shows weights depend on global configuration, not just pairwise distances. This explains why moving a point closer to one entity can decrease its weight if it moves away from the bulk. It distinguishes SNAP from local kernel methods.
  - Quick check question: In the three-point example (Figure 2), why does w(x_2(t)) first increase then decrease as x_2 moves toward x_3?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Distance matrix computation -> Row sums & total sum -> Normalized disagreement -> Set median -> Centered scores -> Robust scale estimation -> Kernel application -> Weight normalization -> Task-specific weighted computation
- **Critical path:** Steps 2–8 constitute the SNAP weighting module. This must be completed before any weighted computation. The quadratic distance computation (Step 1) is the main bottleneck.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Kernel choice: Laplacian yields exponential outlier suppression e^{-cn}; Gaussian yields super-exponential e^{-cn^2} but may be more sensitive to scale estimation.
  - Bandwidth estimator: MAD-based vs. median-based. MAD is more robust to extreme outliers in Δ*; median may be more efficient if inlier Δ* is symmetric.
  - Complexity vs. robustness: Quadratic O(n^2) weighting is feasible for moderate n; for large n, approximate methods (e.g., nearest neighbor graphs) can reduce cost but may degrade theoretical guarantees.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. Outlier cluster formation: If outliers form a tight cluster, their mutual agreement may lead to higher Δ_i for inliers, causing mis-weighting.
  2. High-dimensional collapse: In very high dimensions, all pairwise distances concentrate, reducing discrimination. SNAP relies on relative ordering preservation, which may degrade.
  3. Extreme contamination: If outlier fraction > 50%, the ARH majority assumption fails; inliers become minority, and weights may flip.
  4. Non-differentiable distances: If d(·,·) is not smooth, gradient-based Jacobian analysis (Proposition IV.6) does not apply, but weights still computed.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Vector averaging in R^2: Generate 200 points: 70% inliers from N(0, I_2), 30% outliers uniform in [0,10]^2. Compute SNAP weights (κ_g with σ_MAD), weighted mean, compare MAE to Weiszfeld and inlier mean. Expect SNAP to outperform both.
  2. PCA with 20% outliers: Generate 500 points in R^2: inliers N(0, Σ) with Σ=[[2,1],[1,1]], outliers N([3,8], 2·I_2). Compute SNAP-weighted covariance with κ_g and σ_MAD. Measure angle error vs. inlier PCA. Expect ~1° error vs. PCA's 33°.
  3. Moving average smoothing: Generate time series x_t = sin(t) + ε_t, with 10% of points replaced by large Gaussian noise (σ=0.8). Apply SNAP moving average with window size s = 2/α -1, α=0.2, κ_l with b_MAD. Compare RMSE to EMA. Expect SNAP to lower RMSE.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling to large datasets**: SNAP's O(n^2) complexity limits its use on very large datasets. The paper explicitly notes this as an important task. Resolution would require efficient approximation techniques (e.g., subsampling, nearest neighbor graphs, sketching) with theoretical guarantees and empirical validation showing comparable accuracy at lower computational cost.
2. **Task-dependent weighting**: The current SNAP uses task-independent weights. Extending to task-dependent schemes, especially for function fitting, could improve performance. This is unresolved because the weighting does not incorporate the loss function's structure. Evidence for resolution would include a framework for task-dependent weights, theoretical properties (consistency, robustness), and experiments on robust regression or classification showing advantages over the baseline.
3. **Application to discrete domains**: Although SNAP is defined for continuous spaces, extending to discrete domains (e.g., rankings, graphs) is left for future work. Challenges include defining appropriate distance measures and kernel parameter estimation for discrete data, and ensuring theoretical guarantees hold. Resolution would involve defining SNAP for specific discrete tasks, implementation, and comparison with state-of-the-art discrete robust methods, showing both effectiveness and retention of robustness properties.

## Limitations
- The Agreement–Reliability Hypothesis (ARH) that inliers form a concentrated cluster and outliers are dispersed may fail if outliers form a tight cluster or if there are multiple inlier clusters.
- The theoretical exponential outlier decay relies on the Linear Disagreement Gap Assumption, which may not hold if some outliers are close to inliers.
- Quadratic computational complexity restricts SNAP's application to moderate dataset sizes; scaling to large n requires approximate methods not yet evaluated.
