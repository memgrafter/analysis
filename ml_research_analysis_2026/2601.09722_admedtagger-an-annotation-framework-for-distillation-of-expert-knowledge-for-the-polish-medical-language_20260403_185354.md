# ADMEDTAGGER: an annotation framework for distillation of expert knowledge for the Polish medical language

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.09722
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09722
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** DistilBERT, trained on LLM-generated pseudo-labels, exceeds F1 0.80 for all five clinical categories (Cardiology, Hypertension, Radiology, Pathology, Oncology) and surpasses 0.93 for three, while being ~500× smaller and several hundred times faster than the teacher LLM.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the scarcity of labeled data for multi-class classification of Polish medical texts. It introduces ADMEDTAGGER, a framework that uses a large multilingual LLM (Llama3.1-70B) as a teacher to auto-annotate a large unlabeled corpus via few-shot prompting. A small, physician-validated subset forms a gold test set. Three BERT-based student models are then fine-tuned on the pseudo-labeled data. The key finding is that the multilingual DistilBERT student achieves high performance (F1 >0.80 on all five categories), offering a compact, efficient model suitable for clinical deployment compared to the massive teacher.

## Method Summary
1.  **Data:** An unlabeled corpus of 21,073 Polish medical texts across five clinical categories is assembled. Physicians manually label a small, stratified subset to serve as a final test set and to provide 2–3 in-context examples per category.
2.  **Teacher Annotation:** For each training text, a few-shot prompt (system message with tag list, in-context examples, target text) is sent to Llama3.1-70B-Instruct. The model outputs annotated text fragments, which are parsed into pseudo-labels.
3.  **Student Training:** DistilBERT (multilingual), BioBERT (English biomedical), and HerBERT (Polish) are fine-tuned on the full pseudo-labeled corpus. Training uses cross-entropy loss with class weighting to handle imbalance, for up to 100 epochs.
4.  **Evaluation:** Student models are assessed on the physician-validated test set using macro F1, AUROC, and accuracy with 95% confidence intervals.

## Key Results
*   DistilBERT achieves macro F1 scores exceeding 0.80 for all five clinical categories and surpasses 0.93 for Radiology, Oncology, and Hypertension.
*   Compared to the teacher Llama3.1-70B, the distilled DistilBERT model is approximately 500 times smaller, consumes 300 times less GPU VRAM, and operates several hundred times faster.
*   DistilBERT consistently outperforms both BioBERT and HerBERT, suggesting the multilingual pretraining corpus provided a unique advantage for this Polish medical task.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: LLM Teacher Generates Useful Pseudo-Labels
- **Claim:** A multilingual LLM can generate useful pseudo-labels for Polish medical text classification via few-shot prompting.
- **Mechanism:** The teacher model is given a system message defining the clinical specialty and label set, along with 2–3 in-context examples of text with correct annotations. This transfers the model's latent knowledge to the labeling task.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM's pretraining included sufficient exposure to Polish medical language to make accurate predictions from minimal examples.
- **Break condition:** If physician-validated test set shows low agreement with teacher labels on the validated subset, distillation may propagate significant errors.

### Mechanism 2: Students Learn from Noisy Pseudo-Labels
- **Claim:** Student BERT models can learn effectively from teacher-generated pseudo-labels through standard supervised fine-tuning.
- **Mechanism:** The pseudo-labeled corpus is treated as a standard supervised dataset. Models are trained with cross-entropy loss and class weighting to handle imbalance.
- **Core assumption:** The teacher pseudo-labels, despite noise, represent a sufficiently consistent mapping approximating the true classification function.
- **Break condition:** If student performance on the test set is near random or significantly lower than the teacher's (unreported) performance, it indicates poor knowledge transfer.

### Mechanism 3: Multilingual Pretraining Provides Domain-Ready Features
- **Claim:** Multilingual pretraining on a corpus including Polish Wikipedia gives DistilBERT an advantage over Polish-only (HerBERT) or English medical (BioBERT) pretrained models.
- **Mechanism:** The multilingual model's pretraining exposed it to Polish-language text, including medical content from Wikipedia, providing better linguistic and initial domain feature representations for fine-tuning.
- **Core assumption:** The multilingual pretraining corpus contained meaningful Polish medical content.
- **Break condition:** If no Polish medical text was present in the pretraining data, the advantage should disappear, and HerBERT might match performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Knowledge Distillation**
  - *Why needed:* Core method for compressing a large, high-capacity teacher model into a small, efficient student model suitable for deployment.
  - *Quick check:* What is the typical role of temperature in softening the teacher's output distribution to provide richer training signals?
- **Concept: Few-shot Learning with LLMs**
  - *Why needed:* The teacher annotation relies on in-context examples to adapt the generalist LLM to the specialized medical classification task without weight updates.
  - *Quick check:* How might the selection and ordering of in-context examples affect the consistency and bias of teacher annotations?
- **Concept: Class Imbalance Handling**
  - *Why needed:* Medical text datasets are often imbalanced (e.g., fewer Pathology cases); class weighting in the loss function prevents the model from being biased toward majority classes.
  - *Quick check:* How does assigning a higher weight to the loss of a minority class affect the gradient updates during training?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Data collection -> Teacher annotation (Llama3.1-70B + prompts) -> Physician validation (test set creation) -> Student training (DistilBERT/BioBERT/HerBERT) -> Evaluation (F1, AUROC, Accuracy)
- **Critical path:**
  1.  Collect raw Polish medical texts and define the label schema for five clinical categories.
  2.  Physicians manually annotate a small, representative sample per category to create in-context examples and a held-out test set.
  3.  Run the teacher LLM on the full training corpus using the few-shot prompts to generate pseudo-labels.
  4.  Train each student model on the pseudo-labeled data, using class weights and a standard fine-tuning procedure.
  5.  Evaluate all students on the physician-validated test set and analyze per-category performance.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Teacher size vs. annotation cost:** A 70B parameter teacher likely yields higher-quality pseudo-labels but requires significant compute; a smaller LLM could reduce costs but risk more noise.
  - **Pseudo-label coverage:** Annotating the entire corpus maximizes training data but includes more low-confidence labels; a high-confidence threshold would reduce noise at the cost of data volume.
  - **Student capacity vs. efficiency:** DistilBERT's success suggests minimal capacity suffices if pretraining aligns with the domain, favoring deployment efficiency.
  - **Validation budget:** Limited physician time forces a small test set, which may yield less reliable performance estimates, especially for rare labels.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor performance on rare labels (e.g., "OTHER", "MARGINS") suggests the teacher model struggles with ambiguous or infrequent cases.
  - Large confidence intervals on metrics indicate training instability, insufficient data, or an unreliable test set.
  - Similar performance across all student models (as seen in Oncology) might indicate the task is too trivial or the label set is not discriminative.
  - A large gap between validation performance (on pseudo-labels) and test performance (on expert labels) indicates unreliable teacher annotations.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Measure teacher accuracy:** Have physicians label an independent sample from the training corpus. Compute agreement (F1) between Llama3.1-70B's pseudo-labels and these new expert labels to gauge pseudo-label noise.
  2.  **Student ablation:** Compare DistilBERT trained on the full pseudo-labeled corpus versus trained only on the small, physician-validated subset (held-out from training). This isolates the benefit of scaling via pseudo-labels.
  3.  **Prompt variation:** For one category, systematically vary prompt components (number of in-context examples, example selection strategy, output format) and measure the variance in teacher annotation consistency (e.g., Cohen's kappa) on a fixed text subset.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can an LLM effectively distill expert knowledge for a specialized domain using only a few textual examples?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated as RQ1 in the introduction.
- **Why unresolved:** The study validates this only for Llama3.1-70B with a specific prompt and few-shot setup. Generalizability to other LLMs, prompt designs, or different expert knowledge structures is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study varying the teacher LLM (e.g., GPT-4, Mixtral), number/selection of in-context examples, and application to other low-resource specialized domains beyond Polish medicine.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can an LLM successfully perform knowledge distillation for a morphologically complex, low-resource language like Polish?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated as RQ2 in the introduction.
- **Why unresolved:** Success relied on a multilingual LLM. The separate contributions of Polish-language specificity versus general multilingual capability are not isolated. Performance of a monolingual Polish LLM or an English-centric model is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct comparison of multilingual, monolingual Polish, and English-centric teacher models on the identical Polish medical classification task, potentially using controlled translations to disentangle language complexity from domain knowledge effects.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can BERT-family student models reliably learn multiclass classification from LLM-generated pseudo-labels alone?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated as RQ3 in the introduction.
- **Why unresolved:** Only three specific student models were tested. Factors like student model size, pretraining domain (general vs. biomedical), architectural variations, and robustness to label error rates remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training and evaluating a broader spectrum of transformer-based students (e.g., RoBERTa, DeBERTa, other Polish models) with controlled levels of synthetic label noise to determine the boundary conditions for successful distillation from LLM teachers.

## Limitations
*   **Unreleased Dataset:** The specific Polish medical corpus (sources, size, preprocessing) is not public, preventing exact replication of data distribution and label frequencies.
*   **Unverified Teacher Quality:** No metric for the teacher model's (Llama3.1-70B) accuracy against human experts is reported for the full training set; validation was limited to a small, physician-checked subset.
*   **Unconfirmed Pretraining Content:** The claimed advantage of the multilingual DistilBERT relies on the assumption that its Wikipedia-based pretraining contained meaningful Polish medical text, which is not verified.

## Next Checks
1.  **Quantify Teacher Reliability:** Have physicians label an independent, statistically powered sample (e.g., 200 texts per category) from the training corpus. Compute inter-annotator agreement (F1) between the teacher's pseudo-labels and these new expert labels. An agreement below ~0.85 would flag high noise.
2.  **Isolate Distillation Benefit:** Ablate the student training by comparing DistilBERT trained *only* on the small, physician-validated seed set (n≈500/category) versus training on the full pseudo-labeled corpus. A significant performance drop when using only true labels would indicate pseudo-labels are harmful or the seed set is too small.
3.  **Prompt Robustness Test:** For one category (e.g., Oncology), systematically vary prompt components: (a) number of in-context examples (1, 3, 5), (b) example selection (random vs. diverse), (c) output format (free text vs. strict JSON). Measure variance in teacher pseudo-label consistency (Cohen's kappa) across runs on a fixed subset. High variance would necessitate prompt standardization.
