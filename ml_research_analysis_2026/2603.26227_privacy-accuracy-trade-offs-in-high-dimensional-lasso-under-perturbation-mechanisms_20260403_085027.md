# Privacy-Accuracy Trade-offs in High-Dimensional LASSO under Perturbation Mechanisms

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26227  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26227  
- **Reference count:** 10  
- **Primary result:** Output perturbation yields a monotonic privacy‑accuracy trade‑off (cwOnAveKL ↓, generalization error ↑ with noise), while objective perturbation can simultaneously improve both metrics at moderate noise levels but degrades them when noise is excessive.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the privacy‑accuracy trade‑off in high‑dimensional sparse linear regression under differential privacy. The authors compare two canonical mechanisms—output perturbation (adding noise to the LASSO estimate) and objective perturbation (adding noise to the loss)—using approximate message passing (AMP) combined with state evolution. This framework yields asymptotic closed‑form expressions for prediction error (generalization error) and a privacy metric (component‑wise on‑average KL divergence, cwOnAveKL). The analysis shows that output perturbation leads to a monotonic trade‑off, whereas objective perturbation exhibits a non‑monotonic regime where a moderate amount of noise can enhance both privacy and accuracy. Sparsity, induced by ℓ₁ regularization, also improves privacy. The results offer explicit formulas to calibrate privacy noise and uncover qualitative differences between the two perturbation mechanisms.

## Method Summary
The study leverages approximate message passing (AMP), an iterative algorithm well‑suited for high‑dimensional inference, to analyze the LASSO under differential privacy. The data model assumes a Gaussian design matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) with i.i.d. entries and a sparse true parameter \(\beta^{(0)}\) following a Bernoulli‑Gaussian prior. Two privacy mechanisms are examined: (i) output perturbation adds independent Gaussian noise to the LASSO estimate; (ii) objective perturbation injects noise into the loss before optimization. The macroscopic behavior of AMP is tracked via state evolution (SE), a deterministic recursion for two quantities: the mean‑squared error \(E\) and an effective sparsity measure \(V\). The SE updates involve expectations over the prior, privacy noise, and a standard normal variable, evaluated numerically by Monte Carlo sampling. Once the SE converges, the generalization error is directly \(E\), and the privacy metric cwOnAveKL is computed: for output perturbation a simple closed form \(E_0 \rho_0 / (\alpha^2 \sigma_\eta^2)\); for objective perturbation a more complex expression requiring derivatives of the activation probability. The analysis also identifies an AMP stability condition \(b\rho/\alpha < 1\) (where \(b\rho\) is the asymptotic sparsity level). This setup enables sweeping over privacy noise variance \(\sigma_\eta^2\), regularization \(\lambda\), sparsity \(\rho\), and sample‑to‑parameter ratio \(\alpha\) to map out the privacy‑accuracy landscape.

## Key Results
- For **output perturbation**, cwOnAveKL decreases monotonically with noise variance \(\sigma_\eta^2\) (like \(1/\sigma_\eta^2\)), while the generalization error increases linearly with \(\sigma_\eta\).
- For **objective perturbation**, the trade‑off is **non‑monotonic**: there exists an intermediate \(\sigma_\eta\) that simultaneously lowers both cwOnAveKL and generalization error; excessive noise harms both.
- Sparsity (controlled by regularization \(\lambda\)) reduces sensitivity and improves privacy; objective perturbation tends to densify the estimate, which can boost generalization when \(\lambda\) is large and noise is well‑tuned.
- AMP only converges when the asymptotic sparsity level satisfies \(b\rho/\alpha < 1\); otherwise the iterates diverge.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The differing behaviors stem from how noise interacts with the LASSO optimization. In output perturbation, independent Gaussian noise is added directly to each coordinate of the estimate; the resulting marginal KL divergence scales as \(1/\sigma_\eta^2\) independent of the signal, leading to a monotonic improvement in privacy (lower KL) as noise grows, but accuracy uniformly degrades. In objective perturbation, noise is injected into the loss, altering the thresholding function that defines the LASSO solution. This creates a non‑trivial coupling: moderate noise can shrink coefficients more aggressively (acting like additional regularization), which both reduces overfitting (improving generalization) and lowers the sensitivity of the output (improving privacy). However, too much noise destabilizes the thresholding rule, increasing both error and KL.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Differential Privacy** – A formal framework for quantifying individual privacy loss, often via randomized mechanisms. *Why needed:* The paper measures privacy via on‑average KL divergence, a differential privacy‑related metric. *Quick check:* Verify that adding Gaussian noise with variance \(\sigma^2\) satisfies \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP for appropriate \(\varepsilon,\delta\).
2. **LASSO** – ℓ₁‑regularized linear regression, promoting sparsity. *Why needed:* The estimation problem under study. *Quick check:* The LASSO solution minimizes \(\frac{1}{2}\|y-X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_1\).
3. **Approximate Message Passing (AMP)** – Iterative algorithm for high‑dimensional inference with an Onsager correction term. *Why needed:* Provides a computationally efficient way to approximate the LASSO solution in the random design setting. *Quick check:* In AMP, each iteration updates the estimate using a soft‑thresholding of a linear combination of the previous iterate and the residual, plus a correction term.
4. **State Evolution (SE)** – A deterministic recursion that tracks the empirical distribution of AMP iterates in the high‑dimensional limit. *Why needed:* Allows analytic prediction of generalization error and sparsity without running AMP. *Quick check:* The SE equations for LASSO involve expectations over the Bernoulli‑Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise.
5. **High‑Dimensional Asymptotics** – Joint limit \(n,p \to \infty\) with \(n/p \to \alpha^{-1}\). *Why needed:* The AMP/SE equivalence is provable only in this limit; it yields exact predictions for finite‑\(n,p\) when \(p\) is large. *Quick check:* The state evolution parameters \(\Sigma = (1+V)/\alpha\) and \(\sigma_z = \sqrt{E/\alpha}\) encode the aspect ratio.
6. **Sparsity Models (Bernoulli‑Gaussian)** – A prior where components are independently zero with probability \(1-\rho\) or drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). *Why needed:* Captures the assumed sparsity of the true parameter. *Quick check:* The expected fraction of non‑zeros is \(\rho\).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text):  
Data model (X, β^{(0)}, ξ) → AMP iterates (β^t) → State evolution (E, V) → Fixed point → Metrics (generalization error, cwOnAveKL)

**Critical path**:  
1. Initialize \(E\) and \(V\).  
2. Compute \(\Sigma = (1+V)/\alpha\), \(\sigma_z = \sqrt{E/\alpha}\).  
3. Draw large Monte Carlo samples of \((\beta,\eta,z)\) (or \((\beta,z)\) without \(\eta\) for output perturbation).  
4. Compute contributions to \(E\) and \(V\) (or to \(R\) for objective perturbation) using soft‑thresholding integrals.  
5. Update \(E\), \(V\) (and compute \(R\) if objective) and repeat until convergence.  
6. Derive final metrics from the fixed point.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Analytical simplicity* vs. *generality*: The analysis assumes Gaussian design and Gaussian privacy noise to obtain closed‑form integrals; real data may violate these assumptions.
- *Monte Carlo accuracy* vs. *computational cost*: More samples reduce statistical error but increase runtime; \(10^6\) samples are typical but may be insufficient for tail‑sensitive metrics.
- *Asymptotic exactness* vs. *finite‑sample validity*: The theory holds only in the \(n,p \to \infty\) limit; for moderate dimensions, discrepancies may appear.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Divergence** when \(b\rho/\alpha \ge 1\) (AMP blows up).
- **Numerical overflow/underflow** when \(\lambda\Sigma\) is extreme or when \(1-br\) is near zero in the objective perturbation \(R\) calculation.
- **Excessive Monte Carlo noise** causing non‑monotonic or jagged curves, especially for small \(\sigma_\eta\).

**First three experiments**:
1. **Output perturbation monotonicity**: Sweep \(\sigma_\eta\) and verify that cwOnAveKL scales as \(1/\sigma_\eta^2\) and generalization error increases linearly.
2. **Objective perturbation non‑monotonicity**: Sweep \(\sigma_\eta\) for a fixed \(\lambda\) and locate the region where both metrics jointly decrease; confirm the drop‑then‑rise pattern.
3. **Stability boundary**: Vary \(\rho\) and \(\alpha\) to map the phase transition where \(b\rho/\alpha\) crosses 1, observing divergence of SE/AMP.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension beyond LASSO:** How can the AMP/state evolution analysis be extended to broader classes of models, such as generalized linear models (GLMs), elastic net, or non‑linear estimators?  
   *Basis:* The authors explicitly state that future work includes extending the analysis to broader models.  
   *Why unresolved:* Different losses/priors require new state evolution equations and stability analyses, which are non‑trivial.
2. **Comparison with DP‑SGD:** What are the privacy‑accuracy trade‑offs for gradient‑based differential privacy mechanisms (e.g., DP‑SGD) in high‑dimensional sparse linear regression, and how do they qualitatively compare to output and objective perturbation?  
   *Basis:* The paper mentions connections to gradient‑based methods as a promising direction.  
   *Why unresolved:* DP‑SGD introduces noise at each iteration and couples privacy with optimization dynamics; its effect on the typical‑case distribution captured by AMP is not understood.
3. **Data‑dependent noise calibration:** Can data‑dependent noise calibration strategies improve the privacy‑accuracy trade‑off compared to fixed‑variance perturbation in objective or output perturbation?  
   *Basis:* The authors suggest investigating data‑dependent noise calibration.  
   *Why unresolved:* The current analysis uses constant noise variance; adapting noise to data statistics (e.g., leverage scores) may reduce distortion, but rigorous analysis and algorithm design are open.
4. **Robustness to real data:** Do the theoretical predictions for cwOnAveKL and generalization error hold for real‑world datasets that violate the i.i.d. Gaussian design assumption?  
   *Basis:* The paper calls for validation in more complex statistical settings.  
   *Why unresolved:* AMP/SE asymptotics rely on Gaussian design; real data exhibit heteroscedasticity, autocorrelation, and heavy tails, which may alter the derived scalings.

## Limitations
- The privacy noise \(\eta\) is treated as Gaussian in the derivations, but the paper never explicitly states this assumption; non‑Gaussian noise could change the results.
- Finite‑sample behavior is not captured; asymptotic predictions may deviate when \(n,p\) are moderate, especially near the stability boundary \(b\rho/\alpha \approx 1\).
- Essential AMP implementation details (e.g., Onsager correction, message initialization) are omitted in the main text, preventing exact replication of the simulation curves.
- The cwOnAveKL metric is an average KL divergence, not a worst‑case differential privacy guarantee; it does not directly imply \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP bounds.
