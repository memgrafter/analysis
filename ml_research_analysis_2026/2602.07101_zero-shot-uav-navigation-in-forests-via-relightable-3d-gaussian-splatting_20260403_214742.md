# Zero-Shot UAV Navigation in Forests via Relightable 3D Gaussian Splatting
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07101  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07101  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Zero‑shot transfer to real forests yields collision‑free flight up to 10 m/s; success rate under twilight improves from 3/10 to 8/10.  

## Executive Summary
Monocular UAV navigation in unstructured forests suffers from a sim‑to‑real gap largely due to static lighting in conventional 3D Gaussian Splatting simulators. This paper introduces Relightable 3D Gaussian Splatting, a method that decomposes scene geometry (albedo, occlusion) from environmental illumination, enabling explicit relighting of photorealistic forest reconstructions. By randomizing the lighting during reinforcement learning training, the policy learns robust, illumination‑invariant visual features. The resulting system achieves zero‑shot deployment on a physical quadrotor, demonstrating stable, collision‑free flight at speeds up to 10 m/s across diverse lighting conditions, including twilight. The approach effectively narrows the reality gap without any fine‑tuning on real data.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with capturing real forest scenes using a handheld rig equipped with a stereo fisheye camera, LiDAR, and RTK for geo‑referenced poses and dense geometry. A standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) model is first reconstructed from the images. The core Relightable 3DGS module then decomposes each Gaussian’s color into diffuse albedo (ρᵢ) and a geometry‑aware occlusion coefficient (Oᵢ), while factoring out a global spherical harmonics (SH) lighting vector (L_env). A pre‑trained Deep Panorama Lighting (DPL) auto‑encoder provides an HDR prior to estimate L_env from LDR images, which is frozen during optimization to isolate illumination effects. The relightable renderer is embedded into a simulation environment featuring simplified first‑order quadrotor dynamics, point‑cloud collision detection, and an adaptive speed schedule. A PPO agent with a CNN (RGB), MLP (state), and GRU (temporal fusion) is trained to reach a goal while avoiding obstacles. Domain randomization is applied to actions, state, camera extrinsics, and, critically, to L_env via rotation, intensity scaling, and chromatic tinting. Training follows a two‑stage curriculum: initial static lighting, then full lighting randomization. The resulting policy runs onboard a Jetson Orin NX and is deployed on a custom quadrotor with a monocular camera and IMU/GNSS.

## Key Results
- Achieved zero‑shot, collision‑free navigation in real forests at speeds up to 10 m/s.  
- Twilight success rate increased from 3/10 (baseline) to 8/10 with lighting randomization.  
- The method demonstrates effective sim‑to‑real adaptation without any real‑world fine‑tuning.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Physical relighting decomposition**  
Relightable 3DGS separates scene radiance into learnable albedo (ρᵢ), occlusion coefficients (Oᵢ), and global SH lighting (L_env) per the rendering equation. By fixing ρᵢ and Oᵢ, L_env can be arbitrarily edited, producing photorealistic illumination changes while preserving geometry. This explicit factorization, grounded in physics, enables the simulation to present diverse lighting conditions without altering scene structure.

**Mechanism 2: Lighting randomization forces invariant features**  
During RL training, the global lighting L_env is randomly perturbed (rotation, intensity, tint) at the start of each episode. The policy must succeed across these variations, compelling it to ignore photometric cues and rely on geometry and motion patterns. This domain randomization yields features that are robust to the wide illumination changes encountered in real forests.

**Mechanism 3: High‑fidelity simulation narrows the visual gap**  
The simulator renders images from a 3DGS model built from captured forest scenes, preserving intricate textures and irregular geometries. This real‑world data grounding ensures that the visual observations during training are much closer to reality than synthetic assets, reducing the reality gap and supporting zero‑shot transfer.

## Foundational Learning
- **3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)**: A scene representation using anisotropic Gaussians projected with α‑blending. *Why needed*: It forms the basis for reconstruction, relighting, and simulation. *Quick check*: In standard 3DGS, what does the spherical harmonics (SH) coefficient represent and why does it cause entanglement with illumination?
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**: A policy gradient algorithm with a clipped objective for stable updates. *Why needed*: It trains the navigation policy. *Quick check*: How does PPO’s clipping mechanism prevent destructive large policy updates?
- **Domain Randomization for Sim‑to‑Real**: A strategy that randomizes simulator parameters to cover the distribution of real‑world variations. *Why needed*: It is the key to zero‑shot transfer, especially for lighting, dynamics, and perception. *Quick check*: What distinguishes domain randomization from domain adaptation, and why is randomization suitable for zero‑shot deployment?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Data Collection -> 3DGS Reconstruction -> Relightable 3DGS Module -> Simulation Environment (RL Policy Network + Domain Randomization Suite) -> Deployment Stack.

**Critical path**  
1. Capture real‑world forest sequences with handheld multimodal rig.  
2. Reconstruct base 3DGS model from images and poses.  
3. Train relightable 3DGS: optimize albedo and occlusion, freeze HDR lighting prior.  
4. Build simulation: integrate relightable renderer, quadrotor dynamics, collision detection, reward.  
5. Train RL policy with two‑stage curriculum (static lighting → full lighting randomization).  
6. Validate in simulation across lighting conditions.  
7. Deploy to physical drone and conduct real‑world flight tests.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- 3DGS vs NeRF: 3DGS offers real‑time rendering needed for RL but may trade off geometric accuracy and light transport fidelity.  
- Simplified dynamics: Decoupled yaw and force model speeds training but omits high‑speed aerodynamics; an adaptive speed schedule mitigates sideslip.  
- Two‑stage curriculum: Stabilizes initial learning but risks temporary overfitting to static lighting before domain adaptation.  
- HDR prior dependency: Pre‑computed lighting coefficients stabilize decomposition but may limit generalization to extreme low‑light scenes.

**Failure signatures**  
- Twilight failures: Lighting randomization may not cover low‑light regimes or HDR prior is inaccurate.  
- Simulation crashes but real success: Collision geometry too conservative or dynamics model mismatched.  
- Low simulation success with DA: Insufficient policy capacity or suboptimal reward shaping.  
- Unrealistic relit images: Albedo contains baked‑in shadows or occlusion field is noisy; check decomposition quality.

**First three experiments**  
1. *Reconstruction validation*: Render held‑out views from base 3DGS; compute PSNR/SSIM; target >25 dB PSNR.  
2. *Relighting sanity check*: For a fixed pose, manually set L_env to various conditions (e.g., directional sun, overcast) and inspect for plausible shadows/highlights without geometry change.  
3. *Policy learning curve*: Train with/without lighting randomization; plot simulation success rate vs. steps. Expect DA to dip early then surpass; if it never recovers, randomization may be too extreme.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does enforcing a frozen HDR lighting prior during Relightable 3DGS optimization limit the policy’s ability to adapt to novel lighting conditions not present in training?  
2. How robust is the occlusion field (built with a fixed depth threshold and voxel grid) to noise and inconsistencies in 3DGS reconstructions of dense, unstructured forest geometries?  
3. To what extent does the policy’s success depend on forest‑specific characteristics (e.g., vertical structure, tree density) versus general outdoor clutter, and what environmental factors most significantly challenge it?  
4. What is the computational overhead of the Relightable 3DGS rendering pipeline compared to standard 3DGS, and how does it scale with scene size and lighting randomization frequency?  
5. Does the simplified quadrotor dynamics model, combined with domain randomization, fully bridge the dynamics gap, or would incorporating more precise or learned dynamics models improve zero‑shot transfer robustness?

## Limitations
- The exact architecture and weights of the pre‑trained HDR lifting model (Deep Panorama Lighting) are unspecified, risking unstable lighting estimation in low‑light scenes.  
- Critical PPO hyperparameters (learning rates, GRU hidden size, etc.) are omitted, hindering reproducible policy convergence.  
- The decomposition fidelity of albedo and occlusion is not quantitatively validated; baked‑in shadows or noise could produce unrealistic relighting and degrade simulation realism.  

## Confidence
- Relightable 3DGS decomposition: **Medium** (supported by formulation but lacking ablations on quality).  
- Lighting randomization for invariant features: **Medium‑High** (real‑world success rate improvement, but isolated contribution unclear).  
- High‑fidelity simulation narrowing the gap: **Medium** (success indicates good reconstruction, but no quantitative metrics reported).
