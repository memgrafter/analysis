# Constrained Online Convex Optimization with Memory and Predictions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21375
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21375
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces the first algorithms with sublinear regret and cumulative constraint violation for constrained online convex optimization with memory (COCO-M), both without predictions and with short-horizon untrusted predictions.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies constrained online convex optimization with memory (COCO-M), where loss and constraint functions depend on a sliding window of past decisions. For the general setting with time-varying constraints, the authors propose two algorithmic frameworks. In the absence of predictions, an adaptive penalty method combines quadratic penalties with online gradient descent, yielding regret \(O(m^{3/2} \sqrt{T} \log T)\) and cumulative constraint violation (CCV) \(O(\max\{T^{3/4}, m^{3/2} \sqrt{T} \log T\})\) for the double-memory variant COCO-M2, and \(O(T^{3/4})\) CCV for COCO-M (memory only in loss). When short-horizon untrusted predictions of future gradients are available, the problem is reinterpreted as delayed feedback and solved via an optimistic Follow‑the‑Regularized‑Leader (FTRL) algorithm with exponential penalties. This approach achieves regret \(O(\sqrt{E_T(f)})\) and CCV \(O((\sqrt{E_T(g^+)}+m) \log T)\), where \(E_T\) measures total prediction error, thereby improving performance as prediction quality increases. The results unify and extend prior work on constrained OCO and OCO with memory.

## Method Summary
The proposed methods consist of two algorithms tailored to the prediction setting. Algorithm 1 (no predictions) constructs each round a surrogate loss by adding a quadratic penalty on the cumulative constraint violation to the current loss; decisions are then updated by online gradient descent (OGD) on this surrogate. The penalty coefficient scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\), and the accumulated violation appears both in the penalty and in the surrogate gradient bound, enabling a two‑step analysis that decouples memory effects. Algorithm 2 (with predictions) builds a forward loss \(Z_t(x_t)\) that aggregates all future contributions (loss and penalty) of the current decision \(x_t\) over the next \(m\) rounds. Under separability and linearity assumptions, the gradient of \(Z_t\) becomes available after a delay of \(m\) rounds; the learner uses a hint based on predictions to run an optimistic FTRL (ODAFTRL) that adapts to the prediction error. The regularization is tuned via a doubling trick to handle unknown error magnitudes, yielding bounds that explicitly depend on the total squared prediction error \(E_T\).

## Key Results
- The first sublinear regret and CCV guarantees for general COCO‑M with time‑varying constraints.
- Without predictions: quadratic penalty + OGD gives regret \(O(m^{3/2} \sqrt{T} \log T)\); CCV \(O(\max\{T^{3/4}, m^{3/2} \sqrt{T} \log T\})\) for COCO‑M2 and \(O(T^{3/4})\) for COCO‑M.
- With untrusted predictions: optimistic FTRL + exponential penalties yields regret \(O(\sqrt{E_T(f)})\) and CCV \(O((\sqrt{E_T(g^+)}+m) \log T)\), which improve as prediction error decreases.
- For very short memory (\(m \le T^{1/6}/(\log T)^{1/3}\)), exponential penalty without predictions already achieves \(O(\sqrt{T} \log T)\) CCV.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
An adaptive quadratic penalty method combined with online gradient descent (OGD) yields sublinear regret and cumulative constraint violation for constrained online convex optimization with memory (COCO‑M), even with time‑varying constraints.
- **Mechanism:** The learner constructs a surrogate loss at each round that adds a quadratic penalty of the accumulated constraint violation to the current loss. This penalty uses a coefficient \(\lambda = 1/\sqrt{T}\) to balance the two objectives. Decisions are updated by OGD on this surrogate. The accumulated violation appears both in the penalty and in the surrogate gradient bound, enabling a two‑step analysis that decouples memory effects.
- **Core assumption:** Convex compact decision set \(X\); convex Lipschitz loss and constraint functions. Quadratic penalty is convex and increasing.
- **Evidence anchors:** Algorithm 1, Lemma 1 (OGD bound), Lemma 2 (regret decomposition), Theorem 1 for COCO‑M2 and Theorem 2 for COCO‑M.
- **Break condition:** The \(m^{3/2}\) factor degrades performance for large memory windows; bounds rely on finite diameter and Lipschitz constants.

### Mechanism 2
When untrusted short‑horizon predictions of future loss and constraint gradients are available, the memory‑dependent COCO problem can be reinterpreted as a memoryless problem with delayed feedback. An optimistic variant of Follow‑the‑Regularized‑Leader (FTRL) that adapts to prediction errors then achieves regret and violation that improve as prediction accuracy increases.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm defines a forward loss \(Z_t(x_t)\) that aggregates all contributions of the current decision \(x_t\) over the next \(m\) rounds (including the penalty term). Gradients of \(Z_t\) become available only after a delay of \(m\) rounds. At round \(t\) the learner possesses a hint \(h_{t+1}\) that predicts the delayed gradient, built from predictions of future \(f_i\) and \(g_i\). ODAFTRL uses these hints and the revealed gradients to choose \(x_{t+1}\), scaling its regularization by the recent prediction error \(\epsilon_{t-m}\).
- **Core assumption:** Memory functions must be separable (each component depends on a single past decision) and linear so that gradients are constant or simple signs. Predictions are untrusted but their cumulative errors \(E_T(f)\) and \(E_T(g^+)\) are bounded.
- **Evidence anchors:** Construction of \(Z_t\), hint \(h_t\), Algorithm 2, Theorem 3, and Lemma 3. Relies on ODAFTRL guarantees from Flaspohler et al. (2021).
- **Break condition:** For non‑linear or non‑separable memory, forward‑loss decomposition fails. The algorithm compares to a stricter benchmark \(X_T^{mp}\), a subset of the original feasible set.

### Mechanism 3
The penalty function choice (quadratic vs. exponential) and its online tuning interact with the availability of predictions to yield different performance regimes, especially for short memory windows.
- **Mechanism:** A quadratic penalty \(\Phi(V) = \lambda V^2\) with \(\lambda = 1/\sqrt{T}\) gives conservative bounds (\(O(T^{3/4})\) CCV for COCO‑M). When predictions are present, an exponential penalty \(\Phi(V) = e^{\lambda V}-1\) is used because its derivative grows with \(V\), allowing the regret‑decomposition term \(\Phi(V_T)\) to dominate the cumulative error term when predictions are good. The \(\lambda\) parameter can be set via a doubling trick. For very short memory (\(m \le T^{1/6}/(\log T)^{1/3}\)), exponential penalty even without predictions improves CCV to \(O(\sqrt{T} \log T)\).
- **Core assumption:** For exponential‑penalty analysis, initial penalty \(\Phi(V_{m-1})\) is bounded (e.g., \(V_{m-1}=0\)). The doubling trick assumes the unknown complexity parameter can be bracketed by a geometric sequence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4 (Theorem 4) for short‑memory exponential penalty; Section 5 uses exponential penalty for optimistic setting and discusses doubling.
- **Break condition:** If \(\lambda\) is too large relative to Lagrange multipliers, the surrogate may become too steep causing oscillations. The doubling trick adds a \(\log T\) factor. Performance gains require predictions to be sufficiently accurate; with arbitrarily bad predictions exponential penalty may not recover no‑prediction rates.

## Foundational Learning

**Online Convex Optimization (OCO)**  
- *Why needed:* The framework builds on OCO; regret measures performance relative to a static benchmark, and COCO‑M modifies this by comparing to a repeated action over a window.
- *Quick check:* In standard OCO, the regret compares the cumulative loss of the algorithm against the loss of a fixed decision in hindsight. In OCO‑M, the comparator is a static decision repeated over a sliding window.

**Constraint handling via penalty methods**  
- *Why needed:* The paper converts the constrained problem into an unconstrained surrogate by adding a penalty for cumulative constraint violation. The penalty form directly influences theoretical bounds.
- *Quick check:* Penalty methods add a term \(\Phi(V_t)\) to the loss; primal‑dual methods maintain dual variables and update them alongside primal decisions. Quadratic penalties yield a simpler OGD update but may give weaker CCV compared to exponential when predictions are available.

**Optimistic learning with predictions**  
- *Why needed:* When predictions of future gradients are available, the algorithm incorporates them to reduce regret and violation; bounds depend explicitly on total prediction error.
- *Quick check:* \(E_T(f)\) is the cumulative squared norm difference between predicted and actual loss gradients, summed over time and components. Smaller \(E_T(f)\) leads to better regret.

**Delayed feedback and memory equivalence**  
- *Why needed:* Memory in the loss/constraint means a decision affects multiple future rounds, equivalent to delayed gradient feedback. This allows leveraging algorithms for delayed online learning.
- *Quick check:* The forward loss \(Z_t(x_t)\) aggregates all contributions of \(x_t\) over the next \(m\) rounds. By doing so, the effect of \(x_t\) becomes memoryless: all future feedback about \(x_t\) is encapsulated in \(Z_t\).

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Input layer (X, loss oracle, constraint oracle, optional prediction oracles) → Surrogate builder (constructs L_t or Z_t + penalty) → Accumulator (maintains V_t and Φ'(V_t)) → Prediction error estimator (computes ϵ_t) → Optimistic update engine (OGD or ODAFTRL) → Parameter tuner (sets λ, step sizes) → Output decisions.

- **Critical path:**  
  For Algorithm 1 (no predictions): at round t≥m, observe functions → compute f_t and g_t on current window → update V_t → compute surrogate gradient → OGD step → next decision.  
  For Algorithm 2 (predictions): after t≥m, observe → update V_t → compute prediction error ϵ_{t-m} → build hint h_{t+1} → run ODAFTRL to get x_{t+1}.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  Quadratic penalty is simple, gives closed‑form OGD update but weaker CCV without predictions.  
  Exponential penalty with λ tuned via doubling adapts to unknown prediction error but incurs extra log T factor; requires convex smooth penalty.  
  Separability and linearity enable tractable gradient prediction; relaxing them would require predicting full trajectories.  
  Memory length m increases storage (last m decisions) and worsens bounds (m^{3/2} factor without predictions, linear m in CCV with predictions).

- **Failure signatures:**  
  Large Lipschitz constants or domain diameter blow up OGD step size → instability (though asymptotic bounds still hold).  
  Systematically biased hints → ODAFTRL becomes too conservative → linear regret.  
  Violating separability (e.g., product of past decisions) breaks the forward‑loss decomposition and Lemma 3.  
  Doubling trick mis‑implementation → λ far from optimal → degrade bound by log factor.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Appendix A: run Algorithm 1 on a 1‑D quadratic loss / linear constraint problem with m=3, T up to 4000, in stochastic and adversarial settings. Plot average regret R_t/t and average CCV V_t/t to confirm sublinear convergence.  
  2. For Algorithm 2, first use perfect predictions (hint equals true delayed gradient). Expect regret and CCV to converge to O(1) and O(m log T) respectively (perfect‑prediction rates from Theorem 3).  
  3. Study prediction noise: corrupt perfect hints with Gaussian noise of increasing variance. Measure how cumulative regret and violation grow with total squared error E_T; check √‑dependence predicted by theory.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Dynamic regret for COCO‑M with predictions?** The paper currently provides static regret bounds; extending to dynamic regret via static‑to‑dynamic reductions is future work.
- **Adapting penalties over time?** Penalties are fixed (quadratic or exponential). Designing penalties that react to time‑varying windows and constraint hardness could improve practical performance.
- **Removing separability/linearity?** The optimistic algorithm relies on strong assumptions about memory structure. Relaxing these (e.g., nonlinear memory) would broaden applicability but requires new techniques.
- **Minimax lower bounds?** The paper presents upper bounds; lower bounds for COCO‑M in terms of T, m, and constraint parameters are unknown and would help gauge optimality.

## Limitations
- Theoretical results assume separable linear memory; non‑linear dependencies are not covered.
- Quadratic penalty needs knowledge of T for λ; the doubling trick mitigates this but adds logarithmic factors.
- COCO‑M2’s CCV bound contains a \(\max\{T^{3/4}, m^{3/2}\sqrt{T}\log T\}\) term; the m‑dependence might be loose.
- The optimistic algorithm compares against a stricter benchmark \(X_T^{mp}\) rather than the original feasible set.
- Experiments are limited to simple synthetic problems; no real‑world or high‑dimensional validation.

## Confidence
- **Quadratic‑penalty OGD for COCO‑M:** High (detailed proofs, standard assumptions).
- **Optimistic FTRL with predictions:** Medium (relies on strong linear separability and untrusted hints; benchmark is weaker).
- **Exponential‑penalty advantage for short memory:** Medium (asymptotic, regime‑specific).
