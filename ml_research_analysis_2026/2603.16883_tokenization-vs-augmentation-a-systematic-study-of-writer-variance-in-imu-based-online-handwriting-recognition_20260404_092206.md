# Tokenization vs. Augmentation: A Systematic Study of Writer Variance in IMU-Based Online Handwriting Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16883
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16883
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** On writer-independent splits, Bigram tokenization reduces WER by 15.65% relative (15.40% → 12.99%). On writer-dependent splits, concatenation-based augmentation reduces CER by 34.5% and WER by 25.4%.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates two strategies—sub-word tokenization and concatenation-based data augmentation—to address uneven character distributions and inter-writer variability in IMU-based online handwriting recognition. Using the OnHW-Words500 dataset, the authors conduct a systematic comparison across writer-independent (WI) and writer-dependent (WD) evaluation protocols. They find a clear dichotomy: sub-word tokenization (specifically Bigram) significantly improves performance on WI splits by mitigating stylistic variability, while concatenation-based augmentation substantially reduces error rates on WD splits by compensating for intra-writer data sparsity. The core insight is that tokenization primarily targets inter-writer variance, whereas augmentation effectively handles intra-writer sparsity.

## Method Summary
The study employs the OnHW-Words500 dataset (right-handed subset, 13-channel IMU signals from 53 subjects) with two evaluation protocols: writer-dependent (same writers in train/val, different words) and writer-independent (disjoint writers). The baseline model is REWI, a CNN-LSTM architecture trained with Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss. Experimental modifications include: (1) Sub-word tokenization (Bigram, BPE, Unigram) with vocabulary sizes from 100 to 500, applied to character labels before training; (2) Concatenation-based augmentation, where multiple samples from the same writer are joined to form longer sequences, thereby balancing rare character occurrences within a writer’s profile. Tokenization is performed on individual labels prior to concatenation. Models are trained for 300 epochs using AdamW with cosine annealing, and evaluated using Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER).

## Key Results
- **Writer-independent split:** Bigram tokenization (vocabulary size 500) yields a 15.65% relative reduction in WER (15.40% → 12.99%).
- **Writer-dependent split:** Concatenation-based augmentation (two extra concatenations) reduces CER by 34.5% and WER by 25.4% compared to the character-level baseline.
- **Dichotomy:** Tokenization harms performance on WD splits, while augmentation provides negligible benefit on WI splits.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sub-word tokenization mitigates inter-writer stylistic variability
**Claim:** Bigram tokenization reduces WER on writer-independent splits by smoothing over stylistic noise from unseen writers.  
**Mechanism:** Fixed-length bigrams capture local kinematic transition patterns (e.g., how a writer lifts the pen between characters) that are consistent across writers. By predicting pairs of characters, the model focuses on temporal continuities less affected by idiosyncratic character shapes.  
**Core assumption:** Stylistic variations primarily impact isolated character forms, but transitions between adjacent characters remain relatively stable across writers.  
**Evidence anchors:** Bigram consistently outperforms character baseline on WI split as vocabulary size increases (Fig. 1 bottom). Performance degrades on WD splits where vocabulary distribution shifts occur.  
**Break condition:** Fixed bigram vocabulary fails to cover rare character sequences present only in validation data (e.g., in WD splits).

### Mechanism 2: Concatenation-based augmentation compensates for intra-writer data sparsity
**Claim:** Concatenation augmentation reduces error rates on writer-dependent splits by artificially balancing the occurrence of rare characters within a single writer’s corpus.  
**Mechanism:** By joining multiple samples from the same writer, the method creates longer sequences that contain a more uniform representation of that writer’s character inventory. This regularizes the model, preventing overfitting to the naturally skewed distribution and forcing robust learning of character representations.  
**Core assumption:** The main challenge in WD settings is the scarcity of samples for rare characters within a given writer’s data, not style variation.  
**Evidence anchors:** On WD split, augmentation (C2) yields CER reduction of 34.5% and WER reduction of 25.4% (Fig. 2 top). The gain surpasses simply extending training epochs (Table 2), isolating concatenation’s effect.  
**Break condition:** Augmentation provides little to no benefit on WI splits, where the primary difficulty is cross-writer style generalization rather than data sparsity.

### Mechanism 3: Models fall back to short tokens under distributional shifts
**Claim:** When faced with vocabulary mismatches (as in WD splits) or increased sequence variability (from concatenation), sub-word tokenizers increasingly rely on single-character tokens.  
**Mechanism:** Sub-word tokenization algorithms (BPE, Unigram) generate tokens based on training-set statistics. Under distribution shift, confidence in longer, rarer tokens drops, causing the model to default to the safest, most frequent unit—single characters.  
**Core assumption:** Complex tokens become unreliable when validation distribution diverges from training; the model reverts to character-level predictions.  
**Evidence anchors:** Token usage analysis shows single-character rates rise with concatenation (e.g., Bigram: 42.98% → 43.12%, Table 1). BPE catastrophically fails (>99% WER) at high vocabulary sizes on WD due to over-segmentation into rare tokens.  
**Break condition:** In well-matched distributions (WI without augmentation), token usage stabilizes and longer tokens may be used; concatenation disrupts this by reintroducing sparsity signals.

**Why needed:** CTC aligns variable-length IMU sequences with token/character sequences without pre-segmentation, enabling end-to-end training of the REWI model. It is essential for handling the temporal dynamics of online handwriting.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Input Pipeline:** Raw 13-channel IMU sequences → normalization → optional concatenation (joins N samples from same writer) → tokenization (if active, applied to labels pre-concatenation).  
2. **REWI Backbone:** CNN extracts spatial features from IMU channels → LSTM models temporal dynamics → linear layer produces logits over token/character vocabulary.  
3. **Training Loop:** CTC loss between logits and token/character sequences → AdamW optimizer with cosine annealing.  
4. **Inference:** Logits → CTC decode (greedy or beam search) → detokenization (if tokenized) → text output.

### Critical Path
Raw IMU data → [Concatenation?] → [Tokenization?] → REWI features → CTC loss → Decoding. The order "Concatenation → Tokenization" on labels is crucial: tokenization is applied to individual labels before concatenation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Tokenization granularity:** Smaller vocabularies (100 tokens) risk under-expressing language; larger (500) risk rare tokens and collapse (BPE). Bigram’s fixed length offers stability.
- **Concatenation count (N):** More concatenations increase effective data and regularize but also increase sequence length quadratically, raising computational cost and risk of modeling long-range dependencies incorrectly.
- **Character vs. Token output:** Character-level is robust to distribution shifts but struggles with rare characters; token-level abstractly models co-occurrence but fails under vocabulary mismatch.

### Failure Signatures
- **BPE vocabulary explosion:** WER >99% at V=500 on WD split due to over-segmentation into rare tokens.
- **Tokenization degradation on WD:** All tokenizers worsen CER/WER compared to character baseline.
- **Augmentation futility on WI:** Bigram (C2) performs worse than Bigram (C0) on WI split, indicating concatenation hurts when style generalization is primary goal.
- **Fallback to characters:** High single-character token usage in Unigram on WD split indicates model avoiding unreliable longer tokens.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Train character-level REWI on both WD and WI splits to establish baseline metrics (CER/WER ~14.86%/45.10% WD, ~7.41%/15.40% WI).
2. **Tokenization dichotomy test:** Apply Bigram tokenizer (V=500) on both splits. Expect ~15.65% WER reduction on WI (12.99%) but significant degradation on WD (WER ~49-50%).
3. **Concatenation regularization test:** On WD split, apply concatenation (C2) to character-level model only. Expect CER drop from 14.86% to ~10.04% and WER from 45.10% to ~34.52%. Also, test that this gain exceeds simply doubling training epochs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can a handwriting-aware tokenizer, based on kinematic stroke continuity rather than linguistic co-occurrence, improve recognition accuracy beyond standard sub-word methods? The authors propose developing a tokenizer that merges characters based on continuous stroke trajectories or sensor-signal transition frequencies, as current tokenizers are derived from natural language statistics and may not align with handwriting dynamics.

### Open Question 2
What is the synergistic effect of simultaneously applying sub-word tokenization and concatenation-based augmentation on a large-scale, diverse dataset containing high writer and vocabulary variance? Real-world scenarios involve both writer diversity and large, imbalanced vocabularies. It is unknown if the gains are additive, antagonistic, or if one strategy dominates.

### Open Question 3
What is the precise mechanism by which Bigram tokenization stabilizes predictions against stylistic idiosyncrasies in the writer-independent scenario? Bigram consistently outperforms other tokenizers on WI splits, but the exact reason—whether due to reduced hypothesis space for CTC, better handling of common character transitions, or simpler representations—is not experimentally isolated.

## Limitations
- **Incomplete architectural details:** The REWI model specifics are only referenced via [11], hindering exact replication.
- **Lack of corpus evidence:** No direct supporting evidence from neighboring work; cited papers focus on writer identification or dataset creation, not tokenization effects for variance mitigation.
- **Unspecified implementation details:** Tokenization libraries and preprocessing steps are not fully described; sensitivity to concatenation randomness is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Tokenization mitigates inter-writer variance:** High (strong empirical evidence on WI split, clear failure on WD).
- **Augmentation compensates intra-writer sparsity:** High (significant gains on WD, surpassing epoch extension).
- **Token fallback to characters under distribution shifts:** Medium (observed patterns are correlational; mechanistic explanation inferential).
