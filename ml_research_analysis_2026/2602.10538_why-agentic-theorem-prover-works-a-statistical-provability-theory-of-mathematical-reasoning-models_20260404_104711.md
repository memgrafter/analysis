# Why Agentic Theorem Prover Works: A Statistical Provability Theory of Mathematical Reasoning Models
## Quick Facts
* arXiv ID: 2602.10538
* Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10538
* Reference count: 40
* Primary result: Theoretical framework modeling agentic provers as finite-horizon reachability MDPs, proving optimal policy existence, deriving provability certificates, bounding score-guided planning performance, and establishing scaling laws linking sample size, horizon, and geometric complexity.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the puzzle of why modern agentic theorem provers—systems that combine large language models (LLMs), library retrieval, subgoal decomposition, and verifier feedback—succeed at generating formal proofs despite the classical hardness of theorem proving. By modeling the prover as a finite-horizon reachability Markov decision process (MDP) and defining statistical provability as the average time‑bounded success probability over a distribution of theorem instances, the authors provide a rigorous statistical learning foundation for these systems.

Using the Bellman equation, they prove that an optimal policy always exists, derive provability certificates through sub‑ and super‑solution inequalities, and obtain finite‑sample performance bounds for common score‑guided planning strategies (greedy, top‑k, beam search, rollouts). These bounds depend on approximation error, sequential statistical complexity, representation geometry (metric entropy, doubling dimension), and the tails of the action‑gap margin. The resulting scaling laws reveal how reductions in sample size, effective horizon, or geometric complexity can yield exponential gains, thereby explaining the strong empirical performance on biased real‑world theorem distributions while also highlighting inherent limitations in worst‑case settings.

## Method Summary
The authors formulate the agentic theorem prover as an MDP where states encode the current proof context, actions correspond to applying inference rules or retrieving lemmas, and the reward is 1 for reaching a completed proof within a fixed horizon and 0 otherwise. Statistical provability is defined as the expected success probability under a given instance distribution. They leverage the Bellman optimality structure to establish the existence of an optimal deterministic policy. To characterize learnability, they introduce sub‑ and super‑solution inequalities that act as certificates for provability. The performance gap between a score‑guided planner (which selects actions based on an approximating scoring function) and the optimum is bounded by terms involving the approximation error of the scoring function, the sequential Rademacher complexity of the policy class, the metric entropy of the state representation space, the doubling dimension, and the distribution of action‑gap margins. These theoretical results yield scaling laws that connect sample complexity, horizon, and geometric measures, clarifying why agentic provers work well on typical mathematical corpora.

## Key Results
* **Optimal policy existence**: Finite‑horizon reachability MDPs admit optimal deterministic policies via Bellman’s principle.
* **Performance bounds for score‑guided planning**: The regret of greedy, top‑k, beam, and rollout policies scales with approximation error, sequential statistical complexity, representation geometry, and action‑gap tails.
* **Scaling laws**: Exponential improvements can arise from reducing sample size, effective horizon, or geometric complexity, explaining empirical success on biased theorem distributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism underlying the success of agentic theorem provers lies in the interplay between the MDP structure and the inductive biases of real‑world theorem distributions. The MDP formulation captures the sequential nature of proof search, where each decision (e.g., selecting a lemma or applying a rule) affects future possibilities. The Bellman framework guarantees that an optimal policy exists and can be approximated if the scoring function used for action selection is sufficiently accurate and the geometry of the state space is favorable. The derived bounds show that for biased distributions—where similar proof patterns recur—the representation geometry (low metric entropy and doubling dimension) and large action gaps make greedy or modestly exploratory methods (beam, rollouts) highly effective. Moreover, the sub‑/super‑solution certificates provide a way to verify that a given policy is near‑optimal without exhaustive search. Crucially, the scaling laws demonstrate that even modest reductions in effective horizon (e.g., via subgoal decomposition) or geometric complexity (e.g., via informative embeddings) can dramatically lower the sample size needed for high provability, matching the practical observation that agentic provers scale well with additional compute and data.

## Foundational Learning
**Markov Decision Process (MDP)**  
Why needed: Provides the mathematical model for sequential proof search, defining states, actions, transitions, rewards, and horizon.  
Quick check: Can you enumerate the MDP components for an agentic prover? (Answer: State = proof context; Action = inference step; Transition = deterministic update; Reward = 1 if proof closed within horizon else 0; Horizon = max proof length.)

**Bellman Equation**  
Why needed: Central to proving existence of optimal policies and deriving certificate inequalities.  
Quick check: Write the Bellman optimality equation for a finite‑horizon reachability problem. (Answer: \(V_t(s) = \max_a \mathbb{P}(s' \text{ is terminal} \mid s,a) + \mathbb{P}(s' \text{ not terminal} \mid s,a) V_{t+1}(s').\))

**Metric Entropy**  
Why needed: Measures the complexity of the state representation space; appears in sample complexity bounds.  
Quick check: Given a set \(S\) and a covering with balls of radius \(\epsilon\), the metric entropy \(H(\epsilon)\) is the logarithm of the minimum number of such balls needed to cover \(S\). What does a smaller \(H(\epsilon)\) imply? (Answer: Simpler space → lower sample complexity.)

**Doubling Dimension**  
Why needed: Captures the geometric growth rate of the state space; influences sequential statistical complexity.  
Quick check: A metric space has doubling dimension \(d\) if any ball of radius \(r\) can be covered by at most \(2^d\) balls of radius \(r/2\). How does a low doubling dimension help? (Answer: Limits expansion of coverings over time, leading to tighter sequential bounds.)

**Action‑Gap Margin**  
Why needed: Quantifies separation between the optimal action and the best suboptimal one; governs effectiveness of greedy methods.  
Quick check: Define action‑gap at state \(s\) as \(\Delta(s) = Q^*(s,a^*) - \max_{a \neq a^*} Q^*(s,a)\). What happens if \(\Delta(s)\) is large? (Answer: Greedy selection is more likely to pick the optimal action, reducing regret.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
LLM (scoring function) -> Library retrieval (premise fetching) -> Subgoal decomposition (task splitting) -> Verifier feedback (step validation) -> Planning (greedy / top‑k / beam / rollouts). Optionally, the formal environment (prover kernel) sits at the end to execute actions.

**Critical Path**  
The core pipeline: (1) Retrieve relevant lemmas from a library; (2) Decompose the target theorem into a sequence of subgoals; (3) For each subgoal, use an LLM to propose proof steps, guided by verifier feedback that checks correctness; (4) Employ a score‑guided planning algorithm to select the next action (step) based on the LLM’s scores; (5) Iterate until proof completion or horizon exhaustion. The success of the entire system hinges on the quality of retrieval, the granularity of subgoals, the accuracy of the LLM’s scoring, and the planning strategy’s ability to avoid dead ends.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Library size vs. retrieval precision*: Larger libraries increase recall but introduce noise and computational cost; smaller libraries may miss needed lemmas.
- *Subgoal granularity*: Finer subgoals simplify individual steps but increase the effective horizon; coarser subgoals reduce steps but may require more complex reasoning.
- *Exploration vs. exploitation*: Beam search or rollouts explore more alternatives at the cost of additional LLM calls; greedy is cheap but prone to local optima when action gaps are small.
- *Representation complexity*: Rich embeddings (e.g., from large LLMs) improve scoring accuracy but increase metric entropy and doubling dimension, which worsens sample complexity bounds.

**Failure Signatures**
- Retrieval returns irrelevant or spurious lemmas, leading to irrelevant proof paths.
- Subgoal decomposition creates cyclic or unsatisfiable subgoals, causing infinite loops.
- LLM generates syntactically malformed or semantically unsound steps, flagged by the verifier.
- Planning gets trapped in a plateau where all candidate actions have nearly equal scores (small action‑gap), resulting in random walks.
- Verifier feedback loop is slow or inaccurate, allowing errors to propagate.

**First Experiments**
1. **Bound validation on a toy prover**: Implement a minimal finite‑horizon reachability MDP (e.g., grid‑world or small logic) with known parameters; compute theoretical bounds and compare against empirical success rates of greedy vs. beam vs. rollout policies.
2. **Component ablation on a real benchmark**: Using a dataset like LeanStep, successively remove retrieval, subgoal decomposition, or verifier feedback to quantify their impact on provability and sample efficiency, testing the claim that each component reduces effective horizon or geometric complexity.
3. **Scaling law measurement**: Train or fine‑tune an LLM‑based scorer with varying amounts of data; measure provability and compare against the predicted scaling law relating sample size to performance; check for exponential improvements as hypothesized.

## Limitations
* The MDP abstraction ignores dynamic library updates, partial observability, and non‑stationarity inherent in interactive proof assistants.
* The analysis assumes known transition probabilities and rewards, while real systems must estimate them, exposing a gap between theory and practice.
* Geometric complexity measures (metric entropy, doubling dimension) may be intractable to compute for large, high‑dimensional proof state spaces, and the scaling laws currently lack empirical calibration.

## Confidence
