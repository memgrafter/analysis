# What Language Models Know But Don't Say: Non-Generative Prior Extraction for Generalization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17609  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17609  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LoID extracts Bayesian logistic regression priors from LLM token logits to improve OOD generalization on tabular data, recovering up to 59% of the AUC gap to oracle models.

## Executive Summary
LoID is a method that leverages large language models (LLMs) to extract informative Gaussian priors for Bayesian logistic regression, targeting out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization in data-scarce tabular domains. By deterministically probing LLM token logits via semantically paired prompts, LoID captures directional and magnitude signals about feature-target relationships. Evaluated on 10 binary/multi-class tabular datasets with covariate shift—where training data is synthetically limited to a feature quantile—LoID closes up to 59% of the performance gap to an oracle model trained on full data, outperforms two baseline elicitation methods (AutoElicit, LLMProcesses) on 8 of 10 datasets, and runs 1.4–1.5× faster. Optimal performance requires moderate prior uncertainty (σ ≈ 0.4–0.5), enabling the data to override incorrect directional priors while preserving useful magnitude information.

## Method Summary
LoID proceeds in four pipeline stages. First, for each feature in a tabular dataset, it generates *N*<sub>sent</sub> (e.g., 10) semantically paired sentence templates of the form “The impact of `<feature>` on `<target>` is …”. An LLM with token-level logit access (e.g., Gemma-2-27B) is queried to obtain conditional probabilities for “positive” and “negative” tokens given each prefix. Second, a logit preference score is computed: *logit*(*p*) = log(*p*<sub>pos</sub> / (*p*<sub>pos</sub> + *p*<sub>neg</sub>)), where *p* = *P*<sub>pos</sub> / (*P*<sub>pos</sub> + *P*<sub>neg</sub>). Averaging across templates yields the prior mean µ<sub>*j*</sub> for feature *j*. Third, the prior standard deviation σ<sub>*j*</sub> is set to sqrt(α + γ · std(logit scores)), with hyperparameters α=0.2, γ=2.0. Fourth, a Bayesian logistic regression model is defined in PyMC with β<sub>*j*</sub> ~ Normal(µ<sub>*j*</sub>, σ<sub>*j*</sub>²); posterior samples are drawn via NUTS, and the posterior mean of β is used for prediction. Performance is evaluated on a held-out full dataset (test set) via AUC, and the percentage of the gap to an oracle (logistic regression trained on full data) that is closed.

## Key Results
- Recovers up to **59% of the AUC gap** to oracle models across 10 covariate-shifted tabular datasets.
- **Outperforms AutoElicit and LLMProcesses on 8/10 datasets** in terms of gap closure.
- Achieves **1.4–1.5× computational speedup** compared to baseline elicitation methods.
- Optimal prior uncertainty lies in the range **σ ≈ 0.4–0.5**, balancing directional override and magnitude retention.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LoID succeeds because LLMs encode substantial human knowledge about feature-target relationships from their training corpus. By querying with controlled, semantically paired prompts, the logit difference between “positive” and “negative” tokens provides a consistent, deterministic signal of both the direction (sign) and strength (magnitude) of association. This avoids the sampling variability of generative approaches. The derived Gaussian priors regularize Bayesian logistic regression, anchoring coefficient estimates in this external knowledge. Under covariate shift with limited training data, the prior prevents overfitting to the non-representative OOD train split. Crucially, when prior uncertainty (σ) is moderate, the data can still override an incorrectly signed prior while retaining correct magnitude information—explaining the optimal σ range of 0.4–0.5. Too small a σ over-constrains the model even when the LLM’s directional guess is wrong; too large a σ discards useful magnitude cues.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Prompt templates -> LLM with logit access -> Logit preference computation (µ<sub>j</sub>, σ<sub>j</sub>) -> Bayesian logistic regression model (PyMC) -> Posterior sampling (NUTS) -> Posterior mean predictions -> AUC evaluation.

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate *N*<sub>sent</sub> prompts for each dataset feature.  
2. Query LLM, extract “positive”/“negative” token logits, compute logit scores.  
3. Derive per-feature priors (µ, σ).  
4. Define PyMC model with Normal(µ, σ²) priors.  
5. Run NUTS sampling, discard burn-in.  
6. Predict on full dataset, compute AUC and % gap closed.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Deterministic probing vs. generative sampling:* Faster and less variable but requires logit access (limits to open-weight LLMs).  
- *Prior uncertainty scaling (α, γ):* Balances flexibility and prior strength; fixed hyperparameters may not transfer across LLMs.  
- *LLM choice:* Larger models may provide better signals but increase cost; tokenization must include “positive”/“negative” as single tokens.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Target tokens do not exist in the tokenizer or are split into subwords → logit extraction fails.  
- MCMC converges poorly (high R-hat, low ESS) → may indicate extreme σ or model misspecification.  
- LLM probabilities are miscalibrated (e.g., near-uniform) → prior means become uninformative.  
- Prompt templates lack semantic pairing → logit scores become noisy, reducing correlation with true effects.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Token validation:** For a sample feature, confirm “positive” and “negative” are single tokens and yield non-zero logits across templates.  
2. **Convergence check:** Run LoID on a small dataset (e.g., Heart Disease) with default α,γ; inspect trace plots and R-hat for β parameters.  
3. **σ sensitivity:** Ablate γ (set γ=0) on one dataset; compare AUC to default to confirm adaptive uncertainty improves performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Non-monotonic relationships:** How can LoID extend beyond binary directional queries to capture non-monotonic or continuous feature-target effects?
2. **Automated hyperparameter selection:** Can α and γ be chosen automatically for robust, out-of-the-box use without task-specific validation?
3. **Closed-source LLM adaptation:** How can LoID be adapted for models that do not expose raw token logits (e.g., GPT-4 via API)?
4. **Systematic sign bias:** What causes LoID’s observed positive bias in sign coverage, and does it impair performance in domains with many negative effects?
5. **Natural OOD shifts:** Does LoID generalize from synthetic covariate shifts (single-feature quantile splits) to real-world OOD scenarios like temporal or geographic population shifts?

## Limitations
- Exact prompt templates used per dataset are not provided, hindering precise replication of prior means.
- Feature selection process for two datasets (Give Me Some Credit, Adult Income) is underspecified (GPT-4o prompt/criteria unknown).
- The assumption that LLM token logits directly encode directional feature influences may not hold across all models or tokenization schemes.
