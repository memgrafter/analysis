# On the Value of Tokeniser Pretraining in Physics Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05598
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05598
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** In-domain tokeniser pretraining reduces next-frame prediction error by 64% (VRMSE 0.158 vs. 0.439 from scratch) and freezing most parameters improves long-horizon rollouts.

## Executive Summary
This work demonstrates that pretraining a tokeniser with an autoencoding objective before training a transformer-based dynamics model significantly improves computational efficiency and accuracy for physics emulation. The benefit is highly dependent on domain alignment: pretraining on the same physical system yields the largest gains (64% error reduction), while pretraining on a mixture of other physics datasets provides a smaller improvement (19%). Furthermore, freezing the vast majority (98%) of an in-domain pretrained tokeniser's parameters during rollout training matches or exceeds the performance of a fully trainable tokeniser, with the gap widening for longer prediction horizons. These findings highlight tokeniser pretraining as a crucial, parameter-efficient component for scalable physics foundation models.

## Method Summary
The authors build a physics foundation model with a two-stage training pipeline. First, a tokeniser (based on MAGVIT-Simple) is pretrained as an autoencoder to compress spatiotemporal data (e.g., Euler fluid simulations) into compact latent tokens. The tokeniser uses causal 3D convolutions for flexible spatiotemporal compression. Second, a transformer processor—featuring factorised spatial and temporal attention—is trained to predict future frames from the tokenised context. The study systematically ablates the tokeniser: comparing no pretraining, in-domain pretraining (Euler), and out-of-domain pretraining (mixed other physics datasets), and for each, evaluates both a fully trainable tokeniser and a mostly frozen one (only 2% of parameters trainable) during the dynamics training phase.

## Key Results
- In-domain tokeniser pretraining slash variance-normalised RMSE by 64% after 10,500 steps (0.158) versus training from scratch (0.439).
- Out-of-domain pretraining (on a mixed dataset) still provided a 19% improvement (0.355), but freezing the out-of-domain tokeniser degraded performance below the no-pretraining baseline.
- For in-domain pretraining, a mostly frozen tokeniser (2% trainable parameters) matched a fully trainable tokeniser on next-frame prediction and outperformed it on multi-step rollouts, with the performance gap widening over longer horizons (steps 7–18).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Decoupling Representation and Dynamics
**Claim:** Tokeniser pretraining decouples representation learning from dynamics modelling, improving downstream training efficiency.
**Mechanism:** Autoencoding pretraining forces the tokeniser to learn a general-purpose compression of spatiotemporal data into compact latents. This separates the optimisation of the encoder-decoder (focused on reconstruction) from the transformer (focused on temporal prediction), preventing conflicting gradients that arise when both are trained jointly from scratch.
**Core assumption:** The objectives of minimizing reconstruction error and predicting future dynamics are partially misaligned; solving them sequentially yields better local optima for both.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "pretraining the tokeniser with an autoencoding objective prior to training the dynamics model enhances computational efficiency"
- [section 1] "learning both tasks from scratch simultaneously can impede the effectiveness of either process"
- [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers explore pretraining but do not systematically test decoupling via separate stages.
**Break condition:** If tokeniser pretraining uses out-of-domain data, the decoupling benefit diminishes because the learned latent space is misaligned with downstream dynamics.

### Mechanism 2: Domain Alignment Governs Benefit Magnitude
**Claim:** The magnitude of pretraining benefit is governed by domain alignment between pretraining and downstream data.
**Mechanism:** In-domain pretraining embeds physical characteristics (e.g., shock structures) into the tokeniser's latents, providing a well-structured initialisation for the dynamics model. Out-of-domain pretraining introduces latents tuned to different physics (e.g., buoyancy-driven rolls), which transfer less effectively.
**Core assumption:** Latent spaces are not universally transferable; their utility for dynamics prediction depends on semantic similarity of the underlying physics.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "magnitude of this benefit depends on domain alignment: pretraining on the same physical system yields the largest improvements"
- [section 3.2] In-domain reduces VRMSE by 64% vs. 19% for out-of-domain (trainable); out-of-domain frozen degrades below no-pretraining baseline.
- [corpus] Indirect; cross-domain transfer is discussed but not tokeniser-alignment effects.
**Break condition:** When pretraining and downstream datasets have minimal field or dynamical regime overlap, alignment fails and pretraining offers little gain or may hurt.

### Mechanism 3: Freezing Acts as Regulariser for Long Horizons
**Claim:** Freezing most of an in-domain pretrained tokeniser improves long-horizon autoregressive rollouts.
**Mechanism:** Freezing 98% of tokeniser parameters acts as a regulariser. The fixed encoder prevents overfitting to short-term reconstruction noise during rollout training, stabilising latent representations across many prediction steps and mitigating error accumulation.
**Core assumption:** Trainable tokenisers adapt to per-step reconstruction losses that are inconsistent with multi-step dynamics, introducing drift; frozen weights preserve a stable, high-quality latent manifold suited for long-term evolution.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "in-domain frozen tokenisers (2% trainable parameters) matched trainable ones on next-frame prediction and outperformed on multi-step rollouts, with the gap widening over longer horizons"
- [section 3.3] Figure 3 shows frozen in-domain consistently outperforms trainable at steps 7–18.
- [corpus] Weak; no neighboring papers examine freezing strategies for tokenisers in physics models.
**Break condition:** This benefit requires in-domain pretraining; freezing an out-of-domain tokeniser degrades performance below the no-pretraining baseline.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Autoencoding Pretraining**
  - **Why needed here:** The tokeniser is trained with an MSE reconstruction objective before any dynamics exposure, learning to compress high-resolution spatiotemporal data into informative continuous latents without pressure to model temporal evolution.
  - **Quick check question:** If the tokeniser were trained jointly with the rollout model, what conflict might arise between reconstructing current frames and predicting future frames?

- **Concept: Causal Spatiotemporal Convolutions**
  - **Why needed here:** The tokeniser uses causal convolutions to ensure each output token depends only on current and past frames, preserving temporal consistency for autoregressive prediction. Flexible compression (varying strides) adapts token size to downstream compute budgets.
  - **Quick check question:** Why can't non-causal (bidirectional) convolutions be used in a tokeniser for autoregressive rollouts?

- **Concept: Autoregressive Rollout Evaluation**
  - **Why needed here:** The dynamics model is assessed by repeatedly feeding its predictions back as input to generate multi-step trajectories, revealing error accumulation—a key failure mode for physical simulators.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the spectral error metric (NEPS) complement VRMSE in evaluating rollout quality across spatial scales?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Tokeniser (causal-convolutional autoencoder) -> Projection layer (linear) -> Processor (factorised attention transformer)
- **Critical path:** Tokeniser design (compression factors) -> Pretraining data choice (in/out-domain) -> Freezing strategy (fraction trainable) -> Rollout training budget (steps) -> Autoregressive evaluation (short vs. long horizon). Misalignment at any stage causes degradation.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Compression ratio: Higher strides reduce token count but lose fine-scale structure (high-frequency NEPS near 1.0).
  - Trainable fraction: Frozen tokenisers save 98% parameters and improve long rollouts but may limit adaptation to new fields.
  - Domain alignment: In-domain pretraining is costly per system but maximises gain; out-of-domain mixes data cheaply but yields smaller, fragile benefits.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Out-of-domain frozen tokeniser: VRMSE > no-pretraining baseline, spectral errors rise at mid/high frequencies.
  - All models: NEPS ≈1.0 at high frequencies, indicating inability to preserve fine details.
  - No pretraining: Slow convergence; after 10.5k steps VRMSE >0.4.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. In-domain vs. no pretraining: Train tokeniser on Euler then rollout; compare VRMSE at 10.5k steps. Expected: 64% reduction.
  2. Frozen vs. trainable (in-domain): Freeze 98% of in-domain pretrained tokeniser during rollout. Compare long-horizon (steps 7–18) VRMSE. Expected: Frozen outperforms, gap widens with horizon.
  3. Out-of-domain trainable vs. frozen: Pretrain tokeniser on Rayleigh-Bénard/active matter/shear mix; then rollout on Euler with trainable or frozen tokeniser. Expected: Trainable gives 19% gain, frozen degrades below baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the diversity of pretraining data (domain-specific vs. multi-physics mixtures) affect the generalizability and versatility of tokenisers across downstream physics tasks?
2. What mechanism causes freezing most of a pretrained tokeniser to improve long-horizon autoregressive rollout stability compared to a fully trainable tokeniser?
3. Why does freezing the tokeniser severely degrade out-of-domain pretraining performance, and can adaptive fine-tuning strategies (e.g., parameter-efficient methods) recover or surpass the benefits of trainable out-of-domain tokenisers?
4. How can physics-informed evaluation metrics—such as conservation law violations, symmetry checks, and temporal spectral errors—correlate with and improve upon spatial VRMSE for assessing long-horizon rollout quality and physical consistency?
5. What architectural improvements to tokenisers (e.g., inductive biases, capacity scaling, training objectives) can enhance high-frequency detail preservation and overall downstream accuracy in physics foundation models?

## Limitations
- Tokeniser architecture specifics (number of residual blocks, exact channel progression, flexible spatiotemporal compression sampling) are not fully described.
- Training details (sequence extraction stride, train/validation split ratios, learning rate scheduler mapping to steps) remain ambiguous.
- Evaluation protocol (order of VRMSE averaging, NEPS frequency binning) lacks precise definition.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Decoupling):** Medium confidence. Results show significant pretraining benefit, but the claim of conflicting gradients is inferential.
- **Mechanism 2 (Domain Alignment):** High confidence. Large performance gap between in-domain (64% reduction) and out-of-domain (19% reduction) directly demonstrates alignment dependence.
- **Mechanism 3 (Freezing Benefit):** Medium confidence. Frozen in-domain tokeniser outperforms trainable on long rollouts, but this effect is limited to well-aligned pretraining and lacks broader corroboration.

## Next Checks
1. Resolve tokeniser architecture ambiguities: Implement missing design choices (e.g., residual block count, channel growth) and verify that reconstruction MSE reaches reported low values and latent token dimensions match processor inputs.
2. Probe domain alignment granularity: Pretrain tokenisers on multiple curated subsets of Euler data (e.g., varying Mach number, density contrast) and evaluate transfer to a common downstream task to identify which physical features are essential for effective pretraining.
3. Measure latent representation drift: During rollout training, extract token representations for a fixed batch under frozen and trainable tokenisers; compute temporal drift (e.g., cosine similarity between successive steps) to test whether freezing stabilises latents and reduces error accumulation.
