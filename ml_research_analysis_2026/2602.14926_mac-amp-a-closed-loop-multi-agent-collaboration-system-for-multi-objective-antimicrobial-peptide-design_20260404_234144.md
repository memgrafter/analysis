# MAC-AMP: A Closed-Loop Multi-Agent Collaboration System for Multi-Objective Antimicrobial Peptide Design

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14926  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14926  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: MAC-AMP achieves superior antibacterial activity (0.943±0.008 for *E. coli* vs. 0.807) and lower predicted toxicity (0.154 vs. 0.251) with higher structural reliability (0.873 vs. 0.817).

## Executive Summary
MAC-AMP is a closed-loop multi-agent collaboration system that autonomously designs antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) by balancing multiple objectives—activity, toxicity, novelty, and structural reliability—through simulated peer review and adaptive reinforcement learning. The system uses specialized LLM agents to convert qualitative domain evaluations into executable reward signals, which then guide a PPO-trained generator in an iterative, stage-based optimization loop. Across five bacterial targets, MAC-AMP consistently outperforms prior models in antibacterial activity, toxicity reduction, and structural reliability while maintaining AMP-likeness and generating diverse, novel sequences. The framework also demonstrates cross-domain transfer applicability to table-to-text generation.

## Method Summary
The task involves multi-objective AMP design. Inputs: a target bacterium and a dataset of known AMPs with MIC values (from DBAASP v3 and dbAMP 3.0). Objective: maximize antibacterial activity (predicted MIC score), AMP-likeness (Macrel), structural reliability (OmegaFold pLDDT) while minimizing toxicity (ToxinPred). The method comprises four modules: (1) Property Prediction trains a target-specific MIC predictor (fine-tuned ProtBERT) and runs fixed tools to produce explicit signals S and auxiliary evidence V; (2) AI-Simulated Peer Review uses three Reviewer agents to score candidates on four dimensions, with an Area Chair aggregating into a meta-review T and consensus score Sc; (3) RL Refinement co-designs reward functions F via CS-based and Biomedical agents, using sandbox simulation and Pareto selection; (4) Peptide Generation fine-tunes a GPT-2 generator with PPO using the current F. The closed loop iterates over three stages, each with 15 PPO epochs, with reward functions adaptively redesigned based on aggregated logs.

## Key Results
- Superior activity: e.g., 0.943±0.008 for *E. coli* vs. AMP-Designer's 0.807.  
- Lower toxicity: 0.154 vs. 0.251.  
- Higher structural reliability: 0.873 vs. 0.817.  
- Maintains AMP-likeness and diverse, novel sequences; cross-domain transfer to table-to-text generation shown.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Translating Domain Evaluations to Stable Reward Signals
- **Claim**: MAC-AMP converts qualitative, multi-criteria domain evaluations into stable, executable reinforcement learning reward signals.
- **Mechanism**: The AI-Simulated Peer Review module produces a structured meta-review (T) and consensus score (Sc) from multiple reviewer agents. The RL Refinement module then iteratively designs a reward function F that combines explicit signals (Sa from MIC predictor, Sb from AMP-likelihood) with Sc, yielding a PPO-compatible scalar reward. This bridges the gap between free-text/heterogeneous scores and machine-actionable optimization signals.
- **Core assumption**: Domain-specific consensus can be reliably distilled into a mathematical reward function that aligns with multi-objective goals without manual weighting heuristics.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 3.1.3 detail the candidate reward design, agent collaboration, sandbox simulation, and Pareto selection. The corpus shows no direct prior integration of multi-agent consensus into automated reward code generation for molecular design.
- **Break condition**: If the Area Chair cannot resolve semantic conflicts (divergent Tags) or the rule-based validator rejects all candidate reward functions, the loop stalls.

### Mechanism 2: Closed-Loop Stage-Based Adaptive Optimization
- **Claim**: A closed-loop, stage-based adaptive process stabilizes training under conflicting biological constraints.
- **Mechanism**: The Peptide Generation module is trained via PPO using current reward F for a fixed number of epochs (a stage). At stage end, aggregated logs (Sa, Sb, Sc, T) trigger the RL Refinement module to redesign F for the next stage. This allows the reward to co-evolve with the generator’s capabilities, preventing early-stage reward hacking and enabling progressive focus shifts (e.g., AMP-likeness → safety → activity).
- **Core assumption**: Periodic reward reconsideration based on real-time batch-level feedback mitigates misalignment between static reward functions and the shifting policy distribution.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 3.1.3 describe the three-stage adaptive redesign. Appendix D shows reward–episode learning curves with Sa, Sb, Sc co-evolving without hacking.
- **Break condition**: If total reward variance collapses or one component degrades sharply while others improve, indicating misaligned stage transitions.

### Mechanism 3: Multi-Agent Consensus via Lexicon-Guided Review
- **Claim**: Multiple specialized LLM agents act as reviewers with distinct lexicons, and an Area Chair aggregates scores with a divergence penalty to produce a consensus score that reflects multi-dimensional quality without collapsing objectives.
- **Mechanism**: Three Reviewer agents (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5, Perplexity) evaluate candidates across four dimensions (EFF, SAFE, DevStruct, Orig) using weighted lexicons. The Area Chair combines their scores, introduces a divergence penalty (γ) based on Tag overlap, and outputs a meta-review T and consensus score Sc.
- **Core assumption**: A committee of diverse agents, guided by a structured lexicon, yields more robust and interpretable multi-criteria assessment than a single-model score.
- **Evidence**: Section 3.1.2 details the agents and aggregation. Appendix L.2 ablation studies show removing any reviewer degrades balance (e.g., removing GPT-5 increases toxicity).
- **Break condition**: If reviewer agreement (Tag state overlap) falls below a threshold, the divergence penalty γ approaches 0.6, heavily discounting the meta-score.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Property Prediction -> AI-Simulated Peer Review -> RL Refinement -> Peptide Generation (closed loop)

**Critical Path**  
1. User provides target bacterium and AMP dataset.  
2. MIC predictor is fine-tuned (ProtBERT) on the target-specific data.  
3. Initial peptide batch is generated.  
4. Property Prediction computes Sa, Sb and auxiliary values V.  
5. AI-Simulated Peer Review produces T and Sc.  
6. RL Refinement (inner loop) designs candidate reward functions F via CS-based & Biomedical agents, runs sandbox PPO, and selects best F by Pareto optimization.  
7. Peptide Generation performs PPO training for a fixed number of epochs (a stage) using current F.  
8. Aggregated logs trigger next RL Refinement; repeat for three stages (1→2→3).  
9. Final AMPs are output.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Stability vs. computational cost*: Three-stage adaptive optimization increases GPU hours (~47.61) but prevents reward hacking; fixed-reward baselines are cheaper but less stable.  
- *Interpretability vs. automation*: Structured logs and lexicon tags provide auditability but depend on LLM consistency; black-box RL would be simpler but less explainable.  
- *Multi-objective balance vs. complexity*: Including four properties improves real-world utility but requires careful reward fusion and more reviewer agents.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Reward hacking*: One component (e.g., Sa) rises sharply while others drop (e.g., toxicity spikes), visible in stage-wise reward–episode curves.  
- *Diversity collapse*: Generated AMPs show high sequence similarity (>90% Needleman-Wunsch) to each other or training set.  
- *Consensus breakdown*: Area Chair’s divergence penalty γ frequently hits 0.6, indicating persistent reviewer disagreement.  
- *Sandbox failure*: RL Reward Decision agent cannot find a candidate F that improves Pareto front in sandbox runs.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Module isolation test**: Run Property Prediction alone on known AMPs to verify tool outputs (e.g., lower MIC → higher Sa).  
2. **Synthetic consensus validation**: Feed fixed candidate sets to Peer Review to ensure Area Chair correctly computes meta scores and divergence penalties per Algorithm 1.  
3. **Closed-loop dry run**: With a tiny dataset (≈50 AMPs), run one full stage (15 epochs) to verify logging format, F update logic, and that total reward increases without immediate collapse.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Robustness to OOD shifts and evaluator biases**  
   - *Question*: How can MAC-AMP be made robust to out-of-distribution shifts and biases in upstream property evaluators?  
   - *Basis*: Appendix O identifies sensitivity to evaluators and OOD drift as a core limitation, proposing OOD-aware reward specification and adaptively calibrated lexicon weights.  
   - *Unresolved*: Current system lacks OOD detection or dynamic lexicon calibration, leaving it vulnerable to novel peptide distributions or skewed evaluator outputs.  
   - *Resolution evidence*: Systematically evaluate on test sets with known distributional shifts (e.g., unseen bacterial targets, synthetic peptide collections); measure reward stability and AMP quality; demonstrate improvement via OOD-aware reward shaping and auto-adjusted lexicon weights.

2. **Preserving sequence-space diversity**  
   - *Question*: How can MAC-AMP preserve diversity in the closed-loop multi-agent optimization to prevent premature convergence and maintain broad exploration?  
   - *Basis*: Appendix O warns that over long horizons the approach may favor easiest-to-optimize signals, narrowing exploratory diversity.  
   - *Unresolved*: No explicit diversity-preserving constraints are implemented.  
   - *Resolution evidence*: Define diversity metrics (sequence entropy, cluster coverage, pairwise dissimilarity); run extended training experiments with and without diversity constraints; show constraints maintain diversity without sacrificing objective gains.

3. **Generalization to other molecular design domains**  
   - *Question*: To what extent does the MAC-AMP framework generalize beyond antimicrobial peptide design to other molecular design domains (e.g., enzyme engineering, small-molecule drug design)?  
   - *Basis*: Authors highlight cross-domain transfer applicability but only demonstrate on a single non-molecular task (table-to-text); transfer to diverse molecular design spaces remains unevaluated.  
   - *Unresolved*: No systematic experiments on other molecular benchmarks.  
   - *Resolution evidence*: Adapt MAC-AMP with minimal domain-specific changes to multiple molecular design tasks (e.g., protein stability optimization, binding affinity improvement, catalyst design); compare success rates, sample efficiency, and robustness against task-specific baselines; analyze whether closed-loop multi-agent collaboration consistently improves outcomes.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter and architectural details (e.g., PPO clipping, MIC predictor architecture) are incompletely specified, hindering exact replication.  
- Exact agent prompts and rule-based validator logic are not provided, introducing potential variability.  
- Cross-domain transferability is only preliminarily demonstrated on table-to-text, with molecular generalization unverified.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism claims** (converting evaluations to rewards, closed-loop adaptation): **High**  
- **Benchmark superiority** (activity, toxicity, reliability metrics): **Medium**  
- **Generalization** (transfer to table-to-text, diversity preservation): **Low**
