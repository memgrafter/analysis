# COGNAC at SemEval-2026 Task 5: LLM Ensembles for Human-Level Word Sense Plausibility Rating in Challenging Narratives

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15897
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15897
- **Reference count:** 21
- **Primary result:** An unweighted ensemble of six closed-source LLMs using three prompting strategies achieved 0.88 accuracy and 0.83 Spearman’s ρ (average 0.86), ranking 4th; a post-competition ten-model ensemble improved to 0.92 accuracy and 0.85 Spearman’s ρ (average 0.89).

## Executive Summary
This submission addresses the challenging SemEval-2026 Task 5 on graded word sense plausibility rating within ambiguous narratives. The core approach uses multiple closed-source LLMs (GPT and Gemini families) with three prompting strategies—zero-shot, Chain-of-Thought, and comparative—without any model training. Predictions are aggregated via a simple unweighted ensemble. Comparative prompting consistently outperformed other strategies. The official six-model ensemble aligned well with aggregated human judgments despite high annotation variation, demonstrating the effectiveness of heterogeneous LLM ensembles for subjective semantic evaluation tasks. A post-competition expansion to ten models further boosted performance, highlighting the benefit of scale and diversity.

## Method Summary
The task involves predicting a plausibility score (1–5 Likert) for a homonym sense within a short narrative context, given a candidate sense definition and example. The dataset is AmbiStory (3,798 samples, disjoint homonyms across splits: train 2,280, dev 588, test 930). No training is performed; instead, the system queries multiple closed-source LLMs with three prompt templates (Appendix A–C) to generate scores. For comparative prompts, both options are scored and mapped to their corresponding senses. The final prediction per sample is the average of all model–strategy outputs. Evaluation metrics are (i) accuracy (fraction of predictions within ±1 SD of the human mean) and (ii) Spearman correlation between predictions and human means; the final score is their average.

## Key Results
- Official six-model ensemble: average score 0.86 (accuracy 0.88, Spearman’s ρ 0.83), ranking 4th.
- Post-competition ten-model ensemble: average score 0.89 (accuracy 0.92, Spearman’s ρ 0.85).
- Comparative prompting was the most effective strategy across models, while Chain-of-Thought often underperformed zero-shot.
- Ensembles of smaller models rivaled or exceeded individual large models, suggesting diversity outweighs sheer capability for this subjective task.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The ensemble’s strength derives from combining diverse model “perspectives” (different architectures, training data, and internal biases) to approximate the distribution of human judgments. Comparative prompting explicitly aligns with the annotation guidelines, which are inherently comparative (e.g., “less plausible than other meanings”), reducing scale inconsistency and anchoring bias that plague absolute scoring. Averaging across multiple independent models and strategies smooths out idiosyncratic errors and steers predictions toward the consensus human mean, effectively aggregating fragmented semantic intuitions.

Moreover, the superiority of comparative prompting suggests that forcing models to consider relative differences between senses mirrors the human annotation process more faithfully than absolute rating. This may also explain why Chain‑of‑Thought, which encourages analytical decomposition, often underperformed: plausibility judgments in ambiguous narratives rely more on holistic narrative coherence and associative semantics than on step‑by‑step logical analysis. The ensemble’s diversity in model scale and training further ensures that both large‑model nuance and small‑model efficiency contribute, with the unweighted average preventing any single model’s bias from dominating.

## Foundational Learning
- **LLM Ensembles:** Combining predictions from multiple models reduces variance and improves robustness, especially on subjective tasks where no single model captures the full range of valid interpretations. *Why needed:* Human judgments show high dispersion; ensembles approximate an aggregate. *Quick check:* measure prediction variance across models on a sample; high variance suggests ensemble benefit.
- **Comparative Prompting:** Using pairwise or listwise comparisons rather than absolute scoring improves reliability in LLM-based evaluation by forcing relative judgments. *Why needed:* Aligns with the task’s comparative annotation scheme and mitigates scale bias. *Quick check:* compare absolute vs. comparative prompt outputs on a held-out set; comparative should show higher correlation with human means.
- **Graded Semantic Evaluation:** Assigning plausibility scores on a scale captures degrees of acceptability in ambiguous contexts, reflecting real-world language use. *Why needed:* Binary classification is insufficient for nuanced narratives. *Quick check:* compute inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Krippendorff’s α) to confirm meaningful gradation.
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:** Encouraging step-by-step reasoning can improve performance on analytical tasks but may hinder subjective judgment. *Why needed:* To test if structured reasoning aids plausibility assessment. *Quick check:* analyze CoT rationales for over‑analysis of semantic features versus narrative coherence.
- **Annotation Aggregation:** Using the mean human rating as the gold standard compresses multiple valid interpretations into a single target. *Why needed:* Provides a single optimizing objective. *Quick check:* inspect distribution of human scores per sample; high variance indicates potential loss of information.
- **Closed‑Source API Reliance:** Using proprietary LLMs via APIs enables state‑of‑the‑art performance but introduces cost, latency, and reproducibility issues. *Why needed:* Access to cutting‑edge models without local compute. *Quick check:* monitor API quotas and latency; compare outputs from different provider defaults.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input sample (context, homonym, candidate senses) -> Prompt strategies (Zero-shot, CoT, Comparative) -> Multiple LLMs (GPT, Gemini families) -> Score extraction -> Ensemble averaging -> Final plausibility prediction.

**Critical path:** 1. Prepare AmbiStory dataset with structured fields. 2. For each model and strategy, generate API calls with exact prompt templates. 3. Parse JSON outputs; extract scores. 4. Average all scores per sample. 5. Compute accuracy (±1 SD of human mean) and Spearman’s ρ against human means.

**Design tradeoffs:** 
- Cost vs. performance: more models and calls increase accuracy but raise API expenses. 
- Closed vs. open source: closed models give high quality but reduce reproducibility; open‑source fine‑tuning could lower cost but may sacrifice performance. 
- Prompt complexity: comparative prompts are more effective but require handling two scores per call, complicating aggregation.

**Failure signatures:** 
- Malformed LLM outputs (non‑JSON) causing parsing errors. 
- API rate limits or timeouts leading to incomplete coverage. 
- Comparative prompts yielding identical scores for both options, eliminating discriminative power. 
- High variance in individual model predictions not being reduced by ensembling (e.g., if all models share similar biases).

**First three experiments:** 
1. Sanity check each prompt strategy on 10 dev samples: verify JSON output and score ranges (1–5). 
2. Run all individual model–strategy pairs on full dev set; compute accuracy and Spearman’s ρ to identify top performers. 
3. Construct a minimal ensemble (e.g., best three models) and compare against individual best; confirm improvement and measure variance reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does supervised fine‑tuning of a single open‑source LLM on the AmbiStory training set approach or exceed the performance of the multi‑model, multi‑prompt ensemble, while reducing computational cost and increasing reproducibility?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Limitations (Section 7) they “did not explore parameter fine‑tuning despite the availability of labeled data” and cite cost/time constraints for using only the development set with closed‑source APIs.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates that ensembling heterogeneous closed‑source models yields strong results, but it is unknown if a tailored, fine‑tuned model could achieve similar alignment with human judgments more efficiently. This is a critical trade‑off between general‑purpose LLM capability and task‑specific adaptation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative experiment where a representative open‑source model (e.g., Llama, Mistral) is fine‑tuned on the full AmbiStory training set (2,280 samples). Its performance (accuracy within 1 SD, Spearman’s ρ) would be evaluated on the test set and directly compared against the official 6‑model ensemble and the post‑competition 10‑model ensemble, with measurements of total inference cost and latency.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) prompting fail to consistently improve performance for this graded word sense plausibility task, and does this indicate a fundamental mismatch between structured reasoning and subjective, comparative human judgment?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 1 and Section 5.1 show CoT underperformed zero‑shot for 6 of 10 models. The authors speculate in Section 4.1 that “the task’s subjective, human‑perceived plausibility judgments likely limit the benefits of CoT, as step‑by‑step reasoning can steer models toward overly analytical interpretations that diverge from human intuitions.”
- **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis is proposed but not tested. It is unclear whether the CoT framework itself is ill‑suited, whether the defined reasoning steps are inadequate for capturing comparative plausibility, or whether the models’ internal representations of “plausibility” differ from human associative or narrative‑based intuition.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An error analysis comparing CoT vs. comparative prompting failures. This would involve: (1) Qualitatively analyzing model rationales from both strategies to identify systematic divergences (e.g., over‑reliance on syntactic analysis vs. narrative coherence). (2) A human study where annotators rate the quality of model‑generated CoT steps, correlating step quality with final score accuracy. (3) Ablating the CoT steps to see which, if any, contribute positively.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the diversity of model architectures, sizes, and training objectives (rather than sheer number of models) drive the observed ensemble performance gains, and is there a point of diminishing returns?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper ensembles models from three families (GPT, Gemini, Deepseek) of varying sizes. Table 2 shows that even an ensemble of the three weakest individual models (“gemini‑2.0‑flash, gemini‑2.5‑flash‑lite, gpt‑4o‑mini”) achieved an average score of 0.812, rivaling the best single model. The authors note “ensembles of small models often aligning better with aggregated human judgments than larger, more capable models.”
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear if the benefit comes primarily from diversity of “perspectives” (due to different training) or simply from averaging noise. The paper does not systematically vary ensemble composition (e.g., ensembles of only large models vs. only small models vs. mixed) to isolate the effect of heterogeneity. The optimal mixture for this subjective task remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled study creating ensembles with systematic variations: (1) Ensembles of models from the same family. (2) Ensembles of models within a narrow size range. (3) Ensembles mixing models with known different training paradigms (e.g., general instruction‑tuned vs. reasoning‑optimized). Performance on development/test sets, measured by average score and variance reduction, would identify the key diversity drivers.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Are the official evaluation metrics (Accuracy within 1 SD of mean human judgment, Spearman’s ρ with the mean) sufficient for capturing alignment with the *distribution* of human annotations in this highly variable task, or do they risk rewarding only prediction of the mean and ignoring annotator disagreement?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper highlights the substantial inter‑annotator variation (Krippendorff’s α=0.506, σ=0.946) and that ~65% of samples have a human rating range ≥2 (Figure 1). The accuracy metric uses a ±1 SD tolerance, but the authors note this “does not eliminate the ambiguity completely” as extreme ratings (1 or 5) may be excluded even when supported by some annotators. The metric is correlated with the mean, not the full annotation distribution.
- **Why unresolved:** The chosen metrics may prefer models that predict the mean plausibility, potentially penalizing models that capture the *spread* of valid interpretations (e.g., predicting a broader distribution of scores that matches human variance). The task’s core premise is that multiple senses can be plausibly rated differently by different people.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Proposing and evaluating alternative metrics that measure distributional alignment, such as: (1) Wasserstein distance between the model’s predicted score distribution (via multiple sampling or a model that outputs distributions) and the human annotation distribution per sample. (2) Metrics that measure calibration of prediction uncertainty against human rating variance. (3) Correlation of model prediction variance with human annotation variance across items.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the comparative prompting strategy’s superior performance stem primarily from its explicit alignment with the task’s comparative annotation guidelines, or from a more general ability of pairwise/listwise LLM evaluation to reduce scale inconsistency and anchoring bias inherent in absolute scoring?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state comparative prompting “encourages explicit comparison between competing senses and aligns model inference with the comparative nature of the annotation scheme” (Section 4.1). It consistently outperformed other strategies (Table 1). The annotation scale definitions themselves are comparative (e.g., “less plausible than other meanings”).
- **Why unresolved:** It is ambiguous whether the gain is specific to this task’s definition or a broader phenomenon. The “LLM‑as‑a‑judge” literature cited (Section 2) suggests pairwise comparisons are more reliable than absolute scoring. This paper’s result could be a specific instance of that general principle, or it could be uniquely effective here because the gold standard (human mean) is itself an aggregate of comparative judgments across senses within each story setup.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments decoupling the two factors: (1) Apply comparative prompting to a different graded semantic task with absolute annotation guidelines (e.g., emotion intensity scoring) to see if the advantage persists. (2) Modify the AmbiStory prompt for absolute scoring but retain the comparative reasoning structure, and vice‑versa. (3) Analyze if comparative prompting’s benefit correlates with the level of human disagreement per sample—does it help more where human ratings are more spread?

## Limitations
- **Model identity ambiguity:** Exact API names/versions for models like “gpt‑5‑nano” and “gemini‑2.5‑flash‑lite” are undisclosed, hindering direct access and reproducibility.
- **Undocumented decoding parameters:** Temperature, top_p, and max_tokens were set to provider defaults, which vary and change over time, affecting stochasticity and output consistency.
- **Comparative output alignment:** It is unclear how the two scores from comparative prompts are assigned to the corresponding sense definitions during ensemble averaging; this could alter results.
