# CORP: Closed-Form One-shot Representation-Preserving Structured Pruning for Vision Transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05243  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05243  
- Reference count: 38  
- Primary result: One‑shot structured pruning with compensation preserves ImageNet accuracy under up to 50% joint sparsity on DeiT models and yields up to 1.89× throughput with zero inference overhead.

## Executive Summary
CORP introduces a closed‑form method for one‑shot structured pruning of Vision Transformers (ViTs) that operates without labels, gradients, or fine‑tuning. The key insight is that naive removal of entire MLP hidden channels or attention Q/K dimensions introduces a systematic bias that accumulates across layers, collapsing accuracy. CORP models the discarded activations as an affine function of the retained ones, using a small unlabeled calibration set to solve a ridge‑regression problem. The derived compensation is folded directly into the layer weights, incurring no additional inference cost. On ImageNet with DeiT models, CORP maintains accuracy under aggressive sparsity (e.g., 82.8% Top‑1 at 50% joint pruning of DeiT‑Huge) and achieves tangible speedups (1.89×). The entire pipeline runs in under 20 minutes on a single RTX 3090 GPU.

## Method Summary
CORP performs post‑training structured pruning in a single pass using only a small unlabeled calibration set. Activation statistics are collected by forwarding the calibration data through the model. For each MLP layer, hidden channels are scored (e.g., by mean squared activation), and a subset is pruned; an affine predictor of the pruned activations is learned via ridge regression on the retained ones. For each attention layer, Q/K dimensions are similarly scored and pruned; a Sylvester equation is solved to approximate the pruned logits as a bilinear form of the kept dimensions. The resulting compensation matrices are algebraically merged into the existing linear weights and biases, resulting in a standard pruned model with no extra operations at inference. The process is fully closed‑form, avoiding iterative retraining or gradient computation.

## Key Results
- On ImageNet, DeiT‑Huge pruned by 50% in both MLP and attention retains 82.8% Top‑1 accuracy (vs. 84.97% baseline).
- Hardware throughput improves by up to 1.89× for DeiT‑Huge with zero additional inference cost.
- The complete pruning pipeline finishes in under 20 minutes on a single RTX 3090 GPU.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The primary failure mode of one‑shot structured pruning is uncompensated representation bias, not poor importance ranking.  
**Mechanism:** Removing entire MLP hidden channels or attention Q/K dimensions subtracts additive components from internal representations. Without correction, this error accumulates across layers, causing rapid accuracy collapse even at moderate sparsity.  
**Core assumption:** The pruned activations can be approximated as an affine function of the retained activations under the calibration distribution, due to low‑rank redundancy in Transformer activations.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “Without compensation, one‑shot structured pruning causes severe accuracy degradation.” [section] Sec. 3.1: “naive structured pruning fails. Removing entire channels introduces systematic bias that accumulates across layers.” [corpus] Prior post‑training methods focus on importance estimation (e.g., Hessian, activation magnitude) but ignore representation error, aligning with CORP’s hypothesis.  
**Break condition:** If the pruned subspace is poorly correlated with the retained subspace (low linear predictability), the affine predictor will fail and compensation will be ineffective under one‑shot constraints.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Closed‑form ridge regression provides an efficient compensation that exactly cancels the expected representation error.  
**Mechanism:** For MLP layers, fit an affine predictor \(x_P ≈ B x_S + c\) via ridge regression on calibration activations; for attention, solve a Sylvester equation to obtain \(M\) such that \(Q_P K_P^⊤ ≈ Q_S M K_S^⊤\). The closed‑form solutions are then folded into the original weights, eliminating any added inference cost.  
**Core assumption:** A linear (affine) model suffices to capture the dependence of pruned activations on kept ones; regularization \(\lambda\) prevents overfitting when the calibration set is small.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “models discarded activations as an affine function of those retained, using a small unlabeled calibration set to solve a ridge‑regression problem in closed form.” [section] Sec. 3.3: derivation of \(B = Σ_{PS}(Σ_{SS}+λI)^{-1}\) and \(c = μ_P - B μ_S\); Sec. 3.4: Sylvester equation \((A^⊤A)M(B^⊤B)+λM = A^⊤TB\).  
**Break condition:** Insufficient or non‑representative calibration data leads to poor estimates of second‑order statistics; if the true relationship is highly nonlinear, the affine approximation becomes inadequate, causing residual error after compensation.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Compensation folds directly into existing model parameters, yielding zero inference overhead and enabling real hardware speedups under the strict one‑shot post‑training regime.  
**Mechanism:** After computing \(B, c, M\), they are merged into the original weights (e.g., \(\tilde{W}_S = W_S + W_P B\) for MLP; for attention, factor \(I+M=RΣ^{1/2}S^{⊤}\) and update \(\hat{W}_{Q,S}=W_{Q,R}RΣ^{1/2}\)). The resulting model contains only standard linear layers with reduced dimensions; no extra operations are introduced at inference time.  
**Core assumption:** Folding the compensation preserves numerical stability and distributional assumptions of downstream layers; moderate retraining is unnecessary because the calibration‑guided correction already minimizes expected representation error.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “The compensation is folded directly into the layer weights, incurring zero additional inference cost.” [section] Sec. 3.5: “CORP completes pruning in under 20 minutes… introduces no additional computation at inference time.” [section] Table 3: throughput gains up to 1.89× on DeiT‑Huge without kernel optimizations.  
**Break condition:** Aggressive sparsity beyond information‑theoretic limits or severe distribution shift between calibration and deployment data can cause irrecoverable accuracy drops; large condition numbers in \(Σ_{SS}\) may amplify numerical errors during matrix inversion.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Calibration runner → Rankers (RankMLP, RankAttn) → Compensators (CompensateMLP, CompensateAttn) → Weight merger.

**Critical path:**  
1. Run a forward pass on a small unlabeled calibration set and cache activations (MLP: after GELU; Attention: Q/K before softmax).  
2. For each MLP block: score hidden channels, select top \((1-s_{\text{mlp}})\), compute ridge regression matrices \(B\) and \(c\), and update second linear weight and bias.  
3. For each attention block: score Q/K dimensions, select top \((1-s_{\text{attn}})\), solve Sylvester for \(M\), perform SVD folding to update \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\).  
4. Export the pruned model; no additional runtime overhead.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- \(\lambda\) (ridge regularization): larger values improve stability with small calibration but may under‑fit.  
- Sparsity split between MLP and attention: pruning attention yields higher accuracy per parameter; joint sparsity requires careful calibration size.  
- Activation‑ vs. magnitude‑ranking: negligible difference when compensation is used; activation‑based may be slightly better at very high sparsity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Naive pruning (no compensation) → steep accuracy drop vs. sparsity curve.  
- Very small calibration set → noisy compensation matrices; accuracy degrades compared to using a moderately sized set (e.g., 1024 images).  
- DeiT‑Tiny/Small → limited redundancy; even with compensation accuracy decays faster.  
- Joint sparsity > 60% → error interaction between MLP and attention not modeled; further degradation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablation (compensation vs. none):** Fix sparsity (e.g., 50%) and show huge accuracy gap with/without compensation across all model sizes.  
2. **Ranking comparison:** For MLP pruning, compare activation‑based and magnitude‑based ranking both with compensation; observe near‑identical curves, confirming compensation dominates.  
3. **Scaling & joint pruning:** Sweep sparsity for DeiT‑Tiny to Huge, compare MLP‑only, attention‑only, and joint; note that large models maintain accuracy up to 50% joint sparsity, while small models suffer, highlighting redundancy as a scale‑dependent property.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Joint MLP–attention compensation**  
   - *Question*: How can CORP be extended to jointly compensate for interactions between MLP and attention modules, thereby stabilizing accuracy under aggressive joint sparsity?  
   - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] "CORP applies compensation independently to MLP and attention modules. It does not model cross‑module interactions. At high joint sparsity, these interactions can compound errors and reduce stability. ... addressing joint MLP–attention compensation ... remain important future directions."  
   - *Why unresolved*: Independent compensation fails to account for error propagation across modules, leading to compounded degradation when both are pruned heavily.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: A unified compensation framework that leverages cross‑module calibration statistics to solve a joint optimization problem, demonstrating improved accuracy at sparsity levels beyond current limits (e.g., >50% joint pruning).

2. **Robustness to calibration distribution shift**  
   - *Question*: What techniques can improve CORP’s robustness to distribution shift between the small calibration set and the target deployment data?  
   - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] "The method relies on a small calibration set. Distribution mismatch between calibration data and deployment data may degrade recovery quality. ... improving robustness to calibration shift remain important future directions."  
   - *Why unresolved*: Compensation is derived under a specific calibration distribution; significant covariate or domain shift invalidates the affine and logit approximations, causing accuracy loss.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Methods such as calibration set diversification, domain‑adaptive representation recovery, or uncertainty‑aware compensation that maintain performance under measurable distribution shifts.

3. **Theoretical limits of one‑shot structured pruning**  
   - *Question*: What are the fundamental theoretical limits of one‑shot structured pruning in Vision Transformers, and can compensation schemes beyond affine approximations approach these limits?  
   - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] "Extremely aggressive sparsity still causes irreversible information loss."  
   - *Why unresolved*: Current methods assume low‑rank structure and linear recovery; beyond a sparsity threshold, information cannot be reconstructed even in expectation, indicating inherent representational constraints.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Information‑theoretic analysis of recoverable information under structured sparsity, combined with advanced compensation (e.g., nonlinear) that pushes achievable sparsity closer to theoretical bounds.

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters (calibration set size, ridge \(\lambda\)) are not disclosed; optimal values and sensitivity are unknown.  
- Evaluation limited to DeiT on ImageNet; applicability to other ViT families (Swin, PVT) and downstream tasks remains untested.  
- The affine compensation may degrade under severe distribution shift or very high sparsity (>60%).

## Confidence
- Uncompensated representation bias is the primary failure mode of one‑shot structured pruning. **High**  
- Closed‑form ridge regression provides an effective compensation that cancels expected representation error. **High**  
- Compensation folds directly into model parameters, yielding zero inference overhead and tangible hardware speedups. **High**
