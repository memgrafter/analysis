# Knowledge Distillation for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.13765
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13765
- **Reference count**: 10
- **Primary result**: Knowledge distillation with a 3B Qwen teacher to a 0.5B student retains 70–91% of teacher performance in English, up to 95% in Spanish, and up to 93.5% ROUGE‑L on code; chain‑of‑thought + GRPO further improves reasoning; 4‑bit GPTQ quantization reduces memory/latency with negligible loss.

## Executive Summary
Large language models are computationally expensive. This work compresses a Qwen‑3B teacher into a Qwen‑0.5B student via knowledge distillation on English Dolly‑15k, Spanish Dolly‑15k, and code datasets BugNet/PyTorrent. Hyperparameters are tuned on English and transferred. For coding tasks, the distilled model undergoes reinforcement learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on chain‑of‑thought annotated Codeforces data to improve reasoning coherence. Finally, post‑training 4‑bit quantization (GPTQ) is applied to reduce memory footprint and inference latency for on‑device deployment.

## Method Summary
The distillation objective minimizes the token‑wise KL divergence (Eq. 3) between teacher and student token distributions. Models and data vary by domain: English and Spanish use Dolly‑15k prompt‑response pairs; code tasks use BugNet (code correction) and PyTorrent (code generation). After initial KD, the coding model is fine‑tuned with GRPO, sampling multiple completions per prompt and rewarding factors like keyword consistency within `<think>` blocks, use of `Step *` markers, overall correctness, and appropriate length. Advantages are normalized group‑wise to stabilize training. The final student model (for any domain) is quantized to 4‑bit precision using GPTQ, which calibrates scaling factors on a small dataset to approximate the original weights with minimal reconstruction error.

## Key Results
- Student models retain **70–91%** of Qwen‑3B performance on English tasks, **up to 95%** on Spanish, and **up to 93.5% ROUGE‑L** on code after pure KD.
- Adding CoT‑GRPO boosts reasoning coherence and solution correctness beyond KD alone on the code dataset.
- 4‑bit GPTQ quantization significantly reduces memory usage and inference latency, enabling deployment on resource‑constrained devices with negligible accuracy loss.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Knowledge distillation with soft targets enables a small student model to retain most of the larger teacher's performance while using far fewer parameters.
- **Mechanism**: During training, the student minimizes the token‑wise cross‑entropy (or KL divergence) between its predicted token distribution and the teacher's distribution for every token in the sequence (Eq. 3). The teacher's soft probabilities act as "dark knowledge", providing a smoother training signal that encodes the relative likelihoods of incorrect tokens, which improves the student's generalization beyond hard labels.
- **Core assumption**: The student architecture is sufficiently similar to the teacher that it can represent the teacher's distribution; the data used for distillation matches the distribution the teacher was trained on; and the teacher's soft targets carry meaningful information (i.e., the teacher is not over‑confident or heavily mis‑calibrated).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract]: "by compressing them through knowledge distillation... student retained 70–91% of teacher performance in English, up to 95% in Spanish, and up to 93.5% Rouge‑L in code."
  - [Section 3.1]: "The token‑wise distillation objective is defined as the Kullback–Leibler divergence between teacher and student distributions: \(L_{KD} = -\sum_t \sum_v P_T(v|x_{<t}) \log P_S(v|x_{<t})\)."
  - [Corpus]: Neighboring papers such as "Reinforcement‑aware Knowledge Distillation for LLM Reasoning" (FMR 0.65) and "Accurate Knowledge Distillation with n‑best Reranking" (FMR 0.60) actively explore KD for LLMs, indicating broader community interest, though direct evidence for the claimed mechanism remains anchored in the present paper's results.
- **Break condition**: If the student's capacity is too limited relative to the teacher (e.g., depth/width gap too large), the KL divergence may not converge, leading to poor retention. Additionally, if the teacher's predictions are extremely peaked (low entropy), the soft targets behave like one‑hot vectors and provide little extra signal over standard supervised fine‑tuning, diminishing the benefit of KD.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Integrating chain‑of‑thought prompting with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on CoT‑annotated code data improves reasoning coherence and solution correctness beyond pure knowledge distillation.
- **Mechanism**: After initial KD, the model undergoes reinforcement learning with GRPO. For each programming problem, multiple outputs are sampled from the current policy. A reward function evaluates correctness, keyword consistency within `<think>` steps, presence of structured `Step *` markers, and appropriate length. Advantages are computed as group‑relative normalized rewards: \(A_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(r))/\text{std}(r)\). The policy is updated to maximize a clipped surrogate objective with an explicit KL penalty toward a reference policy (Eqs. 5‑7). This shifts the distribution toward longer, more reliable reasoning traces.
- **Core assumption**: The KD‑finetuned student already possesses a baseline level of code generation skill; the designed reward functions accurately capture high‑quality reasoning; and the reference policy provides a stable anchor that prevents catastrophic forgetting.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract]: "integrating chain‑of‑thought prompting with Group Relative Policy Optimization using CoT‑annotated Codeforces data improves reasoning coherence and solution correctness compared to knowledge distillation alone."
  - [Section 4.4]: "The reward signals consisted on several functions evaluating keyword consistency on `<think>...` subsequences, other keywords such as ‘Step *’, overall correctness and length generation, forcing the model to be precise, think procedurally and produce meaningful long steps to reason."
  - [Section 3.2]: GRPO objective (5) and advantage computation (7).
  - [Corpus]: "CoT‑Evo: Evolutionary Distillation of Chain‑of‑Thought for Scientific Reasoning" (FMR 0.52) demonstrates active research on CoT distillation, suggesting the approach is promising, though the present paper provides the primary empirical evidence.
- **Break condition**: Poorly calibrated rewards may incentivize spurious patterns (e.g., inserting `Step` tokens without genuine reasoning). An excessively strong KL penalty can stall learning, while too weak a penalty may cause policy collapse or deviation from the reference. Moreover, if the base KD model is weak, RL may fail to recover correct reasoning and could degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Post‑training 4‑bit weight quantization via GPTQ reduces memory footprint and inference latency substantially while preserving task performance, enabling on‑device deployment.
- **Mechanism**: GPTQ splits each weight matrix into blocks, then calibrates a scale factor \(s\) and zero‑point \(z\) on a small representative dataset to minimize layer‑wise reconstruction error using approximate second‑order information. Each weight \(w\) is mapped to a 4‑bit integer \(\hat{w}\) via uniform affine quantization (Eqs. 8‑9). The quantized model can be executed with integer operations, yielding lower memory use and faster inference.
- **Core assumption**: Quantization error introduced by 4‑bit representation is small enough that downstream task metrics remain within acceptable tolerance; the calibration set faithfully represents the activation distribution during inference.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract]: "Post‑training 4‑bit weight quantization further reduces memory footprint and inference latency."
  - [Section 3.3]: Quantization equations (8)–(9) and reference to GPTQ.
  - [Section 4.3]: "All distilled student models were further quantized to reduce memory footprint and inference latency, facilitating on‑device serving in resource‑constrained environments."
  - [Corpus]: The cited GPTQ work (Frantar et al. 2023) is a known PTQ method; neighboring papers in the corpus focus on KD rather than quantization, so direct corroboration is limited.
- **Break condition**: If the model is highly sensitive to precision (e.g., tasks requiring fine‑grained numerical outputs), 4‑bit quantization may cause a non‑trivial performance drop. Non‑representative calibration data can also amplify error. Furthermore, on hardware lacking efficient 4‑bit kernels, latency improvements may be minimal.

## Foundational Learning
**Knowledge Distillation**
- *Why needed here*: KD is the core compression technique used to transfer knowledge from the 3B teacher to the 0.5B student; understanding KD explains how the small model learns from soft targets.
- *Quick check*: What is the theoretical justification for using the teacher's softmax distribution (soft targets) instead of hard labels in KD?

**Chain‑of‑Thought Reasoning**
- *Why needed here*: The paper integrates CoT with RL to improve coding performance; CoT provides intermediate steps that make complex problem-solving more interpretable and amenable to reward shaping.
- *Quick check*: How does generating intermediate reasoning steps (CoT) potentially improve a model's answer quality on tasks requiring multi‑step logic?

**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**
- *Why needed here*: GRPO is the RL algorithm used to refine the model's CoT outputs; understanding group‑relative normalization and KL penalties is essential to grasp how the model learns from automated rewards.
- *Quick check*: In GRPO, why are advantages normalized by the group (batch) standard deviation instead of using a learned value function baseline?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Datasets (English/Spanish Dolly‑15k, BugNet, PyTorrent, Codeforces CoT) → Teacher model (frozen Qwen 3B/2/2.5) → KD module (token‑wise KL loss) → Student model (trainable Qwen 0.5B) → (coding tasks only) GRPO module (group‑relative advantages, KL penalty) → (all models) GPTQ quantizer (4‑bit) → Final student model.

### Critical path
1. Prepare teacher checkpoint and gather domain‑specific distillation datasets.
2. Tune KD hyperparameters (learning rate, temperature, etc.) on English Dolly‑15k.
3. Train student with KD loss across all target domains, using the English‑derived hyperparameters as defaults.
4. For coding tasks, perform GRPO fine‑tuning on CoT‑annotated Codeforces data.
5. Apply GPTQ quantization to the final student model.
6. Evaluate on domain‑specific metrics (ROUGE‑L, code correctness).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Teacher‑student similarity**: Using the same Qwen family reduces architectural mismatch but limits flexibility; different teachers may require embedding/positional adaptations.
- **Data domain**: Tuning on English and transferring to Spanish leverages multilingual pre‑training but may not generalize to low‑resource languages with distinct syntax.
- **RL vs complexity**: GRPO improves reasoning but adds reward‑engineering complexity and potential instability; simpler supervised CoT fine‑tuning is easier but often less effective.
- **Quantization level**: 4‑bit offers strong compression; lower bit‑width risks accuracy loss, while higher bits reduce memory savings.

### Failure signatures
- **KD divergence**: Student loss plateaus or diverges, KL between teacher and student stays large, performance far below teacher. Possible causes: insufficient student capacity, poor learning rate, or tokenization mismatch.
- **GRPO instability**: KL penalty term (Fig. 5 left) rises without bound, reward curve stagnates/drops, outputs become garbled. May indicate too large step size, poorly shaped rewards, or weak base KD model.
- **Quantization degradation**: After GPTQ, evaluation metrics drop sharply (>5% relative) or inference latency does not improve. Suggests over‑quantization, poor calibration data, or unsupported hardware kernels.

### First 3 experiments
1. **English KD hyperparameter sweep**: Train Qwen 0.5B on Dolly‑15k with LR ∈ {1e‑4, 5e‑4, 1e‑3} for 5 epochs; identify 5e‑4 as optimal via loss reduction and Rouge‑L.
2. **Spanish KD vs SFT**: Fine‑tune Qwen 3B on Spanish Dolly, then distill to Qwen 0.5B; compare against a supervised fine‑tuned Qwen 0.5B baseline. KD yields lower loss and higher Rouge‑L, especially early in training.
3. **Code distillation and RL**: Apply KD on BugNet (correction) and PyTorrent (generation) using Qwen2 models. Then, with Qwen2.5 on PyTorrent, add GRPO on CoT‑annotated Codeforces data; report gains in code quality and reasoning (longer, structured `<think>` blocks).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: Would domain‑specific hyperparameter optimization surpass the cross‑domain transfer of English‑tuned settings for Spanish and code distillation?  
*Basis*: The authors tuned all hyperparameters on the English Dolly‑15k dataset and reused them for Spanish and coding experiments (Sections 4.2, 4.3).  
*Why unresolved*: The study did not explore separate tuning; reported results may not be optimal for those domains.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Perform independent hyperparameter sweeps for Spanish and code tasks, then compare student performance against the transfer baseline.

**Open Question 2**: Does CoT combined with GRPO yield improvements in reasoning and generation for English and Spanish tasks beyond standard knowledge distillation?  
*Basis*: CoT‑GRPO was applied only to coding tasks using Codeforces data; English and Spanish experiments used pure KD (Section 4.4).  
*Why unresolved*: The potential benefits for general language tasks remain untested, leaving uncertainty about the method's broader applicability.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Integrate GRPO with CoT prompts into English and Spanish training pipelines, and evaluate changes in task‑specific metrics and qualitative reasoning.

**Open Question 3**: How does 4‑bit post‑training quantization impact the accuracy of knowledge‑distilled models across different domains?  
*Basis*: Quantization was performed after distillation with reported negligible degradation, but no detailed cross‑domain analysis was provided (Section 4.3).  
*Why unresolved*: It is unclear whether certain tasks (e.g., coding vs. language) are more sensitive to aggressive weight quantization.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Apply varying bit precisions (e.g., 8, 4, 2) to distilled student models and measure performance drops per domain, possibly correlating with model size or task complexity.

## Limitations
- Missing training details (batch size, sequence length, optimizer hyperparameters, KD temperature, GRPO β/ε, reward scaling) hinder exact replication.
- GRPO reward functions are described only at a high level; exact keyword patterns, length thresholds, and correctness criteria are unspecified, risking spurious incentives.
- GPTQ calibration data (size and domain) is not disclosed, making it hard to judge reconstruction quality and quantized model robustness.
- Evaluation is restricted to Qwen family models on English, Spanish, and two code datasets; no tests on low‑resource languages, long contexts, or other task types.
