# Mean Flow Policy with Instantaneous Velocity Constraint for One-step Action Generation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.13810  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13810  
- **Reference count:** 38  
- **Primary result:** MVP models a mean velocity field with an instantaneous velocity constraint to generate actions in one step, achieving state-of-the-art success rates (0.88±0.05) and high efficiency (10.9 ms inference, 153.6 iter/s throughput) on nine sparse-reward robotic manipulation tasks.

## Executive Summary
Flow-based reinforcement learning policies typically require multi-step ODE integration to generate actions, trading off expressiveness for computational cost. This paper introduces the Mean Velocity Policy (MVP), which replaces the instantaneous velocity field of standard flow matching with a learned mean velocity field. This modification collapses the multi-step integration into a single feedforward operation: an action is produced by adding a noise vector to the predicted mean velocity. A key theoretical contribution is the Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC), which acts as a boundary condition for the first-order ODE governing the mean flow. The IVC ensures a unique solution and enhances expressiveness. In an offline-to-online RL setting, MVP employs a generate-and-select (best-of-N) mechanism: for a state, multiple candidate actions are generated in one step, and the one with the highest estimated Q-value is chosen. This selected action is used for environment interaction and as the imitation target for the policy. The critic is trained via standard TD learning. Evaluated on nine challenging sparse-reward manipulation tasks from Robomimic and OGBench, MVP attains an average success rate of 0.88±0.05 and significantly outperforms prior flow-based baselines in training throughput and inference latency.

## Method Summary
MVP parameterizes the policy πθ(a|s) as a one-step operation: a = ε + uθ(ε, 0, 1, s), where ε ∼ N(0, I) is noise and uθ predicts the mean velocity over the interval [0,1]. Training minimizes two losses: mean flow matching (L_MF), which matches the predicted mean velocity to the true average required to reach a target action a*; and instantaneous velocity constraint (L_IVC), which enforces that at the boundary (r→t) the mean velocity equals the instantaneous velocity v = a* - a(0). The weight λ (set to 1.0) balances these losses. The policy is refined in an offline-to-online loop using a generate-and-select scheme: for each state, N candidate actions are sampled from uθ; the critic Qϕ (an ensemble) evaluates them and the highest-value action a* is selected. This a* is used to collect experience and serves as the target for updating uθ (via L_MF and L_IVC). The critic is updated with a TD loss using the same selection procedure for next-state actions. This approach avoids backpropagation through the generator, enabling efficient one-step action generation while maintaining strong performance.

## Key Results
- MVP achieves an average success rate of 0.88±0.05 across nine sparse-reward robotic manipulation tasks, surpassing existing flow-based RL methods.
- It delivers a training throughput of 153.6 iterations per second and an inference latency of 10.9 ms, representing speedups of an order of magnitude over multi-step flow baselines.
- Ablation studies confirm the necessity of both IVC (λ>0) and the mean velocity formulation (vs. standard one-step flow), which alone fail on complex tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mean Velocity Field for One-step Generation
**Claim:** Modeling a mean velocity field uθ(a(0),0,1,s) instead of an instantaneous velocity field enables direct one-step action generation from noise.  
**Mechanism:** The multi-step ODE a(1) = a(0) + ∫₀¹ v(a(t),t,s) dt is collapsed into a single feedforward step: a(1) = a(0) + uθ(a(0),0,1,s). The policy thus produces an action in one forward pass.  
**Evidence:** The derivation from Eq. (6) to (7) in the paper shows this collapse. Contemporary work on "mean flows" supports the concept, but the RL policy formulation with IVC is novel.  
**Break condition:** If uθ fails to approximate the true mean velocity (e.g., due to limited model capacity or poor training signal), the generated actions will be low-quality, degrading performance.

### Instantaneous Velocity Constraint Ensures Unique Solutions
**Claim:** The instantaneous velocity constraint (IVC) acts as a necessary boundary condition for the first-order ODE governing the mean velocity field, ensuring a unique solution and improving expressiveness.  
**Mechanism:** The mean flow identity (Eq. 8) is a first-order ODE. Without a boundary condition, this ODE admits a family of solutions differing by a residual constant C(a,r)/(r-t) (Theorem 2). The IVC loss L_IVC = E[||uθ(a(t), t, t) - v||²] explicitly enforces that at the boundary r→t, the mean velocity equals the known instantaneous velocity v = a* - a(0). This forces C(a,r)=0 (Theorem 3), selecting the unique correct solution.  
**Evidence:** Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 provide the mathematical foundation. Ablation studies (Fig 4, Table 4) show that setting the IVC weight λ to 0 causes a significant performance drop, confirming the necessity of the constraint.  
**Break condition:** If IVC is not applied (λ=0), the learned mean velocity field retains a persistent bias, leading to systematic errors and lower success rates, particularly on complex tasks.

### Generate-and-Select Reinforces the Policy Without Generator Gradients
**Claim:** A generate-and-select (best-of-N) mechanism, combined with the expressive mean velocity policy, enables effective policy improvement in an offline RL setting without requiring Q-function gradients through the generative model.  
**Mechanism:** For a state s, N candidate actions are generated in one step: a_i = ε_i + uθ(ε_i,0,1,s). The critic Qϕ evaluates all candidates, and the highest-value action a* = argmax_i Qϕ(s,a_i) is selected. This a* is used for environment interaction and as the imitation target to train uθ via the flow matching losses. Policy improvement stems from the advantage of best-of-N selection. Theorem 1 provides a lower bound on the value improvement: V^{π_new}(s) - V^{π_old}(s) = Δ_1 (positive best-of-N gain) + Δ_2 (fitting error).  
**Evidence:** Theorem 1 formalizes the improvement guarantee. Comparisons with one-step variants (Fig 5, Table 5) demonstrate that simply using a one-step standard flow (without best-of-N and/or IVC) fails on hard tasks, underscoring the necessity of this mechanism.  
**Break condition:** If N is too small or the critic Qϕ is inaccurate (large ε_Q), the best-of-N selection gain Δ^π_old_N diminishes, weakening the improvement guarantee and risking stagnation.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:  
  Noise ε ∼ N(0,I) → uθ(ε,0,1,s) → candidate action a = ε + uθ(...). Critic Qϕ(s,a) scores each candidate. Selected action a* = argmax Qϕ(s,a) used for environment interaction and as training target for uθ. Losses: L_MF (mean flow matching) + λ L_IVC (instantaneous constraint) update θ; L_Q (TD loss) updates ϕ.

- **Critical path**:  
  (1) Initialize uθ and Qϕ (e.g., from offline BC or random).  
  (2) Sample batch from replay buffer (s, a, r, s'), where a was previously selected via best-of-N.  
  (3) Policy update: For each state s in the batch, generate N candidate actions in one step via uθ; pick a* = argmax Qϕ(s,a_i). Compute L_MF with a* as target (requires JVP to compute d/dt uθ). Compute L_IVC using v = a* - ε. Update θ on L_policy = L_MF + λ L_IVC.  
  (4) Critic update: Obtain TD target r + γ Qϕ(s', a*_next) where a*_next is selected for s' similarly. Update ϕ on L_Q.  
  (5) Repeat.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - IVC weight λ: Higher λ enforces the boundary condition more strictly, improving accuracy but potentially harming training stability if loss scales mismatch.  
  - Best-of-N size N: Larger N increases the policy improvement gain Δ^π_old_N but linearly raises inference and candidate generation cost during training.  
  - Model capacity (width/depth of uθ): Must be sufficient to capture the complex mean velocity field; too small limits expressiveness, too large with weak IVC may overfit to erroneous solutions.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Collapse to unimodality: uθ outputs near-identical velocities for different noises, causing best-of-N candidates to be nearly identical and nullifying diversity.  
  - Training divergence: Very high λ or poorly implemented JVP can cause loss spikes or NaNs.  
  - Critic overfitting: Qϕ overfits to the current policy’s state-action distribution, leading to consistently poor best-of-N selections and hindering improvement.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **IVC ablation**: Train MVP with λ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1.0} on a challenging task (e.g., cube-triple-task4). Expect monotonic success increase with λ, and corresponding decrease in L_IVC.  
  2. **One-step baseline failure**: Implement a vanilla one-step flow matching policy (without IVC and/or best-of-N) and test on hard tasks. Expect near-zero success rates, confirming MVP’s design necessity.  
  3. **Efficiency benchmark**: Measure training throughput (iter/s) and CPU inference latency (ms with JIT disabled) for MVP versus FQL, BFN, and QC. Expect MVP to match/exceed FQL and be orders of magnitude faster than BFN/QC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Memory overhead** – The JVP operation induces significant GPU memory consumption. How can this overhead be reduced without compromising policy expressiveness or performance?  
2. **Generalization** – Does MVP’s advantage extend beyond sparse-reward tabletop manipulation to other robotic domains (e.g., locomotion, aerial control) or tasks with dense, shaped rewards?  
3. **Real-world deployment** – What are the practical challenges and real-world performance of MVP on physical robots, particularly regarding inference latency under hardware constraints, robustness to sensor noise, and safety during online execution?  
4. **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – How sensitive is MVP to λ (IVC coefficient) and N (best-of-N size), and can adaptive or learned values improve robustness and computational efficiency across diverse tasks?

## Limitations
- Missing task-specific evaluation details (e.g., success thresholds, horizon lengths) and incomplete baseline specifications hinder full reproducibility.  
- The training target a* is noisy due to best-of-N selection, which may weaken the theoretical guarantees provided by IVC.  
- The paper does not quantify the risk of mode collapse in the mean velocity model uθ, a potential limitation for diverse action generation.
