# Beyond Consistency: Inference for the Relative risk functional in Deep Nonparametric Cox Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23835
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23835
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** Ensemble subsampling estimator (ESM) for deep Cox models achieves valid inference (95% CI coverage near nominal) and improves predictive accuracy over standard survival methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses key gaps in deep Cox proportional hazards modeling: the impact of optimization error on population risk, control of pointwise bias, and reliable uncertainty quantification for ensemble methods. It introduces the Ensemble Subsampling Estimator (ESM), which trains deep neural networks on random subsamples and aggregates predictions. The authors develop a suite of theoretical guarantees: a nonasymptotic oracle inequality linking population risk to optimization gap, approximation error, and statistical error; a structured neural architecture achieving sharp L∞ approximation rates via overparameterization; pointwise and multivariate asymptotic normality for ESM under a broad range of subsample sizes, where the admissible range is determined by a bias–variance tradeoff and a single-overlap covariance decay condition weaker than prior assumptions; and consistency of an infinitesimal jackknife covariance estimator for Wald inference on log-hazard ratios.

Empirical evaluations on simulated data (n=800) and a lung cancer cohort demonstrate the practical validity of the theory. With subsample size r=n^{0.9}, the 95% confidence intervals achieve coverage close to the nominal level across linear and nonlinear hazard functions. Compared to CoxPH, RSF, GBR, and DeepSurv, ESM yields lower mean absolute error and shorter intervals, with particularly large gains under nonlinearity. On the real data, ESM attains the highest C-index (0.7316) and AUC (0.7987) and the smallest standard errors, underscoring its advantage for nonparametric survival analysis.

## Method Summary
ESM trains an ensemble of deep neural networks on randomly drawn subsamples of the full dataset. Each network is a ReLU-activated multilayer perceptron with architecture (input → 128 → 64 → 1), trained by minimizing the negative Cox partial log-likelihood using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with weight decay (0.02), dropout (0.1), learning rate 0.1, and 500 epochs. The subsample size is set as r = ⌊n^α⌋ with α ∈ {0.85,0.90,0.95} in simulations. Predictions for a covariate vector x* are averaged over B = 1000 fitted networks to form the ensemble estimator \hat{g}_B(x*). Uncertainty quantification is performed via the infinitesimal jackknife (IJ): for each observation, the influence of leaving it out is approximated from the ensemble variations, yielding a variance estimate \hat{σ}^2(x*) for pointwise inference and an analogous estimate for contrasts (log-hazard ratios). The method is evaluated on simulated survival data with 30% censoring and on the Boston Lung Cancer Survival Cohort, using metrics such as mean absolute error (MAE), coverage probability (CP), and concordance index (C-index).

## Key Results
- **Theoretical:** Nonasymptotic oracle inequality bridging population risk to optimization gap, approximation error, and statistical error; structured network yields sharp L∞ rates; CLT for ESM under broad subsample sizes with weak single-overlap covariance decay; IJ covariance estimator consistent.
- **Simulation:** For n=800 and r=n^{0.9}, 95% CI coverage near nominal under both linear and nonlinear hazards; ESM significantly reduces MAE and interval lengths relative to CoxPH, RSF, GBR, DeepSurv, especially under nonlinearity.
- **Real data:** ESM achieves highest C-index (0.7316) and AUC (0.7987) with smallest standard errors in the lung cancer cohort.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Optimization error from gradient-based training can be bounded in population risk for deep Cox models without requiring the exact empirical optimizer.
- **Mechanism:** A nonasymptotic oracle inequality (Theorem 2) links expected population risk to (i) the expected optimization gap \(Q_n(\hat{g}, \hat{g}_{\text{opt}})\), (ii) the optimal approximation error \(\| \hat{g}_{\text{apx}} - g_0 \|_\infty\), and (iii) a statistical error term scaling as \(\phi_n n^{\delta} L \log^2 n\). The inequality is self-referential but resolved by combining empirical process bounds for Cox partial likelihood with covering number arguments over the DNN class.
- **Core assumption:** The trained estimator \(\hat{g}\) belongs to the structured sparse network class \(\mathcal{F}(L, p, s, F)\) (Condition 2) and its optimization gap is controlled relative to the statistical scale (Condition 4).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "First, we establish nonasymptotic oracle inequalities for general trained networks that link in-sample optimization error to population risk without requiring the exact empirical risk optimizer."
  - [section] Theorem 2 and the ensuing inequality (4.2) formalize the bound; Condition 4 explicitly requires \(Q^{\text{opt}}_r \lesssim \phi_r r^{\delta} L \log^2 r\) at the subsample level.
  - [corpus] Weak anchor: corpus papers (e.g., "Debiased Nonparametric Regression") focus on debiasing for smooth functionals, not on bridging optimization error to population risk for Cox partial likelihood.
- **Break condition:** If the optimization gap \(Q_n\) is large relative to \(\phi_n n^{\delta} L \log^2 n\), the bound becomes vacuous and the population risk may not converge, invalidating downstream inference.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Pointwise bias for deep Cox estimators can be calibrated to be asymptotically negligible by controlling the network's approximation rate via overparameterization.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 1 shows that for a compositional Hölder-smooth \(g_0\), the optimal approximation error in \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) is \(O(\phi_n^{(1+\eta)/2})\) where \(\eta\) increases with the overparameterization exponent \(\delta > 0\). Choosing \(\delta > 0\) (undersmoothing) sharpens this bound so that the squared bias becomes negligible compared to the variance of order \(\phi_n n^{\delta} L \log^2 n\) (Corollary 1). This enables pointwise asymptotic normality after bias correction via the ensemble.
- **Core assumption:** The true function \(g_0\) lies in a compositional Hölder class (2.3) and the network architecture satisfies Condition 2 with \(\delta \in [c_{\text{eff}}(1-c_{\text{eff}})/(2-c_{\text{eff}}), c_{\text{eff}})\) for Theorem 3. Condition 3 assumes the trained subsample estimator's bias is dominated by the approximation error of \(\hat{g}_{\text{apx}}\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We then construct a structured neural parameterization that achieves infinity-norm approximation rates compatible with the oracle bound, yielding control of the pointwise bias."
  - [section] Theorem 1 quantifies \(\| \hat{g}_{\text{apx}} - g_0 \|_\infty\); Corollary 1 gives the pointwise MSE bound; Condition 3 explicitly ties the subsample estimator's bias to \(A_r(\hat{g}_{\text{apx}}, x^*) = |\hat{g}_{\text{apx}}(x^*) - g_0(x^*)|\).
  - [corpus] Weak anchor: Corpus titles mention "debiasing" and "bias-targeted," but they address different contexts (e.g., causal mediation) and not the specific bias-variance tradeoff in deep Cox models via network overparameterization.
- **Break condition:** If \(\delta = 0\) (minimax sparsity), the bias is \(O(\phi_n^{1/2})\) which is not negligible relative to the variance; asymptotic normality fails. If the network architecture (width/depth) does not satisfy Condition 2, the approximation bound degrades and bias remains dominant.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Ensemble subsampling with a properly chosen subsample size \(r\) yields asymptotic normality for deep Cox estimators under a relaxed single-overlap covariance decay, enabling valid Wald inference via an infinitesimal jackknife covariance estimator.
- **Mechanism:** The ensemble estimator (3.6) is a U-statistic with overlapping subsamples. Its Hájek projection decomposes it into a sum of independent influence terms plus a remainder. The leading covariance is the single-overlap covariance \(\zeta^{(1)}_r(x^*)\) (4.1). Asymptotic normality holds if \(r = n^\alpha\) where \(\alpha\) lies in an interval \((\alpha_{\text{lower}}, \alpha_{\text{upper}})\) determined by: (i) bias decay (requires \(\alpha\) large enough so approximation bias \(\lesssim \phi_r\) vanishes), and (ii) Hájek domination (requires \(\alpha\) small enough so the remainder variance vanishes). Condition 5 imposes \(\zeta^{(1)}_r(x^*) \gtrsim r^{-\nu}\) with \(\nu \geq 1\), which is weaker than prior assumptions (e.g., near \(1/r\)). The infinitesimal jackknife (3.8)-(3.10) consistently estimates the asymptotic covariance (Theorem 5).
- **Core assumption:** The subsample estimator's optimization error and pointwise bias satisfy Conditions 3–4 at scale \(r\). The single-overlap covariance decays at a polynomial rate \(\nu\) (Condition 5). The evaluation points lie in a high-probability set where pointwise MSE is controlled (4.3). For multivariate contrasts, the normalized covariance matrix \(R_r\) is well-conditioned (Condition 6).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We prove pointwise and multivariate asymptotic normality for subsampled ensemble estimators. We derive a range of subsample sizes that balances bias correction with the requirement that the Hájek–Hoeffding projection remain dominant. This range accommodates decay conditions on the single-overlap covariance [...] weaker than those imposed in the subsampling literature."
  - [section] Theorem 3 gives the CLT with \(\alpha \in (\alpha_{\text{lower}}, \alpha_{\text{upper}})\); Theorem 4 extends to multivariate; Theorem 5 establishes consistency of the IJ variance estimator under \(1/2 < \alpha < \alpha_{\text{upper}}\) and Condition 5.
  - [corpus] Weak anchor: Corpus includes "Estimation of heterogeneous principal effects" which uses U-statistic-like decompositions, but not the specific single-overlap covariance structure for deep ensembles.
- **Break condition:** If \(\alpha \leq \alpha_{\text{lower}}\), the approximation bias at subsample scale \(\phi_r\) is not negligible relative to variance, causing bias in the normal limit. If \(\alpha \geq \alpha_{\text{upper}}\), the Hájek remainder variance does not vanish, invalidating the linear approximation. If \(\nu\) is too small (slow covariance decay), the variance may not be adequately captured by the leading term.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cox proportional hazards model and partial likelihood**
  - **Why needed here:** The target estimand is the log-hazard function \(g_0(\mathbf{x})\) and contrasts \(\psi(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)})\) within this semiparametric survival model. The empirical risk is the negative Cox partial likelihood (3.1), which has a risk-set coupling structure unlike separable losses.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the partial likelihood (3.1) differ from a standard i.i.d. log-likelihood, and why does this complicate empirical process arguments?
- **Concept: Deep ReLU networks and approximation theory**
  - **Why needed here:** The function class \(\mathcal{F}(L,p,s,F)\) (2.2) is the estimation space. Its approximation capacity (Theorem 1) determines the bias term, and its combinatorial sparsity \(s\) impacts statistical error.
  - **Quick check question:** What does the compositional Hölder class \(\mathcal{G}(q,d,t,\gamma,K)\) (2.3) represent, and how does it relate to the network's layer widths and activation properties?
- **Concept: Ensemble subsampling and U-statistic asymptotics**
  - **Why needed here:** The ESM estimator (3.6) is an average over overlapping subsamples. Its distribution is analyzed via Hájek projection, where the single-overlap covariance \(\zeta_r^{(1)}\) (4.1) is key. The admissible \(\alpha\) range balances bias and variance.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does increasing the subsample size \(r\) reduce bias but shrink the admissible \(\alpha\) range for asymptotic normality? What role does the overlap covariance decay \(\nu\) play?
- **Concept: Infinitesimal jackknife for variance estimation**
  - **Why needed here:** Analytical covariances \(\zeta_r^{(1)}\) are intractable. The IJ estimator (3.8)-(3.10) uses the subsampling structure to consistently estimate the asymptotic covariance without additional model assumptions.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the IJ estimator correct for Monte Carlo variability from using \(B\) subsamples, and why is consistency ensured when \(B \gtrsim n\) (Theorem 5)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1.  **DNN Function Class:** Implement a sparse ReLU network architecture satisfying Condition 2: depth \(L \asymp \log n\), widths \(p_i \asymp n\), sparsity \(s \asymp n^{1+\delta} \phi_n \log n\), and output bound \(F \geq \max\{K,1\}\). The \(\delta > 0\) overparameterization is crucial for sharper approximation (Theorem 1).
  2.  **Ensemble Estimator:** For a fixed evaluation point \(x^*\), draw \(B\) random subsamples of size \(r = \lfloor n^\alpha \rfloor\). For each, train a network by maximizing the subsample partial likelihood \(L_r(g; I_b)\) (3.1) to a precision satisfying Condition 4 (optimization gap \(\lesssim \phi_r r^{\delta} L \log^2 r\)). Average the predictions to get \(\hat{g}_B(x^*)\).
  3.  **Variance Estimation:** Compute the infinitesimal jackknife quantities \(Z_{ji}(x^*)\) and \(bV_i(x^*)\) as in (3.8) for each \(x^*\), then combine via (3.8) for pointwise variance or (3.9)-(3.10) for contrast variance.
  4.  **Inference Module:** For log-hazard ratio \(\hat{\psi}_B = \hat{g}_B(x^{(1)}) - \hat{g}_B(x^{(2)})\), use the normal approximation with variance \(b\sigma^2_{1,2}\) to construct Wald CIs.
- **Critical path:**
  1.  Define target \(g_0\)'s smoothness: Determine if it falls in \(\mathcal{G}(q,d,t,\gamma,K)\) and compute bottleneck rate \(\phi_n = n^{-c_{\text{eff}}}\) (2.5).
  2.  Choose network architecture: Set \(\delta \in (c_{\text{eff}}(1-c_{\text{eff}})/(2-c_{\text{eff}}), c_{\text{eff}})\) to balance approximation bias and projection remainder (Theorem 3). Ensure \(L, p_i, s\) meet Condition 2.
  3.  Set subsample size: Pick \(\alpha\) such that \(\alpha_{\text{lower}} < \alpha < \alpha_{\text{upper}}\). Approximate \(\alpha_{\text{lower}} \approx \frac{1}{2-\nu + c_{\text{eff}}(1+\eta)(1-\xi_r)}\) and \(\alpha_{\text{upper}} \approx \frac{1}{\nu + c_{\text{eff}} - \delta}\). Practically, start with \(\alpha = 0.9\) and validate coverage via simulation (Figure 3).
  4.  Ensure optimization is sufficiently precise: Monitor \(Q^{\text{opt}}_r\) during training; if it exceeds \(\phi_r r^{\delta} L \log^2 r\), increase epochs or adjust learning rate.
  5.  Monte Carlo: Set \(B \gtrsim n\) (e.g., \(B=1000\) for \(n \sim 1000\)) to ensure IJ variance consistency (Theorem 5).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **\(\delta\) vs. approximation:** Larger \(\delta\) reduces bias (Theorem 1) but increases the statistical error term \(\phi_n n^{\delta} L \log^2 n\) in Theorem 2, potentially narrowing the admissible \(\alpha\) range (\(\alpha_{\text{upper}}\) decreases).
  - **\(r\) vs. bias-variance:** Larger \(r\) (higher \(\alpha\)) lowers subsample-level bias (\(\phi_r\)) but increases overlap covariance impact, requiring \(\alpha < \alpha_{\text{upper}}\) to keep Hájek remainder negligible.
  - **\(B\) vs. computation:** Larger \(B\) improves IJ variance accuracy but increases training cost. Theoretically, \(B \gtrsim n\) suffices; empirically, \(B=1000\) worked well.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Coverage collapse for large α (e.g., α > 0.95):** As in Figure 3, CP drops while MAE remains stable—indicates Hájek projection failure (\(\alpha \geq \alpha_{\text{upper}}\)) where dependence from overlap dominates.
  - **High bias/low coverage for small α (e.g., α ≤ 0.85):** Elevated MAE leads to undercoverage—indicates bias violation (\(\alpha \leq \alpha_{\text{lower}}\)) where approximation error at subsample level is too large.
  - **IJ standard error diverges from empirical SD:** Especially at extreme α or if B is too small (\(B \ll n\)), the Monte Carlo correction (factor \(n(n-1)/(n-r)^2\)) becomes unstable.
  - **Poor performance on nonlinear g0 versus linear:** As in Table 3, CoxPH fails under nonlinearity while ESM maintains coverage—confirms the model's nonparametric flexibility.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Verify admissible α range:** For a fixed nonlinear \(g_0\) (Case 3), run ESM with \(n=800\), varying \(\alpha \in [0.7, 0.99]\). Plot CP, MAE, and ratio "corrected SE / EmpSD" vs. \(\alpha\). Expect CP to be nominal only in a middle band, SE/EmpSD ≈ 1 only in that band (Figure 3).
  2.  **Benchmark against competitors:** With \(n \in \{800,1000\}\), \(\alpha=0.95\), compare ESM vs. CoxPH, RSF, GBR, DeepSurv on pointwise MAE, EmpSD, SE, CP, AIL for both linear and nonlinear cases (Table 3). Expect ESM to maintain coverage under nonlinearity where CoxPH undercovers.
  3.  **Validate IJ variance consistency:** For \(x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}\) with moderate contrast, compute \(b\sigma^2_{1,2}\) and empirical variance of \(\hat{\psi}_B\) over replications. Check that \(\text{Var}(\hat{\psi}_B) \approx b\sigma^2_{1,2}\) when \(B \gtrsim n\) and \(\alpha \in (\alpha_{\text{lower}}, \alpha_{\text{upper}})\) (Theorem 5). Failure if \(B\) is small (e.g., \(B=100\)) or \(\alpha\) is too close to 1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the ESM framework be extended to provide valid inference for the survival function S(t|x) and simultaneous confidence bands across time points?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "we will extend inference from the risk function g0(·) to the survival function S(t|x*). Since survival probabilities are nonlinear functionals of the log-hazard, the Gaussian limits for bg(·) enable pointwise intervals and simultaneous bands via an infinite dimensional delta method, accounting for uncertainty in both the DNN and the Breslow-type baseline hazard estimator."
- **Why unresolved:** The current theory only addresses inference for g0 and its linear contrasts. The survival function involves the baseline hazard λ0(t), which is estimated nonparametrically (e.g., Breslow), and the nonlinear exponential transformation. Joint asymptotic behavior and potential curse of dimensionality for simultaneous inference remain unaddressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive the joint limiting process of (bg(x), Λ0(t)) and apply the delta method to obtain limiting distribution of S(t|x); validate via simulation with simultaneous bands achieving nominal coverage; develop efficient covariance estimation for the joint process.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the full theoretical justification for using ESM to estimate conditional average treatment effects (CATE) in survival analysis, and how should variance be constructed for valid Wald-type inference?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion outlines: "the framework extends naturally to treatment effect estimation... define τ(t|x) = S1(t|x) - S0(t|x). Under standard unconfoundedness conditions, this can be estimated by applying ESM separately to the treated and control cohorts... A full theoretical treatment of this causal extension remains for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only treats estimation of g0 under random sampling; causal inference introduces treatment assignment, unconfoundedness, and potential positivity violations. The independence between treated and control cohorts simplifies variance but does not account for confounding or estimation of propensity scores. The behavior of ESM under overlapping treatment groups and the impact of optimization error on causal estimands are unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formal semiparametric theory for CATE with deep Cox models under unconfoundedness; proof of asymptotic normality for τ̂(t|x) incorporating both ESM variance components; simulation studies showing valid coverage under realistic treatment assignment mechanisms and positivity.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do common implicit regularization techniques (early stopping, dropout) interact with the Cox partial likelihood optimization and affect the validity of the proposed inferential procedures?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion notes: "Further work is needed to understand how implicit regularization from practical training strategies, such as early stopping and dropout, interacts with the Cox loss and affects inference."
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical framework (Theorem 2, Condition 4) bounds the optimization gap Qopt, but does not relate it to early stopping epochs or dropout rates. In practice, these methods control optimization error but may introduce additional bias or alter the sampling distribution of the estimator in ways not captured by the current oracle inequality. The coverage properties of ESM under such training regimes are not theoretically guaranteed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies quantifying the effect of early stopping patience and dropout probability on the optimization gap and resulting CI coverage; theoretical analysis deriving expected optimization error under early stopping based on stochastic gradient dynamics and incorporating it into the oracle bound; possibly modified IJ variance estimation to account for regularization-induced dependence.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can the subsample size r and overparameterization level δ be chosen in practice based on data, given their trade-off between bias control and H\'ajek domination?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The theory (Theorem 3, Condition 5) requires α ∈ (α_lower, α_upper) where these bounds depend on ceff, δ, ξr, and ν, which are unknown. The simulations use fixed values (α=0.85,0.90,0.95) without a data-driven selection rule. There is no guidance on tuning δ, which controls approximation sharpness and bias- variance trade-off.
- **Why unresolved:** In real applications, smoothness parameters (γ, ti) are unknown, so ceff and η cannot be computed. The admissible α range shrinks as δ changes, but no method is proposed to select δ or r to achieve desired coverage. The current approach may be sensitive to these choices, especially in finite samples.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a cross-validation or hold-out criterion that selects r and δ to minimize estimated mean squared error or maximize empirical coverage; perhaps an adaptive procedure that estimates the effective smoothness from the data (e.g., via hold-out risk) and then sets α accordingly; simulation studies comparing different selection strategies.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (optimization gap bound, single-overlap covariance decay) are not empirically validated.
- Critical implementation details (Cox partial likelihood with ties handling, subsampling scheme, real data preprocessing) are missing, hindering replication.
- Simulation true functions for Cases 2–3 are only verbally described, not provided as code, preventing exact bias calculation.

- **Pointwise bias control (Theorem 1)**: High confidence.
- **Ensemble normality & IJ (Theorems 3–5)**: Medium confidence.
