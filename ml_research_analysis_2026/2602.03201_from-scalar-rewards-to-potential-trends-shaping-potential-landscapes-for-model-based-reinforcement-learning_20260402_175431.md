# From Scalar Rewards to Potential Trends: Shaping Potential Landscapes for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03201
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03201
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SLOPE achieves 92.0% average success on ManiSkill3, surpassing DEMO3-semi-sparse by 18.0%, and reaches 100% success on real-robot Press Button task.

## Executive Summary
Model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) struggles in sparse-reward environments because predicting scalar rewards yields flat landscapes that provide no gradient for planning. This paper introduces SLOPE, which reformulates reward modeling as constructing informative potential landscapes via potential-based reward shaping (PBRS). SLOPE uses optimistic distributional regression to estimate high-confidence upper bounds, amplifying rare success signals into dense exploration gradients while preserving optimal policy invariance. Evaluated on over 30 tasks across five benchmarks, SLOPE consistently outperforms leading MBRL baselines. On ManiSkill3, it achieves 92.0% average success, an 18.0% improvement over DEMO3-semi-sparse. Real-robot trials show 100% success on Press Button and 65% on Grasp Cube, with significantly lower human intervention rates. Ablations confirm the critical role of both the optimism-driven landscape and training enhancements.

## Method Summary
SLOPE modifies the TD-MPC2 MBRL backbone by replacing scalar reward regression with potential-based reward shaping. The potential Φ(s) = η max_a Q(s,a) is derived from the value function estimate, and the shaped reward e_r = r + γΦ(s') - Φ(s) is used for both value learning and planning. To generate steep potential gradients even with sparse data, SLOPE employs optimistic distributional regression via a quantile-weighted cross-entropy (QCE) loss that penalizes underestimation more heavily (τ > 0.5). Additionally, training enhancements—including a three-phase seeding procedure, warm-started MPPI using the prior policy, and dynamic augmentation of the demonstration buffer with successful online trajectories—stabilize learning from sparse data. The resulting system maintains a dense, informative reward landscape that guides planning effectively.

## Key Results
- On ManiSkill3, SLOPE achieves 92.0% average success, surpassing DEMO3-semi-sparse by 18.0%.
- In real-robot trials, SLOPE reaches 100% success on Press Button and 65% on complex Grasp Cube tasks, with significantly lower human intervention rates than competing methods.
- Across 30+ tasks in five benchmarks (ManiSkill3, Meta-World, Robosuite, Adroit, DMC), SLOPE consistently outperforms leading MBRL baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Potential-Based Reward Shaping Transforms Flat Landscapes
- **Claim:** SLOPE replaces scalar reward regression with potential-based reward shaping (PBRS), transforming a flat, gradient-free reward landscape into a dense field that guides planning.
- **Mechanism:** For each transition, SLOPE computes a shaping term Δr = γΦ(s′)−Φ(s), where the potential Φ(s) = η max_a Q(s,a) is bootstrapped from the agent’s evolving value estimate. The shaped reward e_r(s,a) = r(s,a) + Δr is then used for value learning and planning.
- **Core assumption:** The learned Q-function provides a meaningful ranking of states even under sparse rewards, such that its gradient approximates a direction toward the goal.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states "shifts reward modeling from predicting scalars to constructing informative potential landscapes." Section 4.1 describes PBRS theory and the bootstrapped potential. Section 4.1 GridWorld example demonstrates dense gradients from a sparse goal.
- **Break condition:** If the Q-function severely underestimates values, the potential gradient vanishes; this motivates optimism.

### Mechanism 2: Optimism-Driven Distributional Learning Amplifies Success Signals
- **Claim:** Optimism-driven distributional learning amplifies rare success signals by biasing value estimates toward high-quantile returns, creating steeper potential gradients.
- **Mechanism:** SLOPE uses a quantile-weighted cross-entropy (QCE) loss: L_QCE = E_D[w(δ)·CE(Ψ(y), Q(s,a))], where w(δ) = τ·1_{δ<0} + (1−τ)·1_{δ≥0} and δ = predicted Q − target. Setting τ > 0.5 penalizes under-estimation more heavily, pushing the value distribution’s upper tail upward.
- **Core assumption:** An optimistic bias in value estimates yields a more discriminative potential landscape without causing catastrophic overestimation that diverges planning.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "optimistic distributional regression to estimate high-confidence upper bounds." Section 4.2 formalizes QCE loss and asymmetric weighting; Proposition 4.2 gives a contraction guarantee for η < (1−γ)/(1+γ). Section 5.6 hyperparameter analysis shows empirical best at τ=0.55, η=1.0 (exceeding theoretical bound).
- **Break condition:** Excessive optimism (τ too high) distorts the potential; insufficient optimism (τ≈0.5) yields a flat landscape.

### Mechanism 3: Training Enhancements Stabilize Sparse-Data Learning
- **Claim:** Training enhancements—multi-phase seeding, warm-started MPPI, and demonstration buffer augmentation—stabilize learning from sparse data and maintain a high-quality potential landscape.
- **Mechanism:** (i) Warm-start MPPI using the prior policy instead of random noise accelerates early planning. (ii) The demonstration buffer is dynamically augmented with successful online trajectories to combat dataset imbalance.
- **Core assumption:** Demonstration data provides initial coverage; online successes are rare but highly informative for shaping.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3 describes enhancements. Section 5.5 ablation shows significant performance drops when removed. Table 7 shows SLOPE uses more memory but achieves 92% success vs 74% for DEMO3-semi-sparse.
- **Break condition:** Low demo quality or absent online successes may limit buffer augmentation; warm-start might bias exploration prematurely.

## Foundational Learning
### Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (TD-MPC2)
- **Why needed:** SLOPE is built as a modification of TD-MPC2. Knowing its world model (encoder, dynamics, reward, value, prior) and MPPI planning loop is essential to see where shaping intervenes.
- **Quick check:** Explain why, in sparse rewards, the learned scalar reward model R(s,a) leads to a flat planning objective for MPPI; how does replacing it with a potential-based signal change the optimization?

### Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS)
- **Why needed:** SLOPE’s theoretical guarantee (policy invariance) relies on PBRS. Understanding the transformation e_r = r + γΦ(s′)−Φ(s) clarifies why the optimal policy is preserved regardless of the specific potential.
- **Quick check:** Given Φ(s) = η max_a Q(s,a), derive the shaped value function e_V(s) = V(s)−Φ(s) and show that argmax_π e_V = argmax_π V.

### Distributional Reinforcement Learning & Quantile Bias
- **Why needed:** The optimism mechanism uses a distributional value model (categorical support) and asymmetric loss weighting to push estimates upward.
- **Quick check:** Contrast a standard distributional RL loss (e.g., C51’s cross-entropy with a fixed target distribution) with SLOPE’s QCE loss. How does the weight w(δ) depend on the error sign, and what effect does τ have on the returned quantile?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Sparse-reward environment + demonstration dataset -> TD-MPC2 backbone (encoder h, dynamics d, reward predictor R, value predictor Q (distributional), prior policy π_prior) -> SLOPE modules (Potential calculator Φ(s)=η max_a E[Q(s,a)], Shaping module Δr=γΦ(s')-Φ(s), Optimistic learner with QCE loss, Training boosts: demo buffer augmentation, MPPI warm-start) -> Planner (MPPI) -> Policy π_θ
- **Critical path during an update:** 1. Sample batch from demo ∪ replay. 2. Compute shaped reward e_r = r + γΦ(s') - Φ(s). 3. Compute target y = e_r + γ max_a' E[Q_target(s',a')]. 4. Update Q with QCE loss: minimize weighted CE between predicted distribution Ψ(y) and target. 5. Update other modules (dynamics, encoder, prior) with standard TD-MPC losses. 6. Run MPPI with horizon H; initial distribution parameters from prior (warm-start). 7. Periodically add successful online trajectories to demo buffer.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - η (potential weight): Theory suggests η < (1−γ)/(1+γ) but empirically η=1.0 works best. Larger η yields stronger gradients but risks violating contraction.
  - τ (quantile coefficient): τ=0.55 optimal; higher increases optimism but risks bias; lower reduces gradient.
  - Φ(s') estimation: Use ensemble minimum for next-state potential to avoid over-optimism; use average for current potential to reduce variance.
  - Warm-start MPPI: Accelerates early search but may reduce diversity; keep moderate prior weight.
  - Demo buffer augmentation: Improves data balance but increases memory; avoid overfitting to narrow successes.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Persistent flat shaped rewards: Q estimates collapsed; check Q distribution variance, increase τ, inspect replay buffer class balance.
  - MPPI ignores potential term: |γΦ(s') - Φ(s)| too small vs |r|; ensure η not zero and Q-values reasonable.
  - Training instability/divergence with high η: Monitor L_QCE volatility; reduce τ or add gradient clipping.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **GridWorld ablation:** Implement 10×10 sparse goal. Run TD-MPC2 vs SLOPE; visualize shaped reward heatmap at iterations 10,20,40,100. Expect gradient to spread from goal.
  2. **Quantile sweep:** On a simple sparse task (e.g., ManiSkill3 poke-cube-sparse), train with τ ∈ {0.5,0.55,0.6,0.7,0.8}. Plot success vs steps; expect peak near 0.55.
  3. **Potential weight sanity check:** Same task, sweep η ∈ {0.4,0.6,0.8,1.0}. Confirm η=1.0 best but watch for instability at higher values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **η discrepancy:** Why does SLOPE achieve superior empirical performance with η = 1.0 despite the theoretical convergence condition requiring η < (1-γ)/(1+γ) (≈0.025 for γ=0.95)? What modifications would tighten this bound for practical settings?
   - *Basis:* Proposition 4.2 and Remark 4.3 acknowledge the gap; function approximation and sparsity likely relax it.
   - *Unresolved:* Finite samples and approximation errors change contraction dynamics; new bound needed.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Derive bound with approximation errors or run η sweeps measuring Jacobian spectral radius.

2. **Generality to other MBRL:** How generally applicable is SLOPE beyond TD-MPC2 and DreamerV3, especially with different planning loops (shooting vs. policy gradient) or world models (deterministic vs. stochastic)?
   - *Basis:* Validated on two backbones; not tested on PETS, MBPO, etc.
   - *Unresolved:* Interaction between shaping and planning may depend on value function usage.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Implement SLOPE in two additional MBRL frameworks with distinct planning and evaluate on common sparse tasks.

3. **Demo data requirements:** What is the minimal quantity and quality of demonstration data required to bootstrap SLOPE’s potential landscape, and how do these scale with task complexity (horizon, state dimension)?
   - *Basis:* Impact Statement highlights demo reliance; Appendix C.6 ablates quality but not quantity systematically.
   - *Unresolved:* Practical deployment needs data budget; fixed demo counts prevent general conclusions.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Sweep demo set sizes (1,5,10,25,100) on tasks of varying complexity; fit scaling law.

4. **Safety for autonomous operation:** Can optimism-driven exploration be combined with safety constraints to achieve fully autonomous real-world operation without human intervention, and what safety specifications preserve convergence guarantees?
   - *Basis:* Impact Statement identifies exploration risks; real-robot experiments still require intervention.
   - *Unresolved:* Optimism may push into unsafe states; safety constraints could break invariance or shaping gradients.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Integrate SLOPE with safe RL method (e.g., shielded MDPs) and evaluate zero-intervention success on real robots over dozens of seeds.

5. **Landscape geometry and online adaptation:** Do SLOPE’s reward landscapes exhibit quantitative geometric properties (gradient magnitude, curvature, smoothness) that correlate with learning speed, and can these adapt shaping parameters online?
   - *Basis:* Figure 12 visualizes landscapes qualitatively; no metrics linking properties to learning.
   - *Unresolved:* Understanding such relationships could enable principled tuning of η and τ.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Extract reward predictions during training, compute spatial gradient norms and curvature, correlate with episode returns across tasks/hyperparameters.

## Limitations
- **Theoretical-Empirical Gap:** The core potential weight η=1.0 used in experiments exceeds the contraction guarantee bound (η < (1-γ)/(1+γ) ≈ 0.025), with no explanation for why theory is violated without instability.
- **Reproducibility Gaps:** Exact demonstration trajectory counts for all tasks and the precise real-world human intervention trigger/policy are unspecified, blocking exact replication.
- **External Validation:** Cited neighboring literature has very low citation counts; the paper’s benchmarks are not independently validated, so claimed superiority relies primarily on its own evaluations.

## Confidence
- **Core Mechanism (Optimistic PBRS):** Medium. PBRS policy invariance is sound and GridWorld demo is elegant, but η discrepancy and Q-function break condition limit generalizability confidence.
- **Training Enhancements (Demo Buffer, Warm-start):** High. Ablations show significant performance drops when removed, providing strong empirical evidence.
- **Real-World Performance:** Low-Medium. Results are compelling but undefined intervention policy and limited task scope (2 tasks) reduce confidence in broad conclusions about reduced human intervention.
