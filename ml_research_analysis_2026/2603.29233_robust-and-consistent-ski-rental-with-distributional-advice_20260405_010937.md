# Robust and Consistent Ski Rental with Distributional Advice

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.29233
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29233
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Full distributional predictions enable both robustness (bounded worst-case expected competitive ratio) and consistency (performance approaches optimal as predictions improve). The Clamp Policy (deterministic) and Water‑Filling algorithm (randomized) achieve these guarantees, with experiments showing 5–20% consistency gains over point‑prediction baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper revisits the classic ski rental problem where a retailer must decide each morning whether to rent (cost 1 per day) or buy (cost b > 1) skis, given a predicted distribution over the number of skiing days. Unlike prior work that uses a point estimate, the authors assume the algorithm receives a full predictive distribution. They design deterministic and randomized policies that simultaneously guarantee a bounded worst‑case expected competitive ratio (robustness) and converge to the optimal expected cost when the prediction error (measured by Wasserstein distance) vanishes (consistency). The deterministic Clamp Policy simply clamps the optimal threshold derived from the predicted distribution to a safe interval, yielding an unconditional robustness bound and consistency under Lipschitz stability. The randomized Water‑Filling Algorithm optimizes a stopping distribution subject to linear robustness constraints, producing a geometric‑like pattern under monotone costs. Theoretical analysis is complemented by experiments on Gaussian, geometric, and bi‑modal distributions, demonstrating significant consistency improvements (5–20%) over existing point‑prediction methods while preserving robustness.

## Method Summary
The retailer observes a predicted distribution \(\hat{p}\) over the number of skiing days \(D\) and must output a decision rule (deterministic threshold \(t\) or randomized distribution \(F\)). Performance is measured by the expected competitive ratio (ECR) under the true distribution \(p\): \(\mathbb{E}_p[\text{cost}]/\mathbb{E}_p[\mathrm{OPT}]\).

**Deterministic (Clamp Policy):**  
Compute the optimal threshold \(t^*_{\hat{p}}\) that minimizes the predicted expected cost in \(O(n)\) time. Then output \(\tilde{t} = \text{clamp}(t^*_{\hat{p}}, \lceil \lambda b \rceil, \lfloor b/\lambda \rfloor)\) with a tunable \(\lambda\in(0,1)\). This ensures the threshold cannot be too small or too large, giving a robustness guarantee \(\text{ECR} \le 1 + 1/\lambda - 1/b\) for any true \(p\). Consistency follows because the expected cost and offline optimum are Lipschitz in the distribution: as the 1‑Wasserstein distance \(\eta\) between \(p\) and \(\hat{p}\) goes to zero, the ECR of \(\tilde{t}\) converges to the optimal ECR under \(p\).

**Randomized (Water‑Filling):**  
Enforce that for every possible realization \(D=d\), the expected cost does not exceed \(R\) times the optimum: \(\mathbb{E}[C] \le R \cdot \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}]\) for all \(p\). These constraints translate to linear inequalities on the cumulative distribution function \(F\): \(\mu(x)+(b-x)F(x) \le (R-1)x\) for integers \(x \in [1,b-1]\) and \(\mu(\infty) \le (R-1)b\), where \(\mu(x) = \sum_{t\le x} t\,f(t)\). Given \(\hat{p}\), the objective is to minimize \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{p}}[C] = \sum g(t) f(t)\) where \(g(t)\) is piecewise linear. The Water‑Filling Algorithm finds an optimal \(F\) by binary search on a water level \(h\): for a candidate \(h\), it places as much probability mass as possible on days with \(g(t)\le h\) while keeping the constraints tight. On intervals where \(g\) is linear, the mass fills geometrically; across gaps a single atom is added; the tail is handled by a feasibility test. When \(g\) is monotone, the optimal \(F\) has a closed‑form geometric pattern. The algorithm runs in \(O(n\log\text{ precision})\).

## Key Results
- **Clamp Policy:** Deterministic \(O(n)\) threshold computation. Robustness: worst‑case ECR ≤ 1 + 1/λ − 1/b regardless of prediction error. Consistency: as Wasserstein distance between \(p\) and \(\hat{p}\) vanishes, the ECR converges to the optimal ECR under \(p\).
- **Randomized Water‑Filling:** Provides the optimal stopping distribution under \(\hat{p}\) while strictly satisfying \(R\)-robustness. For monotone predicted cost functions, the optimal CDF is a truncated geometric distribution.
- **Empirical gains:** Tested on Gaussian, geometric, and bi‑modal distributions, the full‑distribution approach improves consistency by 5–20% over point‑prediction baselines (majority, mixture from Purohit et al.) while maintaining comparable robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The **Clamp Policy** achieves both robustness and consistency under distributional prediction errors.
- **Mechanism:** Compute \(t^*_{\hat{p}}\) from \(\hat{p}\), then clamp to \([\lceil\lambda b\rceil,\lfloor b/\lambda\rfloor]\). The interval prevents extreme thresholds that could cause unbounded competitive ratios, directly yielding \(\text{CR} \le 1+b-1/\tilde{t} \le 1+1/\lambda-1/b\). Consistency arises because changing the distribution by Wasserstein distance \(\eta\) alters expected costs by at most \(b\eta\) and the optimum by at most \(\eta\); thus if \(\eta\) is small, the clamped threshold nearly minimizes the true expected cost.
- **Core assumption:** Lipschitz stability of cost and optimum with respect to 1‑Wasserstein distance (Lemma 4.2).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “we propose the Clamp Policy … provably maintains a robust worst‑case ECR ≤ 1 + 1/λ − 1/b … achieving consistency”; [section 4.1] Definition 4.1; Lemma 4.2.
- **Break condition:** If the true optimal threshold lies far outside the clamp interval and \(\lambda\) is small, the robustness bound becomes loose (large \(1/\lambda\)). Consistency degrades when \(\eta\) is large relative to \(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{p})\).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The **Water‑Filling Algorithm** computes an optimal randomized stopping distribution that minimizes predicted expected cost while strictly satisfying robustness constraints.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm operates on the piecewise linear predicted cost \(g(t)\). Binary search finds the minimal water level \(h\) such that a feasible distribution \(F\) exists with support only on \(\{t: g(t)\le h\}\). For a given \(h\), feasibility is checked by “filling” probability from day 1 upward: on intervals where \(g\) is linear and the constraint must be tight, the recurrence \(F(x+1)=F(x)+\frac{F(x)+R-1}{b-1}\) (geometric growth) is used; across gaps a single atom’s mass is determined by tightness; after \(b-1\) the remaining mass is placed on a single day \(\ge b\) subject to a tail condition. The minimal feasible \(h\) is the optimal objective value.
- **Core assumption:** The constraints are linear and the optimal solution makes them tight on active support; for monotone \(g\) the active set is an initial interval, yielding a geometric optimal \(F\) (Theorem 5.2).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “We characterize the stopping distribution via a Water‑Filling Algorithm”; [section 5.3] Algorithm 2; Theorem 5.2.
- **Break condition:** The geometric fill relies on monotonicity of \(g\) within the active region; non‑monotonicities may require careful atom updates. Integer boundary handling and tail feasibility must be exact; otherwise infeasibility or suboptimality may result.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The **robustness‑consistency framework** unifies prior learning‑augmented approaches by using full distributional predictions, improving consistency (≈5–20%) while preserving worst‑case robustness.
- **Mechanism:** Both policies are robust: they satisfy a universal upper bound on ECR (Clamp: \(1+1/\lambda-1/b\); Water‑Filling: \(R\)). They are consistent: as the distance between \(\hat{p}\) and \(p\) (Wasserstein or TV) vanishes, the achieved ECR converges to the optimal ECR under \(p\). This decomposition (distribution‑free robustness term plus error‑dependent consistency term) follows from Lipschitz stability of cost functions.
- **Core assumption:** Cost functions are Lipschitz in the distribution; the offline optimum is also Lipschitz.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 2.4] definitions of \((\alpha,\beta)\)-performance; [abstract] “Our framework improves consistency significantly over existing point‑prediction baselines while maintaining comparable robustness.”
- **Break condition:** If predictive distributions are miscalibrated (e.g., overconfident), the Wasserstein error may be large and consistency gains vanish. The Clamp robustness bound depends on \(\lambda\); a poor choice can make the bound very large.

## Foundational Learning
- **Expected Competitive Ratio (ECR):** Ratio \(\mathbb{E}_p[\text{cost}]/\mathbb{E}_p[\mathrm{OPT}]\) under a distribution. It evaluates average performance when predictions are accurate, unlike the worst‑case competitive ratio. Quick check: If a deterministic threshold \(t\) yields expected cost \(f(t)=1.8b\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}(p)=b\), the ECR is 1.8. The worst‑case CR would instead maximize the ratio over all possible realizations of \(D\).
- **Wasserstein distance \(W_1(p,\hat{p})\):** Transportation metric quantifying the minimal cost to move probability mass from \(\hat{p}\) to \(p\). Measures prediction error and induces Lipschitz continuity of expectations. Quick check: For \(p=(0.8,0.2)\), \(\hat{p}=(0.9,0.1)\) on days \(\{1,2\}\), cumulative distributions are \(F_p=(0.8,1)\), \(F_{\hat{p}}=(0.9,1)\). Then \(W_1 = \sum_i |F_p(i)-F_{\hat{p}}(i)| = |0.8-0.9|+|1-1| = 0.1\).
- **Geometric distribution from tight robustness constraints:** In the randomized setting, requiring \(\mu(x)+(b-x)F(x) \le (R-1)x\) for all \(x \le b-1\) with equality for active points yields recurrence \(F(x+1)=F(x)+\frac{F(x)+R-1}{b-1}\). Solving gives \(F(x) = (R-1)\big((1+\frac{1}{b-1})^x - 1\big)\) until saturation, a geometric growth. Quick check: For \(b=50\), \(R=1.7\), compute \(\gamma = 1 + 1/49 \approx 1.020408\). Then \(F(1)=0.7(\gamma-1)\approx0.0142856\); \(F(2)=0.7(\gamma^2-1)\approx0.02887\), etc.
- **Lipschitz stability lemma:** For any threshold \(t\), \(|\mathbb{E}_p[C_t] - \mathbb{E}_{\hat{p}}[C_t]| \le b \cdot W_1(p,\hat{p})\); similarly \(|\mathrm{OPT}(p)-\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{p})| \le W_1(p,\hat{p})\). This enables consistency proofs.
- **Robustness‑consistency trade‑off:** A parameter (λ for Clamp, R for Water‑Filling) controls the trade‑off: smaller λ improves consistency potential but weakens the robustness bound (since \(1/\lambda\) grows); larger λ tightens robustness but may force thresholds near b, harming consistency.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  `Input \(\hat{p}\)` → `Choose deterministic/randomized` → `Clamp Policy` (Algorithm 1 + clamp) OR `Water‑Filling Policy` (binary search on h + geometric fill) → `Output policy` → `Metrics calculator` (ECR, robustness bound, consistency estimate).  
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Receive predicted distribution \(\hat{p}\).  
  2. If deterministic: compute \(t^*_{\hat{p}}\) via linear scan, clamp to \([\lceil\lambda b\rceil,\lfloor b/\lambda\rfloor]\).  
  3. If randomized: compute piecewise linear \(g(t)\) from \(\hat{p}\); binary search minimal feasible \(h\); run fill procedure to produce pmf \(f\).  
  4. Return decision rule.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Deterministic vs Randomized:* Deterministic simpler (\(O(n)\)) but cannot beat classic deterministic bound 2 when λ large; randomized (Water‑Filling) can approach \(e/(e-1)\) but requires \(O(n\log)\) and careful handling of integer constraints.  
  - *λ selection:* Small λ allows lower thresholds → better consistency when predictions accurate, but robustness bound \(1+1/\lambda-1/b\) degrades. Large λ tightens bound but may be overly conservative.  
  - *Prediction format:* Full distribution captures uncertainty and tail behavior, but demands more computation than a point estimate.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Clamp:* If \(t^*_p\) is far outside the clamp interval and λ is tiny, actual ECR may approach the robustness bound; if λ is too large, the interval collapses forcing \(t \approx b\), hurting consistency.  
  - *Water‑Filling:* Infeasibility if R is too small (feasible randomized ski rental requires \(R \ge e/(e-1) \approx 1.58\)). Incorrecting handling of integer boundaries may violate constraints \(\mu(x)+(b-x)F(x) \le (R-1)x\). Non‑monotone \(g\) may break the geometric optimality, yielding suboptimal solutions.  
  - *General:* Mis‑estimation of Wasserstein distance undermines consistency claims.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Deterministic vs point baseline:** For a skewed distribution (e.g., \(p(1)=0.8, p(5)=0.2\)) with \(b=50\), compute Clamp (λ=0.3) and compare its ECR to a baseline that buys on the expected value \(\mathbb{E}[D]=1.8\). Clamp should achieve lower ECR.  
  2. **Robustness under Wasserstein perturbation:** True distribution = discretized Gaussian (μ=90, σ=12). For target Wasserstein distances η = 0, 0.5, 1, 2, generate 25 perturbed \(\hat{p}\) by moving probability mass between random days. For each, compute Clamp (λ=0.3) and evaluate its ECR under the true p. Verify that the maximum observed ECR does not exceed \(1+1/λ-1/b ≈ 4.313\).  
  3. **Randomized Water‑Filling vs baselines:** Bi‑modal distribution: 0.7 on day 30, 0.3 on day 120; \(b=50\), \(R=1.7\). Compute optimal \(F\) via Water‑Filling and its ECR. Implement Purohit “majority” and “mixture” baselines using conditional means \(y_{\text{low}}\) and \(y_{\text{high}}\) as point predictions, then apply Water‑Filling to those one‑hot distributions. Compare consistency; Water‑Filling should improve by ~5–20%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the robustness parameter λ (for deterministic Clamp) or R (for randomized Water‑Filling) be chosen automatically, e.g., based on an estimate of prediction error, to achieve the best possible consistency while meeting a desired robustness guarantee?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The algorithms require λ or R as input but the paper provides no guidance on selecting them; they are treated as given tunable parameters.
- **Why unresolved:** Setting these parameters arbitrarily may lead to suboptimal robustness‑consistency trade‑offs or unnecessarily poor performance; a systematic method is needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical characterization of the consistency‑robustness frontier as a function of λ/R and the error distribution, together with a practical tuning procedure (e.g., cross‑validation or meta‑learning) that empirically achieves the desired trade‑off across various distributions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the robust‑consistent framework be extended to multi‑shop ski rental, where multiple rental shops with different purchase costs provide their own distributional predictions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The related work section cites multi‑shop ski rental with point predictions, but this paper focuses solely on the single‑shop setting; distributional advice for multi‑shop is not addressed.
- **Why unresolved:** Multi‑shop introduces combinatorial decisions and potential conflicts between predictions, requiring new algorithmic designs and analysis beyond the current single‑shop techniques.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design of a multi‑shop algorithm that preserves robust‑consistent guarantees (e.g., bounded worst‑case ECR and vanishing‑error consistency), rigorous proof of its performance bounds, and experimental validation on synthetic (and possibly real) multi‑shop distributions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the Water‑Filling Algorithm universally optimal for the robust randomized ski rental problem, or are there predicted distributions for which a non‑geometric stopping distribution yields a lower expected cost?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Theorem 5.2 establishes optimality of the geometric CDF when g is monotone, and Theorem 5.3 extends under a specific non‑monotonic condition; the paper does not claim optimality for arbitrary g, and the water‑filling algorithm’s optimality is not proved outside these cases.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a complete optimality guarantee, practitioners cannot be certain that the algorithm achieves the best possible consistency for distributions that violate the assumptions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Either a proof (e.g., via LP duality or convexity) that the water‑filling construction is optimal for all predicted distributions, or a counterexample showing suboptimality together with a full characterization of the exact class of objective functions g for which the geometric distribution is optimal.

## Limitations
- Theoretical bounds (e.g., Lipschitz constants) may be loose, leading to overly conservative consistency guarantees especially for large b.
- The robustness parameters λ and R must be chosen manually; the paper offers no adaptive or data‑driven method for setting them, which could hinder practical deployment.
- The Water‑Filling algorithm’s optimality is proven only for monotone or specific non‑monotone cost functions; its performance for arbitrary predicted distributions is not fully characterized.

## Confidence
- **Clamp Policy:** High (simple mechanism, unconditional robustness, clean consistency proof).
- **Water‑Filling optimality under monotonicity:** Medium (elegant but relies on several technical assumptions; edge‑case handling may be fragile).
- **Empirical consistency gains (5–20%):** Medium (depends on exact baseline implementations and Wasserstein perturbation generation, which are underspecified in the paper).
- **Overall framework (robustness + consistency with distributional advice):** High theoretical novelty, medium practical confidence due to parameter selection and assumption sensitivity.
