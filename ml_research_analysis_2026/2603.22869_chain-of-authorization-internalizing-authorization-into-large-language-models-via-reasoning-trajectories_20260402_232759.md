# Chain-of-Authorization: Internalizing Authorization into Large Language Models via Reasoning Trajectories

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.22869
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22869
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: CoA achieves near‑100% rejection of unauthorized queries while maintaining utility within 1‑3% of supervised fine‑tuning baselines, and reduces jailbreak attack success rates to near 0%.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often access data without regard to authorization, posing serious security risks from unauthorized leakage. The Chain-of-Authorization (CoA) framework addresses this by embedding permission labels directly into model inputs and requiring the model to generate an explicit, three‑stage authorization reasoning trajectory (resource review, identity resolution, decision‑making) before producing a final response. This trajectory is internalized via supervised fine‑uning on synthetic data covering matched, mismatched, and public authorization states. CoA preserves task performance comparable to standard fine‑tuning while enforcing strict access control, and demonstrates robust resistance to adversarial jailbreak prompts.

## Method Summary
CoA restructures input `X` by injecting user permissions `C_u` and tool permissions `C_g` into a system prompt, and annotating external context `E` with permission labels `C_e`. The model is trained to autoregressively generate an authorization trajectory `T`—comprising resource review (`T_res`), identity resolution (`T_id`), and decision (`T_dec`)—followed by the response `Y`. The training objective `L(θ) = −∑ log πθ(Y,T|X)` binds the trajectory and response, forcing the model to internalize access policy as part of its generation logic. At inference, the model must first reason about the match between required permissions (from `Q`, `E`, `G`) and the user’s `C_u`; only a `match` decision yields a normal answer, while `mismatch` or `public` triggers a refusal.

## Key Results
- **Utility preservation**: On authorized (match) queries, CoA’s accuracy is within 1‑3% of supervised fine‑tuning baselines across WMDP, MMLU, SQuAD, CovidQA, and Mobile‑Actions.
- **Near‑perfect rejection**: On unauthorized (mismatch/public) queries, CoA achieves >99% rejection rates across all datasets and model sizes.
- **Adversarial robustness**: Under jailbreak prompts (style injection, prefix injection, PAIR), CoA reduces attack success rates to near 0% (e.g., 0.14% on style injection for Llama‑3‑8B).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Embedding permission labels into the input establishes a structured causal context that forces the model to consider authorization boundaries from the start of generation.
- **Mechanism**: The input is restructured as `X = Prompt_sys(Cu,Cg) ⊕ Context(E,Ce) ⊕ Q`, explicitly injecting permission markers `C`.
- **Evidence**: [abstract] "embeds permission context at input"; [section 2.2.1] details input reformulation and Equation (1).
- **Break condition**: If model representations at the prompt end do not separate authorization states (e.g., Figure 5 shows overlap at "End of Prompt"), input labels alone are insufficient without the trajectory.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Forcing an explicit authorization reasoning trajectory `T` as a causal prefix before the final response `Y` makes policy evaluation an intrinsic, unskippable step.
- **Mechanism**: The joint distribution `πθ(Y,T|X)` ensures the trajectory causally conditions the response and embodies the policy function `ϕ`.
- **Evidence**: [abstract] "requiring generating explicit authorization reasoning trajectory ... before responses"; [section 2.2] Equation (2) and trajectory stages; [section 3.5] intervention experiments (Figure 6) show logical conflict in any trajectory stage redirects the response to rejection.
- **Break condition**: If the model can generate correct `Y` without a correct `T` (e.g., skipped or inconsistent trajectory), the mechanism fails; intervention results show this does not occur.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Supervised fine‑tuning on synthetic data covering matched, mismatched, and public states internalizes the policy function into model parameters.
- **Mechanism**: The loss `L(θ) = −∑ log πθ(Y,T|X)` binds the trajectory and response, forcing the model to learn permission boundaries.
- **Evidence**: [abstract] "trained via supervised fine‑tuning on data covering matched, mismatched, and public states"; [section 2.2.2] learning paradigm and Equation (4); [section 3.2] near‑100% rejection in unauthorized states and maintained utility (Figures 3,4, Table 1).
- **Break condition**: If rejection rates in mismatch/public states are low (like SFT/Base in Figure 4) or utility drops in match state, the training paradigm is insufficient.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Access Control as a Cognitive Task
- **Why needed**: CoA reframes access control from an external filter to an internal cognitive step, explaining why LLMs must "learn" authorization rather than have it bolted on.
- **Quick check**: What are the two main limitations of existing methods (structural isolation and prompt guidance) that CoA overcomes by internalizing the policy?

- **Why needed**: CoA adapts CoT’s intermediate reasoning to the security domain; recognizing this helps understand the trajectory’s structure (resource review, identity resolution, decision‑making).
- **Quick check**: How does CoA’s reasoning trajectory differ in purpose from a standard CoT used for arithmetic or commonsense reasoning?

### Concept: Supervised Fine‑Tuning for Policy Alignment
- **Why needed**: CoA uses SFT on multi‑state data to embed policy, contrasting with RLHF or prompt engineering for safety.
- **Quick check**: Why does CoA require three distinct authorization states (matched, mismatched, public) in its training data, and what would happen if one were missing?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input Preprocessor -> CoA‑Tuned LLM (Permission Labeling Service external)

### Critical path
1. **Input structuring**: Inject `C_u`, `C_g` into system prompt; annotate `E` with `C_e`.
2. **Forward generation**: Model autoregressively produces `T_res` (reviews required permissions from `Q`, `E`, `G`), `T_id` (resolves user `C_u` and tool `C_g`), `T_dec` (computes `ϕ(C_req, C_u)` and decides match/mismatch/public), then `Y` conditioned on `T_dec`.
3. **Output**: Full sequence `T` + `Y` returned.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Utility vs. security**: Aim for minimal utility loss (<3% vs. SFT) while achieving ~100% rejection; requires high‑quality training data for authorized states.
- **Training cost**: Requires synthesizing three‑state data and fine‑tuning, more expensive than standard SFT/prompt methods.
- **Vocabulary expansion**: Adding permission tokens increases embedding size; improper initialization may affect performance.
- **Model size dependency**: Larger models (e.g., 8B vs. 1.7B) show more consistent rejection, but smaller models still work—tradeoff between robustness and inference cost.

### Failure signatures
- **Security failure**: Rejection rate <<100% in mismatch/public states (e.g., SFT in Figure 4).
- **Utility failure**: Accuracy drop >3% in match state vs. SFT (Table 1).
- **Representational failure**: t‑SNE (Figure 5) shows overlapping clusters for match/mismatch at reasoning end.
- **Causal failure**: Intervention (Figure 6) shows modifying `T_dec` does not change `Y`—trajectory ignored.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Authorized utility benchmark**: Run CoA on WMDP, MMLU, SQuAD, etc. under match state; expect accuracy within 1‑3% of SFT baseline (Table 1).
2. **Unauthorized rejection test**: Evaluate on mismatch and public states across datasets; expect rejection rate >99% (Figures 3,4).
3. **Adversarial robustness stress test**: Apply jailbreak prompts (style injection, prefix injection, PAIR) on mismatch state; expect attack success rate (ASR) near 0% (Tables 2,3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scalability to large permission spaces**: How does CoA scale to real‑world scenarios with thousands of fine‑grained, dynamically changing permission labels and hierarchical access control? The paper lacks evidence on performance degradation or data requirements as permission space expands.
- **Inference latency overhead**: What is the computational overhead and latency impact of generating explicit authorization reasoning trajectories during inference, and how does it trade off against security‑utility gains? The paper reports no metrics on speed, memory, or cost per query.
- **Architectural generalizability**: Can CoA’s internalization mechanism be effectively transferred to non‑autoregressive or encoder‑decoder model architectures, and does it require architectural modifications? All experiments use decoder‑only models; applicability to others remains untested.
- **Adaptation to policy evolution**: How can CoA adapt to evolving permission policies (e.g., role changes, new access rules) without full retraining, and what is its robustness to poisoning attacks on the permission label injection? The paper assumes static permission mappings and trustworthy label injection.

## Limitations
- Synthetic training data may not capture real‑world authorization complexity.
- Evaluation covers only five datasets; generalization to other domains (e.g., biomedical, legal) is unverified.
- Inference cost of generating the full trajectory is not reported; could impact practical deployment.
- System depends on an external permission labeling service; labeling errors could break security guarantees.
- No probing analysis confirms the model truly internalizes policy rather than exploiting superficial token patterns.

## Confidence
- **Utility preservation** (accuracy within 1‑3% of SFT across benchmarks): **High** – consistent empirical results over multiple datasets and model sizes.
- **Unauthorized rejection** (near 100% in mismatch/public states): **High** – rejection rates exceed 99% in all tested settings.
- **Adversarial robustness** (ASR near 0% on tested jailbreaks): **Medium** – evaluated on known jailbreak prompts; future or adaptive attacks may behave differently.

## Next Checks
1. **Out‑of‑domain generalization**: Test CoA on at least two entirely new domains (e.g., medical records, legal contracts) without further fine‑tuning. Measure task accuracy and rejection rates to map transfer boundaries.
2. **Trajectory necessity ablation**: Train a variant that receives permission labels but is not forced to generate the three‑stage reasoning trajectory. Compare rejection rates on mismatched/public states against full CoA; a significant drop would confirm the trajectory’s causal role.
3. **Comprehensive adversarial suite**: Evaluate CoA against a broader set of jailbreak methods, including multi‑turn attacks, adaptive attacks (AutoDAN, GCG), and role‑playing scenarios, across several model architectures. Report attack success rates to quantify robustness under a more realistic threat model.
