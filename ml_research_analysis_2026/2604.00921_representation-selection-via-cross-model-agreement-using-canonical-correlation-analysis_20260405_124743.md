# Representation Selection via Cross-Model Agreement using Canonical Correlation Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.00921
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00921
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: CCA-based representation selection yields consistent improvements over unprojected and PCA-reduced baselines, with up to 12.6% accuracy gain via post-hoc transfer of fine-tuning signal, but fails on severely imbalanced data.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of overcomplete, model-specific representations in pre-trained vision encoders. It proposes a training-free method that uses canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to align two independently pre-trained encoders, identifying linearly correlated subspaces that capture shared semantic structure. By projecting representations onto these subspaces, the method performs post-hoc representation selection and refinement based on cross-model agreement. Evaluations on multiple benchmarks (ImageNet-1k, CIFAR-100, MNIST) show that CCA projections outperform both unprojected representations and PCA-based reduction, achieving significant dimensionality reductions (over 75%) while improving accuracy. The approach also enables a frozen model to benefit from another model's fine-tuning signal, boosting its accuracy by up to 12.6%. However, the method is sensitive to class imbalance, as demonstrated by failure on Caltech-101.

## Method Summary
Given two pre-trained vision encoders, the method first extracts frozen embeddings from a shared dataset (e.g., a subset of training data). It then computes canonical correlation analysis (CCA) on these embeddings to obtain linear projection matrices for each encoder that map their representations into a joint subspace where the dimensions are maximally correlated. The dimensionality of this subspace is deterministically set to the minimum of the two encoders' representation dimensions. For any new input, the encoders' outputs are projected using the learned transformations. These projected representations can then be used downstream, e.g., to train a linear classifier (linear probe). The process is training-free for the encoders and requires only a one-time CCA computation on the auxiliary dataset.

## Key Results
- CCA projections yield higher linear probing accuracy than both unprojected baselines and PCA reduction across multiple model pairs, with average gains of 1.02% over baselines and 2.0% over PCA.
- Dimensionality reductions exceeding 75% (e.g., 1024 → 192) still improve accuracy; fixed-dimensional refinement (e.g., aligning a larger model's output to a smaller model's dimension) gives gains up to 10.5%.
- Post-hoc transfer of fine-tuning signal: when one encoder is fine-tuned on a downstream task and the other is frozen, CCA alignment allows the frozen model to gain task-relevant structure, improving its accuracy by up to 12.6% without label access or gradient updates.
- The method fails on severely imbalanced datasets (Caltech-101), where accuracy drops below baseline and PCA, indicating sensitivity to training data distribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Cross-model agreement identifies shared semantic structure.
Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) computes linear transformations that map each encoder’s representation into a joint space where dimensions are maximally correlated. The canonical correlation coefficients quantify mutual predictability between corresponding projected dimensions. The hypothesis is that highly correlated directions reflect common causal factors (e.g., object shape) rather than model-specific noise, because two independently trained models on similar data/objectives will converge to overlapping representations of true explanatory factors. Retaining top canonical dimensions thus filters out non-shared variation. Evidence: abstract, section I, and Table II showing higher linear probing accuracy for CCA vs. baselines and PCA across ViT pairs.

### Mechanism 2: Deterministic model-driven dimensionality selection.
The number of canonical correlations is exactly min(d_X, d_Y). Projecting to this dimensionality retains maximally correlated dimensions while discarding the rest. This provides a principled compression factor without tuning, determined solely by the model pair. Evidence: abstract, section II.A, Table II showing reductions of 50%–81.3% with accuracy improvements.

### Mechanism 3: Post-hoc transfer of fine-tuning signal.
When one encoder is fine-tuned on a downstream task, its representations become task-adapted. CCA alignment finds a projection that aligns the fine-tuned and non-fine-tuned encoders into a maximally correlated subspace. In this subspace, the fine-tuned encoder’s projections contain task-specific information that is linearly predictable from the non-fine-tuned encoder’s projections. Applying the same projection to the non-fine-tuned encoder reshapes its representations to be more similar to the fine-tuned ones, effectively inheriting some task-relevant structure without retraining. Evidence: abstract, Table IV (e.g., ViT-T non-fine-tuned 29.0% → CCA 41.6% +12.6%).

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)
**Why needed**: The core method uses CCA to compute linear projections that align two representation spaces. Understanding CCA’s objective—maximizing correlation between linear combinations of two variable sets—is essential to grasp why the resulting projections are meaningful.  
**Quick check**: "What does CCA optimize? (Answer: It finds linear projections U and V such that the correlation between U X and V Y is maximized, with the constraint that the projected dimensions are uncorrelated within each set.)"

### Concept: Linear Probing for Representation Evaluation
**Why needed**: The paper evaluates representation quality by training a linear classifier on frozen features (linear probe). This standard protocol measures how linearly separable the representations are, a proxy for downstream usefulness. Without it, improvements from CCA could not be quantified.  
**Quick check**: "Why use a linear probe instead of fine-tuning the entire model? (Answer: To isolate the quality of the representations themselves, without the confounding effect of task-specific adaptation.)"

### Concept: Dimensionality Reduction (PCA vs. CCA)
**Why needed**: The paper contrasts CCA with PCA to argue that cross-model agreement is a better inductive bias than single-model variance. Understanding PCA’s goal (retaining directions of maximum variance within one dataset) highlights why it might fail to capture shared semantics and why CCA’s cross-covariance objective differs.  
**Quick check**: "How does the objective of PCA differ from that of CCA? (Answer: PCA uses a single data matrix and retains directions that explain the most variance; CCA uses two data matrices and retains directions that are maximally correlated across them.)"

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Two frozen pre-trained vision encoders (M_X, M_Y) -> CCA operator (computes projection matrices U, V) -> Projection applied to any representation from M_X or M_Y -> Projected representations -> Linear classifier (trained separately) -> Evaluation metrics (accuracy).

### Critical path
1. Select two pre-trained encoders M_X and M_Y with dimensions d_X, d_Y.
2. Choose a dataset I_cca to compute CCA. Extract representations I_X = M_X(I_cca) (d_X × N) and I_Y = M_Y(I_cca) (d_Y × N).
3. Compute means μ_X, μ_Y and covariance matrices Σ_X, Σ_Y from I_X, I_Y.
4. Compute CCA to obtain U (d × d_X) and V (d × d_Y) where d = min(d_X, d_Y), and diagonal Λ of canonical correlations.
5. For any new representation x from M_X, project: x' = U Σ_X^{-1/2} (x - μ_X). Similarly for y from M_Y: y' = V Σ_Y^{-1/2} (y - μ_Y).
6. Replace original encoder outputs with these projected vectors downstream. Train a linear classifier on projected training representations; evaluate on projected validation representations.
7. Compare performance against baseline (no projection) and PCA projection (to d dimensions).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Computational cost**: CCA requires O(min(d_X,d_Y)^2 * N) for covariances and SVD, feasible for d up to ~1000 on GPU. PCA on concatenated representations would be more expensive O((d_X+d_Y)^3), but both are one-time offline costs.
- **Model pair selection**: Assumes encoders share some semantic structure. Models with similar pre-training (e.g., both ImageNet-21k pretrained) work well; disjoint objectives may fail.
- **Dataset for CCA**: Should be representative of downstream task distribution. Small or biased sets yield poor covariance estimates, especially under class imbalance.
- **Dimensionality**: Projection dimensionality is fixed to min(d_X, d_Y). If the shared subspace has lower intrinsic dimensionality, one could truncate based on Λ thresholds, but the paper does not explore this.
- **Whitening**: The whitening step (Σ^{-1/2}) can amplify noise if covariances are estimated from few samples; regularization (e.g., adding εI) may be needed when N is small.

### Failure signatures
- **Performance drop vs. baseline/PCA**: Especially on datasets with severe class imbalance (e.g., Caltech-101). If accuracy declines, check class distribution of the CCA dataset.
- **Unstable CCA solution**: Near-singular covariance matrices (e.g., N < d_X or d_Y) cause numerical instability in whitening. Regularization of covariances may help.
- **Low canonical correlations**: If top λ values are all very small, models have little linear agreement, indicating they are too dissimilar or the CCA dataset is unrepresentative. Projections may then destroy semantic content.
- **Over-compression**: If min(d_X, d_Y) is much smaller than the true shared subspace dimensionality, information is lost. Monitor diminishing returns when increasing partner capacity.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate Table II on MNIST**: Use two ViT models (e.g., ViT-T and ViT-B) pre-trained on ImageNet-1k. Compute CCA on 10% of ImageNet-1k training data. Reduce the larger model’s representations to the smaller model’s dimensionality (192). Train a linear probe on MNIST and compare accuracy against baseline and PCA.
2. **Fixed-dimensional refinement (Table III)**: Take ViT-T (192-dim) with ViT-B (768-dim) as partner. Since min dim is 192, ViT-T’s dimension remains 192 but its orientation is refined. Train linear probe on ImageNet-1k and verify accuracy improvement.
3. **Imbalance sensitivity test**: On Caltech-101, create training subsets with varying imbalance ratios. Compute CCA using ViT-B and ViT-L (CLIP) on the imbalanced subset. Train linear probe and plot accuracy vs. imbalance ratio to confirm the failure mode.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Robust covariance estimation under class imbalance**: The paper identifies class imbalance as a structured failure mode on Caltech-101 and explicitly lists "robust covariance estimation" as a future direction. Standard CCA relies on covariance estimates dominated by majority classes, obscuring shared semantic structure and degrading downstream accuracy. A resolution would involve developing and evaluating CCA variants with robust covariance estimators (e.g., class-weighted or shrinkage methods) on progressively imbalanced datasets, measuring improvements in linear probing accuracy.
2. **Class-balanced alignment techniques**: The paper suggests "class-balanced alignment" as a future extension to ensure reliable representation selection across diverse data distributions without retraining. Current CCA assumes roughly balanced data for accurate cross-covariance estimation; imbalance leads to misalignment. Evidence would come from implementing CCA with class reweighting or balanced sampling during covariance computation, testing on multiple imbalanced benchmarks, and comparing accuracy and alignment quality.
3. **Interactions with training-based methods**: The paper mentions exploring interactions between agreement-based post-hoc alignment and training-based distillation or compression methods. CCA already transfers fine-tuning signal post-hoc but without joint optimization. Evidence for synergy would require experiments where CCA projections are applied before or during fine-tuning/distillation, evaluating if this reduces sample or compute requirements while maintaining or improving accuracy compared to standard fine-tuning alone.

## Limitations
- CCA implementation details (regularization, whitening) and exact model checkpoints/preprocessing are unspecified, risking reproducibility.
- The assumption of shared linearly correlated subspaces may not hold for heterogeneous models or severely imbalanced data (Caltech-101 failure).
- Evaluation focuses on ViTs/CLIP with similar supervised pre-training; generalizability to CNNs, self-supervised, or cross-architectural pairs is unclear.

## Confidence
- **Cross-model agreement mechanism**: Medium (empirical gains support it but underlying assumption of shared semantics is unverified and breaks under imbalance).
- **Deterministic dimensionality**: High (the dimensionality min(d_X,d_Y) is a mathematical property; the claim is sound though practical utility is empirical).
- **Post-hoc fine-tuning transfer**: Medium (strong results for ViT fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k, but broader applicability unknown).

## Next Checks
1. **Implementation robustness**: Replicate key experiments (e.g., Table II) with a standard CCA library (e.g., `sklearn.cross_decomposition.CCA`) to quantify variance due to implementation choices.
2. **Model pair diversity**: Test cross-architecture (ResNet-50 vs ViT-B) and cross-objective (supervised vs MAE) pairs to map the boundary of successful alignment.
3. **CCA dataset sensitivity**: Systematically vary balance, size, and domain of the CCA dataset; measure canonical correlation strength and downstream accuracy; evaluate regularization (e.g., shrinkage) for imbalance mitigation.
