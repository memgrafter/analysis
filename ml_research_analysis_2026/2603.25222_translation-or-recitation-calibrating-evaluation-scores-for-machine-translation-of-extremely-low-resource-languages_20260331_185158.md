# Translation or Recitation? Calibrating Evaluation Scores for Machine Translation of Extremely Low-Resource Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25222
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25222
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** The Retrieval Proxy (R) metric, simulating memorization via train-test overlap, explains 58% of performance variance in extremely low-resource MT, revealing that many benchmark scores are inflated by data contamination rather than genuine translation capability.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of unpredictable and often incomparable performance numbers in machine translation for extremely low-resource languages (XLR). It introduces the FRED Difficulty Metrics—Fertility Ratio (F), Retrieval Proxy (R), Pre-training Exposure (E), and Corpus Diversity (D)—as intrinsic, dataset-level measures to contextualize evaluation scores like BLEU. Analysis across diverse language groups shows that simple train-test overlap (R-score) is the strongest predictor of performance, explaining 58% of variance. High R-scores correlate with inflated scores in low-diversity corpora (e.g., ancient languages), while poor tokenization (high F) and low pre-training exposure (low E) create significant transfer barriers. Many XLR systems fail to even match the R-score baseline, indicating chronic underfitting. These metrics provide a standardized lens to separate benchmark difficulty from model capability, enabling more transparent cross-study comparisons.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes MT performance across multiple XLR language families (African, Americas Indigenous, Formosan, ancient) and high-resource baselines capped at 10k sentences. Models are fine-tuned from mBART-50. The FRED metrics are computed as follows: F measures tokenization inefficiency (tokens/characters) using the mBART tokenizer; R is a retrieval proxy computed by finding, for each test source sentence, the most similar training source sentence (by chrF) and averaging the corresponding target-side chrF; D measures corpus diversity as the average chrF between all training and test target sentences; E measures pre-training exposure by indexing the NLLB pre-training bitext with infini-gram and averaging 4-gram frequencies in test target sentences. These intrinsic metrics are then correlated with reported model performance (BLEU/ChrF) via hybrid regression to explain variance.

## Key Results
- Train-test overlap (R-score) explains 58% of performance variance, far outweighing training data size.
- High R-scores (e.g., >30 for Akkadian) correlate with inflated model scores in low-diversity corpora, suggesting memorization dominates over learning.
- Poor tokenization (F ≈ 1.0 for Formosan and extinct languages) severely hinders transfer, causing model BLEU to fall below R-baseline.
- Many XLR systems do not surpass the R-score baseline, indicating systemic underfitting relative to the dataset's inherent recitation potential.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** High train-test overlap (R-score) inflates MT performance by enabling models to rely on memorization rather than learning genuine translation.
- **Mechanism:** The Retrieval Proxy (R) simulates a perfect nearest-neighbor system. When R is high, a trivial retrieval approach already yields strong scores, indicating that test instances are too similar to training data. Models can therefore “recite” from the training set instead of acquiring cross-lingual mapping skills.
- **Core assumption:** That retrieval performance approximates a memorization ceiling and that overlap is the primary driver of inflated scores.
- **Evidence anchors:** R² = 0.582 in metric correlation; Akkadian R-BLEU=32.10 vs. high-resource baseline 3.24; BLEU=44.41 close to R.
- **Break condition:** If train-test sets are rigorously disjoint (R ≈ 0), this mechanism would no longer explain performance and other factors would dominate.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Poor tokenization (high Fertility Ratio F) limits transfer for languages outside the pre-trained model’s vocabulary, causing underperformance relative to the R-score baseline.
- **Mechanism:** F = tokens/characters; values near 1.0 indicate nearly every character is a separate token. This yields long sequences with inefficient subword representations, which the pre-trained model cannot process effectively because it lacks shared vocabulary items.
- **Core assumption:** That subword coverage is necessary for efficient cross-lingual transfer.
- **Evidence anchors:** Formosan and ancient languages have F ≈ 1.0 and BLEU far below R-score; high-resource F=0.25–0.66.
- **Break condition:** If a language-specific tokenizer is adopted and F drops, transfer may improve; thus the mechanism is contingent on using a fixed multilingual tokenizer (mBART‑50).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Low pre-training exposure (E-score) reduces cross-lingual transfer because the model lacks prior lexical knowledge of the target language.
- **Mechanism:** E measures average 4-gram frequency in the pre-training corpus. Low E means test text contains phrases rarely seen during pre-training, so the model cannot leverage its monolingual knowledge and must learn from scratch with limited parallel data.
- **Core assumption:** That pre-training exposure facilitates transfer learning; without it, the model is effectively “cold-started”.
- **Evidence anchors:** Americas languages have E-scores 0.24–0.65 (100× lower than high-resource 86–114) and underperform; Wayuunaiki (guc) E=0.24 vs. high-resource ~100; BLEU=2.22 (below R=23.89).
- **Break condition:** If the pre-training corpus is expanded to include substantial data from the low-resource language, E would increase and transfer should improve.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input corpora -> Compute F (tokenizer) -> Compute R and D (similarity search) -> Index pre-training data for E -> Output {F, R, E, D}
- **Critical path:**  
  - Input: train/test corpora.  
  - Step A: Compute F (single pass, O(N+M)).  
  - Step B: Compute R and D (nested loops O(N·M), parallelizable).  
  - Step C: For E, first index pre-training data (one-time cost), then query each test 4-gram (O(total_ngrams)).  
  - Output: {F, R, E, D}.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • Similarity function for R/D: BLEU vs. chrF – chrF may be more robust for morphologically rich languages.  
  • n-gram size for E: paper uses 4-grams; smaller n increases coverage but may be noisier.  
  • Tokenization policy: for non-Latin scripts, language-specific tokenizers may be needed; using the same tokenizer for F ensures fairness.  
  • Computational cost: R/D scale quadratically; for large datasets consider subsampling or approximate nearest-neighbor methods. E indexing requires ~60 GB storage but enables fast queries.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  – R > 30 suggests severe train-test contamination; results may be artifactually high.  
  – F ≈ 1.0 indicates tokenization failure; the model likely cannot represent the language.  
  – E ≈ 0 means the pre-training corpus has virtually no overlap; expect poor transfer.  
  – Model BLEU < R indicates systematic underfitting (e.g., poor hyper-parameter tuning).  
  – Extreme discrepancy between D and R may hint at domain mismatch.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline sanity check:** Run FRED on a high-resource pair (e.g., Finnish-English) with 10k capped data. Expect moderate R (~3), low-moderate F (~0.25), high E (80–110), D (~1–2).  
  2. **Ancient language validation:** Compute FRED on Akkadian-English (50k). Expect very high R (~32), F=1.0, very low E (~0.08), and note that the model’s BLEU (~44) is close to R, confirming memorization dominance.  
  3. **Underperformance diagnosis:** Take an underperforming language (e.g., Zulu-English, 3k). Observe R=32.85 but model BLEU=31.1 (near baseline) or lower; check F≈0.33 and E=99.6 – here the high R is not matched by high model score, suggesting underfitting despite decent tokenization and exposure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Is the variability in MT performance due to inherent linguistic properties, or is it an artifact of how benchmark datasets were collected?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The abstract explicitly states, “Without a standardized way to provide context for these results, it is difficult to interpret whether a high BLEU score indicates an effective model or a benchmark with unnaturally low complexity,” and raises the fundamental question, “Is the variability in MT performance due to inherent linguistic properties, or is it an artifact of how benchmark datasets were collected?”  
  *Why unresolved:* The FRED metrics describe dataset characteristics that correlate with performance but do not directly disentangle linguistic complexity from benchmark collection artifacts; they show associations without proving causality.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled benchmarks where languages with similar linguistic profiles are evaluated on corpora built with different collection protocols, or a meta-analysis using causal inference to isolate linguistic properties from dataset factors.

- **Open Question 2:** Why do many XLR MT systems fail to surpass the trivial retrieval (R-score) baseline?  
  *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper observes that “Many XLR MT systems fail to surpass the R-score baseline, indicating underfitting,” and cites examples like Zulu and some Americas languages where model BLEU is below R-score.  
  *Why unresolved:* The reason is unclear; it may be due to poor hyperparameter tuning, insufficient model capacity, or fundamental limitations; the paper speculates but does not analyze systematically.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* A large-scale study comparing architectures and optimization settings on XLR languages with high R-scores, measuring how often and by what margin models can exceed retrieval performance, accompanied by error analysis.

- **Open Question 3:** How can token fertility be reduced for languages with poor coverage in pretrained tokenizers (e.g., Formosan and extinct languages)?  
  *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper identifies high token fertility (near 1.0) as a structural bottleneck for transfer, noting that “Formosan and ancient languages suffer from poor tokenization coverage,” but does not propose remedies.  
  *Why unresolved:* Tokenization remains a fundamental limitation; without addressing it, models struggle to represent these languages efficiently. Current tokenizers are fixed and not adapted to such languages.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Development and evaluation of new tokenization strategies (e.g., language-specific subword learning, grapheme-based modeling, or adaptive merging with pretrained vocab) that lower fertility and improve downstream MT performance.

- **Open Question 4:** What is the relationship between monolingual data availability, pre-training exposure (E-score), and cross-lingual transfer quality?  
  *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The limitations state, “We wish we had more time to investigate the correlation between monolingual data and model performance. We could also apply a similar E-score to monolingual data,” and the paper notes low E-scores for underperforming groups.  
  *Why unresolved:* The role of monolingual data in explaining E-score and its impact on MT for XLR languages is unexplored; understanding this could explain performance gaps and guide data collection.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Collect monolingual corpora for a diverse set of XLR languages, compute E-score on monolingual data, correlate with MT performance given limited parallel data, and test whether augmenting training with monolingual data improves both E-score and translation quality.

## Limitations
- Exact dataset versions, preprocessing, and train-test splits are not centrally documented, jeopardizing reproducibility.
- Tokenization rules for non-Latin and extinct languages are underspecified, impacting Fertility and similarity calculations.
- The quadratic cost of R and D may force approximations that reduce fidelity; Exposure (E) is tied to the NLLB pre-training corpus, limiting comparability.
- The analysis is correlational, so causal interpretations of the mechanisms are provisional.

## Confidence
- **R-score dominance:** High (strong 58% variance explanation, consistent across groups, retrieval baseline convincing).
- **Tokenization bottleneck (F):** Medium (correlation clear but contingent on fixed mBART tokenizer; language-specific tokenizers could change effect).
- **Pre-training exposure (E):** Medium (significant correlation but smaller effect size than R; dependent on specific pre-training corpus).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-corpus replication:** Apply the FRED pipeline to an independent XLR benchmark (e.g., LORES-2025) with clearly documented splits; verify that R remains the strongest predictor and assess whether the 58% variance figure generalizes.
2. **Tokenizer ablation:** For languages with F ≈ 1.0, train a dedicated sentence-piece tokenizer on available monolingual data, recompute F and model BLEU, and compare the BLEU–R gap before and after; a reduced gap would confirm the tokenization bottleneck.
3. **Exposure manipulation:** Pick a language with low E (e.g., Guaraní-English), fine-tune mBART with and without supplemental in-domain pre-training data collected from the web to raise E; a corresponding BLEU increase relative to R would validate the exposure mechanism.
