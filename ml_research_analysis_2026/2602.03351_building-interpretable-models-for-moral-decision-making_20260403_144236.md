# Building Interpretable Models for Moral Decision-Making

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03351
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03351
- **Reference count:** 2
- **Primary result:** A custom 2-layer transformer with structured input embeddings achieves 77% accuracy on Moral Machine data; interpretability analyses localize biases to distinct layers, reveal a moral hierarchy via causal intervention, and identify a sparse 45-neuron subnetwork causally involved in moral scoring.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how neural networks make moral decisions by building a transparent, shallow transformer model trained on Moral Machine trolley dilemmas. The model accepts structured inputs encoding character identity, count, and team assignment, and reaches human-competitive accuracy. Through a suite of interpretability methods—causal intervention, layer-wise attribution, and circuit probing—the authors demonstrate that different moral biases (legality, species) occupy separate computational stages, quantify a clear moral hierarchy via average treatment effects, and pinpoint a highly sparse subnetwork in the second-layer MLP that drives the final moral score. This work showcases the value of mechanistic interpretability for auditing moral reasoning in AI systems.

## Method Summary
The authors designed a two-layer transformer encoder (d_model=64, 2 attention heads) that processes tokenized representations of each character in a moral scenario. Each token combines embeddings for character type, count, and team assignment; the model aggregates information via a CLS token and passes it through a two-layer MLP head to output a preference probability. Training uses a subset of the Moral Machine dataset (5.4M scenarios), with validation on unique scenarios only. To enforce decision symmetry (order of outcomes should not matter), inference averages predictions from both outcome orderings. For interpretability, three complementary analyses are applied: (1) Causal intervention with DoWhy quantifies each character type’s influence on decisions by backdoor adjustment for total group size; (2) Layer-wise attribution correlates attention weight variance and bias scores to localize biases to specific layers/heads; (3) Circuit probing learns sparse binary masks over neurons to identify a minimal subnetwork that causally supports the model’s moral scoring signal, validated by ablation.

## Key Results
- Model achieves **77% accuracy** on held-out Moral Machine scenarios after symmetrization.
- Moral biases **localize distinctly**: legality bias emerges primarily in Layer 0, while species bias emerges in Layer 1.
- Causal intervention reveals a **moral hierarchy**: e.g., “Pregnant” exerts a strong positive influence (+0.12 ATE) and “Criminal” a strong negative influence (−0.10 ATE).
- Circuit probing discovers a **sparse causal subnetwork** of 45 neurons (out of 256) in the Layer 1 MLP; ablating these neurons causes a measurable drop in agreement with the model’s original scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Causal Intervention isolates character influence
- **Claim:** Backdoor adjustment via DoWhy isolates the effect of individual character types on moral decisions, controlling for confounding group sizes.
- **Mechanism:** For each character type, the framework models its presence as a treatment, uses total individuals per outcome (`total0`, `total1`) as a backdoor set, and estimates the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) on preference probability through linear regression.
- **Core assumption:** Total group size is the sole significant confounder; structural knowledge of scenario teams suffices for adjustment.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] mentions using causal intervention;  
  [section 4.1] describes synthetic scenario generation and backdoor adjustment for ATE;  
  corpus: weak, no external validation of this specific method.
- **Break condition:** Non-linear relationships between character presence and group size, or unmeasured confounders (e.g., character combinations), would invalidate ATE estimates.

### Bias localization to computational stages
- **Claim:** Distinct moral biases localize to separate layers: legality bias to Layer 0, species bias to Layer 1.
- **Mechanism:** For contrastive scenarios, bias score (preference for a privileged group) is computed. Each attention head’s importance equals the product of its attention weight variance (selectivity) and absolute correlation with the bias score (alignment). Summing across heads shows which layers encode each bias.
- **Core assumption:** Attention patterns that are both highly variable and consistently aligned with bias are the primary computational locus; moral reasoning mainly occurs via attention.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] states use of layer-wise attribution;  
  [section 4.2] presents Figure 3 showing legality bias in Layer 0 and species bias in Layer 1;  
  corpus: weak, no independent replication of this localization pattern.
- **Break condition:** If biases are distributed across all layers or the correlation–variance metric captures spurious correlations, the localization claim fails. The method also ignores potential contributions from MLP or residual streams.

### Circuit probing identifies sparse moral subnetwork
- **Claim:** A minimally sparse subnetwork in the Layer 1 MLP (45 neurons) causally drives the model’s moral scoring signal.
- **Mechanism:** Sparse binary masks over neurons are learned while the model is frozen, optimizing for nearest-neighbor accuracy on a target signal (model-derived moral score). Causal contribution is validated by ablating the selected neurons and measuring behavioral accuracy drop compared to random subnetworks.
- **Core assumption:** Such a sparse mask exists and the learned mask identifies functionally critical neurons; the target label is a valid proxy for moral scoring.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] mentions circuit probing for sparse causal subnetworks;  
  [section 4.3] reports high KNN accuracies (≈0.95) and sparsity (45/256 neurons); ablation reduced agreement from 0.921 to 0.908;  
  corpus: limited, circuit probing is adapted from prior work but not independently validated for moral tasks.
- **Break condition:** If the subnetwork is not uniquely identified (high sensitivity to initialization), the target signal is noisy, or the ablation effect falls within random subnetwork variance, the causal claim is undermined.

## Foundational Learning
### Transformer Architecture & Attention
- **Why needed:** The model is a 2-layer transformer; understanding self-attention, residual streams, MLP blocks, and the CLS token is essential to interpret where moral reasoning occurs.
- **Quick check:** In this architecture, how does the `team` embedding enable cross-outcome comparison without positional encoding?

### Causal Inference (Backdoor Adjustment)
- **Why needed:** The core causal intervention method adjusts for confounders (total individuals) to isolate the effect of a character’s identity on decisions.
- **Quick check:** Why are `total0` and `total1` a valid backdoor set for estimating the effect of character `c` on the decision? What unmeasured confounder might violate this?

### Mechanistic Interpretability (Circuit Probing)
- **Why needed:** The paper aims to map *how* the model reasons, not just its accuracy. Circuit probing involves sparse autointerpretation and causal scrubbing.
- **Quick check:** What is the logical difference between finding neurons that are *predictive* of a scoring signal (probing) and proving they are *causally necessary* for the behavior (circuit probing with ablation)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input Encoder → Sequence Formatter → Transformer Encoder → Classification Head → Inference Symmetrizer

**Critical Path:**  
Structured Scenario → Tokenization & Embedding → Transformer Processing (Layer0 → Layer1) → CLS aggregation → MLP Head → Logit → Sigmoid → (optional: Symmetrization). Interpretability analyses hook into intermediate states (attention weights, neuron activations, CLS updates).

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Accuracy vs. Interpretability:** Chose L=2, H=2, d=64 (77.1% acc) over L=3 (77.5%) to keep the computational graph tractable.  
- **Structured Input vs. Generalization:** Fixed 23-character vocabulary and count encoding sacrifices natural language flexibility for explicit moral features.  
- **Symmetry Enforcement:** Inference-time symmetrization corrects order-sensitivity but adds computational cost.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- **Symmetry Violation:** Pre-symmetrization predictions for (A vs B) and (B vs A) differ by >5%.  
- **Bias Divergence:** Same bias appears strongly in both layers or shows no clear localization.  
- **Circuit Collapse:** Probing selects >90% of neurons or random masks show identical ablation effect.  
- **ATE Instability:** ATE ranks vary widely across synthetic scenario seeds.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Reproduce Base Accuracy & Symmetry:** Train the specified model; report accuracy before and after symmetrization.  
2. **Replicate Causal Hierarchy:** Run ATE estimation on 20k synthetic scenarios; verify the reported moral hierarchy (e.g., Pregnant ≈ +0.12, Criminal ≈ −0.10).  
3. **Validate Circuit Sparsity & Causality:** Apply circuit probing to Layer 1 MLP; confirm ~45/256 neurons are selected, ablation of exactly those neurons causes a significant accuracy drop (>0.01), and random masks cause a smaller drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do the biases and moral mechanisms discovered in the small transformer generalize to large language models (LLMs) when applied to moral decision-making tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion states hope to extend to traditional LLMs.
- **Why unresolved:** LLMs have vastly different architectures, training objectives, and may organize moral reasoning differently; the interpretability pipeline might not scale.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the same interpretability pipeline (causal intervention, layer-wise attribution, circuit probing) to several LLMs using Moral Machine or comparable datasets; compare bias localization, circuit sparsity, and accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can targeted interventions derived from bias localization (e.g., clamping specific attention heads or modifying neuron activations) effectively reduce specific moral biases without significantly harming overall decision accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** Authors suggest interventions like orthogonalizing representations or clamping attention weights.
- **Why unresolved:** Interventions were not implemented or evaluated; trade-offs unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement interventions (e.g., zeroing out attention weights from identified heads, editing representations) and measure bias reduction and accuracy on held-out scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the observed bias localizations (e.g., legality bias in Layer 0, species bias in Layer 1) and the sparse MLP circuit consistent across different moral domains (e.g., honesty, fairness, care) beyond trolley-style dilemmas?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from using only Moral Machine dataset; other domains not tested.
- **Why unresolved:** Different moral domains may involve distinct reasoning patterns (e.g., reciprocity) that could change localization.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train equivalent models on other moral datasets (e.g., ETHICS, Social Chemistry 101) and repeat interpretability analyses; compare layer-wise bias importance and circuit sparsity.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the individual contribution of each component in the compositional input embedding (character identity, cardinality count, team assignment) to the model’s moral decisions and bias?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from the design; no systematic ablation performed.
- **Why unresolved:** Without isolating components, it is unknown which factor drives the moral hierarchy or biases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct ablation experiments: replace character embeddings with random/uniform vectors, zero out count embeddings, remove team markers; evaluate changes in accuracy and bias metrics.

## Limitations
- **Missing critical details:** Exact 23-character vocabulary and embedding mapping, full training hyperparameters, dataset split construction, and the GitHub code link are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Unverified assumptions:** Interpretability claims rely on strong assumptions (e.g., total group size as sufficient backdoor, attention variance × correlation as localization metric) without external validation or ablation tests.
- **Limited generalizability:** The structured input format sacrifices natural language flexibility, and the need for inference-time symmetrization indicates the model did not natively learn order invariance.

## Confidence
- **Accuracy & Symmetry Fix (77% post-symmetrization):** Medium – result is reported but hyperparameter gaps prevent verification of optimality.
- **Bias Localization (legality→L0, species→L1):** Low – metric is correlational; no counterfactual validation; evidence is limited to the paper’s own analysis.
- **ATE Moral Hierarchy (e.g., Pregnant +0.12):** Medium – depends entirely on the validity of backdoor adjustment; precise but unproven assumption.
- **Sparse Causal Circuit (45 neurons):** Low – circuit probing results are self-consistent but lack external validation; ablation effect may be within noise of random subnetworks.

## Next Checks
1. **Test Attention Causal Necessity:** Generate minimal contrastive scenarios differing only in a single bias-relevant attribute (e.g., legal vs. illegal). Ablate or intervene on the specific Layer 0 heads identified as legality bias loci and measure whether the bias disappears, controlling for other layers.
2. **Stress-Test ATE Stability:** Replicate causal intervention across 100 random seeds for synthetic scenario generation, including scenarios with non-additive character interactions (e.g., “Pregnant+Criminal”). Check consistency of ATE rank order (e.g., Pearson’s r > 0.9).
3. **Validate Circuit Specificity:** Perform a targeted ablation study on the reported 45-neuron subnetwork: (a) ablate exactly those 45 neurons, (b) ablate 45 random neurons, (c) ablate the 45 highest-activation neurons. Compare drops in test accuracy and bias metrics using paired t-tests; the claimed circuit must show a significantly larger drop than all controls (p < 0.01).
