# Conflict-Resolving and Sharpness-Aware Minimization for Generalized Knowledge Editing with Multiple Updates

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03696  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03696  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CoRSA improves generalization by an average absolute 12.42% over LoRA and 10% over MEMIT, reduces catastrophic forgetting by 27.82% in continual revision settings, and transfers to code updates with a 5.48% Pass@5 gain on CodeUpdateArena.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles three core challenges in knowledge editing for large language models: poor generalization to paraphrased inputs, instability under sequential updates, and conflicts between new and retained knowledge. It introduces CoRSA, a parameter-efficient framework built on LoRA that combines a Conflicting Knowledge Suppression objective (using Direct Preference Optimization to explicitly widen the likelihood margin between new and outdated outputs) with Sharpness-Aware Minimization (to flatten the loss landscape for better generalization and update stability). Gradient conflicts between the supervised and preference objectives are resolved via PCGrad. Evaluations across CounterFact, ZsRE, MQuAKE, and code benchmarks show consistent improvements over baselines like LoRA and MEMIT, especially in multi-update scenarios.

## Method Summary
CoRSA trains a low-rank adapter (LoRA) on a frozen base LLM. The training objective combines a standard SFT loss (minimizing cross-entropy on new target outputs) and a DPO loss that maximizes the log-likelihood margin between new (preferred) and old (dispreferred) outputs for the same prompts. To improve generalization and stability, SAM is applied: it first perturbs the LoRA parameters along the worst-case direction within radius ρ (using the PCGrad-resolved gradient), then computes gradients at the perturbed point and updates the original parameters. PCGrad is used twice—once for the current gradient and once after perturbation—to project conflicting gradient components between SFT and DPO, preventing destructive interference. Key hyperparameters are λ=1 (SFT:DPO weight), ρ=0.05 (SAM radius), β=0.1 (DPO inverse temperature), and rank=32 for factual tasks (rank=128 for code).

## Key Results
- **Generalization:** Average absolute 12.42% improvement over LoRA and 10% over MEMIT on CounterFact, ZsRE, and MQuAKE when tested on paraphrased prompts.
- **Continual revision:** Reduces catastrophic forgetting by 27.82% compared to LoRA while maintaining higher update efficacy in sequential update settings (AToKe-ME and cross-dataset).
- **Transfer to code:** Surpasses prior methods by 5.48% on Pass@5 in CodeUpdateArena, demonstrating domain adaptability with adjusted rank (128) and learning rate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Explicitly maximizing the log-likelihood margin between new and outdated outputs via DPO reduces interference and improves stability under continued updates.  
**Mechanism:** The combined loss LUpdate = LSFT + λ LDPO directly increases the margin γ (Eq. 1). DPO uses a sigmoid link that lowers loss as the margin grows, pushing old outputs to lower probability.  
**Core assumption:** Old knowledge can be represented as dispreferred outputs for the same input prompts; a larger margin ensures the edited model does not fall back to outdated responses.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "Conflicting Knowledge Suppression objective using Direct Preference Optimization to explicitly maximize the likelihood margin between new and outdated outputs."  
- [section 2.2] Derivation shows that a larger γ satisfies the stability condition in Eq. (3).  
- [corpus] DPO is established (Rafailov et al., 2023), but its use for explicit knowledge suppression in editing is novel; corpus shows SAM in other contexts but not for LoRA editing.  
**Break condition:** If old outputs are unavailable or not semantically comparable to new ones (e.g., different input phrasing), the DPO term may fail to correctly suppress prior knowledge.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Flattening the LoRA adapter’s loss landscape via SAM improves generalization to varied inputs and stability under future updates.  
**Mechanism:** SAM solves a min‑max problem: perturb parameters within ρ to find worst‑case loss direction, then update at the perturbed point to encourage flat minima (small curvature κ).  
**Core assumption:** Solutions in flat regions (low κ) generalize better and suffer less performance drop under parameter perturbations (e.g., subsequent updates).  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "Sharpness‑Aware Minimization to flatten the loss landscape, improving generalization and update stability."  
- [section 2.2] Shows that reducing κ mitigates the risk of fallback (m(ϕ⋆+Δ)≤0).  
- [section 3] "we optimize LUpdate using Sharpness‑Aware Minimization (SAM)."  
- [corpus] SAM is used in federated and domain generalization, but not adapted to LoRA editing prior to this work.  
**Break condition:** SAM doubles computational cost; too large ρ may over‑regularize and slow convergence, too small may not flatten enough. The curvature assumption may also break in highly non‑convex landscapes.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Resolving gradient conflicts between SFT and DPO via PCGrad prevents destructive interference, enabling effective SAM updates.  
**Mechanism:** PCGrad identifies gradients with negative inner product (conflict) and projects one to remove the opposing component before merging. Applied twice: for SAM perturbation direction and final update direction at perturbed parameters.  
**Core assumption:** Simple weighted sum of conflicting gradients can lead to suboptimal minima; projecting them retains useful information while reducing interference.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "Gradient conflicts between the supervised fine‑training and preference objectives are resolved via PCGrad."  
- [section 3] Explains that without PCGrad, SAM can still suffer from conflicts (see Figure 2b oscillations).  
- [section 3] Details the projection formula and application in both SAM stages.  
- [corpus] PCGrad was introduced for multi‑task learning (Yu et al., 2020); here applied to knowledge editing objectives.  
**Break condition:** If gradient conflict is not the primary instability source (e.g., due to poor hyperparameters), PCGrad may not suffice. Projection could discard useful signal if gradients are misidentified as conflicting.

## Foundational Learning
- **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation):** Decomposes weight updates into two low-rank matrices (A·B), enabling parameter-efficient adaptation. *Why needed:* CoRSA stores edits in a LoRA adapter; its rank controls capacity vs. efficiency. *Quick check:* How does LoRA’s low-rank structure limit the capacity for new knowledge, and why is that beneficial for editing?
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** Bypasses reward modeling by directly optimizing a likelihood margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs. *Why needed:* Provides the loss that increases the margin between new and old outputs. *Quick check:* In DPO, what role does the β parameter play, and how does the loss relate to the margin m(ϕ)?
- **Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM):** Minimizes a worst-case loss within a perturbation ball to find flat minima. *Why needed:* Core mechanism for flattening the loss landscape, improving generalization and update stability. *Quick check:* Why does SAM’s two‑step process (perturb then gradient step) encourage flat minima compared to standard SGD?
- **Gradient Conflict in Multi-Objective Optimization:** Occurs when gradients from different losses point in opposing directions (negative inner product). *Why needed:* SFT and DPO gradients can conflict; resolving this is key for stable SAM. *Quick check:* What does a negative inner product between two loss gradients indicate, and how does PCGrad modify one gradient to remove the conflict?
- **Knowledge Editing Metrics:** Include Generality (success on paraphrased prompts), Specificity (retention of unrelated knowledge), Forgetting (performance drop on prior edits). *Why needed:* Quantify success and failure modes. *Quick check:* Why is Generality a stricter test than simple edit success, and how does it differ from Specificity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Base LLM (frozen) -> LoRA adapter (trainable low-rank matrices) -> Data pipelines (D_new for SFT, D_pairs for DPO) -> Loss calculators (LSFT, LDPO) -> SAM & PCGrad modules (perturbation and conflict resolution) -> Optimizer (e.g., Adam).

**Critical path:**  
1. Sample minibatch from D_new and D_pairs.  
2. Compute LSFT and LDPO separately → obtain gSFT and gDPO.  
3. Apply PCGrad to (gSFT, gDPO) → gPC (current).  
4. SAM perturbation: ϕ~ = ϕ + ρ·gPC/(∥gPC∥+ε).  
5. Recompute LSFT and LDPO at ϕ~ → get g~SFT, g~DPO.  
6. Apply PCGrad to (g~SFT, g~DPO) → g~PC.  
7. Update ϕ ← ϕ − η·g~PC.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LoRA rank: factual tasks → rank 32; code → rank 128 (higher capacity).  
- λ (SFT≔DPO weight): optimal at 1.0; lower biases toward adaptation, higher toward suppression.  
- ρ (SAM radius): fixed at 0.05; too large over‑regularizes, too small insufficient flattening.  
- β (DPO inverse temperature): 0.1 (standard) to control KL to reference.  
- Learning rate: factual 2e‑4; code 2e‑5 (more complex).

**Failure signatures:**  
- High forgetting in continual updates → λ too low or ρ too small.  
- Low Generality → SAM omitted or DPO margin not enforced.  
- High old knowledge retention → DPO loss weak; check λ and reference model.  
- Training oscillations → PCGrad not applied; gradient conflicts persist.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Single‑update evaluation:** Measure Generality & Specificity on CounterFact, ZsRE, MQuAKE; compare to LoRA, MEMIT, F‑Learning.  
2. **Continual revision:** Use AToKe‑ME and cross‑dataset (CounterFact→MQuAKE); report Update Efficacy and Forgetting.  
3. **Transfer to code domain:** Test on CodeUpdateArena; compute UPass and SPass.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do the LoRA rank and SAM radius ρ jointly affect the trade-off between update efficacy and catastrophic forgetting in multi-update settings?
2. Does SAM’s benefit stem primarily from flattening the DPO margin curvature (∇²m), or from generalizing the SFT objective, and how do these effects interact under gradient conflict?
3. Are there more effective gradient surgery methods than PCGrad for reconciling SFT and DPO gradients, particularly under high conflict where old/new knowledge are semantically opposed?
4. How does CoRSA’s performance scale with the number and diversity of sequential updates? Does its advantage diminish as the update stream grows larger and more heterogeneous?

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility:** No official code release; critical details omitted (prompt templates, train/test splits, LoRA target modules, training steps).
- **Hyperparameter generality:** λ and ρ sweeps only on Qwen-3-4B; unclear transfer to larger models or diverse domains without retuning.
- **DPO dependency:** Requires high-quality old outputs; noise or semantic drift may misalign preference signal and harm suppression.
- **SAM overhead:** Doubles training time and memory; scaling to large batches or distributed settings not analyzed.

## Confidence
- **Performance improvements** (Generalization, Forgetting reduction, Code transfer): **High** (consistent across multiple benchmarks and baselines).
- **Mechanistic explanations** (DPO margin, SAM flattening, PCGrad conflict resolution): **Medium** (theoretical support but causal isolation lacking; ablations show necessity but not sufficiency).
- **Ablation necessity** (each component required): **Medium** (single-component removals degrade performance, but interactions between components not fully tested).
