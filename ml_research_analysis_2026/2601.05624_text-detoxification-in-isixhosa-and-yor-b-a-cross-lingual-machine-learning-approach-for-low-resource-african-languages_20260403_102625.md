# Text Detoxification in isiXhosa and Yorùbá: A Cross-Lingual Machine Learning Approach for Low-Resource African Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05624  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05624  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Built a functional detox system for isiXhosa and Yorùbá using a TF‑IDF/Logistic Regression detector and lexicon‑guided rewriter, achieving detection accuracies of 61‑72% and 72‑86% respectively and successfully detoxifying all detected toxic sentences.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the scarcity of text detoxification tools for low‑resource African languages by devising a lightweight, interpretable pipeline for isiXhosa and Yorùbá. The system combines a TF‑IDF feature extractor with a Logistic Regression classifier for toxicity detection, followed by a rewriting module that first consults a curated parallel corpus for an exact neutral counterpart and, failing that, replaces toxic tokens using a language‑specific lexicon. Experiments on a small manually annotated dataset (178 sentence pairs per language) yield detection accuracies in the 60‑86% range and show that the rewriting stage detoxified every detected toxic sentence while preserving all non‑toxic inputs. The approach stresses computational efficiency and cultural adaptiveness, providing a practical benchmark for low‑resource text style transfer.

## Method Summary
Detection: raw sentences are normalized (lowercasing, diacritic removal, punctuation stripping) and vectorized via TF‑IDF (unigrams + bigrams). A Logistic Regression model, trained on the parallel corpus’s binary labels with balanced class weights, learns a linear decision boundary; the classification threshold is tuned per language (τ = 0.45 for isiXhosa, τ = 0.50 for Yorùbá) to maximize toxic‑class recall. Rewriting: for sentences flagged toxic, the system first searches for an exact normalized match in a parallel corpus of toxic→neutral pairs. If none is found, it tokenizes the input, normalizes tokens, substitutes any token found in a curated lexicon of toxic terms with its neutral equivalent, and reconstructs the sentence. Non‑toxic sentences are returned unchanged. The entire pipeline runs on a standard CPU laptop, avoiding large generative models.

## Key Results
- **Detection performance:** Stratified K‑fold accuracies: isiXhosa 61‑72%, Yorùbá 72‑86%; ROC‑AUC up to 0.88.  
- **Rewriting:** 100% of detected toxic sentences were detoxified; 100% of non‑toxic sentences were preserved.  
- **Efficiency & interpretability:** System operates on CPU hardware; feature weights highlight known toxic tokens (e.g., `kuzisola` for isiXhosa, `ifarapa` for Yorùbá).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A TF‑IDF feature extractor paired with a Logistic Regression classifier can reliably detect toxic language in isiXhosa and Yorùbá using a small, manually curated dataset.  
**Mechanism:** Input text is normalized (lowercasing, diacritic removal, punctuation stripping) to reduce orthographic noise. The normalized text is vectorized via TF‑IDF (unigrams + bigrams), capturing the frequency of toxic tokens and short offensive collocations. A Logistic Regression model, trained on the parallel corpus’s toxic/non‑toxic labels, learns a linear decision boundary. Its weights provide direct interpretability of which lexical features drive a “toxic” prediction.  
**Core assumption:** Toxicity in these languages exhibits consistently distinct lexical surface forms (specific words, idioms, slurs) that are separable from neutral language via bag‑of‑words patterns, despite morphological complexity.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract describes the approach as “lightweight, interpretable TF‑IDF+Logistic Regression”; Section 3.4 details the vectorisation and training; Sections 4.2–4.3 present detection metrics and feature weight visualisations highlighting toxic tokens; the parallel corpus and lexicon are native‑speaker validated.  
**Break condition:** The lexical assumption fails when toxicity is mainly implicit, context‑dependent, or uses novel obfuscations not present in the training corpus; performance degrades especially for isiXhosa due to idiomatic/morphological complexity.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A two‑stage rewriting process (full‑sentence parallel lookup followed by token‑level lexicon substitution) can produce meaning‑preserving, non‑toxic outputs for detected toxic sentences.  
**Mechanism:** For a sentence classified as toxic, the detoxifier first attempts an exact normalized match against a curated parallel corpus of toxic→neutral sentence pairs. If a match is found, the pre‑written neutral counterpart is returned. Otherwise, the sentence is tokenized, each token normalized and checked against a language‑specific lexicon of toxic tokens; detected toxic tokens are replaced with their pre‑defined neutral equivalents and the modified token list is reconstructed into a sentence.  
**Core assumption:** The majority of toxic utterances can be neutralized by substituting a finite set of key tokens or by mapping entire known toxic phrases to neutral templates, without requiring open‑ended generative paraphrasing.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes “controlled lexicon‑ and token‑guided rewriting… successfully detoxified every detected toxic sentence while preserving 100% of non‑toxic inputs”; Section 3.5 defines Algorithm 1; Section 4.4 reports GUI‑based qualitative validation; the parallel corpus (178 pairs per language) and token lexicons are manually constructed.  
**Break condition:** The mechanism fails for toxic sentences not covered by the parallel corpus and lacking tokens in the lexicon, or when toxicity is conveyed through syntax or discourse rather than individual tokens. Note: the “100% detoxification” claim applies only to sentences that the detector correctly identified as toxic.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** This hybrid, rule‑augmented pipeline is computationally resource‑efficient and culturally adaptive, making it suitable for deployment in low‑resource African language contexts.  
**Mechanism:** By avoiding large pre‑trained generative models, training and inference require minimal computational power (demonstrated on a CPU laptop). Native‑speaker validated data and explicit lexicons/parallel sentences ensure respect for orthographic (Yorùbá diacritics), agglutinative (isiXhosa), and cultural nuances (idioms, code‑switching). The Logistic Regression’s feature weights offer transparency for auditing decisions.  
**Core assumption:** In severely data‑constrained environments, a controlled, deterministic approach with high precision on known patterns is more valuable and deployable than a stochastic generative model that risks producing fluent but inappropriate or semantically distorted outputs.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract positions the solution as “computationally efficient and culturally adaptive”; Sections 1 and 3.1 contrast with “resource‑intensive, black‑box LLMs”; Section 4.1 states experiments ran on an HP EliteBook without GPU; Sections 3.2 and 4.4 emphasize native‑speaker annotation and GUI validation; Section 4.5 compares with SOTA LLM‑based methods.  
**Break condition:** The approach’s limitations include bounded creativity and coverage; it cannot handle novel toxic expressions, sarcasm, or implicit aggression, and its cultural adaptiveness depends entirely on the quality and completeness of the curated lexicon and parallel corpus.

## Foundational Learning

### TF‑IDF Vectorization
**Why needed here:** Converts normalized text into a numerical feature space where the presence and importance of toxic lexical markers can be learned by a simple classifier. It is particularly suited for small datasets where deep contextual embeddings would overfit.  
**Quick check question:** How does the use of bigrams (in addition to unigrams) help capture toxic multi‑word expressions like “u useless gan” in Yorùbá, and what is a limitation of this approach for agglutinative languages like isiXhosa?

### Logistic Regression for Binary Classification
**Why needed here:** Provides a lightweight, interpretable model that can learn a linear decision boundary from TF‑IDF features. Its coefficient weights directly indicate which tokens are predictive of toxicity, which is crucial for informing the token‑replacement lexicon and for auditing the system.  
**Quick check question:** What does a positive coefficient weight for a token (e.g., `ifarapa` in Yorùbá) in the trained Logistic Regression model imply about that token’s relationship to the “toxic” class?

### Style Transfer via Lexical Substitution & Parallel Corpus Lookup
**Why needed here:** Defines the detoxification operation not as open‑ended text generation but as a constrained lookup‑and‑replace task. This guarantees semantic fidelity for known phrases and provides a deterministic fallback (token substitution) for partial matches, which is critical for low‑resource settings where generative models would be unstable.  
**Quick check question:** When the detoxification system encounters a toxic sentence not in the parallel corpus, what is the exact sequence of operations it performs, and what is the primary risk of this token‑level fallback strategy?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw sentence → Normalizer → Classifier → [if toxic] → Detoxifier → Output.  
Detoxifier: (a) exact normalized match against parallel corpus D → return stored neutral sentence; (b) tokenization → normalize tokens → replace tokens found in lexicon Lₗ → reconstruct sentence. Non‑toxic sentences bypass Detoxifier and return original.

**Critical path:** Input → Normalizer → Classifier → (if detected toxic) → Detoxifier → Result. Classification thresholds τ are language‑specific (isiXhosa τ=0.45, Yorùbá τ=0.50) and critically affect the recall‑precision trade‑off.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Interpretability vs. contextual nuance: Chose Logistic Regression/lexicon over neural seq2seq for transparency and control, sacrificing handling of context‑dependent toxicity.
- Data efficiency vs. coverage: Small parallel corpus (178 pairs) and lexicon enable training with minimal resources but limit coverage to known patterns.
- Determinism vs. fluency: Lookup and token replacement guarantee grammatical correctness for covered examples but may produce less fluent outputs than generative models for edge cases.

**Failure signatures:**
- Detection: false positives on non‑toxic sentences containing words that overlap with toxic tokens (e.g., formal terms coincidentally using a toxic root), especially in isiXhosa as seen in confusion matrices.
- Rewriting: for a toxic sentence outside D and with tokens missing from Lₗ, the output remains toxic (“lexicon gap”); token substitution may also break grammatical agreement, particularly in agglutinative isiXhosa.
- Systemic: poor performance on code‑switched sentences if the lexicon/parallel corpus lacks mixed‑language examples.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Hyperparameter Tuning & Threshold Optimization:** Run grid search on the 80% training split for Logistic Regression (e.g., `C` value, class weight) and find the optimal classification threshold τ that maximizes F1‑score on a validation set, acknowledging the higher cost of false negatives.
2. **Parallel Corpus Coverage Test:** Take a held‑out set of 50 toxic sentences from the validation data. Measure the percentage that exactly match (post‑normalization) an entry in the parallel corpus D. This quantifies reliance on lookup vs. fallback token substitution.
3. **Token Lexicon Stress Test:** For 20 toxic sentences that failed the lookup test, manually inspect the output after token substitution. Log cases where: (a) a toxic token was not in Lₗ (coverage failure), (b) substitution broke grammar/semantics, or (c) substitution created an unintended meaning. Identifies lexicon gaps and reconstruction flaws.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What is the relationship between parallel dataset size and detoxification performance for isiXhosa and Yoruba?**  
   *Basis:* The paper states that the curated parallel datasets (178 pairs per language) should be expanded to enhance robustness, especially for idiomatic expressions.  
   *Why unresolved:* The current limited size may restrict generalization and coverage.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically collect additional pairs and measure detection accuracy (ROC‑AUC) and rewriting quality on incrementally larger subsets.

2. **Can parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (e.g., adapters, LoRA) of multilingual sequence‑to‑sequence models (mT5, Flan‑T5) match or exceed the current approach while remaining computationally feasible?**  
   *Basis:* The authors suggest exploring such models but note computational overhead.  
   *Why unresolved:* The study only evaluated a lightweight classifier; generative models’ potential trade‑offs remain unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning on the parallel dataset, compare automatic metrics and human judgments against the baseline, and report training/inference costs.

3. **How does a hybrid architecture combining lexicon‑guided rewriting with a lightweight neural generator affect output quality, interpretability, and context‑awareness?**  
   *Basis:* The authors propose hybrid approaches to combine flexibility with interpretability.  
   *Why unresolved:* Current rewriting relies solely on lookup and token substitution; a neural component might handle unseen patterns better but could reduce transparency.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Build a hybrid system (e.g., lexicon fallback with a small seq2seq model), evaluate fluency (BLEU), semantic preservation (BERTScore), toxicity reduction (classifier scores), and interpretability (feature attribution).

4. **Does incorporating fine‑grained toxicity categories (insults, threats, harassment) improve detoxification effectiveness, and what are the language‑specific markers of these categories?**  
   *Basis:* The paper recommends exploring multi‑category detection for a more detailed analysis.  
   *Why unresolved:* Toxicity is treated as binary, possibly missing nuanced patterns.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Annotate the dataset with multi‑label categories, train a multi‑task classifier, analyze lexico‑syntactic features per category, and assess whether category‑specific rewriting improves quality.

## Limitations
- Small parallel corpus (178 sentence pairs per language) likely fails to cover the full diversity of toxic expressions, dialects, and code‑switching patterns.
- Token‑level substitution may produce grammatical errors in agglutinative isiXhosa and misses toxic tokens absent from the lexicon.
- Detection accuracy is moderate (61‑72% for isiXhosa), with false positives arising from lexical overlap with neutral terms.

## Confidence
- **Detection efficacy:** Medium  
- **Rewriting success:** Medium  
- **Resource efficiency and cultural adaptiveness:** High  

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑lingual zero‑shot assessment:** Train a detector on one language (e.g., Yorùbá) and test on the other (isiXhosa) without any target‑language data to measure transferability of toxic lexical cues.
2. **Coverage audit on real‑world data:** Collect 200+ toxic sentences per language from external sources (e.g., social media). Compute exact match rate with the parallel corpus and lexicon token coverage. Human‑evaluate detoxification quality for uncovered sentences.
