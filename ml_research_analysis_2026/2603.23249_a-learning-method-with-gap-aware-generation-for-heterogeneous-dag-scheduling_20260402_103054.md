# A Learning Method with Gap-Aware Generation for Heterogeneous DAG Scheduling
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23249
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23249
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Makespan reductions up to 11.7% over the best heuristic and 7.3% over neural baselines, with inference comparable to classical heuristics.

## Executive Summary
Heterogeneous DAG scheduling involves assigning tasks with diverse resource demands and compatibility coefficients to a set of resource pools to minimize the overall makespan. Existing learning-based approaches either suffer from suboptimality gaps inherent in greedy list scheduling or incur high inference costs due to iterative refinement. This paper introduces WeCAN, an end‑to‑end reinforcement learning framework that generates schedules in a single pass. It uses a weighted cross‑attention encoder combined with a longest directed distance GNN to produce task‑pool scores and skip parameters. A skip‑extended list scheduler then uses a decreasing skip score to “wait” and thereby recover optimal schedules that traditional list scheduling cannot reach. On TPC‑H and computation‑graph benchmarks, WeCAN achieves up to 11.7% makespan improvement over the best heuristic and 7.3% over neural baselines, while maintaining inference speeds on par with classical heuristics. The method also generalizes well across different problem sizes and resource pool configurations.

## Method Summary
WeCAN operates in two tightly coupled stages. First, a weighted cross‑attention (WeCA) encoder interleaved with a longest directed distance GNN (LDDGNN) processes the DAG and resource pool descriptions, producing task and pool embeddings. The encoder outputs two key quantities: task‑pool affinity scores u(v,c) (which explicitly incorporate the compatibility matrix Kacc(v,c)) and skip parameters ψ. Second, a skip‑extended list scheduling algorithm (Algorithm 2) iteratively selects actions: it considers ready tasks, samples from a distribution proportional to exp(u + M) (with M masking infeasible assignments), or invokes a skip action with score uskip(k). The skip score is designed to decrease with the step index k, ensuring that in greedy mode the reachable set of schedules (“fiber”) always includes an optimal solution for the given scores. The entire pipeline is single‑pass: all scores and skip parameters are computed once from the encoder output. The policy is optimized using REINFORCE with an average reward baseline, directly minimizing the makespan.

## Key Results
- Up to **11.7%** makespan reduction compared to the best classical heuristic.
- Up to **7.3%** makespan reduction over prior neural scheduling baselines.
- Inference time comparable to classical heuristics and far faster than multi‑round neural approaches, while generalizing well to varying DAG sizes and resource pool counts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The single‑pass design eliminates the need for expensive iterative refinement, giving inference speeds on par with classical heuristics. The weighted cross‑attention explicitly gates task‑pool interactions by the given compatibility matrix Kacc, enabling the model to learn precise affinities. The critical innovation is the skip mechanism: by allowing the scheduler to wait (i.e., skip assignment at a step), the algorithm can effectively reorder tasks without swapping them, thereby overcoming the fundamental limitation of list scheduling that restricts reachable schedules. The order‑space analysis proves that if the skip score is strictly decreasing, the greedy reachable set contains at least one optimal schedule, provided the task‑pool scores are accurate. REINFORCE then directly optimizes these scores and the skip parameters for the makespan objective. The LDDGNN injects strong inductive biases about precedence constraints and critical paths, further guiding the policy toward high‑quality solutions. Together, these components yield both state‑of‑the‑art performance and computational efficiency.

  Why needed: List scheduling is a fast greedy heuristic; adding a skip action expands its reachable set to include optimal schedules while preserving single‑pass inference.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
DAG & Resources → WeCAN Encoder (WeCA + LDDGNN) → Decoder (Score MLP + Skip MLP) → Skip‑Extended List Scheduler (Algorithm 2)

**Critical Path**:  
The longest directed distances computed by LDDGNN are injected into attention masks and bias terms, making DAG precedence constraints a central factor shaping the task‑pool scores.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Single‑pass inference yields speed but forces the skip design to guarantee optimality; multi‑round methods are slower but naturally explore reorderings.  
- Full quadratic attention between tasks and pools is heavier than sparse alternatives, yet acceptable for moderate instance sizes.  
- Compatibility gating leverages the given Kacc matrix, but performance may suffer if Kacc is noisy or incomplete.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Skip score not strictly decreasing → greedy reachable set may miss optimal schedules (caught by failure on P0).  
- Incorrect LDD computation → wrong masks, leading to precedence violations or poor solution quality.  
- Training divergence (e.g., NaN loss) → likely due to mis‑computed baseline or excessive learning rate.

**First Experiments**:  
1. **Validate LDDGNN**: On a 4‑node DAG, manually compute longest directed paths; ensure LDDGNN outputs match and masks follow the 8‑head scheme.  
2. **Test skip on P0**: With greedy mode and hand‑tuned ψ (e.g., α=1, β=0, γ=1), achieve makespan 3.2; in sampling mode, verify the optimal schedule has non‑zero probability.  
3. **Run a single training batch**: Use 10 synthetic instances; perform one REINFORCE step and check that loss does not become NaN and gradients are finite.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to stochastic or communication‑aware scheduling**: Can the order‑space analysis and single‑pass principles handle stochastic processing times or inter‑task communication delays? This would require new theoretical foundations and algorithmic adjustments.  
2. **Necessity and alternatives for the decreasing skip score**: The paper shows a constant skip fails and proposes a decreasing function, but is this form necessary? Could other (even non‑monotonic) skip rules also restore optimality while keeping single‑pass efficiency?  
3. **Comparison with broader heterogeneous GNN architectures**: The evaluation adapts only two HGNN baselines; a systematic study of other heterogeneous graph networks (e.g., relational GNNs, hypergraph models) is lacking and could identify more powerful or scalable designs.  
4. **Scalability to large numbers of tasks and pools**: WeCAN’s O(|V||C|) attention may become prohibitive as the number of pools grows into the hundreds or thousands. What architectural modifications (e.g., sparsification, hierarchical pooling) can preserve performance at such scales?

## Limitations
- Unreleased TPC‑H dataset and baseline adaptations prevent exact replication of reported makespan numbers.  
- Skip parameter training lacks a theoretical guarantee of convergence to a strictly decreasing uskip(k); ensuring stability in practice remains an open issue.  
- Theorem 1 proves that an optimal schedule is reachable in greedy mode given sufficiently accurate scores, but does not guarantee that REINFORCE will learn such parameters; thus the empirical elimination of the optimality gap is not theoretically assured.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority** (11.7% vs. heuristics, 7.3% vs. neural): Medium (some results rely on unreleased data).  
- **Theoretical guarantee** (skip restores fiber coverage): High (rigorous proof provided).  
- **Efficiency & generalization** (fast inference, scalability tests): High (experimental evidence robust).
