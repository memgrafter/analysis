# Watson & Holmes: A Naturalistic Benchmark for Comparing Human and LLM Reasoning
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19914
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19914
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Over nine months in 2025, LLM performance rose from the lower quartile to the top 5% of a human comparison group (Computer Science undergraduates), with reasoning models achieving about the 98th human percentile.

## Executive Summary
The study introduces Watson & Holmes (WnH), a naturalistic reasoning benchmark adapted from a detective tabletop game. It uses incrementally presented narrative evidence, open-ended questions, and unconstrained responses, with an AI-based autograder validated against human assessors. The benchmark reveals that LLM performance improved dramatically over nine months in 2025, climbing from the lower quartile to the top 5% of human CS undergraduates. Reasoning-oriented models (e.g., GPT-5, o3-pro) reached the 98th human percentile, while completion models lagged behind. Models performed worse on longer cases but showed an early inductive reasoning advantage. The benchmark is expected to saturate by late 2026.

## Method Summary
The WnH benchmark adapts the Watson & Holmes board game into a structured evaluation protocol. Cases consist of an introduction (~600 words) and about 15 location cards (~160 words each). Models (or humans) are presented with the introduction and initial questions, then iteratively choose an unvisited location, receive its narrative, and answer the same set of questions. This simulates incremental evidence gathering and belief updating. Responses are open-ended and scored 0–3 on correctness/completeness by an AI autograder (GPT-4.1 Mode 5) that generates a rubric from expert examples and applies it. The autograder achieves human-level inter-rater reliability (87.4% agreement). Overall scores combine progressive (average over all stages) and final scores. Human baseline data was collected from five CS undergraduates under incentivized conditions.

## Key Results
- LLM performance improved dramatically between early and late 2025, with reasoning models (GPT-5, o3-pro) achieving ~98th percentile of human CS undergraduates, while completion models (GPT-4.1) scored near the 53rd percentile.
- Models exhibited an inductive reasoning advantage early in cases (with limited evidence) but this diminished as more evidence accumulated; additionally, models performed worse on longer cases (1900–4000 words) relative to humans.
- WnH correlates moderately with other benchmarks (ARC-AGI, HLE, CBA), suggesting it assesses complementary reasoning constructs; however, saturation is expected by late 2026, after which the benchmark may primarily serve small-model evaluation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Incremental evidence presentation tests dynamic reasoning.
- **Claim:** Incremental evidence presentation tests dynamic reasoning akin to human investigation.
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark structures cases as sequences of location visits, revealing narrative clues progressively. Models must integrate new information with prior context without full upfront knowledge, simulating real-world detective work.
- **Core assumption:** Naturalistic reasoning is not a single inference but a process of belief updating over time.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "incrementally presented narrative evidence"; [section 3.1] "The adapted process flow... locations can be visited in any order... Play continues until all locations have been visited."; corpus neighbors confirm game-based incremental interaction as a design pattern.

### Mechanism 2: Unconstrained open-ended responses elicit richer reasoning traces.
- **Claim:** Unconstrained, open-ended responses elicit richer reasoning traces than multiple-choice formats.
- **Mechanism:** By requiring free-text answers, the benchmark allows models to express partial reasoning, justifications, and uncertainty, avoiding the "guess-the-option" bias of MCQs.
- **Core assumption:** Reasoning quality is transparently expressed in language when not constrained by choices.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "open-ended questions and unconstrained responses"; [section 2.3] "open-ended questions provide a more faithful measure of reasoning ability... MCQ formats limit... assessment to answer accuracy rather than the quality of the reasoning process."; corpus neighbors focus on MCQ limitations.

### Mechanism 3: AI-based autograding, validated against humans, enables scalable evaluation.
- **Claim:** AI-based autograding, validated against human assessors, enables scalable, consistent evaluation of open-ended reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** A rubric-based autograder (GPT-4.1 Mode 5) is tuned to match human inter-rater reliability (87.4% agreement vs 88.6% human-human), allowing large-scale, replicable scoring.
- **Core assumption:** A sufficiently aligned LLM can serve as a reliable, scalable proxy for human judges on complex reasoning tasks.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "AI-based autograder validated against human assessors"; [section 3.2.2] Table 2 shows Mode 5 human-grader agreement (87.4%) near human-human (88.6%), with RMS 0.41 vs 0.40; corpus weak direct support but recent "LLM-as-judge" literature supports feasibility.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Case Repository -> Prompt Templates -> Autograder -> Scoring Engine -> Human Comparison Dataset

- **Critical path:**  
  Initialize → Intro Prompt (get initial answers) → Loop per location: Choose Location Prompt → Question Answering Prompt → Score via Autograder → Until all locations visited → Compute Overall Score.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Fresh prompt vs. context window: Each step is a fresh prompt (no persistent chat) with a full recap; this avoids context-length drift but increases token use.  
  - Scoring granularity: 0–3 scale vs. binary allows measuring partial progress but requires robust rubric-based autograding.  
  - Strategic elements removed: The adaptation removes bidding/locking to isolate reasoning, losing some naturalistic complexity.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Model-specific: cModels plateau near human average; rModels show early inductive advantage but degrade on long cases (word count effect).  
  - Benchmark lifecycle: Approaching saturation (o3-pro score 2.14 vs max ~2.45), diminishing returns for frontier models by 2026.  
  - Autograder risk: Mode 5 chosen for best human agreement; if rubric generation is inconsistent, scoring noise increases.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Smoke Test: Run the full adapted game flow with a single model (e.g., GPT-4o) using provided prompt templates to verify JSON parsing, prompt formatting, and autograding invocation.  
  2. Autograder Validation Replication: Using sample answers from the paper's test dataset, run Mode 5 vs. Mode 4 to confirm reported agreement boost (87.4% vs 86.8%).  
  3. Human-Model Correlation Check: Run 1 human participant and 1 main rModel on the same 11 cases. Compute correlation of location ordering and instantaneous score progression to verify patterns before full sweep.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do reasoning models exhibit an inductive reasoning advantage specifically at early stages of case solving with limited evidence, and why does this advantage diminish as more evidence becomes available?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper reports an advantage for reasoning models at early stages when evidence is scant, linked to inductive reasoning (Section 4.7, Table 9), but this effect does not persist with full case evidence (Section 5.4.2).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not explain the cognitive or architectural mechanisms behind this temporal pattern.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyses of model attention patterns, intermediate reasoning traces, or ablation studies comparing inductive versus deductive prompt strategies across case stages.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What causes the decline in model performance on longer cases (1900–4000 words) despite contemporary models supporting much larger context windows, and how does this relate to inherent limitations in processing extended narratives?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Models perform worse on longer cases relative to humans, a trend significant at 90–95% (Section 5.4.1, Fig 14), even though lengths are well within claimed context limits.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper notes this contradicts expectations about context length scalability but does not investigate whether it stems from attention dilution, loss of coherence, or reasoning depth degradation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying narrative length while isolating factors like information density, positional bias, or retrieval difficulty; probing model representations across document regions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What specific architectural or training innovations in reasoning-oriented models (rModels) account for their step-change performance improvement over completion models (cModels), beyond increased compute?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper attributes roughly half the improvement to a "step change associated with reasoning-oriented model architectures" without detailing which features (e.g., RL, explicit reasoning traces, inference-time scaling) drive this.
- **Why unresolved:** The models compared differ in multiple ways simultaneously; the paper does not isolate architectural contributions from training data or scale.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct comparisons of model variants where reasoning-specific mechanisms (e.g., RL rewards for reasoning, CoT fine-tuning) are toggled, or analysis of internal representations during case-solving.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Given the moderate correlations between WnH and other benchmarks (ARC-AGI, HLE, CBA), what distinct cognitive or reasoning constructs does the WnH benchmark uniquely assess that are not captured by existing benchmarks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper shows WnH correlates moderately (0.64–0.73) with other benchmarks and MDS indicates it is distinct but not radical, implying it measures complementary aspects.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not characterize what reasoning dimensions (e.g., abduction, narrative integration, evidence weighting) WnH emphasizes that other benchmarks miss.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Factor analyses across multiple benchmarks; human reasoning studies comparing performance on WnH versus other tasks; diagnostic items targeting specific reasoning modes.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How should the research community design next-generation reasoning benchmarks that remain challenging as LLMs approach and exceed human-expert performance on current naturalistic tasks like WnH, and what properties should they prioritize?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper concludes that WnH will saturate by late 2026 for frontier models, and while still useful for small models, new benchmarks are needed to track continued progress.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not propose concrete designs for future benchmarks—whether they should be more interactive, multimodal, adversarial, or target specific reasoning bottlenecks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Surveys of remaining model failures on WnH; exploration of game variants with increased complexity; development of benchmarks testing robustness to distractors, temporal reasoning, or collaborative problem-solving.

## Limitations
- **Case corpus not released:** The 11 evaluation case texts and expert marking examples are not provided, blocking exact replication and score verification.
- **Model access/versioning:** Top-performing models (GPT-5, o3-pro) may be proprietary or unavailable; using different model versions for autograding risks score drift.
- **Human baseline generalizability:** Original human data (5 CS undergrads) may not represent broader populations; incentive structure and game familiarity could bias results.
