# Regularized Latent Dynamics Prediction is a Strong Baseline For Behavioral Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15857
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15857
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Regularized latent dynamics prediction (RLDP) matches or exceeds state-of-the-art BFM methods in zero-shot RL, and significantly outperforms them on low-coverage D4RL datasets.

## Executive Summary
Behavioral foundation models (BFMs) aim to generalize to new tasks via learned state features, but typically rely on complex objectives and sufficient data coverage. This paper asks whether a simple latent dynamics prediction objective suffices and finds that naive prediction leads to feature collapse, harming the representable reward span. The proposed solution, regularized latent dynamics prediction (RLDP), augments the prediction loss with an orthogonal regularizer to maintain feature diversity. RLDP avoids Bellman backups and out-of-distribution queries, using only observed state-action pairs. Empirical results on DeepMind Control Suite and D4RL show that RLDP matches or exceeds state-of-the-art BFM methods in zero-shot RL and significantly outperforms them on low-coverage datasets, establishing it as a strong, simple baseline.

## Method Summary
RLDP is a two-phase method for learning task-agnostic representations. In the pre-training phase, a state encoder $\phi$ maps raw states to latent vectors. A dynamics module $g$ takes the current latent and action and predicts the next latent, with its output normalized to lie on the unit sphere. The loss combines a mean-squared error between predicted and actual next latents ($L_d$) and an orthogonal regularizer $L_r$ that penalizes dot products between different states’ embeddings to encourage diversity. The encoder and dynamics are trained on an offline dataset without any policy interaction. In the second phase, $\phi$ is frozen, and a successor feature network $\psi$ and a policy $\pi$ are trained in an alternating manner to adapt to a distribution of tasks, using the fixed $\phi$ to define features. At test time, a new task’s reward function is projected onto the span of $\phi$ to obtain a task embedding $z$, and the corresponding policy $\pi(\cdot,z)$ is executed without further environment interaction.

## Key Results
- RLDP achieves normalized returns of $88.55\pm6.31$ on halfcheetah-medium-expert, $75.53\pm12.70$ on hopper-medium-expert, and $101.30\pm3.92$ on walker2d-medium-expert, significantly outperforming prior BFM methods (FB, PSM, HILP) on low-coverage D4RL tasks.
- On DeepMind Control Suite, RLDP matches or exceeds state-of-the-art zero-shot RL performance across a variety of tasks.
- The method scales to high-dimensional humanoid control, showing competitive performance in that domain as well.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Feature Collapse in Latent Dynamics Prediction
- **Claim:** Latent dynamics prediction alone suffers from feature collapse, reducing the span of representable reward functions.
- **Mechanism:** Minimizing prediction error for future latent states without constraints leads to representations where different states become overly similar, as measured by increasing cosine similarity. This collapse limits the diversity of state features $\phi$, constraining the set of reward functions that can be expressed as a linear combination $r(s) \approx \phi(s)^\top z$.
- **Core assumption:** The latent dynamics prediction objective is insufficiently constrained to preserve feature diversity.
- **Evidence:** Empirical increase in cosine similarity over training when using only $L_d$ (Figure 1); limited direct evidence in the broader corpus.
- **Break condition:** Monitor pairwise cosine similarity; if it does not decrease with regularization, the orthogonal loss $L_r$ is ineffective.

### Mechanism 2: Orthogonal Regularization Preserves Feature Diversity
- **Claim:** Adding an orthogonal regularization on latent representations prevents collapse and maintains feature diversity.
- **Mechanism:** The regularizer $L_r(\phi) = \mathbb{E}[\phi(s)^\top \phi(s')]$ penalizes similarity between encodings of different states, encouraging orthogonality (especially when combined with spherical normalization of outputs). This expands the effective span of $\phi$, enabling a wider class of reward functions to be linearly expressible.
- **Core assumption:** Enforcing orthogonality among state embeddings is a sufficient proxy for maintaining a diverse and expressive feature basis for zero-shot RL.
- **Evidence:** Cosine similarity decreases when regularization is applied (Figure 1); performance drops without spherical normalization (Table 5).
- **Break condition:** If zero-shot performance does not improve with increasing $\lambda$ after a point, or cosine similarity remains high, the regularizer may be too weak or encoder capacity insufficient.

### Mechanism 3: Policy-Independent Objective Avoids OOD and Bellman Issues
- **Claim:** RLDP avoids out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization errors and Bellman backup instability by using a policy-independent objective.
- **Mechanism:** Unlike successor measure methods that require Bellman backups over a fixed set of policies or rewards (which can query OOD actions), RLDP’s loss only depends on observed state-action pairs from the offline dataset. This removes reliance on bootstrapping with potentially OOD actions, making representation learning more stable and robust, especially under limited data coverage.
- **Core assumption:** A policy-independent representation objective generalizes better in low-coverage settings than objectives that implicitly or explicitly evaluate policies beyond the data distribution.
- **Evidence:** Outperformance on low-coverage D4RL tasks (Figure 4, Table 9); discussion of Bellman backup issues in Section 4.1.
- **Break condition:** If the offline dataset has extremely poor coverage of state transitions, even RLDP’s prediction task may suffer from insufficient data, limiting dynamics model accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Behavioral Foundation Models (BFMs) and successor features  
  **Why needed here:** BFMs are the downstream goal; successor features $\psi(s,a,z)$ enable zero-shot policy extraction via linear combination with task embedding $z$ (where $r(s)=\phi(s)^\top z$). Understanding this link is essential to see why $\phi$’s span matters.  
  **Quick check question:** Given a learned $\phi$ and $\psi$, how is the zero-shot policy for a new reward $r$ obtained without additional environment interaction?

- **Concept:** Successor measures and their relationship to value functions  
  **Why needed here:** The BFM framework parameterizes successor measures $M^\pi(s,a,s') = \psi(s,a,z)^\top \phi(s')$, linking to Q-values. RLDP’s claim is that its $\phi$ still produces accurate successor measures despite not training with Bellman backups.  
  **Quick check question:** How does predicting latent dynamics relate to predicting a successor measure distribution?

- **Concept:** Feature collapse in representation learning  
  **Why needed here:** The core problem RLDP solves. Collapse means $\phi$ maps many states to similar vectors, shrinking the span of possible $z$ and harming generalization.  
  **Quick check question:** What observable metric (e.g., cosine similarity) indicates feature collapse, and how does RLDP combat it?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**  
  1. State encoder $\phi(s)$: MLP (2×256 hidden) maps state to latent of dimension $d$.
  2. Dynamics module $g(\phi(s), a)\cdot w$: Action is linearly projected ($A$), concatenated with $\phi(s)$, passed through $g$ (2×512 hidden), then linearly to $w$. Output is spherically normalized to $S^{d-1}$.
  3. Orthogonal regularizer $L_r$: Computes mean dot product of a batch of $\phi(s)$ embeddings.
  4. Successor feature network $\psi(s,a,z)$: Used in BFM phase after $\phi$ is frozen (same architecture as FB).
  5. Policy network $\pi(s,z)$: Conditioned on latent $z$.

- **Critical path**  
  1. Pre-train $\phi$, $g$, $w$ offline using $L_\text{RLDP}$ (prediction + orthogonality) on dataset $\rho$.
  2. Freeze $\phi$.
  3. Pre-train $\psi$ and $\pi$ by alternating successor measure loss (Eq 10) and policy improvement (Eq 5) using the frozen $\phi$ and sampled $z$ (random or goal-encoded).
  4. At test time, for a new reward $r$, infer $z$ via linear regression on $\phi(s)$, then execute $\pi(\cdot,z)$.

- **Design tradeoffs**  
  - Simplicity vs. expressivity: RLDP uses single-step prediction loss ($L_d$) instead of multi-step successor measure losses with Bellman backups. Simplicity avoids OOD issues but may require careful regularization to match expressivity.
  - Regularization strength $\lambda$: Too low → collapse persists; too high → dominates prediction loss, harming dynamics accuracy. Ablation study in section A.4.2.
  - Spherical normalization: Applied to $g$’s output; stabilizes training and improves performance (Table 2). Without it, variance increases.

- **Failure signatures**  
  1. Representation collapse: Cosine similarity among $\phi(s)$ increases over training (monitor Figure 1). Indicates $L_r$ ineffective.
  2. Poor zero-shot generalization: Low normalized returns on DMC or D4RL tasks despite sufficient pre-training steps.
  3. Policy instability during BFM pre-training: If frozen $\phi$ is poor, successor measure learning ($L_\text{zsrl}$) may not converge; monitor Bellman residual.

- **First 3 experiments**  
  1. Orthogonality ablation: Sweep $\lambda$ (0, 0.01, 1.0) on DMC tasks; verify cosine similarity decreases and returns peak at intermediate $\lambda$ (section 5.3, Figure 5).
  2. Encoding horizon $H$ sweep: Compare $H=1,5,10,20$; validate performance is stable across modest $H$ (section A.4.2, Figure 8).
  3. Encoder architecture ablation: Remove spherical normalization on $g$ and/or linear action projection $A$; confirm full RLDP encoder outperforms variants (Table 2, section A.4.2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** What is the theoretical relationship between orthogonal regularization and the span of reward functions that can be represented in BFMs?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors argue that orthogonal regularization maintains feature diversity, which preserves the span of possible reward functions, but they do not provide a formal theoretical link connecting orthogonality to the dimensionality or expressivity of the reward function span.  
**Why unresolved:** Without a rigorous analysis, it is unclear why orthogonality specifically maximizes span or whether it is optimal; alternative diversity-promoting methods might achieve comparable or better performance.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A theorem deriving the connection between feature orthogonality (e.g., bounded cosine similarity) and the rank of the feature matrix, which determines the span. Complementary experiments measuring feature rank/condition number across regularization strengths and correlating with zero-shot performance would also provide strong evidence.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can RLDP be successfully extended to high-dimensional visual observations (e.g., pixel inputs) and what architectural modifications are required?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper evaluates RLDP solely on state-based control tasks and claims the method “does not require any reconstruction, making it able to handle high-dimensional observation space” without empirical validation on images.  
**Why unresolved:** Visual domains introduce challenges such as partial observability, translation invariance, and higher input dimensionality; the effectiveness of simple MLP encoders and latent dynamics prediction on pixels remains an open question.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Applying RLDP to standard visual benchmarks (e.g., DMControl with pixels, Atari, or robotics image tasks) and comparing against pixel-friendly BFMs. Ablating encoder choices (CNN vs. ViT) and investigating whether orthogonality regularization still prevents feature collapse in visual feature spaces would test the claim.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How does the encoding horizon $H$ in the latent dynamics loss influence representation quality and zero-shot RL performance across different MDP structures?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors use fixed horizons (e.g., $H=1$ or $5$) and note performance is relatively stable across $1$, $5$, $10$, but they do not explore larger $H$ or environments with long-term dependencies.  
**Why unresolved:** The effect of capturing multi-step dynamics on learning abstract, temporally-extended representations is not well understood; a longer horizon might better preserve information needed for tasks with delayed rewards or complex stochasticity.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments sweeping $H$ from $1$ to large values on a diverse suite of tasks, measuring not only final zero-shot returns but also predictive accuracy of the learned dynamics model at various horizons, and analyzing how performance correlates with task horizon requirements.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Are there alternative regularization strategies that can match or surpass orthogonal regularization for preventing feature collapse in latent dynamics prediction, and what are their trade-offs?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper compares only “no regularization” to orthogonal regularization and shows the latter is crucial, but does not evaluate other diversity-inducing losses such as maximizing feature entropy, decorrelation penalties, or contrastive approaches.  
**Why unresolved:** Orthogonality may not be the most effective or sample-efficient way to encourage feature diversity; other methods might yield higher span or better sample complexity, particularly in low-coverage regimes.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive ablation study of various regularization techniques (e.g., Barlow Twins loss, VICReg, max entropy, weight decay) on the same environments and dataset coverage levels, with metrics including cosine similarity, feature rank, and zero-shot RL performance. Computational cost and hyperparameter sensitivity should also be reported.

## Limitations
- Feature span is not directly measured; only cosine similarity is used as a proxy, and the theoretical link between orthogonality and representable reward span remains informal.
- The method is evaluated solely on state-based benchmarks; its effectiveness on high-dimensional visual inputs or with different encoder architectures is unknown.
- No theoretical guarantees are provided that the learned representation $\phi$ yields optimal zero-shot generalization under specific data coverage assumptions.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Feature collapse without regularization)**: High – Strong empirical evidence (Figure 1, Table 5) shows increasing cosine similarity when $L_r=0$.
- **Claim 2 (Orthogonal regularization maintains diversity)**: Medium – Empirical results support reduced similarity and performance gains, but the sufficiency of orthogonality as a proxy for feature span is not rigorously validated and may not hold for more complex tasks.
- **Claim 3 (Avoids OOD/Bellman issues)**: Medium – Outperformance on low-coverage D4RL suggests robustness, yet no direct diagnostic confirms the mechanism; OOD effects could still play a role.

## Next Checks
1. Direct feature span assessment: Compute the rank or minimum singular value of the matrix of $\phi(s)$ over a held-out state set, with and without $L_r$, to quantify the actual expansion of the feature subspace.
2. Architecture & modality stress test: Replicate RLDP on image-based benchmarks (e.g., Procgen, Maze) and with transformer encoders to test if orthogonal regularization generalizes beyond MLP state encoders.
3. Longitudinal training stability: Monitor the evolution of the spectral norm of the encoder’s Jacobian or the condition number of the covariance matrix of $\phi(S)$ throughout pre-training to diagnose whether orthogonality merely delays collapse rather than prevents it.
