# How well can off-the-shelf LLMs elucidate molecular structures from mass spectra using chain-of-thought reasoning?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06289
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06289
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Zero‑shot CoT prompting yields high format adherence but near‑zero chemical accuracy (DBE accuracy ~4%, exact‑match 0%) on MassSpecGym.

## Executive Summary
Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) is a cornerstone technique for molecular identification, yet predicting complete molecular structures from raw spectra remains a difficult inverse problem. This paper investigates whether off-the-shelf large language models (LLMs) can perform this task by leveraging chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning—a method that prompts the model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before final output. The authors design a domain-specific CoT prompt that encapsulates expert chemist steps: degree of unsaturation (DBE) calculation, peak identification, neutral loss analysis, and fragment assembly. Using this prompt, they evaluate four state-of-the-art LLMs (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-70B, Llama-3-8B) in a zero-shot setting on the MassSpecGym benchmark, which pairs MS/MS spectra with molecular structures.

The results are striking: while all models achieve high format adherence (e.g., Claude outputs valid SMILES in 93.7% of cases), chemical correctness is virtually absent. DBEs are accurate in only ~4% of predictions, molecular formula consistency is 0%, and exact SMILES matches never occur. Structural similarity metrics (Tanimoto, MCES) remain low (0.09–0.17). Moreover, CoT reasoning frequently contradicts the final SMILES, indicating that the models generate explanations that are ungrounded in the spectral data or chemical principles. When contrasted with spectrum-trained models like DiffMS (Tanimoto 0.39), the LLMs underperform dramatically. The study concludes that CoT alone, without domain-specific constraints or spectral grounding, cannot yield accurate molecular elucidation from mass spectra.

## Method Summary
The study uses the MassSpecGym dataset, which contains tandem MS spectra (peak lists of m/z and intensity) and corresponding molecular structures in SMILES format. The core method revolves around a handcrafted prompt that instructs the LLM to reason like a chemist. The prompt includes four explicit steps: (1) calculate DBE from the molecular formula (estimated from the spectrum?), (2) identify the most intense peaks, (3) analyze neutral losses, and (4) assemble fragments to propose a full structure. The LLM is asked to show its work step-by-step and finally output a SMILES string. Four off-the-shelf LLMs are tested: Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-70B, and Llama-3-8B. All models are used in their default zero-shot configuration, i.e., no examples of spectrum-to-structure mappings are provided.

Model outputs are parsed to extract the final SMILES and the intermediate reasoning. Evaluation employs a suite of metrics: SMILES validity (syntactic correctness via RDKit), DBE accuracy (whether computed DBE matches the ground truth), formula consistency (atom counts match), exact match (identical to ground truth SMILES), Tanimoto similarity (based on Morgan fingerprints), and Maximum Common Edge Substructure (MCES) distance. Additionally, the authors manually or automatically check for contradictions between the CoT text and the final SMILES.

## Key Results
- Format adherence is high (e.g., valid SMILES in >90% of cases for Claude), but chemical correctness is negligible: DBE accuracy ~4%, formula consistency 0%, exact-match 0% across all models.
- Structural similarity remains low (Tanimoto 0.09–0.12, MCES 0.14–0.17), far below spectrum-trained models (e.g., DiffMS Tanimoto 0.39).
- CoT reasoning often contradicts the predicted SMILES (e.g., reasoning identifies a certain DBE but the SMILES implies a different one), revealing ungrounded explanations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism underlying the observed failure is the lack of chemical and spectral grounding in off-the-shelf LLMs. These models are trained on general text and can produce fluent reasoning and syntactically valid SMILES, but they do not inherently understand the physics of mass spectrometry fragmentation or enforce chemical validity. Consequently, their CoT often generates plausible but incorrect intermediate steps that are not consistent with the final output. The textual representation of spectra as peak lists fails to provide the numerical pattern recognition needed for accurate structure elucidation. Without explicit constraints (e.g., from a spectral encoder or a chemistry-aware module), the LLM’s predictions remain disconnected from the underlying data, leading to near-zero chemical accuracy despite high format adherence.

## Foundational Learning
**1. Mass Spectrometry (MS)**  
- *Why needed*: Provides the experimental fragmentation data (m/z and intensity) that serves as input for the structure prediction task.  
- *Quick check*: What two key pieces of information are extracted from an MS/MS spectrum? (Answer: m/z ratios and ion intensities.)

**2. Chemical Structure Representation (SMILES)**  
- *Why needed*: The target output format; encodes molecular graphs as strings that LLMs can generate and programs like RDKit can interpret.  
- *Quick check*: What does a SMILES string represent? (Answer: A linear notation for a molecular structure, specifying atoms and bonds.)

**3. Degree of Unsaturation (DBE)**  
- *Why needed*: Used in the expert reasoning steps to constrain possible structures; indicates the total number of rings and π bonds.  
- *Quick check*: How is DBE computed from a molecular formula CₓHᵧN_zO_w? (Answer: DBE = x - y/2 + z/2 + 1, assuming halogens are treated like hydrogens.)

**4. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**  
- *Why needed*: Encourages the LLM to produce explicit intermediate reasoning, mimicking how a chemist would break down the problem.  
- *Quick check*: What is the main purpose of CoT prompting in complex problem solving? (Answer: To decompose the task into intermediate steps, improving the model’s ability to reach correct final answers.)

**5. Off-the-shelf Large Language Models**  
- *Why needed*: Act as the general-purpose reasoning engine; the study tests whether pre-trained LLMs can be repurposed for domain-specific tasks without fine-tuning.  
- *Quick check*: Name one LLM evaluated in the study and its general characteristics (e.g., size, provider). (Answer: Claude-3.5-Sonnet, a commercial model from Anthropic.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Mass Spectrum (peak list) → Domain-specific CoT Prompt → LLM → Generated Text (reasoning + SMILES) → SMILES Extractor

**Critical Path**:  
1. The spectrum is formatted into a textual peak list.  
2. The prompt builder inserts this list into the CoT template, which enumerates the reasoning steps.  
3. The LLM processes the prompt and auto-regressively generates the reasoning and final SMILES.  
4. The output parser extracts the SMILES (typically the last line or a tagged section).  
Failures can occur at any step, notably during LLM generation where chemical constraints are violated.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Zero-shot vs. Few-shot*: Zero-shot avoids selecting in-context examples but sacrifices accuracy; few-shot may improve performance at the cost of prompt engineering.  
- *Textual vs. Numerical Spectrum Encoding*: Textual peak lists are natural for LLMs but lose intensity nuances and may not respect permutation invariance; numerical embeddings could preserve more information but require adaptation.  
- *Off-the-shelf vs. Specialized Models*: Using general LLMs is convenient but they lack built‑in chemical knowledge; fine-tuning on MS data could improve accuracy but reduces portability.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Contradictions between CoT reasoning and the final SMILES (e.g., DBE mismatch).  
- DBE accuracy near random (~4%) and absolute formula inconsistency (0%).  
- Moderate SMILES syntactic validity but chemically invalid structures.  
- Low structural similarity metrics (Tanimoto <0.12).

**Three First Experiments**:  
1. **Single-Example Walkthrough**: Select a random spectrum, run the full pipeline, and manually inspect the CoT and SMILES for internal consistency and chemical plausibility.  
2. **Baseline Performance on a Subset**: Evaluate all four LLMs on the first 100 MassSpecGym samples; compute SMILES validity, DBE accuracy, and Tanimoto similarity to establish a quantitative baseline.  
3. **Prompt Ablation Pilot**: Remove one of the four expert steps (e.g., DBE calculation) from the CoT prompt, re-run on the same 100 samples, and measure the change in DBE accuracy to gauge the step’s importance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can reinforcement learning be leveraged to fine-tune large language models to produce chemically accurate and self-consistent chain-of-thought reasoning for mass spectral structure elucidation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors conclude that “future work that combines domain knowledge and reinforcement learning to achieve chemically grounded AI reasoning” is necessary, noting that zero‑shot LLMs fail to achieve chemical accuracy.
- Why unresolved: Current zero‑shot CoT prompting yields low DBE accuracy and high contradiction rates; RL fine‑tuning on spectral reasoning tasks has not been investigated.
- What evidence would resolve it: An RL‑based fine‑tuning framework that optimizes models on metrics like DBE correctness, formula consistency, and structural similarity, with reward functions that penalize contradictions between reasoning and output. Success would be demonstrated by significant improvements over zero‑shot baselines on MassSpecGym.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can LLMs be effectively integrated with spectral encoders and chemical constraint modules to ground their structure predictions in the underlying fragmentation physics?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The discussion states that “Progress in MS‑to‑structure prediction will therefore require integrating LLMs with chemical constraints, spectral encoders, or physics‑informed priors.”
- Why unresolved: Existing LLMs treat spectra as text, lacking a mechanism to interpret raw m/z‑intensity data; it is unknown how to combine perceptual spectral features with generative language modeling.
- What evidence would resolve it: A multimodal architecture where a spectral encoder processes the spectrum into embeddings that are fused with the LLM’s textual reasoning; evaluation would show improved DBE accuracy, formula consistency, and structural similarity compared to text‑only baselines.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can ReAct‑style agents that interleave natural‑language reasoning with external chemical tools (e.g., RDKit, fragmentation simulators) resolve the high contradiction rates between CoT explanations and predicted SMILES?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The introduction mentions ReAct‑style agents as a promising direction for iterative reasoning in physically grounded domains, but this remains “largely unexplored” for mass spectral elucidation.
- Why unresolved: Zero‑shot CoT leads to >70% contradictions; tool use could enable verification and correction of intermediate steps like DBE calculations or substructure assembly.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement a system where the LLM can call tools to compute DBE, validate atom counts, or simulate fragmentation, and assess reduction in contradictions and improvements in chemical metrics.

### Open Question 4
- Question: To what extent does multimodal pretraining on paired mass spectra, molecular graphs, and textual descriptions improve the spectral grounding and structural accuracy of LLMs compared to text‑only models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that “substantial architectural and multimodal advances will be necessary to bridge this gap,” implying that current text‑only models are insufficient.
- Why unresolved: LLMs lack direct exposure to spectra; pretraining on spectra‑molecule pairs could align textual reasoning with spectral features, but it is unclear how to design such pretraining and whether it transfers to zero‑shot reasoning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train a multimodal LLM on a large corpus of MS/MS spectra with corresponding SMILES and natural language descriptions, then evaluate on MassSpecGym with CoT prompting; compare performance to text‑only LLMs.

## Limitations
- Limited to four off‑the‑shelf LLMs and a single zero‑shot prompt; results may not generalize to fine‑tuned or few‑shot setups.
- Evaluation on only the MassSpecGym dataset, which might not represent the full diversity of real‑world mass spectra.
- Metrics emphasize final SMILES correctness without deep analysis of reasoning quality beyond contradiction checks.

## Confidence
- Empirical performance numbers and contradiction observations: **High**.
- Broad conclusion that CoT alone cannot achieve accurate elucidation without domain constraints: **Medium**.
- Generalization to all mass spectrometry tasks or LLMs: **Low**.

## Next Checks
1. **Few-shot prompting**: Integrate 2–5 annotated examples of spectrum-to-structure mapping into the CoT prompt to evaluate whether in-context learning improves chemical correctness.
2. **Prompt component ablation**: Remove each expert step (DBE, peak ID, neutral loss, fragment assembly) from the CoT template to isolate which parts contribute to performance.
3. **Cross-dataset replication**: Apply the same zero-shot CoT protocol to independent mass spectrometry benchmarks (e.g., NIST, MoNA) to assess robustness across instruments and sample types.
