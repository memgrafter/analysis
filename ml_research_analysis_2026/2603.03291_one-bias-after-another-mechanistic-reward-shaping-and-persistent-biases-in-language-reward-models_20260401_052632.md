# One Bias After Another: Mechanistic Reward Shaping and Persistent Biases in Language Reward Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03291  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03291  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Low‑complexity biases (length, uncertainty, position) in reward models are linearly separable and can be mitigated via null‑space projection without retraining; high‑complexity biases (sycophancy, model‑style) remain resistant.

## Executive Summary
Language reward models (RMs) used for aligning large language models exhibit persistent, varied biases that lead to reward hacking. The authors categorize these biases by complexity: *low‑complexity* (e.g., length, uncertainty, position) correlate with spurious linear features in RM representations, while *high‑complexity* (e.g., sycophancy, model‑style) are entangled and not easily corrected. They propose a lightweight, model‑internal intervention: *mechanistic reward shaping* via linear activation probes (difference‑of‑means) that identify bias directions and nullify them by projection. Applied to five existing RMs across multiple benchmarks, the method significantly reduces targeted biases—closing the accuracy gap between concise and verbose correct answers, improving calibration for hedging, and lowering position variance—without sacrificing overall ranking accuracy on RewardBench‑2. The approach generalizes out‑of‑distribution and is data‑efficient. However, linear probes fail for high‑complexity biases, underscoring fundamental limits of this technique.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces a post‑hoc debiasing technique for reward models. For each low‑complexity bias, a small contrastive probe dataset (≈500 examples) is constructed: pairs of responses that are identical except for the bias attribute (e.g., correct verbose vs. correct concise). The final‑layer hidden state (prior to the reward head) is extracted for these examples, and a linear *diff‑mean* probe vector **p** is computed as the normalized difference of the mean activations of the two groups. During inference, the RM’s activation **h** for a new input is projected onto the orthogonal complement of **p** (or an orthonormal set of probes for multi‑direction biases like position) to remove the bias component before passing to the reward head. The method requires no retraining, uses frozen RMs, and is evaluated on bias‑specific metrics (e.g., accuracy gaps, variance, calibration) as well as overall ranking accuracy on RewardBench‑2 to ensure non‑inferiority.

## Key Results
- Length bias mitigation closes the accuracy gap between concise and verbose correct answers; reward‑length correlation on RewardBench‑2 is significantly reduced.  
- Uncertainty debiasing increases preference for hedging when answers are incorrect and improves calibration (Spearman correlation with confidence rises from 0.18 to 0.39 for Skywork‑Qwen8B).  
- Position bias variance decreases after applying an orthonormal probe set.  
- Overall ranking performance on RewardBench‑2 remains non‑inferior (p < 0.001).  
- The intervention generalizes out‑of‑distribution to benchmarks beyond the probe’s training domain.  
- High‑complexity biases (sycophancy, model‑style) are unaffected by linear projection, confirming their entangled nature.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Linearity of Low‑Complexity Biases
- **Claim:** Low‑complexity reward model biases (e.g., length, uncertainty, position) are mediated by approximately linear directions in the RM’s activation space, which can be identified and nulled via linear probes without retraining.  
- **Mechanism:** Construct a “difference‑of‑means” (DiffMean) probe vector that captures the bias direction (e.g., verbose vs. concise correct answers). During inference, project the RM’s final‑layer activation onto the orthogonal complement of this probe direction, thereby removing the component that encodes the spurious correlation before computing the reward score.  
- **Core assumption:** The Linear Representation Hypothesis applies—the bias signal is linearly separable and aligns with a dominant direction in latent space.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - The abstract states that low‑complexity biases “correlate with spurious linear features in RM representations.”  
  - Section 3.1 defines low‑complexity biases as those “captured by linear directions.”  
  - Section 4 presents experiments where nulling these probes significantly reduces each targeted bias while preserving overall ranking accuracy on RewardBench‑2.  
- **Break condition:** Fails for high‑complexity biases (sycophancy, model‑style) where the signal is entangled and not well‑approximated by a single linear direction (Section 5).

### Mechanism 2: Bias Complexity Predicts Linear Amenability
- **Claim:** Categorizing RM biases by complexity (low vs. high) is predictive of whether simple linear interventions will succeed, guiding targeted debiasing efforts.  
- **Mechanism:** Systematically test whether a bias can be mitigated by removing a linear probe direction. Success indicates the bias is primarily low‑complexity (correlational). Failure, where intervention harms desirable behavior or has no effect, indicates high‑complexity (entangled) bias.  
- **Core assumption:** Bias complexity correlates with the linear decomposability of its underlying representational signal.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - The abstract notes: “We categorize these by complexity: low‑complexity biases… high‑complexity biases (sycophancy, style) are entangled.”  
  - Section 3.1 provides the formal taxonomy and hypothesis linking complexity to linear amenability.  
  - Section 5 shows sycophancy and model‑style probes fail to disentangle harmful from helpful agreement signals, confirming high complexity.  
- **Break condition:** A bias shows mixed results—partially mitigable by linear probes but requiring additional constraints, or varies across models/tasks. The binary categorization may be too coarse.

### Mechanism 3: Generalization of Linear Probes
- **Claim:** Linear probes trained on narrow, synthetic or domain‑specific bias datasets generalize to out‑of‑distribution, naturalistic benchmarks (e.g., RewardBench‑2), indicating they capture fundamental, transferable bias directions.  
- **Mechanism:** Probes built from controlled contrasts (e.g., verbose vs. concise correct answers on GSM8K) are applied to RM activations from diverse tasks (chat, safety, reasoning). Effective decorrelation of reward with the spurious feature (e.g., length) on OOD data confirms the probe’s generality.  
- **Core assumption:** The spurious correlation manifests as a consistent linear feature across contexts, not a dataset‑specific artifact.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - The abstract mentions generalizes out‑of‑distribution.  
  - Section 4.4 shows length‑specific reward‑length correlations on RewardBench‑2 significantly decrease for DeBERTa and adjust over‑correction for newer models. Also shows LLM‑as‑a‑judge confirmation for uncertainty probes.  
  - Figures 4–8 provide per‑model reward vs. length plots before/after debiasing, demonstrating consistent pattern shifts.  
- **Break condition:** Distribution shift is too extreme (e.g., new modality, language) or the bias is context‑dependent and not captured by the original probe construction.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Linear Representation Hypothesis**  
  - *Why needed here:* The entire intervention assumes high‑level concepts (like “bias toward length”) are encoded as linear directions in activation space. Without this, the probe‑nulling approach lacks theoretical grounding.  
  - *Quick check question:* If a concept is linearly represented, what mathematical operation would you use to remove its influence from a vector?
- **Concept: Reward Hacking / Spurious Correlation**  
  - *Why needed here:* The problem being addressed. RMs exploit easy‑to‑learn signals (length, position) that correlate with human preference but are not causal of quality.  
  - *Quick check question:* Give an example of a spurious feature an RM might prefer for a correct, concise answer versus an incorrect, verbose one.
- **Concept: Null‑Space Projection**  
  - *Why needed here:* The core technical operation. Given a probe direction **p**, the intervention computes **h_null = h – (pᵀh)p**, removing the component along **p**.  
  - *Quick check question:* If you have two orthogonal probe directions, how would you remove both simultaneously?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Probe datasets → Probes computed via difference‑of‑means → Stored probe directions **pₖ**. During RM inference: input → RM base transformer → activation **h** (final layer, last token) → Projector: **h′ = h – Σ αₖ(pₖᵀh)pₖ** → RM reward head → debiased reward score.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Define bias & construct contrastive probe dataset (minimal examples, often synthetically generated).  
  2. Extract RM activations for probe set, compute DiffMean probe vector(s).  
  3. Integrate projection into RM inference: modify forward pass to subtract projected components before reward head.  
  4. Evaluate on bias‑specific test set (should show reduced bias) and RewardBench‑2 (should show non‑inferior ranking accuracy).

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Simplicity vs. coverage:* Linear probes are cheap and interpretable but only catch linear biases; complex biases require more sophisticated interventions.  
  - *Probe specificity:* A length probe may inadvertently capture other correlated features if not carefully constructed (e.g., “verbose correct” might also be more detailed).  
  - *Strength α:* Over‑subtraction (α>1) can degrade performance; under‑subtraction (α<1) leaves bias residual. Calibrate using a validation set.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Bias metrics unchanged after projection → bias is high‑complexity or probe direction poorly estimated.  
  - Ranking accuracy on RewardBench‑2 drops significantly → probe removed non‑spurious signal or over‑subtracted.  
  - Inverse bias introduced (e.g., concise now preferred) → α too high or probe direction inverted.  
  - High‑complexity biases (sycophancy, style) unaffected → expected, linear probes only target low‑complexity.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Length bias replication:** On GSM8K‑MC, verify baseline RM prefers verbose correct over concise correct (or vice versa). Apply length probe and confirm the accuracy gap between these conditions closes.  
  2. **Uncertainty calibration:** On a held‑out set, compute Spearman correlation between RM reward and answer correctness after appending hedging phrases (“I’m not sure”) vs. direct answers. Probe should increase correlation (better calibration).  
  3. **Position bias in MCQ:** For a multiple‑choice dataset, compute accuracy variance across positions A–D. Apply position probe (orthonormal set for A,B,C,D). Variance should decrease significantly.

## Limitations
- Exact architecture details (layer for activation extraction, reward head structure) are unspecified, risking implementation errors.  
- Probe construction details (positive/negative split ratio, Gram–Schmidt order for multi‑direction probes) are not provided, affecting reproducibility.  
- The binary complexity taxonomy may be too coarse; some biases might be partially linear or context‑dependent.  
- High‑complexity biases (sycophancy, model‑style) remain unaddressed by this method.

## Confidence
- *Low‑complexity biases are linearly separable and correctable:* Medium‑High  
- *Bias complexity predicts linear amenability:* Medium  
- *Linear probes generalize OOD:* Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Probe direction separation test:** On a held‑out probe set (e.g., 100 length‑contrast pairs), compute mean(pᵀh) for positive vs. negative examples. A significant t‑test (p < 0.01) validates probe strength.  
2. **Orthogonalization order impact:** For position bias, vary Gram–Schmidt direction order (A→D vs. D→A) and measure: (a) position variance reduction, (b) RewardBench‑2 accuracy delta. Identical results confirm robustness.  
3. **Extreme OOD stress test:** Apply a trained length probe to a multilingual reward dataset (e.g., M‑RewardBench). Measure residual reward‑length Spearman correlation; a significant reduction (<0.1) confirms generalization beyond English/domains.
