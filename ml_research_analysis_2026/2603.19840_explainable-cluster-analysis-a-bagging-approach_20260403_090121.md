# Explainable cluster analysis: a bagging approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19840
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19840
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The proposed bagging ensemble with feature dropout improves clustering stability, robustness, and explainability, with feature importance scores that correctly identify relevant variables and outperform existing methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a bagging-based ensemble clustering framework that integrates feature dropout to enhance explainability. By generating multiple bootstrap samples with randomly dropped features, the method computes feature importance via mutual information weighted by the Dunn index, and selects a consensus partition by minimizing weighted variation of information. Empirical evaluations on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate superior clustering stability and accuracy compared to K-means and hierarchical clustering, as well as more reliable feature rankings. The Bayesian bootstrap variant further improves performance in small-sample scenarios.

## Method Summary
The method proceeds as follows: For each bootstrap replicate, a random sample of instances (with replacement) is drawn, and a random subset of features is dropped. A base clustering algorithm (e.g., K-means) is applied to the reduced data to obtain a partition. This yields an ensemble of partitions. Feature importance is quantified by the normalized mutual information between each feature (considered individually) and the cluster labels, averaged across replicates and weighted by the Dunn index of each partition (to prioritize higher-quality partitions). The final consensus partition is the weighted Fréchet mean of all partitions under the variation of information metric, minimizing the total weighted VI. The approach is model-agnostic regarding the base clusterer and includes a Bayesian bootstrap option for small samples.

## Key Results
- The method significantly improves Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Fowlkes-Mallows Index (FMI) over K-means and hierarchical clustering on benchmark datasets.
- Feature importance scores accurately rank true relevant features above noise, outperforming state-of-the-art explainable clustering techniques like URF and CUBT.
- The Bayesian bootstrap variant shows particular robustness in low-sample regimes, maintaining high stability and interpretability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The bagging ensemble reduces variance and increases robustness by aggregating many perturbed clusterings. Feature dropout allows each feature's contribution to be assessed by examining how its absence affects the partition (via mutual information). Weighting by the Dunn index ensures that higher-quality partitions have more influence on both feature importance and consensus selection. Minimizing weighted variation of information for consensus ensures that the final partition is central in the ensemble with respect to a metric that respects cluster structure. The Bayesian bootstrap provides better uncertainty estimates when data is scarce.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating)**  
  - *Why needed*: To create diverse replicates that capture different aspects of the data distribution, thereby reducing overfitting and improving stability.  
  - *Quick check*: Confirm that each replicate uses a different random seed and that the sample size matches the original.

- **Feature Dropout**  
  - *Why needed*: To evaluate the importance of each feature by measuring the impact of its omission on clustering quality.  
  - *Quick check*: Verify that each replicate randomly excludes a predetermined proportion of features, and that the excluded set varies across replicates.

- **Mutual Information (MI)**  
  - *Why needed*: To quantify the statistical dependence between a single feature and the cluster assignments, indicating how well the feature explains the clusters.  
  - *Quick check*: For a categorical feature, check that MI reduces to entropy-based calculation; for continuous, ensure appropriate discretization or density estimation.

- **Dunn Index**  
  - *Why needed*: To measure the quality of a partition (compactness and separation), which is used to weight the importance of each replicate.  
  - *Quick check*: Compute Dunn index on a synthetic dataset with well-separated clusters; confirm that it is higher for better separated clusters.

- **Variation of Information (VI)**  
  - *Why needed*: A metric between clusterings that is symmetric and satisfies triangle inequality, used to find a consensus partition via the Fréchet mean.  
  - *Quick check*: Validate that VI is zero for identical partitions and increases with dissimilarity.

- **Weighted Fréchet Mean**  
  - *Why needed*: To aggregate partitions in a way that accounts for their quality (weights) and respects the geometry of the VI metric.  
  - *Quick check*: For a set of partitions with given weights, compute the weighted Fréchet mean by minimizing the sum of weighted VI to the mean; confirm that the solution is unique (or check algorithm converges).

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data -> [Bootstrap & Feature Dropout] -> Base Clustering -> Partition Collection -> Feature Importance Computation -> Weighted Consensus Solver -> Output (Final Partition + Feature Scores)

### Critical Path
1. Generate B bootstrap replicates with feature dropout.  
2. For each replicate, run the base clustering algorithm with predefined K to obtain a partition.  
3. Compute Dunn index for each partition (for weighting).  
4. For each feature, compute its normalized mutual information with the partition labels across replicates; aggregate using Dunn weights to get feature importance.  
5. Compute the weighted Fréchet mean (consensus partition) by minimizing total weighted variation of information to all replicate partitions.  
6. Output the consensus partition and ranked feature importances.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Ensemble size (B)**: Larger B improves stability but increases computational cost. Diminishing returns after a certain point.
- **Dropout rate**: Higher dropout increases diversity and emphasizes feature interactions but may degrade individual partition quality if too many features are removed.
- **Base clustering algorithm**: More flexible algorithms (e.g., DBSCAN) may capture non-spherical shapes but introduce additional hyperparameters; K-means is simple but assumes spherical clusters.
- **Weighting scheme**: Using Dunn index gives more influence to better partitions but may overweight partitions from small clusters if Dunn index is biased by cluster size.

### Failure Signatures
- Feature importance scores become unstable (high variance across runs) when dropout rate is too low or too high.
- Consensus partition quality degrades when base clusterer is inappropriate for the data (e.g., K-means on non-convex clusters).
- Dunn index weighting may produce biased feature importances if partitions have extreme size imbalances (Dunn index tends to favor large clusters? Actually Dunn index uses inter- and intra-cluster distances; it can be sensitive to noise and outliers).
- High-dimensional data with many irrelevant features can cause mutual information estimates to be noisy; may need dimensionality reduction.

### First Experiments
1. **Synthetic validation**: Generate synthetic data with known true clusters and 5 relevant features plus 5 noise features. Run the method and verify that the top-ranked features are the relevant ones, and that the consensus ARI is high.
2. **Stability comparison**: Compare the stability (e.g., average pairwise VI across replicates) of the consensus method against single-run K-means on real datasets (e.g., Iris, Wine) to quantify improvement.
3. **Parameter sweep**: Evaluate the effect of dropout probability (e.g., 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) and ensemble size (e.g., 50, 100, 200) on feature importance consistency (correlation across runs) and computational time, to identify robust settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How should the number of clusters K be determined within the proposed bagging framework, and how does the choice of K-estimation method affect the quality and interpretability of the results?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] In the conclusion, the authors state that "estimation of the number of clusters K can be implemented through BBC method [31], other optimal K selection approaches (e.g. silhouette [58]), or cluster algorithms that do not need a predefined K (e.g. DBSCAN [6])."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper assumes K is known in all experiments, leaving a gap for real-world applications where K is unknown. Different K-estimation strategies might yield different consensus partitions and feature importance scores, but their comparative performance is not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic empirical comparisons of various K-estimation techniques (e.g., silhouette, gap statistic, BBC) integrated into the bagging pipeline across diverse datasets, measuring both clustering validity (ARI, FMI) and feature importance coherence, would clarify the best approach.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does the choice of base clustering algorithm (e.g., K-means vs. hierarchical vs. DBSCAN) influence the stability, accuracy, and feature importance scores produced by the proposed method?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The framework is presented as applicable to "any clustering algorithm" (abstract), but the implementation and evaluation exclusively use K-means. No experiments with alternative algorithms are reported.
- **Why unresolved**: Different clustering algorithms have distinct sensitivities to noise, initialization, and cluster shape. The interaction between the base algorithm and the bagging/feature dropout mechanism may significantly affect the robustness and interpretability of the outputs, but this remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A comprehensive experimental study applying the same bagging scheme with multiple base clustering algorithms on benchmark datasets, assessing clustering performance (ARI, FMI) and feature importance consistency (e.g., correlation with known ground-truth features), would reveal algorithmic dependencies and guide method selection.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can the mutual information-based feature importance score be refined to account for feature redundancy and interactions, particularly in high-dimensional or correlated datasets?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The method computes normalized mutual information for each feature independently, weighted by the Dunn index. No mechanism is included to adjust for inter-feature correlations, which may cause redundant features to receive inflated importance.
- **Why unresolved**: When features are correlated, the current score may assign high importance to multiple correlated variables even if only a subset is truly discriminative, potentially leading to misleading interpretations and suboptimal feature selection.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development and evaluation of extensions such as conditional mutual information or redundancy-aware measures, compared against the current approach on datasets with known correlated features, would quantify the benefits and necessity of such adjustments.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What are the theoretical properties of the weighted Fréchet mean consensus partition under the variation of information metric, such as consistency or convergence rates as the number of bootstrap replicas increases?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The consensus partition is defined as the weighted Fréchet mean of the observed partitions with respect to VI, but the paper does not provide any theoretical guarantees about this estimator.
- **Why unresolved**: Without theoretical foundations, it is difficult to understand the asymptotic behavior of the method, choose tuning parameters (e.g., number of replicas B) with confidence, or assess its reliability in challenging scenarios.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A formal analysis proving (or disproving) consistency of the weighted Fréchet mean estimator as B → ∞ under model assumptions, supplemented by simulation studies illustrating convergence behavior and sensitivity to B, would strengthen the methodological groundwork.

## Limitations
- Assumes known number of clusters K; real-world applicability limited when K is unknown.
- Relies on mutual information estimation which can be biased for continuous features without proper preprocessing.
- Dunn index weighting may be sensitive to cluster shape/size and introduces computational overhead; scalability to large datasets uncertain.
