# HamVision: Hamiltonian Dynamics as Inductive Bias for Medical Image Analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21377
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21377
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Introduces a damped harmonic oscillator as a structured inductive bias for medical image analysis, achieving state-of-the-art Dice scores on four segmentation benchmarks and top accuracy on two classification benchmarks with 8.57M parameters.

## Executive Summary
HamVision proposes a novel neural architecture that uses the dynamics of a damped harmonic oscillator as an inductive bias for medical image analysis. The oscillator’s phase-space decomposition yields three distinct representations: position (filtered features), momentum (spatial gradients encoding boundaries), and energy (saliency). These are integrated into a U‑Net‑like segmentation model (HamSeg) and a classification model (HamCls) via task‑specific heads. HamSeg achieves SOTA Dice on ISIC 2018, ISIC 2017, TN3K, and ACDC with only 8.57M parameters. HamCls achieves SOTA accuracy on BloodMNIST and PathMNIST and competitive results on other MedMNIST benchmarks. The method demonstrates modality‑agnostic performance across dermoscopy, ultrasound, MRI, microscopy, and pathology.

## Method Summary
HamVision builds on a ConvNeXt encoder and introduces a Hamiltonian bottleneck that processes features through a parallel oscillator bank. The oscillator is implemented via four‑directional (horizontal and vertical) scans over the spatial extent, with per‑channel learnable frequency, damping, and step size. The complex state z = q + ip evolves according to dz/dt = (-ν + iω)z + u, producing real part q (position) and imaginary part p (momentum). Energy H = ½|z|² is computed per channel. A squeeze‑and‑excitation module produces a spatial energy map Hmap. The bottleneck fuses the oscillator output with a ConvNeXt branch via a gate initialized with a +2 bias. For segmentation (HamSeg), the decoder uses energy‑gated skip connections (centered and scaled by learnable γ) and injects momentum at every level via concatenation. The coarsest decoder stage also includes phase‑space attention. For classification (HamCls), global average pooling of q, |p|, and Hmap forms a phase‑space feature vector, followed by a MLP classifier. Training uses AdamW, cosine decay, and standard augmentations.

## Key Results
- HamSeg SOTA Dice: ISIC 2018 (89.38%), ISIC 2017 (88.40%), TN3K (87.05%), ACDC (92.40%) with 8.57M parameters.
- HamCls SOTA accuracy: BloodMNIST (98.85%), PathMNIST (96.65%); competitive on other MedMNIST benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of HamVision arises from three interconnected mechanisms:
1. **Phase‑space decomposition from oscillator dynamics** – The damped harmonic oscillator’s intrinsic dynamics produce three functionally distinct, interpretable representations—position q (feature content), momentum p (spatial gradients encoding boundaries), and energy H (integrated saliency)—from a single shared bottleneck without task‑specific supervision. The momentum acts as a bandpass differentiator, peaking at boundaries, while energy combines both q and p into a saliency map.
2. **Task‑specific exploitation of phase‑space outputs** – For segmentation, energy gates skip connections to emphasize salient regions, and momentum is injected at all decoder levels to provide boundary information. For classification, global pooling of the three streams creates a phase‑space feature vector that captures discriminative texture and saliency. The shared physics‑structured bottleneck enables parameter‑efficient specialization.
3. **Modality‑agnostic inductive bias** – The oscillator’s outputs are based on fundamental image statistics—spatial derivatives for boundaries, integrated activity for saliency, filtered signal for content—which are relevant across medical imaging modalities. Empirical results across dermoscopy, ultrasound, MRI, microscopy, and pathology confirm this generality.

## Foundational Learning
Understanding HamVision requires familiarity with:
- **Hamiltonian Mechanics & Damped Oscillator** – The core is the damped harmonic oscillator in complex form, where q and p are canonical variables and H is energy. The damping ν and frequency ω control decay and oscillation. *Quick check*: If |z| is constant, what physical property does the system have? (Answer: Conservative, ν=0, energy conserved.)
- **Complex State‑Space Models & Diagonalization** – The efficient parallel implementation uses a complex diagonalized form; eigenvalues λ = -ν/2 ± iω_d determine stability and oscillation frequency. *Quick check*: What does the imaginary part of λ represent in the spatial domain? (Answer: The oscillation frequency ω_d, which sets the preferred spatial scale.)
- **Encoder‑Decoder & U‑Net Architecture** – HamSeg is a U‑Net variant; skip connections preserve high‑resolution details. *Quick check*: In a standard U‑Net, what is the primary purpose of skip connections? (Answer: To preserve high‑resolution spatial details from the encoder for the decoder.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Shared Encoder (ConvNeXt stages) → Hamiltonian Bottleneck (parallel ConvNeXt branch and Oscillator Bank with four‑directional scans, fusion gate, SE, energy map) → Task Heads (HamSeg: U‑Net decoder with energy‑gated skips, momentum injection, phase‑space attention; HamCls: global pooling of q, |p|, Hmap → MLP classifier).

**Critical path (segmentation)**: Input → Encoder → Bottleneck (outputs f, p, Hmap) → Decoder level 3 (with phase‑space attention) → Decoder level 2 (with gated skip e2 and mom p2) → Decoder level 1 (with gated skip e1 and mom p1) → Segmentation Head.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Parallel scan vs. spatial completeness: Four‑directional (row/column) scans are efficient but miss diagonal gradients; tradeoff between computational cost and isotropic boundary detection.
- Gate initialization: Bias +2.0 ensures early stability but requires the gate to drop (observed ~0.54) for oscillator contribution; risk of underutilization if gate remains near 1.
- Energy gate centering: Subtracting spatial mean (H - Ĥ) prevents saturation of σ; essential for spatial variation.

**Failure signatures**:
- Gate collapse: mean gate g ≈ 1.0 on validation → oscillator path ignored.
- Momentum incoherence: |p| maps appear noisy, not showing interior/boundary/exterior gradient.
- Energy saturation: Hmap values collapse to constant after SE.
- Overflow in scan: For high resolution, cumulative |L_t| too large → exp(L_t) overflows; current fix limits scan to bottleneck resolution (28×28).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Oscillator functionality check** – Train HamSeg on a small subset (e.g., 100 ISIC images). Extract q, p, H from bottleneck. Verify p shows higher magnitude near lesion boundaries vs. interior/exterior (region‑conditioned means). Verify Hmap after centering and scaling shows non‑saturated spatial variation.
2. **Gate behavior diagnostic** – After full training, compute mean and std of gate g spatially and across batches. Expected mean ~0.54, small std. If mean >0.9, oscillator largely unused.
3. **Head isolation test** – Train three models on one dataset (e.g., ACDC): (A) full HamSeg, (B) HamSeg without momentum injection, (C) HamSeg without energy‑gated skips. Compare Dice; expect significant drops for both removals, especially momentum (paper notes +5.59% impact on ACDC).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does applying the oscillator bottleneck at multiple encoder resolutions improve segmentation and classification performance?
2. Does the row/column scanning strategy fail to capture diagonal boundary information, and can incorporating diagonal scans improve performance?
3. Would a higher‑order discretization (e.g., exponential‑trapezoidal) of the oscillator dynamics improve expressiveness while preserving stability?
4. Does the Hamiltonian inductive bias generalize to natural image analysis tasks beyond medical imaging?

## Limitations
- Oscillator parameter initialization (ω, ν, Δ) is unspecified, potentially causing training instability.
- Classification head details (MLP architecture, dropout) and some training hyperparameters are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation limited to 10 datasets across 5 modalities; common modalities like X‑ray or CT are absent, weakening modality‑agnostic claim.
- No ablation data isolating contributions of momentum injection and energy‑gated skips; the claimed importance of these mechanisms remains assumed.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (distinct q, p, H representations): Medium (theoretical sound, visual evidence, but lacking quantitative disentanglement).
- Mechanism 2 (task‑specific head exploitation): Low (no ablation provided; gains could be due to other factors).
- Modality‑agnostic inductive bias: Medium (strong cross‑modality results but limited modality coverage).
- Overall performance claims (SOTA Dice/Accuracy): High (results clearly reported and competitive).

## Next Checks
1. Perform an ablation study on HamSeg (remove momentum injection, remove energy‑gated skips, both) to quantify each component’s contribution.
2. Compute representation disentanglement metrics: region‑wise mean |p| and H on multiple datasets; correlate H with ground‑truth saliency.
3. Test generalization to an unseen modality (e.g., chest X‑ray segmentation on JSRT or OCT classification) against standard baselines.
