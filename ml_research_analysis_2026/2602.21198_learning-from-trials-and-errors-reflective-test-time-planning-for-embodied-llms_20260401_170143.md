# Learning from Trials and Errors: Reflective Test-Time Planning for Embodied LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21198
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21198
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Reflective Test-Time Planning yields substantial gains on long-horizon embodied tasks, achieving 33.65% success on household chores and 60.2% fit rate on cupboard fitting, far surpassing baselines (10-12% and <10%).

## Executive Summary
Embodied large language models (LLMs) often fail to learn from mistakes during deployment, leading to repeated errors on complex, long-horizon tasks. This paper introduces Reflective Test-Time Planning, a framework that endows embodied agents with two complementary reflection modes: reflection-in-action, which internally scores multiple candidate actions before execution via test-time scaling, and reflection-on-action, which updates the agent's policy and internal reflection model after execution using external reflections and hindsight. A retrospective mechanism further re-evaluates past actions to address temporal credit assignment. Evaluated on two new benchmarks—Long-Horizon Household and MuJoCo Cupboard Fitting—the method achieves average success rates of 33.65% and 60.2% respectively, dramatically outperforming prior approaches (10-12% and <10%). Ablations confirm that both reflection modes and the retrospective update are essential for robust learning from experience.

## Method Summary
The approach initializes three separate LLM modules—an action policy πθ, an internal evaluator Vϕi, and an external evaluator Vϕe—from a single base model (LLaVA-3D-7B or Qwen2.5-VL-3B) via supervised fine-tuning on trajectories generated by GPT-5. These modules are trained to produce natural-language actions and reflections with calibrated numeric scores (0–100). During test-time, the agent first samples N diverse candidate actions from πθ using high temperature, scores each with Vϕi, and executes the highest-scoring one. After execution, Vϕe generates an external reflection and score; the tuple (observation, action, reflection, score) is stored in a working memory buffer W. When W reaches a predefined size K or a milestone (e.g., exiting a room) occurs, the agent performs retrospective reflection: Vϕe re-evaluates all recent actions with full hindsight, producing revised scores. These hindsight-corrected pairs, combined with regularization samples of unexplored actions, form a self-supervised dataset D_train. The agent then performs two concurrent test-time updates: (1) supervised learning on Vϕi to predict the revised reflections, and (2) policy gradient (REINFORCE) on πθ using the revised scores as rewards. Both updates use LoRA for efficiency and are run for a few epochs/steps before clearing W and continuing. This loop enables the agent to adapt its reasoning and behavior within a single deployment episode, learning from its own trials and errors.

## Key Results
- **Long-Horizon Household**: average task success rate 33.65% vs. 10-12% for baselines.
- **MuJoCo Cupboard Fitting**: fit rate 60.2% vs. <10% for baselines.
- **Ablation study**: removing either reflection-in-action or reflection-on-action, or disabling retrospective revision, leads to drastic performance drops, confirming all components are critical.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Reflection‑in‑action
**Claim:** Reflection-in-action uses test-time scaling to avoid committing to the first plausible action by internally simulating and scoring multiple candidates before execution.  
**Mechanism:** At each decision step, the agent samples N diverse candidate actions via high-temperature sampling from the action LLM πθ. Each candidate is evaluated by an internal reflection LLM Vϕi, which produces a reflective assessment and a numeric confidence score. The action with the highest internal score is executed. This “mentally trying out” phase filters out actions that are plausible in isolation but strategically poor (e.g., blocking future placements).  
**Core assumption:** The internal reflection model Vϕi can reliably differentiate between strategically sound and unsound actions based on the current observation and task context, even before execution. Its scoring must capture long‑horizon strategic value, not just immediate feasibility.  
**Evidence:** The abstract mentions “uses test-time scaling to generate and score multiple candidate actions using internal reflections before execution.” Section 3.1 details the algorithm and equations for candidate sampling, internal scoring, and best‑action selection. The corpus contains related ideas but does not specify the scoring-and-selection of multiple candidates as a distinct pre‑execution mechanism, highlighting novelty.  
**Break condition:** The mechanism fails if candidate diversity (N, temperature) is too low (greedy‑like behavior) or if Vϕi is misaligned and consistently scores poor strategies high. Persistent execution of actions later identified as blocking or suboptimal would indicate failure.

### Mechanism 2: Reflection‑on‑action
**Claim:** Reflection-on-action enables learning from execution outcomes by using external reflections to generate self‑supervised training signals that update both the action policy (πθ) and the internal reflection model (Vϕi) during deployment.  
**Mechanism:** After executing the selected action, an external reflection LLM Vϕe assesses the outcome, generating an external reflection and score. At predefined milestones (e.g., K steps, room exit), these external reflections, combined with retrospective reflections that re‑evaluate past actions with hindsight, form a dataset D_train. This dataset is used for two test‑time updates: (1) supervised learning for Vϕi to predict hindsight‑corrected reflections, and (2) policy gradient (REINFORCE) for πθ using retrospective scores as rewards. These updates modify the underlying model parameters, not just a text buffer.  
**Core assumption:** Hindsight reflection provides a corrected and more accurate assessment of an action’s long‑term strategic value than the immediate external score. This hindsight signal serves as a valid supervisory target for both the action model (to increase probability of good actions) and the internal model (to learn to anticipate long‑horizon consequences).  
**Evidence:** The abstract states “test‑time training to update both its internal reflection model and its action policy based on external reflections after execution.” Sections 3.2.2 and 3.2.3 define D_train construction and the two loss functions. Related work (e.g., Reflexion) stores reflections as contextual text without parameter updates; this paper’s mechanism is distinct in converting reflections into gradient‑based updates.  
**Break condition:** The mechanism fails if hindsight re‑evaluation is inaccurate (e.g., Vϕe itself is flawed) or if test‑time training hyperparameters cause catastrophic forgetting or overfitting on the small, deployment‑specific D_train. Evidence: performance degradation after update cycles or loss of previously successful strategies.

### Mechanism 3: Retrospective reflection
**Claim:** Retrospective reflection mitigates the temporal credit assignment problem in long‑horizon tasks by re‑evaluating historical decisions with the benefit of later outcomes, enabling the agent to correct non‑local errors (e.g., an early placement that blocks later success).  
**Mechanism:** When a milestone is reached (e.g., |W|=K, exiting a room), the external reflection LLM Vϕe iterates over recent actions in the working memory buffer W. For each historical action a_j, it constructs a retrospective prompt x_j^retro that includes the entire working memory context and the current state, then generates a revised reflection f_j^r and score s_j^r. This revised assessment overwrites the previous reflection for a_j in a retro‑buffer D_retro. These hindsight‑corrected scores are then used in the test‑time training losses.  
**Core assumption:** The external reflection model Vϕe can perform counterfactual reasoning—given the full subsequent trajectory (W), it can accurately infer the true long‑term value of a past action, separating immediate success from strategic failure. The model must understand causal chains across multiple steps.  
**Evidence:** The abstract mentions “retrospective reflection, allowing the agent to re‑evaluate earlier decisions and perform model updates with hindsight for proper long‑horizon credit assignment.” Section 3.2.1 details the retro prompt construction and revision process. Ablation results (sections 4.3 & 5) show removing retrospective reflection causes significant performance drops, especially on tasks with hidden dependencies (Fitting, Preparation).  
**Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the working memory window K is too small to capture the consequences of early actions, or if Vϕe cannot perform the necessary counterfactual reasoning. Evidence: persistent credit assignment errors where early blocking actions are not downgraded in retrospect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Test‑Time Adaptation (TTA)**  
  Why needed: The entire framework is a form of TTA/Test‑Time Training (TTT). The agent modifies its parameters (θ, ϕi) during deployment using self‑supervised signals from its own execution, without external labels or prior data. Understanding TTA’s distinction from standard offline training or online RL (e.g., no environment reward signal, only self‑generated verbal reflections) is fundamental.

- **Embodied LLM Architectures**  
  Why needed: The base agents are multimodal LLMs that process 3D point clouds and generate natural language actions/reflections. Understanding how these models fuse visual‑spatial inputs with language reasoning is prerequisite to grasping why reflection is needed (their initial competence is static).  
  Quick check: Why might a pre‑trained embodied LLM (e.g., fine‑tuned on demonstration data) still fail during deployment in novel scenes, even if it understands the task?

- **Verbal Reflection as a Supervisory Signal**  
  Why needed: The core innovation is converting natural language reflections into numeric scores and using them as regression (for value) and RL reward (for policy) targets. This bridges symbolic reasoning with gradient‑based learning. One must understand the semantic calibration of scores (0‑100) and the assumption that language critiques can be distilled into scalar values for optimization.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
The system comprises: (1) an action policy πθ that generates candidate actions given task, observation, and history; (2) an internal reflection model Vϕi that scores each candidate before execution; (3) an external reflection model Vϕe that assesses outcomes after execution and performs hindsight re‑evaluation; (4) a working memory buffer W that stores recent (observation, action, external reflection, score) tuples; (5) a test‑time training loop that constructs D_train from retrospective and regularization samples and performs supervised and RL updates on Vϕi and πθ respectively, using LoRA for efficiency.

### Critical path
1. **Step‑level loop**: At timestep t, construct prompt x_action for πθ and sample N candidate actions via high‑temperature sampling.  
2. **Internal scoring**: For each candidate, construct prompt x_internal for Vϕi to obtain reflective assessment f_i and confidence score s_i.  
3. **Selection and execution**: Choose action a*_t with highest s_i and execute it, receiving next observation o_{t+1}.  
4. **External reflection**: Prompt Vϕe with x_external to get f_e and s_e; store (o_t, a*_t, f_e, s_e) in W.  
5. **Trigger**: If |W| reaches K or a milestone (e.g., room exit) is reached, run consolidation.  
6. **Consolidation**: For each entry in W (and D_retro), construct retrospective prompt x_retro containing full working memory context; query Vϕe to obtain revised reflection f_j^r and score s_j^r. Update D_retro. Form D_train = D_retro ∪ D_reg (where D_reg contains unexplored actions scored by current Vϕi).  
7. **Updates**: Perform E epochs of supervised training to update ϕ_i by maximizing log‑likelihood of f_r. Perform RL_steps of REINFORCE to update θ, using reward = 2*s_r/100 − 1 derived from retrospective scores.  
8. **Clear memory**: Reset W and continue with next timestep.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Number of candidates (N)** vs. compute: More candidates improve selection quality but linearly increase Vϕi calls; paper found N≈6 optimal for household tasks.  
- **Full‑weight updates vs. LoRA**: Full updates have higher capacity but risk catastrophic forgetting; LoRA is efficient and stable but may limit expressiveness. Paper uses LoRA (rank 8, alpha 16) for safety.  
- **Single‑step vs. receding horizon**: The method prefers single‑step decisions aligned with learning from real outcomes; receding horizon imaginative planning wastes compute and can conflict with reality‑grounded updates.  
- **Working memory size K**: Larger K provides richer hindsight context but increases prompt length and retrospection cost; paper uses K=5 for cupboard but does not specify for household.

### Failure signatures
- **No learning / repeated errors**: Internal scores do not correlate with actual progress; likely Vϕi misaligned or insufficient training updates. Monitor correlation between internal and external scores.  
- **Performance drop after update**: Catastrophic forgetting or overfitting on small D_train; check loss curves, gradient norms, and consider reducing learning rates or LoRA rank.  
- **Mode collapse in LoRA**: Vϕi outputs nearly identical scores for all candidates, often with high‑rank LoRA; reduce rank or learning rate.  
- **Persistent early credit assignment errors**: Retrospective revision fails to downgrade early blocking actions; may indicate Vϕe cannot perform counterfactual reasoning or K is too small.

### First 3 experiments
1. **RIA‑only**: Enable multi‑candidate scoring (reflection‑in‑action) but disable all test‑time training and external reflection beyond immediate prompts. Hypothesis: initial improvement over greedy but no long‑term learning; similar failures recur across episodes.  
2. **ROA‑only**: Fix action generation to greedy (N=1) while enabling reflection‑on‑action with test‑time updates. Hypothesis: updates occur but low‑quality D_train from poor initial actions leads to plateaued or degraded performance.  
3. **Retrospective ablation**: Enable reflection‑in‑action and immediate external reflection but disable the retrospective revision step (use only immediate external scores for D_train). Hypothesis: significant performance drop on tasks with non‑local dependencies (e.g., cupboard fitting), demonstrating the need for hindsight‑corrected scores in credit assignment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can reflective test‑time planning be extended to incorporate additional sensory modalities beyond vision, such as tactile or auditory feedback?**  
   Basis: Explicitly stated in the conclusion. Why unresolved: The current framework focuses on vision‑language; adding other modalities would require new architectures and reflection mechanisms to handle multimodal streams and their physical implications. Evidence: Build a version that processes tactile or auditory inputs, generates corresponding reflections, and demonstrates improved success on tasks where force or sound cues are essential (e.g., assessing grasp stability).

2. **How can the stability of test‑time training be improved to prevent catastrophic forgetting and mode collapse, especially under aggressive adaptation hyperparameters?**  
   Basis: Inferred from hyperparameter analysis showing collapse with larger LoRA configurations. Why unresolved: The method is sensitive to LoRA size and learning rates; understanding the failure modes and designing robust training schedules or regularization would make the approach more reliable. Evidence: Identify causes (e.g., gradient variance, small dataset) and propose modifications (e.g., adaptive learning rates, conservative policy updates) that expand the safe hyperparameter range without performance degradation.

3. **Can a single unified reflection model perform both reflection‑in‑action (pre‑evaluation) and reflection‑on‑action (post‑evaluation) without sacrificing performance, thereby reducing computational overhead?**  
   Basis: Inferred from the multi‑model setup (three separate LLMs). Why unresolved: A unified model might suffer from interference between pre‑action and post‑action tasks, and it is unclear whether a single model can maintain the complementary benefits of the two reflection modes. Evidence: Train one versatile LLM to produce both internal and external reflections depending on prompt conditioning, compare its success rate and efficiency on the benchmarks against the multi‑model setup, and analyze any degradation.

4. **Does the combination of reflection‑in‑action and reflection‑on‑action provide formal guarantees on sample efficiency or convergence for long‑horizon credit assignment?**  
   Basis: Inferred; only empirical evidence is presented. Why unresolved: A theoretical foundation could elucidate why the combination works, guide choices like working memory size or retrospection frequency, and increase confidence in applying the method to new domains. Evidence: Formalize the process (e.g., as an MDP with verbal reflection states) and prove that retrospective updates reduce credit assignment error or accelerate convergence compared to non‑reflective baselines, possibly with bounds on the improvement.

## Limitations
- **SFT data generation details missing**: The exact GPT‑5 prompting templates used to create the training trajectories are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction of the supervised fine‑tuning phase.
- **Underspecified components**: The strategy for sampling “unexplored actions” for D_reg regularization is vague, and the working memory size K for the household benchmark is not given; these ambiguities may affect test‑time stability and final performance.
- **External reflector calibration**: The external reflection model Vϕe is frozen and its scoring calibration or hindsight accuracy is not validated; if its assessments are misaligned, the self‑supervised updates may be corrupted.
