# Repetition Without Exclusivity: Scale Sensitivity of Referential Mechanisms in Child-Scale Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.13696
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13696
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** All child-scale autoregressive models exhibit robust anti-mutual exclusivity repetition priming that never crosses zero; its magnitude inversely correlates with perplexity, and apparent ME with nonce tokens is fully explained by embedding similarity.

## Executive Summary
We investigate whether text-only language models trained on child-directed speech develop mutual exclusivity (ME), the bias to map novel words to novel referents. Using autoregressive GPT-2 models of three sizes (2.9M–33.5M parameters) and five random seeds each (45 models total), we evaluate ME via referential suppression—decreased probability for a labelled familiar noun in a two-referent context. Every model shows strong anti-ME repetition priming (85–100% of items, p < 2.4e-13). The priming magnitude weakens as language modeling improves (Spearman ρ = -0.533 with perplexity) but never approaches zero. A context-dependence diagnostic demonstrates that ME-like effects with nonce tokens disappear once embedding similarity is controlled, indicating that distributional learning on child-directed speech yields repetition-based reference tracking rather than lexical exclusivity. These results suggest that referential grounding may be necessary for ME to emerge.

## Method Summary
**Training regime:** Autoregressive GPT-2 models were trained from scratch on the American Oxford-CHILDES (AO-CHILDES) corpus, a 10-million-token collection of child-directed English speech. Three model scales—Small (2.9M), Medium (8.5M), and Large (33.5M) parameters—were each trained for 5, 10, and 20 epochs with five random seeds, totaling 45 models. Training followed standard next‑token prediction with Adam optimization and a transformer decoder architecture.

**Evaluation:** The primary test battery measured *referential suppression*, operationalized as the change in probability assigned to a familiar noun label when its referent is presented alongside a competing familiar object. Suppression below zero indicates anti-ME (repetition priming). Additional tests probed context sensitivity and introduced nonce (nonsense) word tokens to disentangle embedding‑similarity confounds from genuine referential disambiguation. Results were aggregated across seeds and scales, and correlations with perplexity were computed.

## Key Results
- All 45 models exhibited robust anti‑ME repetition priming (85–100% of items, p < 2.4e-13).
- Priming strength decreased with better language modeling performance (Spearman ρ = -0.533), yet never crossed zero.
- Apparent ME‑like effects with nonce tokens were fully accounted for by embedding similarity; orthogonalizing nonce embeddings eliminated the effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The models learn surface co‑occurrence patterns: when a noun label repeatedly appears in contexts containing its referent, the label’s embedding becomes associated with the referent’s representation. In a two‑referent setting, the presence of the competing referent re‑activates the label’s embedding, leading to competition and thus a *decrease* in probability for the labelled referent—a phenomenon known as repetition priming. As the model’s general language modeling improves (lower perplexity), it integrates broader contextual information, which weakens reliance on simple repetition but does not give rise to genuine mutual exclusivity. The fact that nonce‑token ME‑like effects vanish when embedding similarity is removed shows that these models lack a mechanism for abstract referent differentiation based on discourse novelty; instead, they are driven by distributional overlap in the token embedding space.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mutual Exclusivity**: Why needed: Central hypothesis; tests whether models acquire a word‑learning bias analogous to children. Quick check: Does the model’s probability for a familiar noun decrease when a novel label is paired with it? (The paper finds the opposite.)
- **Referential Suppression**: Why needed: Operational definition of ME; a decrease in probability signals the model’s assumption of exclusivity. Quick check: Compare model predictions for the target noun with vs. without a competitor in the context.
- **Autoregressive Training**: Why needed: The training objective of GPT‑2 that shapes context representations. Quick check: Monitor validation perplexity; lower values indicate better next‑token prediction.
- **Child‑Directed Speech**: Why needed: Simulates the linguistic input children receive; determines what distributional cues are available. Quick check: Analyze corpus statistics (e.g., noun repetition rates, contextual diversity).
- **Embedding Similarity**: Why needed: Confound that can produce spurious ME‑like effects with nonce tokens. Quick check: Replace nonce tokens with orthogonal embeddings; ME‑like effects should disappear if driven solely by similarity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Token ID → Embedding Lookup → Positional Encoding → [Transformer Block] × N → Final LayerNorm → Linear (LM Head) → Logits

**Critical path**  
During training, an input sequence is tokenized, embedded, and summed with positional encodings. The resulting tensor passes through N decoder layers, each applying masked self‑attention and a position‑wise feed‑forward network, with residual connections and layer normalization. The final hidden state is projected by the LM head to vocabulary logits. Cross‑entropy loss between predicted and true next tokens drives backpropagation. Inference follows the same forward pass to obtain the next‑token distribution.

**Design tradeoffs**  
Autoregressive GPT‑2 provides efficient next‑token prediction but may over‑fit surface patterns, especially on relatively small child‑directed corpora. Increasing model capacity (parameters, depth) can lower perplexity but risks diminishing returns or memorization. The trade‑off between training data volume (10 M tokens) and model size was explored by scaling to three parameter budgets and by varying training duration (5–20 epochs).

**Failure signatures**  
- Persistently high anti‑ME repetition priming across all scales, never converging toward ME.  
- Nonce‑token ME‑like effects that vanish when embedding similarity is neutralized (orthogonal tokens).  
- Lack of sensitivity to multi‑sentence referential context in models trained on isolated sentences (e.g., BabyBERTa), indicating an inability to track discourse entities.

**First experiments**  
1. Compute validation perplexity for each model scale and training epoch to quantify language modeling performance.  
2. Run the referential suppression task with real common nouns to measure anti‑ME repetition priming.  
3. Introduce nonce tokens with random or orthogonal embeddings and repeat the suppression task to test the embedding‑similarity account.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can mutual exclusivity emerge in text‑only models at scales, architectures, or with training objectives beyond those tested?**  
   *Basis*: The authors limited their study to GPT‑2 models up to 33.5 M parameters and an autoregressive objective; they did not explore vastly larger scales, alternative architectures (e.g., state‑space models), or contrastive/denoising objectives.  
   *Why unresolved*: Their results show no crossing of zero in anti‑ME, but the parameter range may be insufficient to observe a phase transition; trajectories show no sign of approaching reversal, yet orders of magnitude remain untested.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Train and evaluate models with hundreds of millions to billions of parameters, or with different architectures/objectives, on the same child‑scale corpus using the identical ME battery.

2. **Does the distributional structure of other child‑directed or child‑accessible corpora alter the balance between repetition priming and potential exclusivity signals?**  
   *Basis*: Experiments used only AO‑CHILDES; the full BabyLM 10 M corpus includes books, subtitles, and other genres that may contain more contrastive reference (“the X or the Y”) and different discourse structures.  
   *Why unresolved*: Such variation in distributional statistics could change the relative strength of repetition versus exclusivity cues, potentially favoring ME emergence.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Train identical model architectures on the full BabyLM corpus (or other diverse child‑directed collections) and evaluate anti‑ME rates and priming magnitudes compared to AO‑CHILDES‑trained models.

3. **Is referential grounding a causal requirement for ME, or merely correlated?**  
   *Basis*: The authors connect their text‑only findings to previously published visually grounded models that show ME, but this comparison is correlational and confounds modality (text vs. vision) with the presence of a discrete referent space.  
   *Why unresolved*: It is unknown whether providing an explicit, symbolic referent set (e.g., a differentiable memory of discourse entities) alongside text—without visual input—could induce ME.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Train a text‑only model on AO‑CHILDES augmented with an explicit referent‑tracking module and compare its ME behaviour to a standard text‑only control and to a visually grounded model.

4. **Can masked language models, when trained on multi‑sentence contexts, develop sensitivity to multi‑sentence referential context sufficient to exhibit ME?**  
   *Basis*: BabyBERTa, a masked LM trained on individual sentences, was completely insensitive to multi‑sentence referential context. The authors attribute this to its sentence‑level training regime, not an inherent limitation of masked LMs.  
   *Why unresolved*: It remains unknown whether a masked LM trained on concatenated multi‑sentence inputs (like the autoregressive models) would develop different referential mechanisms.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Train a RoBERTa‑style masked LM from scratch on the same 128‑token AO‑CHILDES streams used for the GPT‑2 models and evaluate it with the identical ME battery.

## Limitations
- Models are limited to ≤33.5 M parameters and text‑only input, lacking the multimodal grounding available to children.
- The study uses only English AO‑CHILDES; findings may not generalize to other languages or corpora. Referential suppression is an indirect ME measure and may miss subtleties.
- Nonce‑token results are potentially confounded by embedding similarity, though the authors control for it in diagnostics.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: All models show robust anti‑ME repetition priming (85–100% of items, p < 2.4e-13), with magnitude inversely correlated with perplexity (ρ = -0.533) and never crossing zero.
- **Medium confidence**: Apparent ME‑like effects with nonce tokens are fully explained by embedding similarity once properly controlled.
- **Low confidence**: Referential grounding is a necessary condition for ME to emerge; this remains a correlational inference across studies.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale extension**: Train GPT‑2 (or similar) models up to 1 B parameters on AO‑CHILDES and assess whether anti‑ME priming weakens, disappears, or reverses.
2. **Orthogonalized nonce tokens**: Replace nonce word embeddings with random, orthogonal vectors; if ME‑like effects vanish, this confirms the embedding‑similarity confound.
3. **Multimodal grounding comparison**: Train a visually grounded model (e.g., a transformer with image input) and a text‑only control on identical language data; directly test whether grounding induces ME.
