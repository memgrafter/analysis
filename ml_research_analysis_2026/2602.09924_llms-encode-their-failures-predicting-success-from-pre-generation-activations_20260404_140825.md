# LLMs Encode Their Failures: Predicting Success from Pre-Generation Activations
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09924
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09924
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** Pre-generation activations encode linearly decodable success signals (AUROC >0.7), enabling probe-guided routing that matches the best single-model accuracy while reducing inference cost by up to 70% on MATH, and revealing a model-specific difficulty that diverges from human IRT as reasoning scales.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) encode information about their likely success on a given task before generating any output. The authors train linear probes on residual stream activations extracted prior to generation to predict binary success outcomes under decoding policies such as majority voting. Across multiple math and coding benchmarks, these probes achieve AUROC scores greater than 0.7, substantially outperforming text-based baselines like TF-IDF or question length. Building on this signal, the study demonstrates probe-guided routing policies that dynamically select among a pool of models based on predicted success, achieving accuracy comparable to the best single model while reducing inference cost by up to 70% on the MATH dataset. Furthermore, analysis using the E2H-AMC dataset reveals that models develop a notion of difficulty distinct from human psychometric estimates (IRT), and this divergence grows as models allocate more test-time compute via extended reasoning or larger voting ensembles.

## Method Summary
The core method involves extracting fixed-layer, fixed-position activation vectors from a frozen LLM’s residual stream immediately after the instruction template but before token generation. These activations serve as features for linear probes—logistic regression for binary success classification (e.g., Maj@5 pass/fail) or ridge regression for continuous success rate. Probe training uses rollout data: for each query, multiple stochastic generations are sampled, answers are parsed, and success labels are assigned. The trained probes predict the probability of success for new queries without any generation. To optimize inference cost, a router estimates each candidate model’s success probability via its probe and applies a simple policy (e.g., threshold or utility maximization) to select a model whose full generation (with a fixed decoding policy) is then executed. The pipeline relies on frozen models and linear probes, keeping overhead minimal.

## Key Results
- Linear probes on pre-generation activations predict binary success (Maj@5) with AUROC > 0.7 across math and coding tasks, beating TF-IDF and length baselines by large margins.
- Probe-guided routing (e.g., cascade or utility-based) matches the accuracy of the strongest single model while reducing inference cost by up to 70% on MATH and by 37% on AIME.
- Models encode a model-specific difficulty signal that diverges from human IRT difficulty; this divergence increases with extended reasoning (longer chain-of-thought or larger majority voting), even as overall task accuracy improves.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Pre-generation residual stream activations contain linearly decodable information about a model's likelihood of success on a specific query under a given decoding policy.
- **Mechanism:** A linear probe (a trained linear classifier/regressor) is applied to a fixed-layer, fixed-position activation vector from the model's residual stream before any output tokens are generated. This probe is trained on labeled examples (pass/fail outcomes from multiple rollouts) to map the activation pattern directly to a success probability.
- **Core assumption:** The relevant signal for predicting a stochastic decoding outcome (e.g., Maj@5) is present and linearly separable in the aggregated pre-generation representation of the input prompt.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We train linear probes on pre-generation residual stream activations to predict task-specific success... substantially outperforming surface features."
  - [section 3.2] Describes extracting activations at "post-instruction template positions" and training linear probes for binary success classification (AUROC > 0.7) and success rate regression.
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus support; related work (Cencerrado et al., 2025) finds weaker results on math, while this paper's supervised approach achieves stronger discrimination.
- **Break condition:** Probe performance (AUROC) degrades monotonically as the model's internal reasoning budget (e.g., chain-of-thought length) increases, even as task accuracy improves.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The notion of "difficulty" encoded in pre-generation activations is model-specific and diverges systematically from human psychometric difficulty (IRT), with the gap widening as the model allocates more test-time compute via extended reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** Using the E2H-AMC dataset (same questions, human IRT labels, model rollout results), separate linear probes are trained from identical activations to predict (a) human IRT difficulty and (b) model success rate. The correlation between the two probe outputs (or their alignment with the respective ground truths) is measured across different model reasoning modes. The divergence is quantified by the drop in Spearman correlation between model-derived difficulty and human IRT as reasoning increases.
- **Core assumption:** Human IRT scores provide a stable, model-agnostic ground truth for "difficulty," against which model-internal signals can be contrasted.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "models encode a model-specific notion of difficulty that is distinct from human difficulty, and this divergence increases with extended reasoning."
  - [section 3.3] "Human IRT difficulty is consistently more linearly decodable (ρ=0.83–0.87) than model success rate (ρ=0.40–0.64)... model success rate becomes less linearly accessible as reasoning capability increases." Figure 1 shows alignment (ρ) between probe-predicted model difficulty and human IRT drops from ~0.65 to ~0.45 as reasoning increases.
  - [corpus] Strong anchor: This paper's core comparative analysis on E2H-AMC is novel; Lugoloobi & Russell (2025) correlated probes with IRT but did not systematically contrast it with model-specific difficulty under varied reasoning.
- **Break condition:** The divergence saturates or reverses; i.e., model difficulty becomes more aligned with human IRT at very high reasoning budgets, which is not observed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Probe-derived success estimates can drive simple routing policies (cascade, utility-based) that match or exceed the accuracy of the strongest single model while significantly reducing inference cost.
- **Mechanism:** For a pool of candidate models {M_i} with known cost-per-token profiles, a probe (trained per model to predict its Maj@5 success) estimates `p_i(x)` for a new query `x`. A routing policy selects model `M_i` based on `p_i(x)` and `cost_i`. Cascade routing uses a threshold on a single cheaper model's `p` to escalate to a stronger model. Utility-based routing selects `argmax_i (p_i(x) - λ * norm(cost_i))`.
- **Core assumption:** The probe's estimated success probability is a sufficiently reliable and calibrated predictor of the model's actual deterministic outcome (e.g., Maj@5) to make cost-aware allocation decisions that improve over random or uniform allocation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "probe-guided routing can match the best single-model accuracy while reducing inference cost by up to 70% on MATH."
  - [section 4.2] Figures 3 & 4 and Tables 8-10 show Pareto frontiers where probe routers dominate random routing and approach oracle performance. Cost reduction is demonstrated on MATH (70%), AIME (37%), and cost-aware selection on GSM8K.
  - [corpus] Related: Chen et al. (2024) "FrugalGPT" uses LLM API calls for confidence; Ding et al. (2023) uses input heuristics. This paper's key differentiator is using *internal activations* without additional generation.
- **Break condition:** Probe AUROC drops below a practical threshold (e.g., ~0.6), causing the router's accuracy-cost tradeoff to collapse toward random or single-model baselines, as hinted in the discussion for GPT-OSS-20B high-reasoning mode.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Linear Probing of Neural Representations**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire method hinges on extracting a linear decision boundary from frozen model activations. Understanding that a linear map can decode a complex task outcome (success/failure) from a high-dimensional vector is foundational.
  - **Quick check question:** If a probe achieves AUROC > 0.8 on a test set, what does that imply about the separability of success/failure activations in the chosen layer and position?
- **Concept: Item Response Theory (IRT) as a Human Difficulty Proxy**
  - **Why needed here:** To establish the key divergence claim, one must understand that IRT scores (e.g., `b(q)`) are psychometric estimates of item difficulty derived from large-scale human performance, intended to be model-agnostic.
  - **Quick check question:** If a model's internal difficulty probe has a Spearman ρ=0.6 with IRT but ρ=0.9 with its own empirical success rate, what does that suggest about the relationship between human and model difficulty?
- **Concept: Test-Time Compute Scaling (Chain-of-Thought, Majority Voting)**
  - **Why needed here:** The central finding is that increasing reasoning budget (longer CoT, larger `k` for Maj@k) improves accuracy but degrades the linear accessibility of pre-generation difficulty signals. This links a deployment knob (compute) to probe reliability.
  - **Quick check question:** For a fixed model, what would you expect to happen to (a) task accuracy and (b) pre-generation probe AUROC if you increase the maximum CoT length allowed during rollout?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1. Frozen LLM – extracts pre-generation activations for any prompt.  
2. Probe Trainer – fits linear probes (logistic/ridge) to (activation, success label) pairs.  
3. Rollout System (offline) – samples multiple stochastic generations per query to compute empirical success labels.  
4. Router Policy – applies trained probes to new activations and selects a model via threshold or utility rules.  
5. Inference Engine (online) – runs the chosen model with its full decoding policy (e.g., Maj@5) to produce the final answer.

**Critical path:**  
Input prompt → LLM.forward() to EOI position → Extracted activation vector h → Trained probe w → Predicted success σ(w·h) → Router policy → Selected model → Full generation & answer.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Probe Position:* Post-instruction vs. final token vs. pooled. Paper uses a fixed template-based EOI position. Deeper layers might capture more abstract representations but could be noisier for linear separation.
- *Linear vs. Non-linear:* Linear probes are cheap, interpretable, and trainable on limited data. Non-linear probes (e.g., MLP) might recover more signal, especially under extended reasoning, but risk overfitting and lose simplicity.
- *Single vs. Multi-position:* Probing a single position is simple but discards information. Averaging or concatenating multiple positions could help but increases dimensionality and may require more training data.
- *Routing Policy Simplicity:* Threshold/utility rules are transparent and require no online learning. More complex learned policies (e.g., contextual bandits) could adapt but add overhead and complexity.

**Failure signatures:**
- Probe AUROC on validation set is low (<0.65) or degrades sharply when moving from greedy to Maj@5 or low-to-high reasoning.
- Aligning probe predictions with empirical success (via a held-out rollout set) shows poor calibration (high ECE) even if AUROC is moderate.
- Router's accuracy on a held-out test set is close to the worst model in the pool or to random routing.
- Cost savings are negligible or negative because probes consistently escalate to the most expensive model.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate Table 2 (Binary Success AUROC):** For a given model (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B), train probes to predict Maj@5 success on MATH. Report AUROC per layer/position, compare to TF-IDF and length baselines. Confirm AUROC > 0.7.
2. **Reasoning Budget Impact:** For a model with controllable reasoning (GPT-OSS-20B), fix the probe training data (e.g., all from 'low' reasoning mode). Test the probe's AUROC on held-out data from 'low', 'medium', and 'high' reasoning modes. Expect a monotonic drop in AUROC as reasoning increases, even while task accuracy rises.
3. **Simple Cascade Routing Proof-of-Concept:** Using probes trained on two models (Cheap M_s, Expensive M_l), implement threshold routing: M_l if p_s(x) < τ else M_s. Sweep τ on a validation set. Plot accuracy vs. cost (using output token estimates) against the two single-model baselines. Expect a Pareto frontier where at some τ, you match M_l's accuracy at lower cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do non-linear probes recover success prediction signals that degrade with extended reasoning, indicating that difficulty information is encoded non-linearly rather than being lost?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations state: "exploring non-linear probes may reveal whether difficulty information is lost under extended reasoning or merely becomes less linearly accessible."
- **Why unresolved:** The study only uses linear probes; probe performance degrades with reasoning but it’s unclear if this is due to linear limitations or genuine signal loss.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train neural network probes (e.g., MLPs) on activations from high-reasoning models and compare AUROC to linear probes; if non-linear probes restore performance, it suggests non-linear encoding.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does probing at intermediate generation positions (e.g., after initial reasoning steps) provide more reliable difficulty estimates than pre-generation activations for models using extended chain-of-thought?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work mentions: "probing at multiple or intermediate generation positions could recover signals that are absent pre-generation."
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis probes only pre-generation activations; extended reasoning may make difficulty signals more accessible during generation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extract activations at various token positions during generation for the same inputs, train probes, and evaluate routing performance using these intermediate probes versus pre-generation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How transferable are pre-generation difficulty probes across domains (e.g., from mathematics to coding) and between different model families?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work: "understanding cross-domain and cross-dataset probe transfer would enable more practical, reusable probes."
- **Why unresolved:** Probes are trained per model and domain; it’s unknown if a probe trained on math tasks generalizes to coding or across model architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train probes on one domain (e.g., MATH) and test on another (e.g., LiveCodeBench) for same/different models; measure drop in AUROC and routing effectiveness.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can adaptive routing strategies that dynamically adjust the majority voting size k per query based on difficulty estimates further improve the cost-accuracy tradeoff compared to fixed-k utility routing?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work: "adaptive routing strategies that dynamically adjust k based on estimated difficulty or combine probe signals with input features may further narrow the gap to oracle performance."
- **Why unresolved:** The routing experiments use fixed maj@5; adaptive k could optimize compute allocation more granularly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a router that selects k (e.g., 1,3,5,7) per query using probe uncertainty estimates; compare Pareto frontier to fixed-k routing on benchmarks like MATH.

## Limitations
- **Generalization:** Probes are trained and evaluated on the same model architecture and task distribution; transferability to unseen model families or out-of-domain prompts is uncertain.
- **Mechanism Specificity:** The analysis assumes a fixed "post-instruction" activation position; the optimal layer/position for linear decodability and whether non-linear probes would recover more signal under high-reasoning modes remains unexplored.
- **Practical Routing Overheads:** Cost reduction calculations use token-count estimates; real-world latency, memory bandwidth, and probe inference overhead (especially for large models) are not accounted for, which could erode reported savings.

## Confidence
- **Pre-generation activations encode linearly decodable success signals:** High
- **Model-specific difficulty diverges from human IRT, widening with reasoning:** Medium
- **Probe-guided routing matches best-model accuracy at lower cost:** Medium
