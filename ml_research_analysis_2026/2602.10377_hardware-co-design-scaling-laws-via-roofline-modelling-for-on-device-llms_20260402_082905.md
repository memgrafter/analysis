# Hardware Co-Design Scaling Laws via Roofline Modelling for On-Device LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10377
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10377
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A co-designed architecture achieves 19.42% lower perplexity on WikiText-2 than Qwen2.5-0.5B at equivalent measured latency on NVIDIA Jetson Orin.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a hardware co-design scaling law that jointly models training loss as a function of architectural hyperparameters and inference latency via roofline modeling. This framework enables direct accuracy-latency correspondence and Pareto frontier identification for on-device LLM deployment under strict hardware constraints. Empirical evaluation on NVIDIA Jetson Orin with 1,942 candidate architectures (170 trained for 10B tokens each) demonstrates a 19.42% perplexity reduction over a strong baseline at equal latency. The approach reduces architecture search from months to days and provides the first operational co-design scaling law for on-device LLMs.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three core models: (1) a polynomial scaling law that predicts training loss from architectural hyperparameters (depth, width, MoE activation rate, FFN ratio, GQA) trained on a fixed token budget; (2) a roofline-based latency estimator that computes per-layer compute and memory requirements to predict prefill and decode latency on a target hardware platform; (3) a Pareto frontier constructor that evaluates thousands of candidate architectures using the two predictive models to identify non-dominated trade-offs. A theoretical optimizer derives closed-form solutions for optimal parameters under different constraint regimes. The framework is validated by training and measuring selected architectures on real hardware.

## Key Results
- Co-designed architecture reduces WikiText-2 perplexity by 19.42% compared to Qwen2.5-0.5B at equivalent measured latency on Jetson Orin.
- The scaling law achieves R² = 0.975 on training and 0.952 on validation configurations.
- Latency model predictions correlate strongly with measured vLLM latencies, with typical errors of 10–20%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Training loss for transformer-based LLMs can be modeled as an explicit, separable polynomial function of architectural hyperparameters (depth, width, MoE activation rate, FFN ratio, GQA) when training compute and data budgets are fixed.
- **Mechanism**: Train 170 diverse architectures (spanning depth 4–32, width 768–3072, MoE configurations, etc.) for 10B tokens each. Fit a nonlinear least-squares model of the form in Equation 2, which includes distinct terms for depth, sparsity-width coupling, base capacity, FFN expansion, and KV-cache dimension. The model achieves R² = 0.975 on training and 0.952 on held-out validation configurations.
- **Core assumption**: The loss landscape is smooth and additive across architectural dimensions, and the polynomial form captures dominant scaling trends within the explored space. Training data, optimizer, and token budget are held constant.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "We model training loss as an explicit function of architectural hyperparameters."
  - [section 4.1] Details the scaling law fitting procedure, search space, and validation R².
  - [corpus] RooflineBench and Scaling Inference-Efficient Language Models also use scaling laws for efficiency; this paper extends to joint accuracy-latency.
- **Break condition**: The fitted law may not generalize to architectures far outside the training distribution (e.g., extreme depth/width ratios, novel attention mechanisms) or to significantly different training budgets (e.g., 100B tokens) without refitting.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Inference latency on a fixed hardware platform can be accurately approximated via roofline analysis, which classifies each operator as compute-bound or memory-bound and estimates latency as `max(FLOPs/peak_compute, memory_bytes/bandwidth)`.
- **Mechanism**: For each operator (matrix multiply, attention, etc.), compute FLOPs and memory traffic (weights, activations, KV-cache). Compare arithmetic intensity (FLOPs/byte) to the hardware's peak compute-to-bandwidth ratio. Sum per-layer costs over prefill (parallel input processing) and decode (autoregressive generation) phases using derived coefficients (e.g., ξ_F for FLOPs, ξ_dec_W for decode weight traffic). The model takes hardware specs (peak FLOPS π_H, bandwidth β_H) and workload config (batch size, sequence lengths).
- **Core assumption**: Hardware operates near ideal peak values; roofline model correctly identifies the bottleneck for each operator; standard transformer components (attention, FFN, MoE) follow the derived FLOP/memory formulas.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "characterise inference latency via roofline modelling."
  - [section 3.3] Presents the latency equations (Equation 4) and roofline foundation.
  - [section 4.2] Validates predictions against vLLM measurements, reporting strong correlation.
  - [corpus] RooflineBench and LLM Inference Unveiled apply roofline to LLM inference; this paper integrates it into a co-design scaling law.
- **Break condition**: Real systems exhibit kernel launch overhead, operator fusion, and cache effects not captured in the analytical model, causing 10–20% deviations. The model assumes batch size 1; batching may alter bottleneck regimes. Non-standard architectures (e.g., SSM hybrids) require re-derivation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Coupling the loss scaling law with roofline latency modeling enables direct accuracy-latency correspondence and Pareto-optimal architecture identification under hardware constraints, reducing search from months to days.
- **Mechanism**: For ~1,942 candidate architectures, predict loss (from scaling law) and latency (from roofline). Construct Pareto frontier by eliminating dominated points (no other architecture has both lower loss and lower latency). Use adaptive sampling to efficiently explore the space. Select architectures matching target latency budgets from the frontier.
- **Core assumption**: The loss and latency models are sufficiently accurate to correctly rank architectures and identify the true Pareto frontier. The frontier constructed from predictions is valid for actual trained models.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "By coupling this scaling law with latency modelling, we establish a direct accuracy-latency correspondence and identify the Pareto frontier."
  - [section 4.3] Shows Pareto frontiers under prefill/decode/total latency, demonstrates that a co-designed architecture achieves 19.42% lower perplexity than Qwen2.5-0.5B at equivalent measured latency (Figure 8).
  - [corpus] TriGen and Scaling Inference-Efficient consider hardware-aware design; this paper provides the first operational co-design scaling law for on-device LLMs.
- **Break condition**: Inaccuracies in either model (loss extrapolation error, latency estimation bias) can produce a frontier that does not match true trade-offs. The frontier is specific to one hardware platform (Jetson Orin) and workload (batch-1, 1K input/16 output tokens); transfer to other platforms requires re-deriving hardware parameters.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Roofline Performance Model
- **Why needed here**: To predict whether a given operator or model layer will be compute-bound or memory-bound on the target hardware, which determines how latency scales with architectural changes.
- **Quick check**: Given hardware with 100 TFLOPS peak compute and 500 GB/s memory bandwidth, what is the arithmetic intensity threshold above which an operation becomes compute-bound?  
  *Answer: Threshold = peak_compute / bandwidth = 100e12 FLOPS / 500e9 bytes/s = 200 FLOPs/byte. Operations with intensity > 200 FLOPs/byte are compute-bound.*

### Concept: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Routing
- **Why needed here**: MoE decouples total model capacity from per-token compute, enabling larger models without proportional latency increase. The paper finds MoE dominates Pareto frontiers for on-device deployment.
- **Quick check**: If an MoE layer has E=8 experts and activates K=2 per token, by what factor does the total parameter count increase relative to a dense layer with the same activated parameters?  
  *Answer: Total parameters increase by a factor of E/K = 8/2 = 4, because the dense layer would have parameters equal to the activated experts' parameters, but MoE stores all experts.*

### Concept: Hardware-Aware Pareto Optimization
- **Why needed here**: To systematically explore the trade-off between model accuracy (loss) and inference efficiency (latency) and identify non-dominated architectures for a given hardware budget.
- **Quick check**: In a Pareto frontier plot (loss vs. latency), what characterizes a Pareto-optimal point?  
  *Answer: A point is Pareto-optimal if no other point has both lower loss and lower latency; i.e., you cannot improve one metric without worsening the other.*

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Scaling Law Fitter -> Roofline Latency Estimator -> Pareto Frontier Constructor -> Theoretical Optimizer -> Validation Harness

### Critical Path
1. Define architecture search space (depth, width, MoE, FFN ratio, GQA) and hardware/workload parameters (π_H, β_H, memory budget, B, S_in, S_out).
2. Train a representative subset (~170 models) for a fixed token budget (e.g., 10B) to fit the loss scaling law; validate on held-out configs.
3. Derive or measure hardware parameters (peak FLOPS, sustained bandwidth, memory capacity).
4. For all candidate architectures, predict loss (using scaling law) and latency (using roofline model). If memory is constrained, also compute memory footprint.
5. Construct Pareto frontier by eliminating dominated points; optionally use theoretical formulas (Section 5) to predict optimal regions.
6. Select target architecture from frontier for given latency budget; train fully and measure real latency/perplexity to validate.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Training cost vs. coverage: Fitting a reliable scaling law requires training hundreds of models (13,600 GPU-hours in the paper). Reducing this risks poor generalization.
- Model expressivity vs. interpretability: The polynomial loss model is interpretable but may miss complex interactions; a neural network could fit better but obfuscates insights.
- Latency model fidelity: Roofline is fast but approximate. Ignoring kernel overhead, cache effects, and quantization penalties (e.g., INT8 non-linear ops) can cause 10–20% error.
- Search space restrictions: The paper’s space excludes hybrid architectures (e.g., SSM-Transformer) and alternative attention patterns. Results may not transfer to such models.
- Hardware specificity: Optimal architectures are tied to a specific platform’s roofline (π_H, β_H) and memory budget. Porting to new hardware requires re-measuring specs and recomputing frontier.

### Failure Signatures
- Loss scaling law: Validation R² drops below ~0.9, or systematic prediction errors (e.g., always overestimating loss for wide models).
- Latency model: Predicted vs. measured latency correlation weakens (e.g., R² < 0.8), especially for long sequences or quantized models.
- Pareto frontier: Selected architecture misses latency budget by >10% or fails to outperform baseline (e.g., Qwen2.5-0.5B) in perplexity at same measured latency.
- Generalization: Theoretical formulas (e.g., ρ* from Theorem 5.2) yield infeasible values (e.g., ρ* < ρ_min or ρ* > 1) for given width d, indicating the regime assumptions are violated.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Loss model validation**: Train 5–10 held-out architectures (not in the 170) for 1–2B tokens. Compare actual validation loss to scaling law prediction. Expect error within the paper’s reported validation RMSE.
2. **Latency model calibration**: For 10 Pareto-optimal candidates, measure end-to-end decode latency on target hardware (using vLLM with isolated processes). Compare to roofline predictions; compute mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). Expect MAPE < 15% as in Appendix D.
3. **Pareto selection test**: Choose a latency budget (e.g., matching Qwen2.5-0.5B’s measured decode latency). Pick the architecture from the predicted frontier at that latency. Train it for 10B tokens, measure WikiText-2 perplexity and latency. Expect ≥15% perplexity reduction vs. baseline at same latency (replicating the paper’s 19.42% result).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does the hardware co-design scaling law generalize to hardware platforms with different compute/memory balances (e.g., TPUs, ASICs) beyond the NVIDIA Jetson Orin studied?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper only validates on NVIDIA Jetson Orin and suggests future work should validate across broader hardware including TPUs and specialized accelerators. The theoretical framework uses roofline parameters (peak compute, bandwidth) that vary by platform.
- **Why unresolved**: The scaling law coefficients and Pareto-optimal architectures are derived from roofline modeling that depends on hardware-specific ratios; different compute/memory balances may alter which constraint regimes dominate and thus optimal designs.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematically apply the PLAS framework to at least 3 distinct hardware platforms (e.g., a TPU pod, an edge ASIC, a different GPU) by measuring hardware parameters, fitting per-platform loss models, and comparing predicted vs. empirical Pareto frontiers to assess transferability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can the loss scaling law (Equation 2) be extended to include training dynamics such as learning rate schedules, optimizer choices, and data distribution shifts?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper states as a future direction: "incorporating training dynamics (learning rate schedules, optimizer state) into the loss model." The current law is empirically fitted under a single training protocol (10B tokens, AdamW, specific data).
- **Why unresolved**: The polynomial loss model only captures architecture under fixed training conditions; varying dynamics could change effective scaling exponents and intercepts, limiting the law's applicability to new training setups.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train a diverse set of architectures under varied LR schedules, optimizers (e.g., AdamW vs. Sophia), and data mixtures; fit an extended scaling law that includes training dynamic parameters; test its predictive accuracy on held-out configurations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How accurate is the roofline-based latency model in predicting real-world inference times when accounting for kernel fusion, cache hierarchy effects, and system-level interference?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper acknowledges: "the latency model assumes idealized roofline behavior... real systems exhibit kernel launch overhead, cache effects, and operator fusion that may cause deviations of 10–20% from theoretical predictions." They use it for fast search but validate top candidates empirically.
- **Why unresolved**: The analytical model simplifies system-level complexities; potential 10-20% error could misrank architectures near the Pareto frontier, especially in tight constraint regimes.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct a comprehensive empirical study measuring latency of thousands of architectural configurations; correlate deviations with specific kernel patterns, fusion opportunities, and cache miss rates; refine the model to incorporate these factors and quantify improvement in prediction fidelity.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do the derived Pareto-optimal architectural patterns (e.g., wide-and-shallow, sparse MoE) hold for hybrid architectures like SSM-Transformer or linear attention models?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper notes: "extensions to hybrid architectures such as SSM-Transformer blends or linear attention mechanisms would require re-deriving constraint forms and may exhibit qualitatively different scaling behaviors." The current theory assumes standard Transformer components.
- **Why unresolved**: Hybrid models alter compute/memory profiles (e.g., SSMs have different recurrence patterns); the roofline coefficients and optimization results might change fundamentally.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Adapt the roofline constraint derivations for SSM and linear attention; identify Pareto frontiers for these architectures via search; compare the optimal patterns to those found for standard Transformers to see if principles like "wide-and-shallow" persist.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: In dual-constrained regimes, what is the empirical frequency of different constraint combinations (latency vs. memory bottlenecks) in real on-device deployments, and how sensitive are the optimal architectures to regime misclassification?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The theoretical analysis defines regimes via ratios η and ηp but does not empirically measure how often these occur in practice. The framework assumes clear regime boundaries.
- **Why unresolved**: Real deployments may lie near regime boundaries; small errors in measuring hardware parameters or workload could lead to choosing suboptimal architectures if the wrong closed-form solution is applied.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Profile a wide range of on-device LLM applications (autonomous driving, smart home, etc.) to map their (compute, memory) requirements relative to hardware limits; quantify the prevalence of pure vs. dual regimes; stress-test the framework by perturbing η values and measuring performance degradation.

## Limitations
- The polynomial loss scaling law may not generalize to architectures far outside the training distribution (e.g., extreme depth/width ratios, novel attention mechanisms) or to significantly different training budgets without refitting.
- The roofline latency model ignores kernel launch overheads, cache effects, and operator fusion, leading to 10–20% prediction errors, especially for long sequences or quantized inference.
- The framework is hardware-specific; optimal architectures are tied to the roofline parameters and memory budget of a given platform (e.g., Jetson Orin) and may not transfer to other systems without re-characterization.
