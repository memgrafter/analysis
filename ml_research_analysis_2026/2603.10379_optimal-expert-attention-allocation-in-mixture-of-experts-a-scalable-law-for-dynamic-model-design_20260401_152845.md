# Optimal Expert-Attention Allocation in Mixture-of-Experts: A Scalable Law for Dynamic Model Design

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10379
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10379
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** The optimal FLOPs ratio between expert and attention layers in MoE models follows a sparsity-modulated power law with total compute, enabling compute-optimal design via an extended Chinchilla scaling law.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a scalable law for allocating compute between expert and attention layers in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Transformers under fixed training budgets. Through extensive experiments on GPT-style models, it shows that the optimal FLOPs ratio \( r^* = C_E/C_A \) follows a power law with total compute \( C \), with coefficients that depend on sparsity \( S \) (fraction of inactive experts). These coefficients themselves obey power laws with the fraction of active experts \( (1-S) \). The authors integrate this allocation law into an extended Chinchilla scaling framework by adding penalty terms for deviations from \( r^* \), achieving high predictive accuracy for training loss across scales and sparsity levels. The work provides a principled method to determine internal model architecture given a compute budget, bridging a gap in MoE scaling theory.

## Method Summary
The authors train GPT-style autoregressive MoE Transformers with varying sparsity levels (\( S = (E-3)/E \) for expert counts \( E \in \{17,33,65,129\} \)) and per-token compute budgets. For each \( (C,S) \) pair, they sweep the FLOPs ratio \( r = C_E/C_A \) while keeping total compute constant, and identify the optimal \( r^* \) that minimizes validation loss. They then fit a power law \( r^* = \alpha_r(S) C^{\beta_r(S)} \) to the extracted minima. Next, they fit the dependencies \( \alpha_r \propto (1-S)^{-1.23} \) and \( \beta_r \propto (1-S)^{0.21} \) across sparsity levels. Finally, they augment the standard Chinchilla scaling law \( L(N,D) \) with two penalty terms that quantify inefficiency from suboptimal expert-attention allocation and excessive expert compute relative to parameters. The extended law is validated on held-out sparsity levels and compared to prior MoE scaling laws.

## Key Results
- The optimal FLOPs ratio \( r^* \) scales as a power law with total compute: \( r^* = \alpha_r(S) C^{\beta_r(S)} \), where \( \alpha_r \) and \( \beta_r \) depend on sparsity.
- The scaling coefficients follow \( \alpha_r \propto (1-S)^{-1.23} \) and \( \beta_r \propto (1-S)^{0.21} \), indicating that lower sparsity (more active experts) increases both the baseline optimal ratio and its growth with compute.
- The extended Chinchilla law, incorporating misallocation penalties, accurately predicts training loss for MoE models across a wide range of compute budgets and sparsity levels, outperforming prior scaling laws.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The optimal FLOPs ratio \( r^* = C_E/C_A \) between expert and attention compute increases as a power law with total training compute \( C \).
- **Mechanism:** Under a fixed per-token compute budget, diminishing returns for additional computation are modeled separately for attention (sublinear scaling with \( C_A \)) and expert layers (sublinear scaling with \( C_E \), but with sparsity-modulated elasticity). The optimal allocation \( r^* \) is derived by equating the marginal utility per FLOP for both components, yielding \( r^* \propto C^{\beta_r} \).
- **Core assumption:** The marginal improvement in loss from an additional FLOP allocated to experts depends on sparsity \( S \); more activated experts (lower \( S \)) increase the marginal utility of expert FLOPs relative to attention FLOPs.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the power-law relationship with sparsity-dependent coefficients; Figure 2(a) shows log-linear fits of \( r^* \) vs. \( C \) for different sparsity levels; the theoretical derivation in Appendix A.1 links \( \beta_r \) to differences in diminishing return exponents and sparsity.
- **Break condition:** The power-law may break down at compute scales beyond the experimental range (up to \( 1 \times 10^{21} \) FLOPs) or for non-autoregressive architectures.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The scaling coefficients \( \alpha_r \) and \( \beta_r \) themselves follow power laws with the fraction of activated experts \( (1-S) \).
- **Mechanism:** Sparsity \( S \) determines how effectively additional expert compute can be utilized. Lower \( S \) (more experts active) increases both the baseline optimal ratio (\( \alpha_r \)) and its growth rate with compute (\( \beta_r \)). This is captured by fitting the coefficients from the per-sparsity power laws.
- **Core assumption:** The benefit of expert compute derives from both capacity and diversity; diversity scales with the number of active experts \( E_{\text{act}} \propto (1-S) \), leading to the observed power-law coefficients.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract provides the exponents \( -1.23 \) and \( 0.21 \); Figures 2(b) and 2(c) plot \( \alpha_r \) and \( \beta_r \) against \( (1-S) \) on log-log scales, showing clear power-law trends.
- **Break condition:** These exponents may not hold for extreme sparsity (e.g., \( S > 99.5\% \)) or if expert specialization saturates, causing the marginal utility of additional active experts to drop faster.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Deviations from the optimal ratio \( r^* \) can be incorporated as a penalty term in a generalized Chinchilla scaling law, significantly improving loss prediction accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** The extended scaling law (Equation 2) adds two terms: one penalizing excessive expert allocation via \( c \cdot e^{R(1-S)^\gamma} / N^\lambda \) and another penalizing misallocation ratio via \( d \cdot r/(r+1) \). These terms quantify inefficiency from suboptimal internal compute distribution without changing total FLOPs.
- **Core assumption:** The loss penalty from misallocating compute between attention and experts is separable from the base scaling terms for parameters \( N \) and data \( D \), and can be modeled with simple functional forms.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports high predictive accuracy; Figure 3 validates on held-out data (excluding \( S=97.67\% \) from fitting); Section D.3 shows superiority over prior MoE scaling laws.
- **Break condition:** The penalty terms may not transfer to settings with adaptive/expert-specific routing, variable sequence lengths, or hardware-aware constraints (e.g., communication costs).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Neural Scaling Laws**
  - **Why needed:** The paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law, which relates model loss to compute-optimal allocations of parameters \( N \) and training tokens \( D \). Understanding this base framework is essential to see how the expert-attention ratio \( r \) adds a third, internal architectural dimension.
  - **Quick check:** In the standard Chinchilla law, what is the predicted relationship between optimal model size \( N \) and compute budget \( C \)?
- **Concept: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Sparsity**
  - **Why needed:** Sparsity \( S \) is the key modulator of how expert compute effectiveness scales. It defines the fraction of inactive experts and directly influences the coefficients \( \alpha_r, \beta_r \). Without grasping sparsity, the allocation law's dependence cannot be interpreted.
  - **Quick check:** If an MoE layer has 64 total experts and activates 4 per token, what is its sparsity \( S \)?
- **Concept: FLOPs Budgeting and Ratio \( r \)**
  - **Why needed:** The core variable \( r = C_E/C_A \) is a first-order design choice controlling internal compute distribution. Engineers must understand how to compute per-layer FLOPs for attention vs. experts to set \( r \) given a total budget.
  - **Quick check:** For a fixed total per-token compute \( C_{\text{token}} \), if you increase \( r \), what happens to \( C_A \) and \( C_E \)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Input/Embedding -> Transformer Stack (Decoder layers) -> Output Layer. Each decoder layer contains: Causal Self-Attention -> Sparse MoE Layer (Router + E Experts, top-k activation).

**Critical path:**
1. Given total compute budget \( C_{\text{token}} \) and desired sparsity \( S \) (fixing \( E \) and activation count \( k \)).
2. Compute optimal \( r^*(C, S) \) using the power-law formulas \( \alpha_r(S) \) and \( \beta_r(S) \).
3. Solve for \( C_E = r^* \cdot C_A \) and \( C_{\text{token}} = C_A + C_E \) to get target FLOPs for attention and expert sublayers.
4. Adjust layer dimensions (e.g., \( d_{\text{hidden}} \) for attention, \( d_{\text{expert}} \) for experts) to meet these FLOP targets while keeping depth constant.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Expert vs. Attention Capacity:** Increasing \( r \) boosts expert parameters and FLOPs but reduces attention capacity. Under low sparsity (\( S \approx 80\% \)), higher \( r \) is optimal at large scales; under high sparsity (\( S > 95\% \)), attention capacity becomes more critical.
- **Sparsity vs. Specialist Quality:** Higher sparsity (fewer active experts) reduces compute but may harm specialist performance unless expert count \( E \) is sufficiently large to maintain diversity.
- **Routing Overhead:** The paper assumes simple top-k routing. More complex routing (e.g., load balancing, expert parallelism) introduces extra FLOPs not captured in the basic \( C_E \) count, potentially requiring \( r \) recalibration.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Training instability:** If \( r \) is set too low (attention-starved) for a given sparsity, early training may show high loss and poor convergence on tasks requiring long-range context.
- **Inefficient scaling:** Using a constant \( r \) across scales leads to systematic underperformance, especially at large \( C \) where the optimal \( r^* \) is higher (see Figure 1 shift).
- **Held-out sparsity mismatch:** Applying the scaling law (Equation 2) with coefficients fitted on \( S \leq 95.38\% \) to \( S=97.67\% \) without adjustment may yield inaccurate loss predictions (as done deliberately for validation in Figure 3b).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Fixed Sparsity Sweep:** For a given \( S \) (e.g., 90.91%), train models at multiple total compute budgets \( C \), sweeping \( r \in [0.2, 1.5] \). Identify \( r^* \) at each \( C \) as the minimum validation loss. Confirm existence of clear minimum and monotonic increase of \( r^* \) with \( C \).
2. **Sparsity Series:** Repeat Experiment 1 across at least 4 sparsity levels (e.g., \( S \in \{82.35\%, 90.91\%, 95.38\%, 97.67\%\} \)). For each \( S \), fit \( r^*(C) = \alpha_r C^{\beta_r} \). Plot \( \alpha_r \) and \( \beta_r \) vs. \( (1-S) \) on log-log axes and fit power laws to obtain \( \alpha_r(S), \beta_r(S) \).
3. **Scaling Law Validation:** Using the fitted \( \alpha_r(S), \beta_r(S) \), compute predicted \( r^* \) for new \( (C, S) \) pairs (including a held-out sparsity, e.g., \( S=97.67\% \) if not used in fitting). Train models at these settings and compare observed loss to predictions from Equation 2. Check alignment in scatter plot and training trajectory.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does the optimal expert-attention FLOPs ratio scaling law generalize to non-autoregressive MoE architectures, such as encoder-decoder Transformers?
- How does the optimal FLOPs ratio \( r^* \) change when sparsity is dynamic (e.g., variable number of active experts per token) rather than fixed?
- Can the scaling law be extended to multimodal MoE models where expert and attention compute may be distributed unevenly across modalities?
- What is the effect of different expert routing mechanisms (e.g., learned vs. static) on the optimal expert-attention compute allocation?
- How do hardware communication costs in distributed training environments influence the optimal FLOPs ratio \( r^* \), given that expert parallelism may incur additional overhead?

## Limitations
- Validated only on autoregressive language modeling; may not generalize to encoder-decoder or multimodal architectures.
- Assumes fixed sparsity (constant top-k activation); adaptive routing or variable activation counts are not considered.
- Does not account for hardware-level communication costs or distribution strategies that affect actual training time.

## Confidence
- **Optimal \( r^* \) power-law with compute:** Medium. Strong empirical support across 4 sparsity levels and multiple scales, but potential breakdown beyond tested ranges or for non-autoregressive architectures.
- **Sparsity-dependent coefficients \( \alpha_r, \beta_r \):** Medium. Clear log-log trends in Figure 2, but exponents are empirically fitted; generalizability to extreme sparsity or saturated specialization is uncertain.
- **Extended Chinchilla law with misallocation penalty:** Medium. High predictive accuracy on held-out sparsity and superior to prior laws, but penalty terms are simple and unvalidated for adaptive routing or hardware-aware constraints.

## Next Checks
1. **Extreme sparsity test:** Train models at \( S > 99\% \) (e.g., \( E=257, k=2 \)) across compute scales. Check if fitted \( \alpha_r \propto (1-S)^{-1.23} \) and \( \beta_r \propto (1-S)^{0.21} \) hold or diverge.
2. **Routing variation test:** Replace top-k routing with load-balanced or expert-specific routing. Re-measure optimal \( r^* \) at fixed \( C, S \). Determine if penalty terms in Equation 2 need adjustment for routing overhead.
3. **Hardware-aware constraint test:** Impose communication costs (e.g., expert parallelism limits) that decouple theoretical \( C_E \) from actual wall-clock time. Re-derive optimal \( r^* \) under latency budgets and compare to the FLOPs-only law.
