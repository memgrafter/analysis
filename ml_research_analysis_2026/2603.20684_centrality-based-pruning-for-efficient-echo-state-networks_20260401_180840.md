# Centrality-Based Pruning for Efficient Echo State Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20684
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20684
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** Centrality-based pruning reduces reservoir size by 25–40% while improving prediction accuracy (up to 11.3% error reduction on Mackey‑Glass, 44.6% on electricity load).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses redundancy in Echo State Network (ESN) reservoirs by proposing a centrality‑based pruning method. The reservoir is modeled as a weighted directed graph, and centrality measures that differentiate positive and negative connection strengths are used to rank nodes. Nodes with low centrality are removed, and the output weights are retrained. Experiments on the chaotic Mackey‑Glass time series and electricity load forecasting show that moderate pruning (25–40%) often improves accuracy, with task‑specific optimal centralities: C2 for chaotic dynamics and Cout for load data. Performance follows a U‑shaped curve; excessive pruning degrades accuracy. The echo state property is preserved by keeping the spectral radius below one.

## Method Summary
The authors treat an ESN reservoir as a directed graph where nodes are recurrent units and edges are signed weights. Several centrality measures are defined: Cin (sum of absolute incoming weights), Cout (sum of absolute outgoing weights), C1 (normalized difference between positive and absolute negative incoming weights), C2 (similar difference combining incoming and outgoing), and C3 (total absolute weight sum). After initializing a random reservoir with spectral radius <1, centralities are computed from the initial weight matrix. Nodes with the lowest scores are pruned, and the remaining weight matrix is rescaled if necessary to maintain the spectral radius constraint. Finally, the linear readout weights are retrained via ridge regression on the pruned reservoir’s states.

The approach is evaluated on two tasks: 84‑step ahead prediction of the Mackey‑Glass chaotic series and electricity load forecasting. For reservoir sizes ranging from 200 to 700 units, the authors prune increasing percentages of nodes using each centrality measure and compare test normalized RMSE. They identify optimal pruning levels (typically 25–40% removal) and the best centrality per task, demonstrating that moderate pruning often enhances accuracy while preserving stability.

## Key Results
- Pruning removes 25–40% of reservoir nodes while improving prediction accuracy; performance follows a U‑shaped curve with an optimal range.
- On Mackey‑Glass, C2 centrality yields up to 11.3% error reduction; on electricity load, Cout centrality yields up to 44.6% reduction.
- The most effective centrality measure is task‑dependent (C2 for chaotic dynamics, Cout for load data).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Nodes with low graph centrality values contribute less structurally to reservoir dynamics and can be pruned without disrupting core functionality.
- **Mechanism:** The reservoir is modeled as a weighted directed graph; centrality measures (C1, C2, C3) quantify each node's influence based on the strength and sign (positive/negative) of its incoming and outgoing connections. Nodes scoring low are removed, and output weights are retrained.
- **Core assumption:** Structural importance, as captured by these centrality metrics, correlates with a node's functional contribution to the reservoir's transient dynamics and final prediction accuracy.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We model the reservoir as a weighted directed graph and use centrality measures to identify structurally less important nodes."
  - [section 4] Defines C1, C2, C3 with explicit formulas separating positive and negative connection strengths.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., Li et al., 2019; Chowdhury et al., 2025) apply centrality to prune neural networks, but direct application to ESN reservoirs is novel per this paper's focus.
- **Break condition:** If low‑centrality nodes are unexpectedly critical for maintaining the echo state property or capturing specific nonlinear dynamics, pruning them could degrade performance despite high centrality scores.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Separating positive (excitatory) and negative (inhibitory) connection strengths in centrality measures yields a more accurate ranking of node importance than using total magnitude alone.
- **Mechanism:** Measures like C1 and C2 compute a normalized difference between summed positive and absolute negative incoming/outgoing weights. This accounts for the opposing roles of excitation and inhibition in recurrent dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** The sign of a weight qualitatively changes its effect on the reservoir's state evolution; thus, a node with strong balanced positive/negative influences may be functionally different from one with unbalanced or purely strong connections.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4] "Since positive and negative weights contribute differently to the reservoir dynamics, separating them provides a more accurate characterization of node influence."
  - [section 6] Results show C1 and C2 often outperform C3 (total magnitude) in RMSE reduction.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; corpus discusses general centrality in graphs but not sign separation in ESN‑specific contexts.
- **Break condition:** If the reservoir's weight distribution is predominantly positive or if the task is insensitive to inhibitory effects, the separation may add unnecessary complexity without benefit.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Moderate pruning improves accuracy by removing redundancy, but excessive pruning disrupts information flow and degrades performance, forming an optimal pruning range.
- **Mechanism:** Initial pruning eliminates truly redundant nodes, simplifying the model and potentially reducing noise. Beyond a threshold, too few nodes remain to sustain the rich transient dynamics necessary for complex temporal tasks, harming the echo state property's effective realization.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a "sweet spot" where the reservoir retains sufficient connectivity and dimensionality to embed input history while minimizing uninformative units.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Pruning reduces reservoir size by 25–40% and often improves accuracy... Results show an optimal pruning range; excessive removal degrades accuracy."
  - [section 6] Figures 2 and 3 show U‑shaped RMSE curves versus pruned nodes; initial error reduction followed by increase.
  - [corpus] Related pruning studies (e.g., Liu et al., 2022) observe similar trends, supporting a general principle in reservoir optimization.
- **Break condition:** If the initial reservoir is severely overparameterized with mostly noise, optimal pruning might allow higher removal percentages; the breakpoint is task‑ and initialization‑dependent.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Echo State Property (ESP)
  - **Why needed here:** Pruning must preserve ESP to ensure the reservoir's state is a contractive function of past inputs, making the readout layer's training stable and predictions reliable.
  - **Quick check question:** How is ESP typically enforced in practice during and after pruning? (Answer: By maintaining the spectral radius of the reservoir weight matrix below 1.0.)
- **Concept:** Reservoir Computing Paradigm
  - **Why needed here:** Understanding that only the output weights (W_out) are trained via linear regression, while the recurrent reservoir (W) remains fixed after initialization. Pruning alters W, so retraining W_out is essential to adapt to the new structure.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does training only the readout layer make ESNs computationally efficient compared to traditional RNNs? (Answer: It avoids expensive gradient‑based optimization through recurrent connections.)
- **Concept:** Graph Centrality in Neural Context
  - **Why needed here:** To interpret the reservoir's connectivity pattern as a directed graph and apply quantitative metrics (C1, C2, C3) to rank nodes for removal, moving beyond random or correlation‑based pruning.
  - **Quick check question:** What key feature do C1 and C2 incorporate that C3 does not, and why might this matter? (Answer: They separate positive and negative weights, capturing potential excitatory/inhibitory roles which are functionally distinct in dynamics.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** u(n) → W_in → x(n) → W_out → y(n+1); with optional feedback: y(n) → W_back → x(n+1).
- **Critical path:** Input (u) → W_in → Reservoir state (x) → W_out → Output (y). (Pruning intervenes on W before W_out retraining.)
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Spectral radius vs. expressiveness: Lower spectral radius ensures ESP but may limit dynamical richness; pruning reduces size but risks pushing effective spectral radius upward if remaining connections strengthen.
  - Centrality measure choice: Task‑dependent (C2 best for chaotic Mackey‑Glass; Cout best for load data). Requires empirical validation.
  - Pruning aggressiveness: More reduction yields smaller/faster models but risks crossing the breakpoint where dynamics are impoverished.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - RMSE increases monotonically with pruning (no initial improvement), indicating poor centrality ranking or over‑aggressive initial cuts.
  - Dramatic RMSE spike after moderate pruning, suggesting removal of critical nodes or loss of ESP (check spectral radius post‑pruning).
  - A centrality measure that consistently underperforms across tasks (e.g., C3 in Mackey‑Glass) may not capture relevant dynamics for that system.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline & Centrality Comparison:** For a fixed large reservoir (e.g., N=500), compare unpruned ESN vs. pruned versions using C1, C2, C3, Cin, Cout on Mackey‑Glass. Identify best measure and optimal pruning percentage.
  2. **Task Transfer:** Take the best‑performing centrality from Exp 1 (likely C2) and apply it to electricity load forecasting with multiple reservoir sizes (N=200,300,500,700). Verify if the same measure remains optimal or if task‑specificity holds (expect Cout to be better).
  3. **Pruning Curve Mapping:** For the winning measure on each task, systematically vary the number of nodes removed (e.g., 0% to 60% in 5% increments) to precisely locate the U‑shaped performance curve's minimum. Validate that the optimal range aligns with the paper's reported 25–40%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What additional graph centrality measures and adaptive pruning strategies can further optimize ESN efficiency without sacrificing accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "In future work, we plan to explore additional graph‑based and spectral centrality measures, as well as adaptive pruning strategies. We also aim to extend this approach to other reservoir computing models and recurrent neural networks."
- **Why unresolved:** The study only evaluates three simple centrality measures (C1, C2, C3) using fixed pruning thresholds; more sophisticated metrics (e.g., eigenvector centrality) and dynamic, performance‑driven pruning schedules remain untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments comparing a wide range of spectral and path‑based centralities across multiple datasets, with adaptive thresholds that adjust based on validation performance to avoid excessive pruning.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the proposed centrality‑based pruning framework be effectively transferred to other recurrent neural network architectures beyond Echo State Networks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly aims to "extend this approach to other reservoir computing models and recurrent neural networks." The related work also notes that centrality pruning is primarily used in feedforward or convolutional networks, not RNNs.
- **Why unresolved:** ESNs have fixed recurrent weights, whereas standard RNNs (e.g., LSTMs) are trained end‑to‑end. The applicability of static structural centrality to trained, nonlinear recurrent dynamics is unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct application of the same centrality definitions and pruning protocol to common RNN architectures on time‑series benchmarks, with evaluation of stability (e.g., gradient norms) and prediction accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the observed task‑specific centrality preferences (e.g., C2 for chaotic Mackey‑Glass, Cout for electricity load) generalize to a broader range of time‑series datasets with varying statistical properties?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Results in Tables 1 and 2 show different centralities yield optimal reductions for different datasets, but evaluation is limited to only two tasks (synthetic chaotic and one real‑world load dataset).
- **Why unresolved:** It is uncertain whether these preferences are tied to specific dynamics (e.g., chaos vs. periodicity) or dataset idiosyncrasies; without more diverse benchmarks, the conditions favoring each centrality remain vague.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Testing all centrality measures on a diverse suite of time‑series (e.g., UCR archive) covering chaotic, periodic, and stochastic patterns, followed by correlation analysis between performance and dataset characteristics like Lyapunov exponent or seasonality strength.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does static pruning based on initial centrality scores preserve the long‑term dynamic stability and memory properties of the reservoir, or does it introduce hidden instabilities not prevented by maintaining the initial spectral radius?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper enforces the echo state property by keeping the spectral radius below one after pruning, but it does not validate long‑term state washout or memory capacity under extended inputs. Centrality pruning removes nodes based on static structure, potentially disrupting dynamic trajectories over time.
- **Why unresolved:** The echo state property requires that initial states and perturbations vanish; removing low‑centrality nodes might create network bottlenecks or amplify errors in long‑horizon predictions, which spectral radius alone may not capture.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Quantitative assessment of memory capacity and linear response functions before and after pruning, along with long‑horizon simulations to measure state decay rates. Analysis of eigenvalue distribution changes and robustness to input perturbations would also help.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter settings (reservoir sparsity, weight range, exact spectral radius bound, input scale, feedback usage) are not fully specified.
- Output training details (ridge regularization λ, bias term) are ambiguous.
- Definitions of Cin and Cout centralities are unclear; the assumed absolute weight sums may not match the paper's intended formulas.
