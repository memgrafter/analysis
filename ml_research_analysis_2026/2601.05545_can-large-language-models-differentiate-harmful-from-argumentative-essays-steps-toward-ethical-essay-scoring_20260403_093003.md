# Can Large Language Models Differentiate Harmful from Argumentative Essays? Steps Toward Ethical Essay Scoring

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05545
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05545
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Incorporating explicit ethical guidelines into LLM scoring instructions significantly reduces scores for harmful essays (e.g., Llama3.1 harmful score drops from 3.15 to 2.12) while improving agreement (QWK) with human IELTS scores.

## Executive Summary
This paper examines whether automated essay scoring (AES) systems and large language models (LLMs) can reliably distinguish harmful content (such as racism or gender bias) from legitimate argumentative essays. The authors introduce the Harmful Essay Detection (HED) benchmark, comprising 290 LLM-generated essays (100 argumentative, 190 harmful) on sensitive IELTS prompts. Experiments reveal that LLMs struggle with harmful essay classification (low recall) and that standard AES models consistently assign higher holistic scores to harmful essays than to argumentative ones. By augmenting LLM scoring instructions with explicit ethical annotation guidelines (e.g., a clause instructing the model to rescore if harmful content is detected), harmful essay scores drop markedly without sacrificing alignment with human IELTS scores, as measured by Quadratic Weighted Kappa. This work demonstrates a practical path toward more ethical automated essay assessment.

## Method Summary
The study constructs the HED benchmark by prompting LLMs (Mistral, Qwen) with "pretending" instructions and persona variations (e.g., demographic attributes, personality traits) to generate both argumentative and harmful essays in response to 50 IELTS-style writing prompts on socially sensitive topics. The generated essays are validated via human annotation for discriminatory content, hate speech, and ethical issues.

For classification tasks, multiple LLMs (Llama3.1-8B, Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen2-7B) are evaluated under zero-shot instructions that vary in persona description. Performance is measured using precision, recall, and F1. For scoring, four AES models (Hi att, PAES, NPCR, PMAES) trained on the IELTS dataset and the same LLMs are used to predict holistic scores (1–6). The scoring condition compares a baseline rubric-only instruction with an augmented instruction that appends ethical guidelines (e.g., "Rescore if the essay promotes harmful stereotypes"). On the overlapping IELTS test essays, Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) assesses agreement with human scores, while on HED essays, average scores are compared between essay types.

## Key Results
- LLMs exhibit low recall for harmful essays; e.g., Llama3.1 achieves harmful F1 of 82.70 but recall lagging behind precision.
- AES models systematically over-score harmful essays: PMAES assigns 4.58 to harmful vs. 4.14 to argumentative essays.
- Adding ethical guidelines reduces harmful essay scores dramatically (e.g., Llama3.1: from 3.15 to 2.12) and maintains or improves QWK with human IELTS scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: LLMs misclassify harmful content embedded in quality discourse
LLMs are trained to reject explicitly toxic utterances, but when harmful ideology is woven into a coherent, well-structured argument, their safety heuristics fail. The model prioritizes linguistic quality and topical relevance over ethical content, leading to low recall for harmful essays. Evidence includes consistently lower recall rates for harmful essays across all LLMs tested.

### Mechanism 2: AES models lack an ethical dimension in their scoring objective
AES systems are trained on human-scored, non-harmful essays to predict holistic scores based on features like grammar, organization, and vocabulary. Harmful essays that mimic persuasive structure exhibit these quality features strongly, and since the training objective does not penalize ethical content, the models assign high scores. This is evidenced by multiple AES models giving higher average scores to harmful essays.

### Mechanism 3: Explicit ethical guidelines circumvent the quality bias
Appending a conditional "Rescore if..." clause to the scoring instruction forces the LLM to perform a secondary check that overrides default quality-based heuristics. The model complies with the multi-step instruction, reducing scores when harmful patterns are detected. This approach improves ethical scoring while preserving rubric alignment, as shown by significant score drops and stable QWK.

Within Scorer Module: AES Model Wrapper (pre-trained AES) and LLM Scorer (with/without ethical guidelines)

### Critical Path
Data Generation (HED) → Classification Experiment (RQ1) → Scoring Experiment (RQ2) → Guideline Integration (RQ3) → Result Aggregation. Persona and instruction variants are orthogonal layers applied across these stages.

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Benchmark authenticity vs. scalability*: LLM-generated harmful content is controllable and inexpensive but may lack the nuance of human-written hate speech.
- *Instruction complexity vs. model compliance*: Adding ethical guidelines improves ethical behavior but risks confusing the model or conflicting with rubric interpretation, though the study found no QWK degradation.
- *Generalizability vs. specificity*: Testing on 50 IELTS prompts covers topical sensitivity but may not extend to other essay genres (e.g., narrative or source-based tasks).

### Failure Signatures
- Classification: High precision but very low recall for harmful essays (model catches only obvious toxicity).
- Scoring: Any condition where harmful essays receive scores equal to or higher than argumentative essays.
- Guideline Integration: The ethical guide fails to reduce harmful scores or degrades QWK on benign essays.
- Persona Effects: Large performance swings (±10+ F1) with different persona prompts, indicating unstable model representations of identity/ethics.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate Classification Baseline**: Run zero-shot "You are a helpful annotator..." instruction across Llama3.1, Llama3, Mistral, Qwen on the full HED test set (290 essays). Parse outputs to "Argumentative"/"Harmful", compute precision, recall, F1. Expect: Low recall for harmful class.
2. **Replicate Scoring Baseline**: Score all HED essays with the four AES models and each LLM using the rubric-only instruction. Report average scores for Argumentative vs. Harmful subsets. Expect: AES models show higher harmful scores; LLMs show smaller gaps.
3. **Test Ethical Guideline Integration**: For each LLM, rerun scoring with the "Rescore if..." harmful content clause. Compute delta in average harmful essay score and new QWK on the IELTS test set. Expect: Significant harmful score drop and QWK maintenance/improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do larger language models (>13B parameters) perform on HED?** The study only tested models under 10B due to resource limits; performance on larger models is unknown.
2. **How can gold standard scores for harmful essays be reliably annotated?** The HED benchmark lacks such gold scores due to annotation challenges; developing robust guidelines and multi-rater agreement is needed.
3. **What is the alignment between AES/LLM scores and human assessors for harmful essays?** No human scores for harmful essays exist; collecting them would allow evaluation of model alignment.
4. **Why do persona instructions affect different LLMs' classification performance variably?** Models show divergent reactions to persona changes; probing training data demographics or fine-tuning on balanced data could explain and mitigate.

## Limitations
- HED benchmark relies on LLM-generated harmful essays, which may not fully capture the nuance of human-written hate speech.
- The dataset is small (290 essays) and covers only 50 IELTS prompts on socially sensitive topics, limiting statistical power and generalizability to other essay genres or contexts.
- The study only tested models up to 10B parameters due to resource constraints; larger models might behave differently.
- Ethical guidelines consist of a simple "Rescore if..." clause and may not address complex or implicit harmful content.
- There are no human scores for harmful essays, preventing direct evaluation of model alignment on ethical aspects.

## Confidence
The findings are based on a carefully constructed benchmark and consistent results across multiple models, providing moderate confidence in the conclusions. The clear effect of ethical guidelines in reducing harmful essay scores while maintaining alignment with human IELTS scores suggests the approach is viable. However, confidence is limited by the small scale and artificial nature of the HED benchmark, the absence of human scores for harmful essays, and the focus on models with ≤10B parameters. Replication with larger, human-validated datasets would be needed to increase confidence in generalizability.

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate larger language models (>13B parameters) on the HED benchmark to assess whether scale improves harmful content detection and ethical scoring.
2. Obtain human annotations for harmful essays to create a gold standard and directly measure model alignment with human ethical judgments.
3. Test the ethical guideline integration on diverse essay genres (e.g., narrative, source-based) and a broader range of prompts to check generalizability.
4. Investigate the impact of persona variations more systematically to understand and reduce performance instability.
5. Explore advanced methods for ethical integration, such as chain-of-thought reasoning or separate ethical classifiers, beyond simple rescoring clauses.
