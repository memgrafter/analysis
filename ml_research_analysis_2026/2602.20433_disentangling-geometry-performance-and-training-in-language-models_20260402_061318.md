# Disentangling Geometry, Performance, and Training in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20433  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20433  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The effective rank of a language model's unembedding matrix often correlates with downstream performance, but this relationship is neither necessary nor sufficient; training hyperparameters strongly shape rank and confound any causal link.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether geometric properties of a language model's unembedding matrix—particularly its effective rank—serve as reliable predictors of downstream performance. The authors train 108 OLMo-style models with systematic variations in size, token budget, and key hyperparameters (batch size, weight decay, learning rate). They find that higher effective rank generally coincides with lower losses on in-distribution, out-of-distribution, and post-training quantization benchmarks. However, numerous counterexamples demonstrate that effective rank is neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for good performance. Moreover, the same hyperparameters that preserve high rank (e.g., large batch sizes, strong weight decay) also improve loss reduction, confounding a direct causal interpretation. Additional geometric metrics (cosine similarity, isotropy) and the geometry of final-layer representations fail to provide consistent supplemental predictive power. The study also revisits the "saturation" phenomenon in small models, showing that low effective rank co-occurs with but does not cause late-stage degradation. Overall, unembedding geometry appears to reflect training dynamics more than inherent model capability, cautioning against its use as a standalone performance proxy.

## Method Summary
The experimental framework builds upon the OLMo codebase. A total of 108 decoder-only models are pre-trained with identical architecture (6 layers, 4 attention heads, SwiGLU activation, sequence length 2048, vocabulary size 50304) on the Pile dataset. Model configurations vary across four axes: parameter count (4M–75M), token budget (8B–128B), batch size (32–512), weight decay (0.01–0.1), and learning‑rate scale (± factors). After pre‑training, each checkpoint is evaluated on in‑distribution loss (Pile‑10k), out‑of‑distribution loss (Dolma‑100 Code), fine‑tuning loss on StarCoder‑Python and OpenWebMath (fixed schedule: batch size 32, learning rate 10 % smaller than pre‑training), and post‑training quantization loss using GPTQ. Geometric properties of the unembedding matrix and of final‑token last‑layer representations—effective rank, cosine similarity, isotropy—are computed and correlated with performance metrics via Spearman and partial correlations. This design isolates the effects of hyperparameters on geometry and performance.

## Key Results
- Effective rank of the unembedding matrix often correlates with better performance across in‑distribution, out‑of‑distribution, and quantized settings, yet the correlation is neither necessary nor sufficient; many low‑rank models perform well and high‑rank models fail. Low effective rank also co‑occurs with saturation (late‑stage degradation) in small models but does not cause it.
- Training hyperparameters—especially large batch sizes and strong weight decay—exert a dominant influence on effective rank, and these same hyperparameters improve performance, creating a confounding factor that precludes a simple causal link between rank and capability.
- Complementary geometric metrics (cosine similarity, isotropy) and the geometry of final‑layer representations do not yield a consistent additional signal for predicting downstream task performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed correlation arises because effective rank and performance share common determinants in the training configuration. Hyperparameters such as large batch sizes and strong weight decay help preserve the full numerical rank of the unembedding matrix during training, likely by preventing collapse or excessive smoothing of the embedding space. These same hyperparameters also tend to improve optimization and generalization, leading to lower losses. Consequently, effective rank acts as a collateral indicator of training dynamics rather than an intrinsic driver of performance; altering rank directly (e.g., via matrix approximations) may not replicate the benefits associated with high‑rank models because the causal pathway runs through the hyperparameters, not through rank itself.

## Foundational Learning
**Effective rank:** Normalized sum of squared singular values, measuring the effective dimensionality of a matrix. *Why needed:* Captures how many directions in the embedding/unembedding space are meaningfully used. *Quick check:* If near the theoretical maximum, the matrix is close to full‑rank and isotropic; near zero indicates severe collapse.

**Isotropy:** Degree of uniform distribution of vectors across all directions. *Why needed:* An isotropic representation avoids directional biases that may harm generalization. *Quick check:* Compute distribution of pairwise cosine similarities; low variance around zero indicates high isotropy.

**Cosine similarity distribution:** Measures angular relationships between representation vectors. *Why needed:* Clustering of vectors along a few directions suggests under‑utilization of capacity. *Quick check:* A distribution peaked near ±1 indicates anisotropy; a broad distribution centered at zero is desirable.

**Unembedding matrix:** Linear layer mapping hidden states to vocabulary logits. *Why needed:* Its geometry directly shapes the output distribution and is easily measurable. *Quick check:* Effective rank can be computed without dataset sampling and should be stable across checkpoints if training is stable.

**Language model pre‑training:** Training a transformer decoder on large text corpora to predict the next token. *Why needed:* Provides the foundation for all downstream evaluations. *Quick check:* Training loss should decrease steadily and reach values typical for the model scale (e.g., ~2.0 for 75M‑parameter models on the Pile).

**Quantization (e.g., GPTQ):** Post‑training weight quantization to reduce memory and computational requirements. *Why needed:* Tests robustness of model performance under compression; the paper finds that effective rank influences quantization error. *Quick check:* Quantize a checkpoint and measure perplexity increase relative to full precision; smaller increases correlate with higher effective rank.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Token IDs -> Embedding Lookup -> Transformer Blocks (6 × [LayerNorm, Attention (4 heads), SwiGLU MLP, Residual]) -> Final LayerNorm -> Unembedding Linear -> Logits  

**Critical path:** The forward pass flows from token embeddings through six identical transformer blocks with residual connections. The last hidden state undergoes layer normalization and is multiplied by the unembedding matrix to produce logits.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Depth vs. width:* With only six layers and four heads, the model is shallow but relatively wide, favoring expressive attention within a modest parameter budget.  
- *SwiGLU activation:* Introduces gating for smoother optimization at the cost of additional parameters.  
- *Sequence length 2048:* Enables long context but increases memory consumption, limiting feasible batch size.  
- *Vocabulary size 50304:* A power‑of‑two choice for efficient matrix multiplication; a larger vocab expands the unembedding matrix and may affect geometry.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Divergence:* NaNs or sharp loss spikes during pre‑training often stem from too high learning rate or unstable initialization.  
- *Rank collapse:* Effective rank near zero suggests over‑regularization (excessive weight decay) or extremely small batch sizes.  
- *Saturation:* Performance degrades after a certain token budget, sometimes accompanied by a sudden drop in effective rank.

**Three first experiments:**  
1. Pre‑train a base model (e.g., 4M parameters, 8B tokens, batch size 32, weight decay 0.01) following the OLMo recipe; verify that final validation loss falls within the expected range (~5.0 for ID).  
2. Compute the effective rank of the unembedding matrix for this checkpoint; confirm it lies between 0.8 and 0.95.  
3. Increase the batch size to 512 while keeping other hyperparameters fixed and observe the change in effective rank and final loss; expect both rank to remain high and loss to decrease, reproducing the reported batch‑size effect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions, but the findings and limitations point to several natural directions for future work:

- **Causal isolation:** Can the effect of effective rank on performance be disentangled from the hyperparameters that influence both? Controlled experiments where rank is manipulated independently (e.g., via low‑rank constraints) while holding other factors constant would clarify causality.
- **Generality across scales and tasks:** The study uses OLMo-style models up to 75M parameters on the Pile. Do the observed relationships hold for larger models, different architectures, or other downstream benchmarks?
- **Representation geometry beyond unembedding:** The analysis focuses on the unembedding matrix; investigating the geometry of intermediate layers or the aggregate transformer function might reveal additional predictive structure.
- **Mechanism of rank preservation:** Why do large batch sizes and strong weight decay maintain high effective rank? Understanding the optimization dynamics could inform better training practices.
- **Quantitative characterization of counterexamples:** The claim that rank is neither necessary nor sufficient would be strengthened by reporting the proportion of models that defy the trend under various conditions.

These questions stem directly from the paper’s discussion and the limitations highlighted in the report.

## Limitations
- The exact hyperparameter grid for all 108 models is not fully enumerated, making full replication difficult and requiring inference from described ranges.
- The claim that rank‑performance correlation is "neither necessary nor sufficient" lacks quantitative characterization of how frequently counterexamples occur across tasks, leaving the strength of the conclusion open.
- Causal confounders (hyperparameters) are identified but not isolated; the paper does not test whether effective rank alone mediates performance when hyperparameters are held constant.
- The analysis of geometric metrics is limited to a selected set; other representation‑based measures might still offer predictive power.

## Confidence
- Effective rank often correlates with performance: **Medium**  
- Hyperparameters strongly shape effective rank: **High**  
- Other geometric metrics add no consistent predictive power: **Medium**
