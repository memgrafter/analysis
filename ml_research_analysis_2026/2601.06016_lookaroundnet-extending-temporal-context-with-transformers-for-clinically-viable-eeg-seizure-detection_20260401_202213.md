# LookAroundNet: Extending Temporal Context with Transformers for Clinically Viable EEG Seizure Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06016  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06016  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LookAroundNet achieves event‑based F1‑scores of 77.8% (TUSZ), 68.5% (Siena), 47.0% (SeizeIT1), and 31.2% (Kvikna) with ensembling, while maintaining low false positives per day (as low as 0.4) and running in under 6 seconds per hour of EEG on an RTX 5000 GPU.

## Executive Summary
Automated seizure detection from EEG is challenged by high variability across patients, settings, and recording conditions. LookAroundNet introduces a transformer‑based architecture that processes a central target segment together with preceding and following context windows, enabling modeling of long‑range dependencies similar to clinical review practices. Trained on a large, diverse mix of public and proprietary datasets (including home ambulatory recordings), the model achieves strong detection performance across multiple test sets while being computationally efficient for real‑time deployment. Key results show that a single context configuration (−32,32 seconds) yields competitive F1‑scores, and ensembling models with different context alignments further improves results, notably increasing precision and reducing false positives.

## Method Summary
LookAroundNet processes multi‑channel EEG (preprocessed to 18 bipolar channels at 128 Hz) by sampling a window comprising a central 16‑second target segment flanked by look‑behind and look‑ahead context (e.g., 32 s each). The window is split into 1‑second patches; each patch is transformed by two 1D convolutional layers to extract local features. The resulting patch sequences are fed into separate per‑channel transformer encoders (3 layers) that model temporal dependencies across the entire window. After mean pooling over patches per channel, a cross‑channel multi‑head attention module integrates spatial patterns across electrodes. A final classifier outputs a seizure probability for the target segment. The model is trained on a combined dataset of TUSZ clinical recordings and the proprietary Kvikna ambulatory corpus, using AdamW with label smoothing and a fixed decision threshold of 0.85. Inference uses a 2‑second stride and averages overlapping predictions.

## Key Results
- Single LookAroundNet (−32,32) event‑based F1: TUSZ 72.1%, Siena 54.9%, SeizeIT1 26.4%, Kvikna 21.8%; FP/day: 12.7, 3.2, 1.7, 6.5 respectively.  
- Ensemble of three context‑aligned models boosts F1: TUSZ 77.8%, Siena 68.5%, SeizeIT1 47.0%, Kvikna 31.2%; FP/day: 3.4, 0.8, 0.4, 2.5.  
- Computational cost: ~2 GFLOPs per second of EEG; processes 1 hour of data in <6 seconds on an NVIDIA RTX 5000 GPU, enabling real‑time use.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Incorporating Surrounding Temporal Context
The model’s three‑part input (target, look‑behind, look‑ahead) allows it to observe gradual changes or post‑ictal suppression that extend beyond the target segment. Channel‑wise transformers capture long‑range temporal dependencies within each EEG channel, while cross‑channel attention integrates spatial patterns. This mirrors clinical practice, where interpreters use context before and after a suspicious segment. Evidence includes improved performance when using balanced past/future context compared to past‑only or future‑only windows, and the benefit of ensembling models with different context alignments. A limitation is that seizures shorter than half the target length (<8 s) may be missed because they occupy less than 50% of the 16‑second target.

### Mechanism 2: Training on Diverse Datasets
Combining TUSZ (clinical) and Kvikna (ambulatory) exposes the model to varied artifact profiles, seizure prevalence, and recording hardware, encouraging learning of invariant features. Experiments show that including Kvikna in training improves detection trade‑offs across all test sets, including those not seen during training (Siena, SeizeIT1). This suggests that diversity mitigates overfitting to dataset‑specific idiosyncrasies. Potential failure occurs if datasets have incompatible labeling definitions, which could introduce label noise.

### Mechanism 3: Ensembling Context‑Aligned Models
Individual models trained with different context alignments (past‑focused, centered, future‑focused) develop distinct decision boundaries sensitive to different temporal cues. Averaging their predictions reduces variance and shifts the precision‑recall curve toward higher precision at similar sensitivity, outperforming a single model with a wider window. The ensemble’s gain is particularly notable in lowering false positives. If the optimal temporal signature were monolithic, ensembling misaligned contexts could dilute the signal, but empirical results favor diversity.

## Foundational Learning

- **Transformer Self‑Attention for Sequence Modeling**  
  *Why needed:* LookAroundNet’s temporal transformer uses self‑attention to relate any time point within the long input window, enabling direct integration of distant pre‑ictal or post‑ictal patterns.  
  *Quick check:* How does self‑attention allow a patch in the target segment to incorporate information from a subtle onset 20 s before the target?

- **EEG Pre‑processing Pipelines (Montage, Filtering, Resampling)**  
  *Why needed:* Standardizing heterogeneous raw EEG into a consistent format (longitudinal bipolar montage, FIR filtering 0.5–64 Hz + notch, resampling to 128 Hz) is essential for model generalization.  
  *Quick check:* Why is a 50/60 Hz notch filter applied before training, and what artifact does it remove?

- **Clinically Relevant Evaluation Metrics (SzCORE)**  
  *Why needed:* Event‑based metrics (F1, sensitivity, precision, FP/day) reflect clinical utility better than sample‑based accuracy, which is inflated by class imbalance.  
  *Quick check:* In a 24‑h recording with one 30‑s seizure, if a model predicts 10 non‑overlapping 15‑s events, what are sensitivity and FP/day? What does this imply for clinical use?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Input -> Patching & Projection -> Patch‑level Feature Extraction -> Channel‑Wise Temporal Transformer -> Channel‑Wise Patch Pooling -> Cross‑Channel Spatial Transformer -> Classifier.

**Critical path:** The data flows sequentially through the above stages. The look‑around context is incorporated at the input stage, and the channel‑wise temporal transformer is the primary mechanism for modeling long‑range dependencies.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Wider context improves performance but increases latency; past‑only context enables real‑time use but may sacrifice some accuracy.  
- The model’s low parameter count (0.5M) is viable only because of large training data; smaller datasets would underfit.  
- Ensembling boosts metrics but triples computational cost, making the single (−32,32) model a practical compromise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Elevated false positives on ambulatory data likely correlate with motion artifacts or electrode instability.  
- Seizures shorter than 8 s are systematically missed due to the fixed 16‑second target window.  
- Disparities between datasets (e.g., better on TUSZ than Kvikna) indicate overfitting to lower‑artifact clinical data.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate the context alignment study: train the single model with windows (−64,0), (−32,32), (0,64) and evaluate on TUSZ and Kvikna to confirm the relative utility of past versus future context.  
2. Ablate the cross‑channel attention: replace it with mean pooling of channel vectors, train and evaluate to quantify the contribution of explicit spatial modeling.  
3. Test sensitivity to short seizures: curate a test set of seizures <8 s from TUSZ/Kvikna and evaluate the ensemble to measure the expected miss rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **How can artifact‑induced false positives in ambulatory EEG be systematically reduced without compromising sensitivity?** Artifacts from home recordings cause excessive false alarms; integrating state‑of‑the‑art artifact detection/rejection into preprocessing could help but needs validation.  
2. **Can the fixed 16‑second target segment be modified to detect seizures shorter than 8 seconds?** The current architecture misses ~15% of TUSZ seizures due to the >50% occupancy requirement; alternative labeling strategies or variable‑duration windows may be needed.  
3. **What factors cause large performance disparities across datasets, and can standardized training or domain adaptation mitigate this?** Differences in recording quality, labeling practices, and patient demographics hinder generalization; identifying root causes and applying harmonization or adaptation techniques remains open.  
4. **Do the attention mechanisms focus on clinically interpretable EEG patterns, and can this inform model simplification or increased trust?** Understanding attention weights could reveal whether the model learns meaningful temporal dependencies or artifacts, aiding acceptance and failure analysis.

## Limitations

- Implementation details for montage creation and SzCORE scoring tolerance are not fully specified, risking reproduction drift.  
- The fixed 16‑second target inherently limits detection of seizures shorter than 8 seconds.  
- The reason why ensembling context‑aligned models outperforms a single wider‑context window is not deeply analyzed.
