# DGPO: RL-Steered Graph Diffusion for Neural Architecture Generation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19261  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19261  
- Reference count: 32  
- Primary result: DGPO matches NAS-Bench-201 optimum on CIFAR-10 (91.61%), CIFAR-100 (73.49%), ImageNet-16-120 (46.77%) and discovers architectures 7.3 percentage points beyond the training ceiling.

## Executive Summary
DGPO introduces a reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning approach for discrete graph diffusion models to perform neural architecture search (NAS) over directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). The method adapts the DiGress diffusion backbone by incorporating topological node ordering and positional encodings, enabling generation of valid neural network architectures. Pretrained on a small fraction (7%) of a benchmark search space, RL fine-tuning steers the model toward high-accuracy architectures, achieving benchmark optimums on NAS-Bench-201 and demonstrating strong compositional generalization by extrapolating beyond the training distribution. The inverse optimization experiment confirms that the steering is reward-driven rather than an artifact of the prior.

## Method Summary
DGPO employs a two-phase training pipeline. First, a discrete diffusion model (inspired by DiGress) is trained on the full set of architectures from a NAS benchmark (e.g., all 15,625 cells of NAS-Bench-201) using the standard denoising cross-entropy loss. The model processes graphs by applying a topological sort to nodes and adding positional encodings based on the sorted order, which respects the DAG structure. The diffusion forward process uses a cosine noise schedule over T=800 steps, gradually corrupting node types and edge existence. After pretraining, the model checkpoint is fine-tuned with the DGPO objective: for each batch, K=15 full denoising trajectories are sampled, and the final architectures are queried for validation accuracy (reward). The loss is a reward-weighted sum of node and edge cross-entropies along the trajectories, where the advantage is normalized by a running mean and standard deviation. Key hyperparameters include a Transformer with 8 layers, AdamW optimizer, learning rates around 5e-7 (for NB201), freezing 75% of parameters, and training for 60 epochs. This RL fine-tuning shifts the generative distribution toward high-reward regions while preserving the structural prior through partial freezing.

## Key Results
- Matched NAS-Bench-201 optimum across all three datasets: CIFAR-10 (91.61%), CIFAR-100 (73.49%), ImageNet-16-120 (46.77%).  
- Pretrained on only 7% of the search space, fine-tuned model generates near-oracle architectures within 0.32 percentage points of a model trained on the full data and discovers architectures 7.3 percentage points above the training ceiling.  
- Inverse optimization (reward minimization) converges to near random-chance accuracy (∼9.5%), validating genuine reward-driven distribution steering.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DGPO succeeds by combining a strong structural prior (learned via discrete diffusion) with targeted RL fine-tuning. The topological ordering and positional encoding ensure the diffusion process respects DAG constraints, allowing the model to learn valid graph distributions. Pretraining on the full benchmark (or a representative subset) teaches the model sensible node operations and connectivity patterns. The RL fine-tuning stage uses lightweight reward-weighted updates on a small subset of parameters (frozen layers prevent catastrophic forgetting) to shift probability mass towards high-accuracy architectures. The discrete diffusion framework provides dense learning signal across denoising steps and enables exploration of the search space through stochastic sampling. Moreover, the compositional nature of the diffusion prior allows recombination of learned primitives into out-of-distribution architectures that exceed the performance of any training example, evidencing genuine generalization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Discrete Diffusion Models**:  
  *Why needed*: To model probability distributions over discrete structures (graphs) and iteratively denoise from noise to a valid architecture.  
  *Quick check*: Train on a small set of graphs, then evaluate reconstruction accuracy (exact match) when denoising randomly corrupted graphs.  
- **Graph Transformers**:  
  *Why needed*: To capture complex relationships between nodes and edges in variable-sized DAGs through self-attention.  
  *Quick check*: Visualize attention patterns to confirm they reflect connectivity or influence of node types.  
- **Topological Sorting for DAGs**:  
  *Why needed*: To impose a consistent node ordering that respects dependencies, enabling the diffusion process to operate on a strict upper-triangular adjacency matrix.  
  *Quick check*: Apply Kahn’s algorithm to a sample DAG and verify the output order respects all directed edges.  
- **Positional Encodings**:  
  *Why needed*: To inject information about node ordering into the Transformer, compensating for the lack of inherent positional bias in graph data.  
  *Quick check*: Compare model performance with and without positional encodings on a validation split.  
- **Reinforcement Learning for Generative Models**:  
  *Why needed*: To steer the pretrained generative distribution toward external rewards (e.g., validation accuracy) without requiring ground-truth trajectories.  
  *Quick check*: Implement a simple REINFORCE baseline on a toy task and verify reward-correlated improvement.  
- **Neural Architecture Search (NAS) Benchmarks**:  
  *Why needed*: To provide a standardized, queryable set of architectures with known performance for evaluating NAS algorithms.  
  *Quick check*: Query a few architectures from NAS-Bench-201 API and confirm reported accuracies match the published values.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Pretraining Data → DiGress Diffusion Model (Transformer with node/edge embeddings) → Topological Sort + Positional Encoding → Sampler → RL Evaluator (NAS-Bench API for reward) → DGPO Loss (reward-weighted cross-entropy) → Parameter Update (with layer freezing).  
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Encode each benchmark architecture as a DAG; compute a topological ordering and embed nodes with positional encodings.  
  2. Train the diffusion model to denoise corrupted graphs (node types and edges) using standard loss until reconstruction is accurate.  
  3. Initialize RL fine-tuning from the pretrained checkpoint; freeze 75% of Transformer layers to preserve the prior.  
  4. For each batch: sample K full denoising trajectories, query the benchmark API for final architecture accuracies, compute normalized advantages, and update model parameters with the DGPO loss.  
  5. Repeat fine-tuning for 60 epochs, monitoring reward statistics.  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Layer freezing* vs. plasticity: freezing most parameters prevents forgetting but limits adaptation; the 75% split was chosen empirically.  
  - *Batch size K*: Larger K yields lower variance advantage estimates but increases query cost; K=15 balances efficiency and stability.  
  - *Learning rate*: Must be small to avoid overwriting the pretrained prior; 5e-7 worked for NB201 but may need adjustment for other spaces.  
  - *Topological ordering*: A deterministic order (e.g., Kahn’s with fixed tie-breaking) stabilizes training but may restrict expressivity; random tie-breaking could increase diversity at the cost of consistency.  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - High rate of invalid DAGs in generated samples (upper-triangular projection fails).  
  - No upward trend in average/max reward during RL fine-tuning (mode collapse or insufficient gradient signal).  
  - Sudden increase in reconstruction error on clean graphs (catastrophic forgetting).  
  - Inverse optimization getting stuck in a secondary basin (~59% accuracy on NB201) instead of converging to random chance.  
- **Exactly 3 first experiments**:  
  1. Pretrain the diffusion model on the full NAS-Bench-201 training set; evaluate reconstruction accuracy and the percentage of generated graphs that are valid DAGs.  
  2. Perform RL fine-tuning with K=15 for 60 epochs; plot the distribution of validation accuracies for 300 samples before and after fine-tuning, and record the maximum accuracy achieved.  
  3. Run the inverse optimization (reward minimization) procedure; verify that the model’s generated architectures converge to an average accuracy near random chance (∼9.5%) and that no high-reward modes remain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**:  
  - *Question*: Can DGPO's query efficiency be significantly improved via offline RL or sample reuse without sacrificing the quality of generated architectures?  
  - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] The conclusion states the approach "incurs higher query cost than conditioning-based methods" and suggests "reducing the query budget through offline RL or sample reuse strategies" as a future direction. The current online RL paradigm requires ∼2k queries versus 150–192 for other methods.  
  - *Why unresolved*: The paper does not implement or evaluate any offline RL or sample efficiency modifications, leaving the trade-off between query cost and generation quality unquantified for this framework.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Implement an offline RL variant (e.g., using a replay buffer of past trajectories) or a sample reuse scheme (e.g., training on accumulated trajectories over multiple fine-tuning runs). Compare the maximum validation accuracy achieved and the number of benchmark queries required against the current online DGPO setup across multiple seeds.  

- **Open Question 2**:  
  - *Question*: Does the RL-steered diffusion framework scale to substantially larger, open-ended NAS search spaces (e.g., NAS-Bench-301) while maintaining viable generation and steering capabilities?  
  - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] The conclusion explicitly lists "scaling to open-ended search spaces such as NAS-Bench-301" as a promising direction. The current validation is limited to NB101 (423k architectures) and NB201 (15.6k architectures).  
  - *Why unresolved*: The combinatorial explosion and increased structural diversity in larger search spaces may break the assumptions of the topological ordering and positional encoding mechanism or destabilize RL fine-tuning. No experiments probe this scalability limit.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Apply DGPO to NAS-Bench-301 or a similarly large, continuous cell-based search space. Report success rates in generating valid DAGs, the maximum achieved accuracy relative to the space's known optimum, and training stability metrics (e.g., reward variance) compared to results on NB201.  

- **Open Question 3**:  
  - *Question*: What is the mechanistic basis for the model's ability to recombine structural priors from weak-region pretraining to generate out-of-distribution (OOD), high-performing architectures? Is it primarily local motif recombination, global graph assembly, or another process?  
  - *Basis in paper*: [inferred] The paper demonstrates dramatic OOD discovery (e.g., +7.27pp above training ceiling on NB101) and attributes it to "compositional generalization" and "recombining learned primitives." However, no ablation or analysis isolates the contributing factors (e.g., which topological patterns or node-operation combinations are most frequently recombined).  
  - *Why unresolved*: The claim of compositional generalization is supported by the result but not by an analysis of the generated graphs' structural properties. It remains unclear *how* the priors transfer—whether via specific subnet motifs, connectivity schemas, or emergent global patterns.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Conduct a fine-grained graph grammar analysis: (1) Extract common subgraphs or node-operation sequences from the filtered pretraining set versus the final RL-fine-tuned OOD samples. (2) Measure the frequency and performance contribution of these primitives in the OOD set. (3) Perform controlled interventions (e.g., freezing certain motif probabilities) to test their causal role in OOD generation.  

- **Open Question 4**:  
  - *Question*: Why does the inverse optimization (reward minimization) sometimes exhibit a secondary performance basin (~59% on NB201) before converging to random chance, and what does this reveal about the landscape of low-accuracy architectures?  
  - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] Section IV-D notes: "On NB201, one of three seeds temporarily lingers near a secondary basin (∼59%), while others reach the floor, suggesting bimodal structure in the inverse optimization landscape." This phenomenon is observed but not explained.  
  - *Why unresolved*: The paper does not investigate whether this bimodality is an artifact of the reward function, the diffusion process, or inherent property of the search space's low-accuracy region. Understanding this could inform reward design and the robustness of bidirectional steering.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: (1) Map the inverse optimization trajectories in more detail: track architecture statistics (e.g., size, density, operation types) as the model descends into the secondary basin versus the final random-chance basin. (2) Analyze the NB201 validation accuracy distribution: does a cluster of systematically worse-but-not-random architectures exist? (3) Test if the secondary basin appears consistently with different random seeds or under slight perturbations to the reward signal.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter details (topological ordering implementation, positional encoding scheme, Q transition matrices, Transformer architecture, exact layer-freezing split) are not fully disclosed, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- The RL fine-tuning is query-intensive (~2,000 benchmark queries) compared to conditioning-based methods (150–192), raising efficiency concerns.  
- Evaluation is confined to small NAS benchmarks (NAS-Bench-101/201); scalability to larger, open-ended search spaces (e.g., NAS-Bench-301) remains unproven.

## Confidence
- Performance claims (matching NB201 optimum, extrapolation beyond training ceiling): Medium  
- Sample-efficiency claim (pretraining on 7% of space yields near-oracle fine-tuning): Medium  
- Reward-steering diagnostic (inverse optimization near random chance): High  
- DAG generation method (topological ordering + positional encoding enabling diffusion): Low
