# oculomix: Hierarchical Sampling for Retinal-Based Systemic Disease Prediction

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.19939
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19939
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** A hierarchical sampling strategy for mixed sample data augmentation improves 5-year MACE prediction from retinal images, achieving up to 3.0% higher AUROC and 4.4% higher AUPRC internally, and a 12% improvement in C-index on an external dataset compared to image-level mixing.

## Executive Summary
Standard mixed sample data augmentations (MSDA) like CutMix and MixUp combine images and their labels randomly, which can distort patient-specific clinical factors (e.g., age, comorbidities) crucial for predicting systemic disease from retinal scans. Oculomix addresses this by constraining mixing to two clinically valid hierarchical levels: **exam-level** (mixing images from the same patient at the same visit) and **patient-level** (mixing images from the same patient across different visits). For exam-level mixes, it uses the standard mixed label. For patient-level mixes, it replaces the ambiguous mixed label with a pairwise ranking loss that enforces the expected temporal progression of morbidity. This approach preserves critical patient attributes while leveraging longitudinal data, leading to significant performance gains on a 5-year major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) prediction task.

## Method Summary
Oculomix modifies the data sampling pipeline for MSDA. Instead of selecting any two random images (image-level), a hierarchical sampler selects pairs based on patient and exam metadata. Pairs are either from the same exam (exam-level) or from different exams of the same patient (patient-level). Exam-level pairs use the standard CutMix/MixUp procedure with a linearly mixed label and a standard cross-entropy loss. Patient-level pairs are also mixed, but the model receives relative supervision: a ranking loss encourages the prediction for the later exam to be higher (worse prognosis) than the prediction for an earlier same-exam mix from the same patient. The total training objective combines the supervised loss from exam-level mixes and the ranking loss from patient-level mixes.

## Key Results
*   On the Alzeye dataset, Oculomix (Patient+Exam-level with CutMix+MixUp) improved AUROC by up to 3.0% and AUPRC by 4.4% compared to image-level sampling for 5-year MACE prediction.
*   External validation on the HYU MACE dataset showed a 12% improvement in C-index.
*   Ablation studies confirmed that using a ranking loss for patient-level mixing significantly outperforms attempting to create a direct mixed label across exams.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Exam-Level Constraint Preserves Clinical Snapshot
*   **Claim:** Constraining mixing to images from the same exam (same patient, same time point) preserves shared clinical attributes (e.g., age, sex, acute disease state) perturbed by image-level mixing.
*   **Mechanism:** Images from a single visit share a common clinical snapshot. Blending them and their labels maintains the integrity of patient-specific factors present at that time.
*   **Core Assumption:** Clinical factors are stable within a single exam and critical for systemic prediction.
*   **Break Condition:** Rapid intra-exam clinical changes (e.g., acute events) could make this constraint discard useful signal.

### Mechanism 2: Ranking Loss Provides Valid Temporal Supervision
*   **Claim:** A pairwise ranking loss for patient-level mixing (same patient, different times) provides valid supervision without an ambiguous combined label.
*   **Mechanism:** For a pair of exams (t1 < t2), the model predicts on a mixed image from t2 and a same-exam mix from t1. The loss enforces `pred_t2 > pred_t1 - margin`, leveraging the monotonic increase of morbidity.
*   **Core Assumption:** Systemic morbidity generally increases monotonically over time for a patient.
*   **Break Condition:** Non-monotonic disease courses (e.g., due to effective treatment) would violate the assumption and mislead learning.

### Mechanism 3: Hierarchical Combination Balances Signal and Diversity
*   **Claim:** Combining exam-level (clean signal) and patient-level (increased diversity) mixing maximizes MSDA benefit by reducing overfitting while capturing longitudinal trends.
*   **Mechanism:** Exam-level mixing provides constrained supervision; patient-level mixing expands the hypothesis space with valid novel combinations. The combined objective trains robustness to within-exam variation and learning of progression.
*   **Core Assumption:** Patient identity is a dominant predictive factor; temporal progression is a secondary informative signal.
*   **Break Condition:** Excessive variance from mixing across vastly different disease stages or treatment eras could harm performance.

## Foundational Learning

*   **Concept: Vision Transformer (ViT) Training Dynamics**
    *   **Why needed:** Oculomix modifies augmentation for ViTs. ViTs lack convolutional inductive biases and rely heavily on strong augmentations like CutMix/MixUp for generalization. Image-level MSDA is thus standard but can be problematic for patient-level tasks.
    *   **Quick check:** Why might standard image-level CutMix be particularly problematic for a Vision Transformer training on retinal images for systemic disease prediction?

*   **Concept: Mixed Sample Data Augmentation (MSDA) Mechanics**
    *   **Why needed:** Oculomix is a direct modification of CutMix/MixUp. Understanding how these operations create soft labels (`y_mix = λ*y_a + (1-λ)*y_b`) and blend pixel regions is essential to see why cross-patient/time mixing creates clinically invalid labels.
    *   **Quick check:** In standard MixUp, if you mix an image from a 60-year-old patient with one from an 80-year-old, what happens to the interpretability of the resulting "soft" age-related feature representation?

*   **Concept: Hierarchical Structure of Medical Imaging Datasets**
    *   **Why needed:** Oculomix's innovation is operating at the *exam* and *patient* levels, not just *image*. Engineers must model data with three tiers: patient (identity/static factors) -> exam (time-specific snapshot) -> image (specific view).
    *   **Quick check:** Given a dataset with multiple retinal images per patient visit (e.g., left/right eye, macula/disc), which sampling level should you restrict mixing to if you want to preserve a patient's age and sex without dilution?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**
Data Loader (outputs image pairs, labels, exam times, patient IDs) -> Hierarchical Sampler (selects exam-level or patient-level pairs) -> MSDA Engine (applies CutMix/MixUp) -> Model Forward Pass (ViT on mixed image + optionally raw images) -> Loss Calculator (computes L_sup for exam-level mixes and L_ranking for patient-level mixes) -> Total Loss (L = L_sup + L_ranking)

**Critical Path:**
1.  **Data Preparation:** Link all images to patient ID and exam timestamp. Validate that images within the same exam share identical row-level labels.
2.  **Sampler Implementation:** Implement `__getitem__` to return pairs that satisfy hierarchical rules (same patient; for exam-level, also same exam time). This is the highest-risk component.
3.  **Loss Integration:** Modify the training loop to compute `L_sup` and `L_ranking` conditionally based on the sampled pair type, requiring access to timestamps for ranking.
4.  **Validation Check:** In the first epoch, log the batch ratio of exam-level to patient-level pairs to confirm sampler behavior matches design.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   *Exam-level constraint vs. Augmentation Diversity:* Strict exam-level mixing limits combination space, risking quick overfitting. Patient-level mixing increases space but introduces label ambiguity.
*   *Ranking Loss Margin (`m`):* Too small: no constraint. Too large: excessive constraint hinders learning. Paper uses `m=0.1`.
*   *MixUp vs. CutMix:* Paper finds combining CutMix+MixUp yields best results at Patient+Exam level, suggesting complementary regularization effects.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   **Performance collapse on external dataset (HYU MACE):** Suggests overfitting to Alzeye's data collection specifics.
*   **Patient+Exam performs worse than Exam-only:** Indicates ranking loss failure (e.g., incorrect time ordering, unstable gradients) or weak temporal trends in data.
*   **No improvement over Image-level:** Likely a buggy sampler (e.g., cross-patient mixing) or that patient identity factors are not predictive.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  **Sampler Sanity Check:** Run 100 data loader iterations. Assert 100% of pairs have matching `patient_id`. For exam-level pairs, assert matching `exam_timestamp`. Log distribution of time deltas for patient-level pairs.
2.  **Loss Function Isolation:** Train for 5 epochs with *only* `L_sup` (no ranking) on Patient+Exam sampling. Expect performance to drop to near Exam-only levels, confirming ranking loss necessity.
3.  **Ablation of Mixing Types:** Train three models: (a) Exam-level only, (b) Patient-level only (with ranking), (c) Patient+Exam. Compare validation AUROC. Expect (c) > (a) > (b) or (c) > (b) > (a), validating synergy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can mixed labels be properly defined for patient-level sampling in longitudinal oculomics data to reflect continuous disease progression? The paper states that defining a mixed label across exams is inherently ambiguous due to binarized labels vs. continuous progression, hence their use of a ranking loss. A resolution would involve experiments comparing alternative label mixing strategies (e.g., interpolation) against the ranking loss.
*   **Open Question 2:** To what extent does Oculomix's performance gain generalize to other systemic diseases (e.g., dementia, chronic kidney disease) and non-ViT architectures? Evaluation was restricted to 5-year MACE prediction using ViTs. Generalizability requires replication across diverse oculomics tasks and model types.
*   **Open Question 3:** Which patient-specific clinical factors (e.g., genetics, comorbidities, socioeconomic status) are most effectively preserved by hierarchical sampling, and how does this influence model interpretability? The paper asserts preservation but provides no analysis linking sampling to specific attributes or model transparency.
*   **Open Question 4:** What are the computational trade-offs (e.g., training time, memory overhead, convergence speed) of hierarchical sampling compared to standard image-level MSDA? The paper focuses on performance metrics, not resource usage, which is critical for practical deployment.

## Limitations
*   Dataset splits and exact exam definitions are not disclosed, hindering direct replication.
*   The ranking loss sampling protocol (e.g., frequency, minimum time gap) is unspecified.
*   The core monotonic morbidity assumption may not hold for patients with treatments or fluctuating conditions.
*   External validation is limited to a single dataset (HYU MACE), increasing risk of overfitting to the internal Alzeye cohort.

## Confidence
*   **Exam-level mixing preserves clinical attributes:** High (supported by ablation showing Exam-level > Image-level and clinical plausibility).
*   **Ranking loss is superior to direct label mixing for patient-level pairs:** Medium (Table 3 supports, but depends on monotonicity assumption).
*   **Hierarchical combination (Patient+Exam) yields best performance:** Medium-High (consistent gains across internal AUROC/AUPRC and external C-index).
*   **Generalization to other diseases/architectures:** Low (no evidence provided beyond the single task/model family).

## Next Checks
1.  **External Reproducibility:** Test Oculomix on at least two additional, independent retinal cohorts for systemic disease prediction, reporting AUROC/C-index vs. image-level MSDA baselines.
2.  **Monotonicity Stress Test:** Evaluate performance specifically on patient subsets with known non-monotonic disease courses (e.g., post-intervention improvement). Systematically vary the ranking loss margin `m` and track impact on validation AUROC.
3.  **Mixing Ratio Sweep:** Ablate the proportion of exam-level vs. patient-level pairs in a batch (e.g., 0:100, 25:75, 50:50, 75:25, 100:0) to determine the optimal balance for convergence and final performance.
