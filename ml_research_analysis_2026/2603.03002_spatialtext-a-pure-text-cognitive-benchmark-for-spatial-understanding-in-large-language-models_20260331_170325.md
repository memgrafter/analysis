# SpatialText: A Pure-Text Cognitive Benchmark for Spatial Understanding in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03002
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03002
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: LLMs exhibit severe deficits in egocentric spatial reasoning (<32% on mid-tier models), despite high fact retrieval (up to 91%), indicating reliance on linguistic heuristics rather than internal spatial mental models.

## Executive Summary
SpatialText is a theory-driven pure-text benchmark designed to evaluate whether large language models (LLMs) construct internal spatial mental models or rely on surface heuristics. It combines 100 human-annotated real indoor scenes (with egocentric, allocentric, and hybrid descriptions) and 80 code-generated logical scenes (2D/3D, omniscient/non‑omniscient), organized into five reasoning levels. Testing on 7B–14B models shows that basic fact retrieval reaches up to 91% accuracy, but egocentric perspective transformation tasks drop to as low as 32% for mid‑tier models, while the top‑tier DeepSeek‑v3.2 achieves 86%. In synthetic scenes, performance paradoxically declines under omniscient conditions, suggesting models depend on local relational cues rather than global spatial integration. Statistical failures—such as the “Bed‑North” heuristic and inability to recognize undecidable relations—confirm that current LLMs rely on linguistic co‑occurrence statistics rather than constructing verifiable spatial representations. The benchmark isolates spatial reasoning from perception, providing a rigorous test of spatial cognition in language models.

## Method Summary
SpatialText’s methodology comprises two complementary data sources. First, human annotators described 100 real indoor images from three perspectives: egocentric (first‑person), allocentric (world‑centered), and hybrid. Second, a code generator produced 80 logical spatial scenes varying in dimensionality (2D vs. 3D) and observer knowledge (omniscient vs. non‑omniscient). These scenes are mapped to five reasoning levels, ranging from simple fact retrieval to complex perspective transformations and inference of implicit relations. Models are evaluated by generating answers to automatically generated questions, and performance is measured by accuracy and error patterns. The pure‑text format eliminates visual input, forcing models to rely solely on linguistic description and internal representation.

## Key Results
- Fact retrieval accuracy reaches up to 91%, but egocentric perspective transformation drops to 32% for mid‑tier models; DeepSeek‑v3.2 attains 86% on this hardest level.
- In synthetic scenes, performance decreases under omniscient conditions compared to non‑omniscient, indicating reliance on local relational cues rather than global spatial integration.
- Models exhibit statistical heuristics (e.g., “Bed‑North”) and fail to recognize undecidable spatial relations, confirming absence of verifiable spatial mental models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SpatialText isolates spatial reasoning by removing visual input and requiring models to process purely linguistic descriptions of spatial layouts. The inclusion of multiple perspectives (egocentric, allocentric) tests the ability to mentally transform viewpoints—a hallmark of genuine spatial mental models. The synthetic scenes with omniscient vs. non‑omniscient conditions probe whether models construct global representations (omniscient should be easier if they have a coherent mental model) or fall back on local relational patterns (which may break down under omniscient demands). The benchmark’s design thus reveals that LLMs’ high performance on fact retrieval stems from surface-level co‑occurrence statistics, while failures on perspective tasks and the omniscient paradox reflect the absence of an integrated spatial representation. The statistical heuristics observed (e.g., “Bed‑North”) provide a mechanistic explanation: models latch onto frequent object‑relation pairs without constructing a verifiable layout.

## Foundational Learning
- **Spatial Mental Model**: An internal cognitive representation that maintains spatial relationships between objects, enabling reasoning beyond surface cues. *Why needed*: To distinguish true spatial understanding from pattern matching. *Quick check*: Can the model correctly infer the position of an object when viewed from a different perspective?
- **Egocentric vs. Allocentric Perspective**: Egocentric refers to a self‑centered viewpoint; allocentric is world‑centered and independent of the observer. *Why needed*: Perspective transformation tests the flexibility of spatial reasoning. *Quick check*: Does performance degrade when switching from allocentric to egocentric questions?
- **Heuristic Reasoning**: Relying on statistical shortcuts (e.g., “bed is often north of window”) instead of constructing a coherent model. *Why needed*: To interpret systematic error patterns. *Quick check*: Does reversing spatial relations in a scene cause catastrophic failure?
- **Pure‑Text Spatial Benchmark**: Evaluation using only textual descriptions, eliminating visual perception confounders. *Why needed*: Ensures measured ability is purely linguistic spatial reasoning. *Quick check*: Does accuracy correlate with description complexity, indicating parsing rather than reasoning?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Scene Data (real images) -> Human Annotators -> Text Descriptions (egocentric/allocentric/hybrid) -> Question Templates; Code Generator -> Logical Scenes (2D/3D, omniscient/non‑omniscient) -> Question Templates -> Model Inference -> Responses -> Accuracy Metrics.
- **Critical path**: Scene selection → Description generation → Question generation → Model inference → Scoring. The longest dependency chain runs from scene data through description and question generation to model output and final metric computation.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Pure‑text format avoids visual confounders but may disadvantage models that integrate multimodal cues; synthetic scenes offer controlled variation but may lack ecological validity; limited scene count (180 total) balances depth vs. breadth; reasoning levels provide fine‑grained diagnostics but increase evaluation complexity.
- **Failure signatures**: (1) “Bed‑North” heuristic—models predict north of bed regardless of layout; (2) Egocentric drop—sharp accuracy decline on perspective‑transformation questions; (3) Omniscient paradox—lower performance when model is given full knowledge; (4) Inability to detect undecidable relations (e.g., contradictory constraints).
- **First three experiments**: (1) Baseline fact retrieval across all models to establish upper bound; (2) Compare egocentric vs. allocentric accuracy to quantify perspective‑transformation cost; (3) Test synthetic scenes under omniscient vs. non‑omniscient conditions to reveal reliance on local cues.

## Limitations
- The pure‑text format may inadvertently favor text‑based statistical heuristics over genuine spatial computation.
- Model selection focused on 7B–14B scale with one outlier, leaving architecture and training data effects under‑explored.
- The “synthetic scene paradox” could reflect artifacts in the code‑generated distribution or question framing, limiting generalizability.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: LLMs show significant failure on perspective transformations and exhibit statistical heuristics; a clear gap exists between fact retrieval and spatial integration.
- **Medium confidence**: Models rely solely on local relational cues rather than constructing any verifiable spatial mental model; alternative explanations not fully ruled out.
- **Low confidence**: The omniscient decline finding generalizes across architectures and more complex natural scene descriptions; requires replication.

## Next Checks
1. **Counterfactual Layout Testing**: Systematically invert spatial relations (e.g., swap left/right for all objects) to see if heuristic‑reliant models fail catastrophically while models with coherent internal structures retain some consistency.
2. **Attention & Activation Probing**: For high‑performing models (e.g., DeepSeek‑v3.2), analyze attention maps and intermediate activations during spatial tasks to distinguish global spatial integration from local token pattern matching.
3. **Cross‑Benchmark Consistency**: Evaluate top and mid‑tier models on established spatial reasoning benchmarks using different modalities (visual Q&A, embodied simulation) and text formats (navigation instructions) to verify if the observed performance hierarchy and failure modes persist.
