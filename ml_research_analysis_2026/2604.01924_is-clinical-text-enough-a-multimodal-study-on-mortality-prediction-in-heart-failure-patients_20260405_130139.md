# Is Clinical Text Enough? A Multimodal Study on Mortality Prediction in Heart Failure Patients

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2604.01924
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01924
*   **Reference Count:** 0
*   **Primary Result:** Supervised multimodal fusion with entity-aware text embeddings achieves peak AUC 83.8% and F1 48.5%, significantly outperforming unimodal and LLM-based approaches for 90-day mortality prediction.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether clinical text alone is sufficient for predicting 90-day mortality in heart failure patients, or if fusing it with structured Electronic Health Record (EHR) data yields superior performance. Using French clinical notes, the authors compare transformer-based text models (using CLS tokens versus entity-level embeddings from NER), structured-only models, and various supervised multimodal fusion techniques (e.g., gated, cross-attention, late fusion). They also evaluate large language models (LLMs) prompted with unimodal and multimodal inputs. The core finding is that supervised multimodal fusion, particularly when combining entity-aware text representations with structured data, provides the most robust and accurate predictions. In stark contrast, the evaluated LLMs perform poorly and inconsistently, often failing to utilize multimodal information effectively.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on supervised neural models and prompted LLMs. For supervised approaches, clinical notes are processed using a domain-adapted French transformer (CamemBERT-bio). Two primary text representations are tested: the standard [CLS] token embedding and an aggregated embedding of clinical entities extracted via a pre-trained NER model. Structured EHR features are encoded via a simple feed-forward network. These unimodal representations are then fused using several supervised strategies: a gated multimodal unit, a cross-attention mechanism, and a late fusion stack. For LLMs (Mistral, Qwen, MedGemma), the study employs zero-shot prompting with the same unimodal or concatenated multimodal inputs, generating a binary prediction for mortality. Models are evaluated using AUC, F1-score, and statistical significance testing.

## Key Results
*   Supervised multimodal fusion with entity-aware text embeddings (e.g., Gated Fusion, Late Fusion Stack) achieves the best overall performance, with a peak AUC of 83.8% and F1-score of 48.5% using CamemBERT-bio.
*   Entity-level text integration consistently enhances the performance of text-only models compared to using only the [CLS] token.
*   Prompted LLMs perform poorly and inconsistently; text-only prompts yield the highest (but still low) F1 (~25%), and multimodal prompts often cause model degeneration to single-class outputs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superior performance of the supervised multimodal fusion models stems from two key design choices. First, **entity-level text integration** enriches the textual representation by focusing on specific clinical concepts (e.g., diagnoses, procedures, medications) extracted via NER. This provides a more structured and clinically relevant signal within the free-text notes than a single [CLS] vector, which must implicitly learn to aggregate all relevant information. Second, **supervised fusion mechanisms** (like gated units or attention) are explicitly trained to weight and combine the complementary information from the enriched text pathway and the structured EHR pathway. This allows the model to dynamically prioritize the most predictive features from each modality for the mortality outcome, a capability that simple concatenation or unimodal models lack. The poor LLM performance suggests that without fine-tuning or more sophisticated prompting, these general-purpose models struggle to extract the nuanced, temporally integrated signals from both French clinical narratives and tabular EHR data required for this specific, high-stakes prediction task.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
Input Data (French Clinical Notes, Structured EHR) -> Clinical NER -> Entity List / [CLS] Token -> Text Encoder (CamemBERT-bio) -> Text Representation (Entity-Aggregated or CLS Vector). Structured EHR -> Feature Preprocessing -> Structured Encoder (Feed-Forward Net) -> Structured Representation. Fusion Mechanism (Gated Unit / Cross-Attention / Late Stack) -> Joint Representation -> Prediction Head (Linear Layer) -> Mortality Probability.

**Critical Path:** The most performance-sensitive path involves extracting clinical entities from notes, aggregating their embeddings from the text encoder, and passing this entity-aware representation, along with the structured representation, through a supervised fusion module like the Gated Multimodal Unit before final classification.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   **Supervised Fusion vs. Simple Concatenation:** Supervised fusion (gated, attention) adds model complexity and parameters but allows for learned, dynamic weighting of modalities. Simple concatenation is a weaker baseline but is faster to train and less prone to overfitting on smaller datasets.
*   **Entity-Level vs. Document-Level (CLS) Text:** Entity-level processing is computationally more expensive (requires NER step, multiple token aggregations) but yields a more precise clinical signal. CLS is efficient but may lose finer-grained information.
*   **Specialized Supervised Model vs. Generalist LLM:** The supervised model is task-specific, requires training data, but is optimized for the prediction. The LLM is flexible and requires no training but is prone to hallucination, prompt sensitivity, and may not internalize the temporal logic of EHR data.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   LLM outputs degenerating to a single class (e.g., always predicting "survived") despite varied prompts.
*   Multimodal fusion model performance dropping to near the text-only or structured-only baseline, indicating ineffective integration.
*   Consistently low F1-scores (<0.3) despite decent AUC (>0.8), signaling the model's inability to correctly identify the minority positive (death) class, likely due to severe class imbalance or poor representation of death cases in training.
*   Performance collapse when processing notes with very low entity counts, suggesting the entity-aware pathway is brittle to NER failures.

3.  Benchmark against a classical structured model: Train a high-performance gradient-boosted tree (e.g., XGBoost) on the preprocessed structured EHR features alone. Compare its performance to the supervised multimodal neural model. Hypothesis: the neural multimodal model will outperform the best structured-only model, demonstrating the added value of text.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset transparency is lacking:** Key specifics such as dataset size, source (single vs. multi-center), patient demographics, and the exact class balance are unreported, severely limiting the assessment of generalizability and potential biases.
*   **Performance context is unclear:** The peak F1-score of 48.5% indicates a very difficult task, but without comparison to simple baselines (e.g., predicting the majority class) or external benchmarks on a similar heart failure cohort, the absolute magnitude of the reported improvement is hard to interpret.
*   **LLM evaluation details are opaque:** The prompting strategies, token limit management, and specific computational settings (e.g., temperature) for Mistral, Qwen, and MedGemma are not described. The observed poor LLM performance may be an artifact of suboptimal prompting rather than a fundamental limitation of LLMs for this multimodal task.
