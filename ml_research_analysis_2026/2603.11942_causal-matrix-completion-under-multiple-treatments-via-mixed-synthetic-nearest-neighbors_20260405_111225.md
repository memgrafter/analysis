# Causal Matrix Completion under Multiple Treatments via Mixed Synthetic Nearest Neighbors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11942
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11942
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Proposed Mixed Synthetic Nearest Neighbors (MSNN) substantially improves feasible counterfactual estimation for data-scarce treatments in causal matrix completion, reducing mean relative error by 2–3× over standard SNN under MCAR.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a key limitation of Synthetic Nearest Neighbors (SNN) for causal inference in matrix settings with multiple treatment levels: SNN fails when some treatments have very sparse observations because it cannot find enough nearest neighbors within the same treatment group. The authors introduce Mixed Synthetic Nearest Neighbors (MSNN), a method that integrates information across all treatment levels by constructing "mixed" anchor rows and columns. These mixed anchors borrow strength from treatments with richer data, while still leveraging a shared low-rank row factor structure to ensure identifiability. Theoretical results show that under missing completely at random (MCAR), MSNN's expected number of usable subgroups grows exponentially relative to SNN. Synthetic experiments demonstrate large improvements in feasible estimation rates and accuracy for rare treatments, and a case study on California's Proposition 99 shows reliable counterfactual estimation across all treatment groups.

## Method Summary
MSNN extends the SNN framework for causal matrix completion with multiple discrete treatments. Where SNN synthesizes a control unit for a treated unit using only donors from the *same* treatment level, MSNN constructs mixed donor pools by selecting anchor rows and columns that may come from *any* treatment level. This is done while enforcing a shared low-rank factor model for the latent row factors, which allows the method to separate treatment effects from common row-level characteristics. For a given target unit and treatment, MSNN: (1) selects anchor rows and columns based on observed entries under that treatment, (2) builds a synthetic control by combining these anchors weighted by similarity, and (3) uses the synthetic to predict the missing counterfactual outcome. The mixing strategy is what enables borrowing information for data-scarce treatments.

## Key Results
- MSNN dramatically increases the fraction of subgroups for which counterfactuals can be estimated when some treatments are very sparse (e.g., feasible ratio 4.69% vs 0.03% for the lowest prevalence treatment in one synthetic setting).
- For treatments with <4% observed entries, MSNN reduces mean relative error by a factor of 2–3 compared to SNN in synthetic experiments.
- In the California Proposition 99 application, MSNN produces stable yearly counterfactual estimates for all treatment groups, whereas SNN fails for the sparsest treatment groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MSNN works because it circumvents SNN's core limitation: the requirement for enough observed entries within a single treatment level to find similar donors. By allowing anchors to be drawn from any treatment level, MSNN pools information across the entire matrix. The shared low-rank factor model is crucial here—it assumes that row units share a common latent structure that drives outcomes across all treatments. This shared structure justifies using donors from different treatments because their latent factors are comparable, even if their treatment assignments differ. The "mixed" anchor construction essentially creates a more robust synthetic control by expanding the donor pool, which is especially valuable when a particular treatment is rarely observed. The theoretical exponential gain factor quantifies how much larger the effective donor pool becomes under this mixing strategy.

- **Low-rank matrix factorization**: Assumes the outcome matrix is approximately generated by a few latent factors. *Why needed*: Enables sharing of information across units and time; underpins identifiability in MSNN. *Quick check*: What does the rank parameter represent, and how might misspecification harm estimates?
- **Multiple treatment effects**: Extending causal inference beyond binary treatment to several discrete levels. *Why needed*: Many real-world policies have varying intensities or versions. *Quick check*: How does the potential outcomes framework generalize to >2 treatment values?
- **Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)**: The assumption that missingness is independent of observed and unobserved data. *Why needed*: Simplifies theoretical analysis; guarantees that observed entries are a random sample. *Quick check*: Would MNAR invalidate the MSNN theoretical guarantee? Why?
- **Anchor-based synthesis**: Using a small set of representative rows/columns to build a synthetic control. *Why needed*: Reduces computational burden and mitigates overfitting in high dimensions. *Quick check*: What are the trade-offs between using more anchors vs. fewer anchors?

## Architecture Onboarding
Sparse Outcome Matrix (with treatments)
        ↓
Mixed Anchor Selection (per treatment)
        ↓
Mixed Synthetic Construction (weighted combination of anchors)
        ↓
Counterfactual Outcome Estimation
- **Critical path**: The anchor selection step is most critical; it determines which units contribute to the synthetic and directly impacts bias-variance trade-off.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Larger anchor sets reduce variance but may increase bias if anchors are not truly comparable; mixing across treatments increases donor pool size but relies more heavily on the shared low-rank factor assumption.
- **Failure signatures**: (1) If the shared low-rank structure is violated (e.g., treatments have genuinely different causal mechanisms), mixing anchors could introduce bias. (2) If missingness is not MCAR, the theoretical guarantees break down. (3) For extremely high-rank matrices, anchor-based synthesis may be too coarse.
- **First three experiments to run**: (1) Vary the number of anchor rows and columns on synthetic data to see impact on feasible ratio and error. (2) Generate data under MAR and MNAR missingness to test robustness beyond MCAR. (3) Compare MSNN against SNN and standard matrix completion (e.g., softImpute) on a new real dataset with multiple sparse treatments (e.g., educational program participation across schools).

## Limitations
- Theoretical results require MCAR missingness and a shared low-rank factor structure, which may not hold in all applications.
- Empirical validation is limited to one synthetic configuration and a single observational case study (California Proposition 99), so robustness across diverse settings is uncertain.
- Hyperparameter choices (anchor dimensions, rank) are not systematically explored, and computational complexity is not reported.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical claim** (exponential gain factor): Medium – derivation appears plausible but not independently verified.
- **Synthetic experiments** (error reduction for rare treatments): Medium – positive results on limited synthetic scenarios.
- **Case study** (Proposition 99 reliability): Low – single observational dataset, potential for unmeasured confounding.
