# MedPriv-Bench: Benchmarking the Privacy-Utility Trade-off of Large Language Models in Medical Open-End Question Answering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14265
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14265
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** First benchmark to jointly evaluate privacy (contextual leakage) and clinical utility in medical QA, revealing a pervasive trade-off and showing that enforced brevity drastically reduces leakage with minimal utility loss.

## Executive Summary
MedPriv-Bench addresses the overlooked risk of contextual leakage in medical retrieval-augmented generation, where unique combinations of clinical details enable patient re-identification even after de-identification. The benchmark uses a multi-agent, human-in-the-loop pipeline to synthesize realistic medical QA instances that create authentic privacy pressure by requiring models to use injected PHI for optimal answers without explicitly disclosing it. Privacy is quantified via an automated NLI-based judge (roberta-large-mnli) validated at 85.9% human alignment. Evaluation of nine LLMs shows a pervasive privacy-utility trade-off: prompt-based defenses reduce leakage (e.g., GPT-5-mini LRinst from 58.4% to 39.4%) but typically lower utility scores. The study demonstrates that leakage is primarily a generation-stage issue and that enforcing response brevity (as a proxy for HIPAA’s Minimum Necessary Standard) can reduce exposure dramatically (e.g., to 5.7%) with little utility impact. Medical-specific models (e.g., Med42-v2-70B) achieve a more favorable balance.

## Method Summary
MedPriv-Bench constructs a dataset of medical QA instances from de-identified clinical notes (PMC-Patients) via a four-phase synthesis pipeline. First, a PHI Injection Agent selects categories from a predefined taxonomy and rewrites note chunks to embed synthetic PHI naturally. Second, a Question Agent acts as a white-box attacker, generating clinically meaningful questions whose optimal answers implicitly depend on the injected PHI, along with a reasoning path. Third, an Answer Agent produces a gold-standard response that leverages the PHI for accuracy while avoiding explicit disclosure. Fourth, auto-validation (using GPT-5.2) filters for correctness and completeness, with human experts validating 20% of samples. For evaluation, target LLMs generate responses to benchmark instances (with or without a privacy defense prompt). Utility is scored 0–5 by a model judge (GPT-5-mini) against instance-specific rubrics. Privacy leakage is measured by an NLI judge (roberta-large-mnli) that checks entailment between the response and each injected PHI fact; long responses are split into overlapping 512-token chunks.

## Key Results
- A pervasive privacy-utility trade-off exists across all evaluated LLMs; applying prompt-based privacy defenses reduces leakage rates but typically decreases utility scores.
- Enforcing response brevity (e.g., 100-word limit) drastically reduces leakage (e.g., GPT-5-mini LRinst from 39.4% to 5.7%) with minimal utility degradation (4.57 vs. 4.87).
- Medical-specific models (e.g., Med42-v2-70B) achieve a more favorable privacy-utility balance than general-purpose LLMs.
- Leakage is primarily a generation-stage issue; controlling response length is a highly effective mitigation aligned with HIPAA’s Minimum Necessary Standard.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s validity and insights rely on three interlocking mechanisms:

1. **Multi-agent, human-in-the-loop synthesis**  
   Three agents collaborate to create authentic privacy pressure. The PHI Injection Agent rewrites clinical note chunks to embed synthetic PHI naturally. The Question Agent (white-box attacker) generates questions that implicitly require the injected PHI for a high-quality answer. The Answer Agent produces a gold response that uses the PHI for utility without explicit disclosure. Human validation (20% of samples) ensures realism. Evidence: Section 3 details the pipeline; Appendix B provides prompts; human pass rates support quality. Break condition: If PHI integration is unnatural or questions do not genuinely depend on PHI, the benchmark fails to simulate real risk.

2. **NLI-based automated leakage judge**  
   A pre-trained RoBERTa-NLI model detects entailment between each response (premise) and injected PHI fact (hypothesis). Responses >512 tokens are split into overlapping chunks to handle length limits. This captures implicit disclosure beyond keyword matching. Evidence: Abstract reports 85.9% human alignment; Section 4.2 describes the formulation. Break condition: Fragmentation may miss cross-chunk entailment; lack of medical domain knowledge may undercount subtle leakage.

3. **Brevity as a privacy lever**  
   Controlled experiments show leakage correlates strongly with response verbosity. A 100-word limit reduced LRinst from 39.4% to 5.7% with minimal utility drop. This aligns with HIPAA’s Minimum Necessary Standard by limiting disclosed information. Evidence: Abstract and Section 6.4 (Figure 4). Break condition: Overly restrictive brevity may degrade utility; models might adapt to leak concisely.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
PMC-Patents -> Chunking -> PHI Injection Agent -> Question Agent -> Answer Agent -> Auto-Validation (GPT-5.2) -> Human Validation (20%) -> MedPriv-Bench. Evaluation: Target LLM (with/without defense) -> Response -> NLI Judge (roberta-large-mnli) & Utility Judge (GPT-5-mini) -> Metrics (LRinst, LRfact, Utility Score).

**Critical path:**  
1. Load raw clinical note and split into 3 chunks.  
2. PHI Injection Agent selects PHI categories and rewrites chunks.  
3. Question Agent generates question and reasoning path based on injected PHI.  
4. Answer Agent generates gold answer with scoring rubric.  
5. Auto-Validation filters for correctness/completeness.  
6. Human validation on subset.  
7. For evaluation: run target LLM on benchmark instances, collect responses.  
8. Compute utility score via GPT-5-mini judge and privacy leakage via NLI judge.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Synthetic vs real PHI: Synthetic avoids ethics issues but may not capture all real leakage patterns.  
- NLI vs human: NLI scales but may miss subtle entailment; human is gold standard but costly.  
- Open-ended vs MCQ: Open-ended reflects real usage but scoring is noisier.  
- Prompt-based defense vs architectural: Prompt is simple but may be less effective than fine-tuning or DP.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low human validation pass rate (<80%) indicates poor benchmark quality.  
- High NLI-human disagreement (>20%) suggests judging metric issues.  
- Models with high utility but also high leakage (e.g., Deepseek-V3.2 w/o: LRinst 79.8%) indicate over-reasoning risk.  
- Failure to generate clinically meaningful questions in the pipeline leads to low benchmark utility.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce main table: Evaluate all 9 models with and without defense on MedPriv-Bench, report utility and leakage.  
2. Ablation study: Replace NLI judge with another model (e.g., DeBERTa) or vary the PHI taxonomy to see impact on leakage detection.  
3. Sensitivity analysis: Vary the response length constraint (e.g., 50, 100, 200 words) to confirm the brevity effect on leakage, using a fixed model (e.g., GPT-5-mini w/ defense).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the privacy-utility trade-off and contextual leakage risk manifest when evaluating on real-world, unstructured Electronic Health Records (EHRs) instead of the curated PMC-Patients case reports? The benchmark’s data source may not capture the noisiness and varied formatting of production EHRs, which could alter leakage dynamics.
2. To what extent do contextual leakage risks compound across multiple dialogue turns in a conversational medical QA setting, and how do existing privacy-preservation strategies hold up? The benchmark only tests isolated Q/A pairs; cumulative leakage in extended conversations remains open.
3. Can more sophisticated automated evaluation methods for contextual leakage, beyond chunk-based NLI, accurately capture "distributed" disclosures where sensitive facts are implied through combinations of non-sensitive statements across a long response? The current sliding-window NLI may miss holistic entailment.
4. How do imperfect, real-world retrieval components (e.g., retrieving irrelevant chunks or missing critical context) interact with the generation stage to influence the overall contextual leakage rate in medical RAG systems? The study assumed ideal retrieval; real errors may introduce new risks or protective effects.
5. What architectural interventions (beyond prompt engineering) can effectively mitigate contextual leakage in medical LLMs without severely compromising clinical utility, and what is the theoretical bound of this trade-off? The paper only evaluated prompt-based defenses; other methods (e.g., DP fine-tuning) remain unexplored.

## Limitations
- Synthetic PHI injection may not fully replicate real-world contextual leakage patterns.
- NLI judge’s sliding window risks missing cross-chunk entailment and lacks deep medical domain knowledge.
- Utility scoring relies on GPT-5-mini, which may exhibit bias and inconsistency.
- Benchmark focuses on prompt-based defense; other privacy-preserving methods (e.g., DP fine-tuning) remain unevaluated.
