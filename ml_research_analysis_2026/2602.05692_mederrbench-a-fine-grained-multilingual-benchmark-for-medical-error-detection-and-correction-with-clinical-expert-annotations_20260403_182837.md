# MedErrBench: A Fine-Grained Multilingual Benchmark for Medical Error Detection and Correction with Clinical Expert Annotations

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.05692
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05692
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Language-specialized models (e.g., Doubao-1.5, DeepSeek-V3) outperform general and medical-domain LLMs on multilingual error detection, but error correction and localization remain significantly more challenging, with Arabic showing the largest performance gaps.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MedErrBench, the first fine-grained multilingual benchmark for medical error detection and correction, covering English, Chinese, and Arabic. The dataset is constructed by systematically injecting clinician-defined error types into medical exam questions and validating each instance with clinical experts. Evaluations across state-of-the-art LLMs reveal that models specialized for target languages perform best, highlighting a critical misalignment in existing general and medical-domain models. Providing explicit error-type definitions improves zero-shot performance, while few-shot learning gains are sensitive to example difficulty. The work underscores the need for clinically grounded, language-aware AI systems and provides a public resource to advance safe multilingual clinical NLP.

## Method Summary
MedErrBench was created through a multi-stage pipeline. First, medical exam questions (MedQA for EN/ZH, MedArabiQ for AR) were filtered and processed. Next, an LLM-assisted error injection engine rewrote clinical scenarios to insert a single, plausible error corresponding to one of ten clinician-defined error categories (e.g., Diagnosis, Management, Treatment). Each injected instance was then dual-annotated: NLP researchers tagged key phrases, difficulty, and reasoning type, followed by independent clinician review to validate the error's presence, type, and clinical naturalness. The final benchmark comprises train, validation, and test splits with standardized prompts for three tasks: binary error detection, sentence-level localization, and full-text correction, evaluated using accuracy, ROUGE, BERTScore, and BLEURT.

## Key Results
*   Language-specialized models (Doubao-1.5, DeepSeek-V3) consistently achieve the highest detection and localization accuracy across English, Chinese, and Arabic, significantly outperforming general-purpose (GPT-4o, Claude) and medical-domain (MedGemma, HuatuoGPT) LLMs.
*   Providing explicit definitions of the ten error types in zero-shot prompts yields measurable performance gains for several models, but benefits are not uniform (e.g., Doubao's few-shot performance can decrease with added definitions).
*   Error correction and localization are substantially more challenging than detection for all models and languages, with Arabic exhibiting the lowest scores and largest performance drops between tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
*   **Language-Specialized Model Superiority**
    *   **Claim:** Models pretrained on abundant target-language data (e.g., Doubao, DeepSeek) outperform others due to superior grasp of linguistic structure, idiom, and region-specific clinical reasoning patterns.
    *   **Mechanism:** Native-language pretraining yields finer tokenization and richer representations of how medical errors are linguistically expressed (e.g., phrasing of misdiagnoses, treatment recommendations) within a given cultural-educational context.
    *   **Core Assumption:** Error manifestations are linguistically mediated; models without exposure to these patterns will misrepresent or fail to detect them.
    *   **Evidence:** [abstract, section 4.4] Specialized models lead in detection/localization; [Appendix J.1] translation of Arabic data harms performance, confirming irreplaceable native linguistic knowledge.
*   **Definition Scaffolding Improves Zero-Shot Performance**
    *   **Claim:** Including clinician-defined error-type descriptions in the prompt clarifies the task, aligning the model's output distribution with the target taxonomy.
    *   **Mechanism:** Definitions operationalize abstract categories (e.g., "Management" error), reducing ambiguity and guiding the model's internal reasoning toward clinically validated failure modes.
    *   **Core Assumption:** LLMs have latent medical error knowledge but lack a calibrated output space for this specific taxonomy without explicit grounding.
    *   **Evidence:** [abstract, section 4.5] Table 5 shows score increases for DeepSeek-V3 and Doubao with definitions; [section 3.2] taxonomy is clinician-defined to reflect real-world errors.
*   **Few-Shot Efficacy is Difficulty-Dependent and Model-Specific**
    *   **Claim:** Optimal in-context example selection varies by model architecture/training, following a "zone of proximal development" where medium-difficulty examples help some models while easier examples help others.
    *   **Mechanism:** Example difficulty influences cognitive load; medium examples provide maximal signal without noise, but model-specific capabilities (e.g., chain-of-thought tuning) change the optimal point.
    *   **Core Assumption:** Few-shot learning trajectory is non-linear and model-dependent regarding example complexity.
    *   **Evidence:** [section 4.6] Figure 3 shows Doubao prefers "Medium > Easy > Hard" for detection, while DeepSeek prefers "Easy > Medium > Hard" for correction; [section 3.4] difficulty is grounded in clinical cue clarity and reasoning steps.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Clinician-Grounded Error Taxonomy**
    *   **Why needed:** The 10-category schema (Diagnosis, Management, Treatment, etc.) is the foundational framework. Without understanding these distinctions, one cannot interpret model error profiles or design targeted interventions.
    *   **Quick check:** A surgeon delays an emergency appendectomy to order more tests. Which error types apply? (Management: inappropriate non-pharmacologic decision; Treatment: delayed definitive intervention).
*   **Concept: Multilingual Clinical Text Variation**
    *   **Why needed:** Medical terminology, case presentation styles, and error expressions differ structurally across languages, not just lexically. This explains persistent performance gaps (e.g., Arabic) and the failure of translation-based workarounds.
    *   **Quick check:** Why does the Arabic subset skew heavily toward Factual Recall (61.4%) versus English multi-hop reasoning (91%)? (Cultural/educational assessment styles and dataset source limitations, per section 3.1).
*   **Concept: Prompt Engineering for Structured Output**
    *   **Why needed:** Tasks require strictly formatted outputs (e.g., `"X 1 2 \"correction\""`). Deviations cause parsing failures and invalid metric scores. The paper's prompts (Figures S2–S4) are part of the evaluation contract.
    *   **Quick check:** If a model outputs `"Text ID: X, Error: Yes, Sentence: 2, Correction: ..."` instead of the required format, what happens? (Parsing fails; accuracy/ROUGE scores become invalid per section 4.1 assumptions).

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:**
    Data Ingestion Pipeline (MedQA/MedArabiQ) -> Error Injection Engine (LLM-assisted rewriting) -> Annotation Layer (NLP tags + Clinician review) -> Evaluation Harness (Task prompts -> Model inference -> Metrics)
*   **Critical Path:** The clinician review (section 3.5) is the gating quality control step. Without medical validity validation for each injected error, the benchmark's clinical utility collapses.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   Synthetic vs. real errors: Injection ensures controlled experimentation but may lack real-world complexity like concurrent errors.
    *   Sentence-level vs. token-level localization: Sentence-level chosen for clinical practicality ([section B]), trading finer granularity for simpler, more robust evaluation.
    *   Public vs. private data: Using exam questions provides volume but may not reflect noisy, unstructured real clinical notes; Arabic expansion is limited by data scarcity.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   **Localization failure:** Model detects an error but misidentifies the correct sentence span (section 4.4: accuracy drop from detection to localization).
    *   **Language collapse:** ALLAM-7B's near-zero Arabic detection scores (Table 4) indicate training misalignment or extreme prompt sensitivity.
    *   **Overcorrection:** Llama3-8B in Arabic produces many "NA" predictions, inflating true negatives and distorting metric interpretation.
    *   **Definition overload:** Doubao's few-shot performance drops when both error type and definitions are provided (Table 5), suggesting prompt complexity exceeds its instruction-following capacity.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  **Validate error injection fidelity:** Sample 100 injected instances per language; conduct blind clinician review to measure >90% agreement on error presence and type accuracy.
    2.  **Definition ablation across models:** Replicate Table 5's zero-shot setup for top models (Doubao, DeepSeek, GPT-4o) with/without definitions to test consistency of benefit.
    3.  **Cross-lingual transfer test:** Translate the Chinese test set to English/Arabic using a high-quality medical MT system; evaluate a model trained only on native Chinese to quantify the performance delta documented in Appendix J.1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Severity and Equity Integration:** How can severity stratification and equity-related annotations be integrated to reflect real-world clinical impact and fairness? Current dataset lacks these dimensions, limiting evaluation of potential harms or biases. *Resolution requires* new annotations linking errors to severity scores and equity-relevant attributes (e.g., patient demographics).
*   **Arabic Data Scarcity:** What strategies can effectively expand and diversify the Arabic subset given native clinical data scarcity? The current Arabic portion is smaller and less diverse, hindering robust model development for Arabic-speaking regions. *Resolution requires* curated Arabic cases from diverse hospitals/exams with clinician validation.
*   **Correction/Localization Gap:** Why do error correction and localization remain significantly more challenging than detection? Understanding if the gap stems from generative capability limits, context modeling, or task formulation is key. *Resolution requires* fine-grained error analysis of failed outputs (e.g., span errors, hallucination) and ablation studies.
*   **Prompt-Architecture Interplay:** How does model architecture/training objective influence sensitivity to prompt design (e.g., definitions vs. few-shot)? Performance divergence between models (DeepSeek vs. Doubao) suggests prompt design must be model-specific. *Resolution requires* systematic prompt variation studies across diverse architectures, correlated with model size/training data.

## Limitations
*   Exam-sourced data may not generalize to noisy, unstructured real-world clinical notes.
*   The Arabic subset is smaller and less diverse due to limited publicly available native medical data.
*   Synthetic error injection may not capture concurrent real-world mistakes or complex error cascades.
*   Rigid output formatting requirements risk parsing failures that can inflate metric artifacts (e.g., "NA" predictions).

## Confidence
*   Language-specialized models superiority: **High** (consistent cross-lingual results; translation break test confirms).
*   Error-type definitions improve zero-shot performance: **Medium** (significant for some models but inconsistent; Doubao shows few-shot drop with definitions).
*   Few-shot efficacy is difficulty-modulated and model-specific: **Medium** (empirical patterns observed but mechanism inferred; limited to 4 models).

## Next Checks
1.  **Real-world external validation:** Evaluate top models (Doubao, DeepSeek, GPT-4o) on a holdout set of *authentic* clinical notes (EN/CN/AR) with clinician-annotated errors to test generalizability beyond exam questions.
2.  **Fine-tuning transfer gap quantification:** Fine-tune a high-resource model (e.g., Llama-3) solely on English MedErrBench and test zero-shot on Chinese/Arabic. Measure the residual gap versus native-specialized models to isolate irreducible language-specific knowledge.
3.  **Definition robustness ablation:** Systematically vary zero-shot prompt definition length, phrasing (concise vs. verbose), and inclusion of examples. Measure performance variance across models to identify optimal definition schema and reveal model-specific sensitivity to prompt complexity.
