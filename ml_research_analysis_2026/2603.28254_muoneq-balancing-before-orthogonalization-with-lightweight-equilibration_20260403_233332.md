# MuonEq: Balancing Before Orthogonalization with Lightweight Equilibration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28254
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28254
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** MuonEq adds a pre-orthogonalization equilibration layer (row/column normalization) before Newton–Schulz orthogonalization, improving Muon optimizer convergence; on LLaMA2 130M, validation perplexity drops from 20.52 to 19.56 on C4.

## Executive Summary
Muon optimizers use finite-step Newton–Schulz (NS) orthogonalization to approximate matrix orthogonalization, but their efficiency depends on the input matrix's spectral condition. This paper introduces MuonEq, a lightweight technique that performs row/column normalization (RC, R, or C) on the momentum buffer prior to NS, effectively acting as a zeroth-order whitening surrogate. The equilibration reduces condition number and compresses the spectrum, accelerating NS convergence with only O(m+n) auxiliary state. Theoretically, the row-normalized variant (R) preserves the O(T^{-1/4}) stationarity guarantee. Empirically, MuonEq-R consistently outperforms standard Muon in large language model pretraining, achieving significantly lower perplexity on LLaMA2 models of 130M and 350M parameters on the C4 dataset, while maintaining comparable memory usage and only a slight increase in step time. Ablations confirm R as the default choice for hidden matrix weights.

## Method Summary
MuonEq modifies the Muon optimizer by inserting a pre-orthogonalization equilibration step. For each 2D parameter matrix, the optimizer maintains a momentum buffer M. Before applying the five-step Newton–Schulz (NS5) orthogonalization, MuonEq computes either row-wise (R), column-wise (C), or both row and column (RC) normalization factors from the squared sums of M entries. These factors are used to form a diagonal scaling matrix (or a product of two diagonals) that rescales M to have unit marginal variance. The normalized momentum M̂ is then fed to NS5, which outputs an approximately orthogonal matrix O that is used in the parameter update. The R variant (row-only) is recommended for hidden layers of wide networks, as it preserves the theoretical guarantee and delivers the best empirical performance. The additional overhead is O(m+n) extra state and minimal compute, while the NS steps dominate the cost.

## Key Results
- Row-normalized Muon (MuonEq-R) reduces validation perplexity on LLaMA2-130M from 20.52 (standard Muon) to 19.56 on C4.
- For LLaMA2-350M, MuonEq-R improves perplexity from 15.21 to 14.96.
- Both models achieve faster convergence with comparable memory consumption and only a minor increase in per-step runtime; R outperforms RC and C variants in ablations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Finite-step Newton–Schulz orthogonalization is sensitive to the input matrix’s spectral properties (stable rank and condition number); equilibration via row/column normalization (R/C/RC) improves NS convergence by compressing the spectrum before the orthogonalization step.  
**Mechanism:** The NS iteration’s contraction rate varies across singular directions; a broader spectrum (higher condition number) delays convergence for small singular values. Normalization rescales rows/columns to have unit marginal variance, which acts as a diagonal preconditioner reducing the condition number and making the input geometry more isotropic.  
**Core assumption:** The momentum matrix M has nonzero rows/columns so normalization is well-defined; further, the post-normalization condition number is sufficiently reduced to accelerate NS.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states that NS efficiency depends on spectrum and row/column normalization acts as a zeroth-order whitening surrogate. Theorem 3.1 gives error bound ∥Xk−Orth(G)∥F ≥ (1/√r) Σ_i (1−a^k σ_i/α)_+^2, showing dependence on singular values τ_i and condition number width τ_r−τ_1 = log_a κ. Related work (e.g., “On the Width Scaling… Row/Column Normalization”) also studies geometry-sensitive optimizer behavior.  
**Break condition:** If the original matrix has extremely skewed row/column norms such that after normalization the residual Gram matrix ‖C_c‖ or ‖C_r‖ remains large (Prop. 3.2), the zeroth-order surrogate may be insufficient; NS5 error plateaus (Fig. 1 shows condition number compression limits).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Row/column normalization serves as a zeroth-order surrogate for whitening; it removes marginal scale mismatch (differences in row/column norms) exactly, leaving only correlation structure (off-diagonal Gram entries) for NS to correct.  
**Mechanism:** For momentum matrix M, define D_r = diag(rowsum(M⊙M))^{1/2} and D_c similarly. Form N_r = D_r^{-1} M (row-normalized) and N_c = M D_c^{-1} (column-normalized). The expansions Orth(N_r) = N_r − N_r C_r/2 + O(‖C_r‖^2) (Prop. 3.2) show that the leading rescaling error is eliminated; the remaining C_r = N_r N_r^⊤ − I captures only row-wise correlations.  
**Core assumption:** The normalized Gram C_r (or C_c) is small enough to justify the first-order expansion; this typically holds for row-normalized hidden layers in Transformers where rows represent neurons.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 (Prop. 3.2) derives the expansions and notes that row/col normalization separates marginal scale from whitening correction. Section 3.3 (Theorem 3.5) uses this separation to obtain a clean alignment lower bound ⟨M,Orth(P(M)M)⟩ ≥ 1/√m ‖M‖F without auxiliary conditions. Related works like NorMuon and RMNP use row/column statistics similarly.  
**Break condition:** If rows/columns have near-zero norms (making D_r ill-conditioned) or the correlation structure C_r is large, the first-order surrogate may not sufficiently approximate full whitening; two-sided RC might be needed, but it introduces its own bias terms.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** There is a trade-off between stronger two-sided equilibration (RC) and cleaner one-sided geometry (R); for hidden Transformer weight matrices, row normalization (R) is the natural default as it aligns with operator-geometry analyses and yields a simpler nonconvex guarantee (O(T^{-1/4}) without extra conditions).  
**Mechanism:** RC applies both row and column normalization (D_r^{-1/2} M D_c^{-1/2}), giving the largest spectral correction (lowest condition number) but inducing a more complex alignment term; this requires a positivity condition χ_ε > 0 (Prop. 3.4) for descent. R applies only row normalization, which for wide matrices (m ≥ n) targets the left singular vectors (more relevant for hidden layers) and directly satisfies ⟨M,Orth(P(M)M)⟩ ≥ 1/√m ‖M‖F, allowing Theorem 3.5 to avoid auxiliary χ_ε.  
**Core assumption:** The parameter matrix corresponds to a hidden layer weight (typically wide: m >> n) where row-sided geometry dictates optimization dynamics.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes R is the natural default for hidden matrix weights and preserves the Õ(T^{-1/4}) guarantee. Section 3.3: Prop. 3.4 (RC) requires χ_ε ≥ 1/144 for a guarantee; Theorem 3.5 (R) has no such condition. Section 4.2: Ablation on CIFAR-10 and FineWeb shows R consistently outperforms RC and C for hidden-weight tasks. Neighbor paper “On the Width Scaling…” (arxiv:2603.09952) supports R as width-dependent default.  
**Break condition:** If the task involves embedding matrices (tall, n >> m) or output layers, column normalization (C) may be preferable; RC might help if initial condition number is extreme but risks violating χ_ε if ε is too small relative to G_∞.

## Foundational Learning
### Matrix spectral geometry
**Why needed:** Theorem 3.1 shows NS iteration error depends on singular value spread via stable rank sr(G) = ‖G‖_F^2/‖G‖_2^2 and condition number κ(G). Understanding how preconditioning (normalization) affects these metrics is key.  
**Quick check:** If a matrix has condition number 10^4, how many additional NS iterations are needed to achieve the same error as a matrix with condition number 10^2? (Answer: More steps for the smaller singular values; the difference in τ_r−τ_1 = log_a κ scales logarithmically with κ.)

### Finite-step orthogonalization error
**Why needed:** Muon uses only 5 NS steps; the gap ∥NS5(M) − Orth(M)∥_F is not negligible and depends on input spectrum (Eq. (5)). This error term combines finite-step approximation and preconditioning bias.  
**Quick check:** What two terms comprise the decomposition in Eq. (5), and which one does MuonEq directly modify? (Answer: Finite-step approximation error and preconditioning bias; MuonEq reduces the first by improving the input spectrum.)

### Operator geometry and row/column preferences
**Why needed:** Recent analyses link row normalization to steepest descent under the ∞→1 operator norm, which is natural for hidden layers; column normalization aligns with 1→→∞ norms, relevant for embeddings. This justifies the default R for wide hidden layers.  
**Quick check:** For a weight matrix W ∈ ℝ^{m×n} with m > n, which orientation (row-wise or column-wise normalization) better matches the geometry of the input data manifold? (Answer: Row-wise, as rows correspond to output neurons in a wide hidden layer.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Momentum buffer M_t → compute row and/or column squared sums → form diagonal scaling matrices D_r,t, D_c,t → apply inverse square root scaling to obtain normalized momentum N_t (either row-normalized N_r = D_r^{-1} M, column-normalized N_c = M D_c^{-1}, or two-sided D_r^{-1} M D_c^{-1}) → run 5-step Newton–Schulz (NS5) routine on N_t to produce orthogonalized update O_t → update parameters X_{t+1} = X_t − a η_t O_t.

**Critical path:**
1. Compute squared row/column sums of M_t → O(m+n) operations.
2. Form diagonal D_r,t, D_c,t (including small ε for stability) → O(m+n).
3. Apply elementwise scaling M̂_t = D_r,t^{-1/2} M_t (and possibly right-multiply by D_c,t^{-1/2}) → O(mn) but can be fused with NS to reduce memory traffic.
4. Execute 5 iterations of NS: each iteration does a matrix multiply and a polynomial coefficient scaling; total O(mn) per iteration → O(5 mn).
5. Combine with learning rate and update parameters.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **RC vs. R:** RC reduces condition number more aggressively (better for extremely ill-conditioned inputs) but introduces a bias term that requires a positivity condition χ_ε > 0 for theoretical guarantee; R is simpler and empirically better for typical hidden layers.
- **C variant:** Column normalization alone is weaker for wide matrices but may be suitable for embedding layers where columns correspond to features.
- **ε stability term:** A small ε (e.g., 1e-8) prevents division by zero; too large ε dilutes the normalization effect.

**Failure signatures:**
- **High ‖C_r‖ or ‖C_c‖:** The normalized Gram deviates significantly from identity, indicating the first-order surrogate is insufficient and NS error may remain high. Monitor row/column variances after normalization.
- **Numerical instability:** Zero rows/columns lead to division by near-zero if ε is too small, causing NaNs. Ensure minimum row/column norm > ε.
- **Degraded alignment:** If the inner product ⟨M, O_t⟩ falls below the theoretical lower bound (e.g., due to poor NS convergence), the stationarity guarantee degrades. Track the NS residual using Lemma G.2.
- **Performance regression:** If R underperforms Muon on a given architecture, consider switching to RC or adjusting the scaling constant a.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the number of Newton–Schulz iterations in MuonEq be adaptively chosen based on the condition number or stable rank of the momentum matrix?
2. Does MuonEq generalize to non-Transformer architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) and other matrix-valued parameters (e.g., embeddings) without modification?
3. Can the convergence guarantee for the two-sided RC variant be established without the auxiliary positivity condition χ_ε > 0?
4. Does the Õ(T^{-1/4}) stationarity rate for MuonEq (R variant) hold when weight decay and Nesterov momentum are used, as in the practical algorithm?

## Limitations
- Implementation details missing: the exact ε value used for stability, whether Nesterov momentum (γ) is enabled, and the precise set of 2D weight matrices targeted are not fully specified.
- Experiments are limited to LLaMA2 models on the C4 dataset; performance on other architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) and datasets remains unexplored.
- Theoretical analysis assumes wide matrices (m ≥ n); applicability to embedding or output layers (where m < n) is not guaranteed.
