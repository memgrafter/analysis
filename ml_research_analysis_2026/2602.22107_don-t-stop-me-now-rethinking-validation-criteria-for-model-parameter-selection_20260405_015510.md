# Don't stop me now: Rethinking Validation Criteria for Model Parameter Selection
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22107
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22107
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Loss‑based validation criteria (cross‑entropy, C‑Loss, PolyLoss) achieve acceptance rates of 5–19% for model selection, whereas validation accuracy rarely exceeds 2.5%, across diverse datasets, parameterization regimes, and early stopping strategies.

## Executive Summary
The paper challenges the common practice of using validation accuracy for model selection in neural classifiers, particularly with early stopping. Through extensive k‑fold experiments on 35+ UCI datasets with shallow networks, the authors compare validation accuracy against loss‑based criteria (cross‑entropy, C‑Loss, PolyLoss). They find that loss‑based criteria consistently yield higher acceptance rates—meaning the selected model’s test accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from the test‑optimal model—while validation accuracy performs poorly. This suggests that validation accuracy is an unstable and misaligned criterion due to its discrete nature, and instead continuous loss metrics provide a more reliable signal. The results call for reevaluating model selection practices in deep learning.

## Method Summary
The study employs stratified 10‑fold cross‑validation on each dataset. For every fold, 15% of the training split is held out as validation. A single‑hidden‑layer MLP with ReLU is trained by SGD (batch 64, lr 0.01) for up to 20 000 epochs. The training loss varies across runs (cross‑entropy, C‑Loss, PolyLoss). At each epoch, the model’s validation loss (all three losses) and validation accuracy are recorded. After training, for each validation criterion, two model‑selection strategies are applied: (1) early stopping with a fixed patience T (10 or 50), selecting the best checkpoint before the first T epochs without improvement; (2) post‑hoc selection, picking the epoch with the best validation value over the full run. The selected model’s test accuracy is compared to the test‑optimal accuracy (the maximum over all epochs) using a paired one‑tailed hypothesis test (t‑test or Wilcoxon, depending on normality). The acceptance rate—the proportion of configurations where the null hypothesis of equal performance is not rejected—serves as the primary measure of how well a validation criterion identifies near‑optimal models.

## Key Results
- Loss‑based validation criteria (CE, C‑Loss, PolyLoss) achieve acceptance rates of 5–19%, significantly higher than validation accuracy (<2.5%).
- The superiority of loss‑based criteria holds across different training losses (CE, C‑Loss, PolyLoss), parameter‑to‑sample ratios (0.3–50), dataset complexities, and early‑stopping patience values (10/50).
- Validation accuracy’s performance remains below 2.5% even under post‑hoc selection, indicating a fundamental misalignment rather than an artifact of early stopping.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Validation accuracy is a poor selection criterion because its discrete, thresholded nature produces low sensitivity to incremental model improvements, causing unstable early stopping.
- **Mechanism:** Accuracy only changes when predictions cross the decision boundary. During training, many consecutive epochs can yield identical accuracy on the validation set (plateaus and ties), especially with finite validation samples. Early stopping with patience monitors this metric; if the metric does not improve for `T` epochs, training halts. Plateaus and ties make the "no improvement" condition noisy and sensitive to random fluctuations, often stopping prematurely around a suboptimal local minimum.
- **Core assumption:** The validation set is finite, so the empirical accuracy is a noisy estimate of the true conditional accuracy; small changes in model confidence may not flip enough predictions to change the discrete accuracy count.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "accuracy is a discrete, thresholded indicator with low sensitivity... produces long plateaus and frequent ties across epochs—especially on small validation sets—making early‑stopping triggers noisy and unstable."
  - [section 6] "accuracy is excellent for final reporting, but it seems a poor compass to validate over an iterative training process."
- **Break condition:** If the validation set is extremely large (approaching infinite), accuracy plateaus would be rare, and the discrete effect might diminish, potentially making accuracy‑based stopping less noisy. The paper does not test this regime.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Loss‑based validation criteria provide a more reliable selection signal because they are continuous, differentiable, and sensitive to confidence changes, leading to smoother trajectories and more stable identification of improving checkpoints.
- **Mechanism:** Loss functions (cross‑entropy, C‑Loss, PolyLoss) evaluate the full predicted probability distribution, not just the argmax. They change continuously with model confidence, even when the predicted class remains correct. This yields smoother, non‑plateauing validation trajectories. Under early stopping, the "no improvement" condition is triggered only when the loss genuinely ceases to decrease, reducing false stops due to metric noise. Post‑hoc selection also benefits as the smoother curve makes the global minimum more consistently identifiable.
- **Core assumption:** The training dynamics involve gradual changes in predicted probabilities; a continuous loss will reflect these changes monotonically where accuracy might not.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Loss‑based validation criteria yield comparable and more stable test accuracy."
  - [section 5] Results show loss‑based criteria achieve 5–19% acceptance rates vs. <2.5% for accuracy, across early stopping and post‑hoc selection.
  - [section 6] "monitoring validation loss... constitutes a more reliable strategy for model selection."
- **Break condition:** If the loss surface itself becomes extremely flat or rugged in the validation set (e.g., due to severe label noise or dataset shift), the continuity advantage might erode. The paper’s use of standard UCI datasets likely avoids extreme cases.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The loss‑metric mismatch between training loss and validation criterion creates a systematic bias: using accuracy for validation misaligns the selection signal with the optimization trajectory, often selecting checkpoints that are not near the test‑optimal model despite being locally optimal for accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** The model is optimized to minimize a continuous training loss (e.g., cross‑entropy). This shapes the parameter trajectory toward regions that improve probabilistic calibration. Validation accuracy, however, optimizes a different objective (0‑1 risk). The epoch that maximizes validation accuracy (`e*_a,Val`) can be far from the epoch that minimizes validation loss (`e*_l,Val`), and both can be far from the test‑optimal epoch (`e*_a,Test`). The paper finds that `a(Test, e*_a,Val)` is consistently worse than `A*_Test` compared to loss‑based selections, indicating that the accuracy‑based selection criterion does not reliably track the optimization progress that leads to best test accuracy.
- **Core assumption:** The training loss landscape and the accuracy landscape are not perfectly aligned; their optima epochs differ across datasets and parameterizations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "loss–metric mismatch... validation accuracy is an unstable and poorly aligned criterion."
  - [section 3.3] Defines the comparison: `a(Test, e*_a,Val)` and `a(Test, e*_l,Val)` vs. `A*_Test`.
  - [section 5] Statistical tests show low acceptance rates for accuracy‑based selection across all conditions.
- **Break condition:** If the training loss were perfectly aligned with 0‑1 risk (e.g., in a separable linear case with infinite data), `e*_l,Val` might coincide with `e*_a,Val`. The paper’s varied datasets and parameter ratios test, but do not eliminate, this mismatch.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Early Stopping (with Patience)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper’s primary comparison is between validation criteria under early stopping vs. post‑hoc selection. Understanding how patience‑based stopping works (monitor metric, halt after `T` epochs without improvement, select best‑so‑far checkpoint) is essential to interpret why accuracy fails.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the patience parameter `T` interact with a plateau‑prone metric like accuracy to cause premature stopping?

- **Concept: Loss vs. Metric (Loss‑Metric Mismatch)**
  - *Why needed here:* The core hypothesis is that validation should use a loss (surrogate) rather than the task metric (accuracy). This requires understanding why training uses cross‑entropy (differentiable, probabilistic) while evaluation uses accuracy (decision‑based, non‑differentiable), and how this extends to validation.
  - *Quick check question:* Why can’t we simply use accuracy on the validation set to select models if it’s our final goal? What property of accuracy makes it problematic for this iterative selection?

- **Concept: k‑fold Cross‑Validation for Model Selection**
  - *Why needed here:* The experimental protocol uses 10‑fold stratified CV to compute acceptance rates. This is not just for evaluation but for pairing selected‑model test accuracy with test‑optimal accuracy across folds. The statistical tests (paired t‑test/Wilcoxon) depend on this paired structure.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is paired statistical testing across folds necessary here, and what would be the flaw in averaging per‑dataset results without fold pairing?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data loading & preprocessing → stratified 10‑fold split → per‑fold train/validation split (85/15) → training loop (SGD, per‑epoch logging of training loss and all validation criteria) → per‑criterion selection (early stopping or post‑hoc) → test evaluation → statistical aggregation across folds.

- **Critical path:** For each dataset, each fold, each parameter ratio `r`, and each training loss:  
  1. Preprocess features using training statistics only.  
  2. Train MLP for up to 20 000 epochs, recording every epoch: training loss, validation CE, C‑Loss, PolyLoss, accuracy.  
  3. For each validation criterion, determine selected epoch: (a) early stopping (monitor criterion, keep best‑so‑far, stop after T epochs without improvement); (b) post‑hoc (best over all epochs).  
  4. Compute selected model’s test accuracy and the test‑optimal accuracy (max over all epochs).  
  5. Store paired values for that configuration.  
  6. After all folds, perform paired hypothesis test and record acceptance.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Shallow network: isolates validation‑criterion effects, avoiding confounding depth dynamics; may not generalize to deep architectures.  
  - Fixed maximum epochs (20 000): ensures post‑hoc selection has full trajectory; may be excessive for some regimes but guarantees convergence.  
  - Validation set size fixed at 15%: keeps experiments manageable; the study does not explore size effects.  
  - Limited loss functions (CE, C‑Loss, PolyLoss): chosen for diversity; results may not extend to other losses but suggest any continuous loss works.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High variance in the epoch chosen by accuracy across folds for the same dataset/model.  
  - Acceptance rate for accuracy consistently near 0% even with large patience.  
  - Large gap between test accuracy of accuracy‑selected model and the test‑optimal accuracy, regardless of parameter regime.  
  - Discrepancy between `e*_a,Val` and `e*_l,Val` epochs across datasets.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline cross‑entropy training:** Train with CE loss. For each fold, compute all validation criteria. For each criterion, apply early stopping (T=10) and post‑hoc selection. Record test accuracy of selected model vs. test‑optimal. Compute acceptance rates across folds/datasets.  
  2. **Patience sensitivity:** Repeat Experiment 1 with T=50 to check whether larger patience mitigates accuracy’s poor performance.  
  3. **Training loss robustness:** Repeat Experiment 1 with C‑Loss and PolyLoss as training objectives. Verify that loss‑based validation superiority persists regardless of training loss.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do different validation criteria perform when the final evaluation metric is not accuracy but other metrics such as F1, MCC, or PR‑AUC?  
2. How does class imbalance affect the effectiveness of validation criteria for model selection in terms of test accuracy?  
3. Do the findings regarding validation criteria generalize to larger‑scale regimes, such as deeper neural networks or higher parameter‑to‑sample ratios?  
4. What is the impact of validation‑set size on the reliability and performance of different validation criteria for model selection?  
5. Can combining a loss‑based validation selector with post‑selection threshold tuning reduce the gap between selected model test accuracy and test‑optimal accuracy?

## Limitations
- Exact dataset subsets used in some figures are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Random seeds for initialization, data shuffling, and fold generation are not reported, which may introduce variability.
- The multi‑class implementation of C‑Loss is vaguely described (one‑vs‑rest aggregation), leaving room for interpretation.
- Validation set size is fixed at 15%; the paper does not explore its effect on the observed patterns.

## Confidence
- **Loss‑based criteria are more stable than accuracy for early stopping** – High confidence.
- **The acceptance rate gap is robust to training loss and patience** – Medium confidence.
- **The mismatch between training loss and validation accuracy causes misalignment** – Medium confidence.

## Next Checks

2. **Validate C‑Loss multi‑class aggregation:** Clarify and implement the one‑vs‑rest loss aggregation for validation; compare average vs. sum to ensure results are not sensitive to this choice.  
3. **Vary validation set size:** Replicate experiments with different validation fractions (e.g., 5%, 30%) to test whether accuracy’s plateau problem diminishes with larger validation sets as predicted.
