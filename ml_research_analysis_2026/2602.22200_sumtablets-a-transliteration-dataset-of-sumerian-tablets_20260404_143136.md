# SumTablets: A Transliteration Dataset of Sumerian Tablets
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22200
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22200
- Reference count: 7
- Primary result: Fine-tuned XLM-R achieves 97.55 chrF on glyph-to-transliteration test set.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces SumTablets, a large-scale paired dataset of Sumerian cuneiform tablets comprising 91,606 tablets and 6,970,407 glyphs, created by preprocessing Oracc transliterations and mapping readings to Unicode glyphs using ePSD2 and OSL dictionaries. This dataset addresses the previous lack of aligned glyph-transliteration data, enabling application of NLP methods. Two baseline models are evaluated: a weighted dictionary sampler (61.22 chrF) and a transformer-based encoder-decoder (XLM-R fine-tuned) which achieves a character-level F-score of 97.55, demonstrating the effectiveness of modern models for this task. The dataset and models are released publicly.

## Method Summary
The authors construct SumTablets by standardizing raw transliterations from the Oracc corpus, removing editorial annotations, and converting structural markers (line breaks, surfaces) into special tokens. Each reading in the transliteration is mapped to a canonical glyph name via ePSD2, then to its Unicode cuneiform representation via OSL. This yields parallel sequences of glyphs and transliterations. The dataset is split (90/5/5) stratified by period. For modeling, two SentencePiece tokenizers are trained separately on the glyph (~632 tokens) and transliteration (~1024 tokens) vocabularies. The XLM-R encoder is first fine-tuned via masked language modeling on glyph sequences, then combined with a decoder (initialized from XLM-R with random cross-attention) for sequence-to-sequence training in three stages: (1) MLM on encoder, (2) decoder-only with frozen encoder, (3) joint training of both, with oversampling of non-administrative tablets to mitigate genre imbalance. During generation, beam search is used and performance is measured by chrF++ averaged per tablet.

## Key Results
- XLM-R encoder-decoder model achieves 97.55 chrF on the test set.
- Dictionary sampling baseline: 61.22 chrF.
- Dataset provides 91,606 aligned tablet pairs (6.97M glyphs).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach succeeds due to three intertwined mechanisms.

**1. Reverse mapping yields aligned data.** By starting from scholarly transliterations and systematically mapping each reading back to a canonical glyph name and then to its Unicode form, the authors create parallel input-target pairs. This reverse process is nearly complete (99.9% success) and preserves the correspondences needed for supervised learning. A reading that cannot be mapped becomes an `<UNK>` token, which degrades fidelity but provides robustness.

**2. Multilingual transformer adaptation.** The glyph and transliteration sequences are tokenized separately to handle their disjoint symbol sets. The encoder is first pre-trained with masked language modeling on glyphs, allowing the model to learn internal representations of cuneiform signs within the cross-lingual XLM-R framework. The decoder is then trained autoregressively to generate transliteration tokens, with a staged schedule (freeze encoder, then unfreeze) stabilizing training. The model leverages the structural knowledge embedded in XLM-R to handle Sumerian’s agglutinative morphology despite being an isolate.

**3. Structural tokens maintain layout.** Formatting annotations (line breaks, surface changes, rulings) are converted into special tokens inserted at the corresponding positions in both sequences. This parallel scaffolding ensures the model learns to reproduce lineation and section breaks, which are important for correct transliteration and alignment with the original physical tablet.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cuneiform polyvalence and transliteration conventions**  
  *Why needed:* A single cuneiform glyph can have many valid readings; transliteration uses subscripts and hyphens to resolve ambiguity. The model must select the correct reading based on context.  
  *Quick check:* If a glyph has ~22 possible readings on average, how does the model decide which one to output for a given position?

- **Sequence-to-sequence modeling with tokenizers for non-standard alphabets**  
  *Why needed:* Inputs are Unicode glyphs while outputs are Latin-script transliterations; separate tokenizers are required because the symbol sets are disjoint.  
  *Quick check:* Why can't a single tokenizer handle both glyphs and transliterations? What problem would that cause?

- **Data imbalance and stratification**  
  *Why needed:* The corpus is dominated by administrative texts; rare periods/genres risk poor performance. Stratifying the train/val/test splits by period ensures each period is represented.  
  *Quick check:* If you randomly split the tablet data, what systematic bias might appear in the test set, and why does stratifying by period ameliorate it?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Pipeline → Tokenizer Trainer → Encoder → Decoder → Seq2Seq Model → Evaluation

**Critical path:**  
1. Run preprocessing script to convert Oracc JSON into aligned glyph/transliteration strings with structural tokens.  
2. Train separate SentencePiece tokenizers on the glyph and transliteration columns.  
3. Fine-tune the XLM-R encoder via masked language modeling on glyph tokens (50 epochs).  
4. Build the encoder-decoder by initializing the decoder from XLM-R and combining with the pre-trained encoder.  
5. Train the seq2seq model in three stages (decoder-only with frozen encoder, then joint), using oversampling for non-admin tablets.  
6. Generate predictions on the test set (beam search, size 5) and compute chrF++ per tablet.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Separate vs. shared tokenizer:* Separate tokenizers respect the disjoint alphabets and avoid vocab pollution; a shared tokenizer would force unification and reduce efficiency.  
- *MLM pre‑training:* Adds cost but stabilizes encoder representations; skipping may harm convergence.  
- *Oversampling non‑admin:* Boosts minority genre performance but risks overfitting; the authors decayed the oversampling factor.  
- *Chunking at 128 tokens:* Fits GPU memory but breaks long tablets; slicing at newline boundaries preserves structural integrity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High `<UNK>` rate in outputs indicates mapping or tokenizer failures for rare glyphs/readings.  
- Large chrF gap between administrative and literary genres reveals genre imbalance or insufficient representation.  
- Missing structural tokens (`\n`, `<SURFACE>`) in generations suggests the model did not learn to emit them, possibly due to inconsistent preprocessing.  
- High token accuracy but low chrF points to character ordering errors (swaps) that chrF penalizes.

**First three experiments to reproduce:**  
1. Replicate dataset construction: Preprocess Oracc and verify that glyph sequences match expected examples (e.g., 𒀭𒂗𒆤 → {d}en-lil2).  
2. Dictionary baseline sanity check: Compute weighted sampling baseline on a held‑out set; confirm chrF ~61.  
3. Minimal model test: Train a small model on a 1k‑tablet subset to ensure the pipeline runs and yields non‑random scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Non‑Unicode glyph representation** – Some complex compound glyphs are absent from Unicode and are converted to `<UNK>`, losing information. An extended symbolic inventory (e.g., custom IDs) could preserve details, but this remains untested.  
2. **Optimal handling of genre/period imbalance** – The authors used simple oversampling (5× then 3×) but did not compare to other strategies like class‑weighted loss or advanced resampling. The impact on minority genres is unclear.  
3. **Inconsistent transliteration conventions** – Variants such as “saŋ” vs. “sag” fragment pattern learning. No normalization pipeline was implemented; the extent of performance loss is unknown.  
4. **Zero‑ and few‑shot transfer** – Only full fine‑tuning was studied. The sample‑efficiency and inherent cross‑lingual knowledge of XLM‑R on this isolate language remain unexplored.

## Limitations
- Exact chunking logic (e.g., precise token count thresholds, whether line splits always occur at newlines) is not fully specified, which may affect model learning and evaluation.
- The mapping of structural annotations to special tokens might be inconsistent across the corpus, risking misalignment between glyphs and transliterations.
- The upsampling strategy (duplication vs. weighted sampler) is ambiguous, introducing uncertainty in training dynamics and reproducibility.
