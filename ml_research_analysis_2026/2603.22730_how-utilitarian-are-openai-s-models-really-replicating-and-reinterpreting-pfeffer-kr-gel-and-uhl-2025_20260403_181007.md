# How Utilitarian Are OpenAI's Models Really? Replicating and Reinterpreting Pfeffer, Krügel, and Uhl (2025)

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22730
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22730
- **Reference count:** 4
- **Primary result:** Prompt framing drastically alters measured utilitarian responses; GPT-4o's low trolley rate stemmed from safety refusals, not deontological reasoning, and reasoning models produce more stable but not necessarily higher utilitarian judgments across variants.

## Executive Summary
This study replicates and reinterprets Pfeffer et al.'s (2025) investigation of utilitarian responses in OpenAI models. Using four models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o3, o3-mini) and four prompt variants for trolley and footbridge dilemmas, the author finds that the original claim—that reasoning models are more utilitarian—does not hold once prompt confounds are removed. On the trolley dilemma, all models converge to nearly 100% utilitarian responses with neutral phrasing, indicating that advisory framing ("Should I...?") triggers safety refusals that were misinterpreted as anti-utilitarian stances. On the footbridge dilemma, reasoning models show higher utilitarian rates on described prompts (43–71%) but also higher refusal rates. Crucially, single-prompt designs are highly unreliable; even minor wording changes cause swings of tens of percentage points. The work underscores the necessity of multi-prompt robustness testing to infer genuine model preferences.

## Method Summary
The replication follows Pfeffer et al.'s design: for each of four OpenAI models, responses were collected to trolley and footbridge moral dilemmas under four prompt variants: original ("Should I pull the lever?"), reversed answer order ("No or Yes"), described (full scenario narrative), and neutral ("Is it morally permissible...?"). Data collection used the OpenAI API with N=1000 per model per dilemma for the original prompt (to match the prior study) and N=100 for variant prompts. Responses were classified into four categories per the original scheme: "Yes" (utilitarian), "No" (non-utilitarian), "Other" (engaged but unclear), and "N/A" (refusal or non-engagement). The analysis compared response distributions across models and prompt variants, focusing on the proportion of "Yes" answers while accounting for non-engagement rates.

## Key Results
- On the **trolley dilemma**, the original advisory prompt yielded widely divergent utilitarian rates: GPT-4o-mini gave 100% "Yes", while GPT-4o gave only 14% "Yes" due to 69% refusals ("N/A"). With neutral phrasing, GPT-4o's utilitarian rate jumped to 99%, and all models converged to 99–100% across described and neutral prompts.
- On the **footbridge dilemma**, reasoning models (o3, o3-mini) produced higher utilitarian rates on the described prompt (43–71%) than non-reasoning models (0%), but also exhibited higher refusal rates on the original prompt. GPT-4o-mini was an outlier, reaching 92% utilitarian on the neutral prompt.
- Prompt variants caused massive swings within the same model: for example, GPT-4o's footbridge utilitarian rate ranged from 0% to 92% across four variants. Additionally, non-engagement ("Other", "N/A") accounted for most of the variance, not a shift in engaged "Yes" vs. "No" choices.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Advisory framing triggers safety protocols.** Queries phrased as user directives ("Should I pull the lever?") activate the model's safety alignment, which is tuned to avoid assisting with harmful actions. This produces non-engagement (refusals or evasive answers) that is misinterpreted as a deontological moral stance. Evidence: GPT-4o's utilitarian rate on the original trolley prompt was only 14%, but with the neutral prompt ("Is it morally permissible...?") it soared to 99% while refusals dropped from 69% to near zero. If safety refusals persisted even with neutral phrasing or if base (non-aligned) models showed the same pattern, this mechanism would be falsified.

**Mechanism 2: Reasoning architecture yields stable, not more utilitarian, outputs.** Reasoning-specific models (o3, o3-mini) employ chain-of-thought or extended deliberation, which abstracts away from superficial prompt features (e.g., answer order, wording). This yields less variability in utilitarian responses across prompt variants. On the trolley dilemma, reasoning models maintained 86–100% "Yes" across all variants, whereas non-reasoning models fluctuated wildly (e.g., GPT-4o: 14% to 100%). The stability does not necessarily imply higher average utilitarian propensity; it indicates that deliberation produces consistent moral judgments once the model is engaged. If reasoning models showed similar prompt sensitivity on other reasoning-heavy tasks (e.g., math, coding), this robustness claim would weaken.

**Mechanism 3: Non-engagement is the primary confound.** High rates of "Other" and "N/A" responses obscure true model preferences. These categories often shift dramatically across prompt variants, changing the observed "Yes" percentage even if engaged responses ("Yes" vs. "No") remain stable. For GPT-4o on the original trolley prompt, only 30.3% answered "Yes" while 13.7% refused and ~48% gave unclear answers ("Other"). The neutral prompt reduced "Other" from 48% to 0–1%, explaining the jump in "Yes". Researchers must analyze non-engagement rates rather than exclude them; what looks like refusal or indecisiveness may mask a utilitarian disposition. If non-engagement rates were consistently low and random across variants, this confound would be negligible.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Prompt Sensitivity & Format Effects**  
  *Why needed:* LLM outputs are highly sensitive to superficial wording changes. A single-word rephrase ("Should I" vs. "Is it permissible") can invert a model's apparent moral stance. Ignoring this leads to false conclusions about model "beliefs."  
  *Quick check:* To test if a model holds a consistent moral view, how many *semantically equivalent* prompt formulations must you test to be confident? Empirically, at least 3–4 variants to detect framing effects.

- **Concept: Distinguishing Alignment Artifacts from Cognitive Processes**  
  *Why needed:* Safety alignment (refusals, hedges) is often mistaken for genuine moral reasoning. A low "utilitarian" score may reflect a model's guardrails, not its ethical judgment. Separating what the model *is allowed* to say from what it *would choose* if free is essential.  
  *Quick check:* If a model refuses to answer a harm-related question, what two experiments could determine whether this is a blanket safety rule or a specific moral objection? (1) Vary advisory framing (e.g., "Should I" vs. "Is it permissible") while keeping scenario constant; (2) Remove any hint of user agency (e.g., "Is it morally right to...") and see if refusals persist.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component Map: API Client -> Prompt Template Engine -> Response Parser & Classifier -> Metrics Aggregator -> Configuration Store.

Critical Path:
1. Define exact replication prompts and all variant wording.
2. For each (model, prompt_variant) cell, submit N independent API calls.
3. Log raw response, system_fingerprint, and timestamp per call.
4. Classify each response into Yes/No/Other/N/A using deterministic rules.
5. Aggregate category rates per cell; compute confidence intervals.
6. Compare across variants for same model (framing effects) and across models for same variant (reasoning impact).
7. Check within-cell stability (e.g., >5% N/A indicates prompt issues).

Design Tradeoffs:
- N per cell: Original used 1000 (high precision); for robustness checks, N=100 suffices if effects are large (>20pp).
- Forced-choice vs. open-ended: Forced-choice enables clean comparison but may suppress nuance; open-ended requires external classification.
- Classification fidelity: Matching the original scheme is crucial for replication, but may group semantically distinct responses (e.g., long ethical discussions as "Other").

Failure Signatures:
- High N/A or Other rates (>40%): prompt likely triggers safety filters or is ambiguous; "Yes%" is not a clean signal.
- Temporal drift: same (model, prompt) cell shows >10pp "Yes%" change between batches; check system_fingerprint.
- Model-specific quirks: susceptibility to answer order (e.g., "Yes or No" vs. "No or Yes") invalidates assumptions of option-order independence.

First 3 Experiments:
1. **Exact Replication:** Run original Pfeffer et al. prompts for all models with N=1000. Reproduce the *pattern* (reasoning > non-reasoning on footbridge; mixed on trolley), not necessarily identical percentages.
2. **Neutral Prompt Unlock:** For each model, run the neutral trolley prompt ("Is it morally permissible...?"). Expect >95% utilitarian "Yes" across all models, confirming that advisory framing was the confound.
3. **Temporal Stability Check:** Immediately repeat the original trolley prompt for a single model (e.g., GPT-4o) in a second API session days later. Document shifts in "Yes%"/"N/A%" and record system_fingerprints to detect serving-layer changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What causes temporal drift in moral dilemma responses for fixed LLM checkpoints?** The author observed GPT-4o's trolley utilitarian rate drop 16 percentage points over nine days with identical prompts, suggesting possible serving-layer updates. However, without system_fingerprint logging, the exact source (safety filter updates, infrastructure changes) remains unclear. Resolution requires longitudinal logging and controlled ablations.
2. **Why does advisory framing ("Should I...?") trigger safety refusals in some models but not others?** The shift from 69% refusals (original) to ~0% (neutral) for GPT-4o shows framing sensitivity, but the underlying model-specific safety tuning differences are not analyzed. Comparative studies of safety training data or internal activation patching could reveal the cause.
3. **Why do reasoning models show higher utilitarian responses on described footbridge prompts while non-reasoning models remain anti-utilitarian?** Reasoning models' utilitarian rates jump to 43–71% on described variant but stay ≤5% on original; non-reasoning models stay at 0% except GPT-4o-mini neutral. This suggests divergent processing of scenario details, but without inspecting reasoning traces or value encoding, the mechanism is unknown.
4. **How can researchers reliably disentangle safety-driven non-engagement from genuine moral indecision?** Current practice lumps refusals and evasive answers into "Other/N/A," conflating alignment artifacts with ethical doubt. A validated response taxonomy (e.g., via human annotation) correlated with internal confidence scores or safety classifier activations could separate these factors.

## Limitations
- The exact wording of the "described" prompt was inferred conceptually and may not match the original study's phrasing.
- Model snapshots used in this replication may differ from those in the original experiment due to deprecations and serving-layer updates, potentially introducing drift.
- Classification heuristics for Yes/No/Other/N/A may misgroup nuanced or multilingual responses, despite efforts to mirror the original scheme.

## Confidence
- *Prompt framing drastically changes measured utilitarian rates:* **High** (large within-model swings consistently observed).
- *Reasoning models yield more stable (but not necessarily higher) utilitarian answers:* **Medium** (evident on footbridge; needs cross-dilemma verification).
- *Original low GPT-4o trolley utilitarian rate was due to safety refusals, not deontological commitment:* **High** (neutral prompt nearly eliminates refusals and raises utilitarian rate to 99%).
- *Non-engagement (Other/N/A) is a primary confound that obscures underlying preferences:* **High** (directly measured and explains most variance in "Yes%").

## Next Checks
1. **Expand prompt diversity:** Create 10–15 paraphrases per dilemma (varying phrasing, answer format, scenario framing) and run N=200 per model. Test whether the observed stability patterns (e.g., reasoning models' robustness) hold across a larger formulation space and identify phrasing that consistently triggers safety filters.
2. **Generalize to other moral dilemmas:** Replicate the four-variant design on at least two additional classic dilemmas (e.g., Transplant, Lifeboat). This will confirm whether reasoning-model stability and framing effects are specific to trolley-type scenarios or generalizable.
3. **Isolate safety alignment:** Orthogonally vary advisory framing ("Should I take action?" vs. "Is it morally permissible for a bystander to...") while keeping scenario content constant. Use regression to quantify how much variance in "Yes%" is explained by changes in refusal/N/A rates versus genuine moral choice, controlling for engaged responses only.
