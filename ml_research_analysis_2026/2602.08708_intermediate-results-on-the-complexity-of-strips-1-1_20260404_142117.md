# Intermediate Results on the Complexity of STRIPS$_{1}^{1}$

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08708
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08708
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** For STRIPS₁₁ (single precondition, single effect) planning, empirical and theoretical evidence suggests shortest plan lengths grow at most polynomially in the number of variables, with a proven bound of ≤3·2^{n-2} for “good” instances (n≥16), yet NP‑completeness remains open.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the long‑standing open problem of the computational complexity of propositional STRIPS planning with operators that have exactly one precondition and one effect (STRIPS₁₁). The central question is whether shortest plans can be exponential in the number of variables. The authors combine SAT‑based exhaustive search for small numbers of variables (n=5,6) with theoretical analysis of the literal graph and its strongly connected components, as well as compilations to Petri nets and multi‑agent path finding. Their empirical findings show that for n=5 the maximum shortest plan length is 29 actions, and for n=6 it is 39 actions—both far below 2^n. Theoretical arguments for “good” tasks (where each variable has two actions setting it with different preconditions) yield an upper bound of ≤3·2^{n-2} for n≥16, and specific generated families exhibit only Θ(n²) growth. Although these results support the “small solution hypothesis” that shortest plans are polynomial, and the problem may be in NP, a formal proof of NP‑completeness or containment is still lacking.

## Method Summary
The authors employ two complementary approaches. First, they encode the existence of a shortest plan of length L for a given STRIPS₁₁ instance as a SAT problem, with variables representing actions, states, and BFS reachability, and use the Kissat solver to determine the maximum L by iterating L and checking satisfiability. This exhaustive search was applied to all possible action sets for n=5 and to a selected subset for n=6, yielding optimal lengths 29 and 39 respectively. Second, they develop a theoretical framework: the “literal graph” G (nodes = literals, edges = actions) is analyzed via strongly connected components; for “good” instances they prove a bound on shortest plans by counting bidirectional edges in the hypercube‑structured state graph. They also construct infinite families of tasks (Pₙ, Qₙ, Rₙ) and show they require Θ(n²) steps, and they compile STRIPS₁₁ to conservative safe Petri nets to relate the problem to known complexity classes.

## Key Results
- For n=5 variables, the maximum shortest plan length across all STRIPS₁₁ instances is 29 actions (or 30 states), proven via SAT.
- For n=6, a 39‑action (40‑state) plan was found and proved optimal; SAT required weeks of computation.
- For “good” tasks with n≥16, shortest plan length is at most 3·2^{n-2}; this sub‑exponential bound combined with empirical evidence suggests polynomial growth, though a general polynomial bound remains unproven.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hypercube Dimensionality and Action Coverage
Each STRIPS₁₁ action ⟨l₁, l₂⟩ corresponds to a directed copy of an (n‑2)-dimensional hypercube Q_{n‑2} embedded in the full n‑dimensional hypercube of states. Since Q_n has 2^n vertices and n·2^{n-1} edges, but only Θ(n²) distinct STRIPS₁₁ actions exist, no set of actions can cover all vertices with disjoint paths. An exponential‑length shortest plan would need to traverse nearly all vertices with disjoint edges, which is impossible because each action’s Q_{n‑2} subcube overlaps heavily with others. This geometric argument limits the longest shortest plan to be sub‑exponential.

### Mechanism 2: Literal Graph SCC and Subplan Disjointness
The literal graph G has at most 2n nodes and O(n²) edges. For any shortest plan, the subplans for individual goal literals share a common prefix but after diverging operate on disjoint sets of edges within the SCC that contains the goal literal. Because each SCC contains O(n²) edges and each subplan can flip variables only O(n) times, the total plan length is bounded by O(n³). However, the required disjointness property is not proven for all instances; a failed LLM‑assisted proof identified a potential flaw in the tree‑height argument.

### Mechanism 3: Bidirectional Edge Density in Good Instances
In a “good” STRIPS₁₁ instance, for each variable v there exist two actions with effect v but different preconditions, yielding many pairs of opposite directed edges (bidirectional edges) in the state graph. For n≥16, at least 2^{n-2} vertices each have ≥ n/4 such bidirectional incident edges. In a shortest path, at most two vertices from the neighborhood of any such vertex can be used without creating a shortcut, forcing the path to omit at least 2^{n-2} states. This counting argument gives the bound ≤3·2^{n-2} on the number of visited states (or actions). The bound relies crucially on the “good” property; for non‑good instances the density of bidirectional edges may be much lower.

## Foundational Learning
- **Hypercube Graph Theory:** STRIPS₁₁ states are vertices of an n‑dimensional hypercube Q_n; actions correspond to subcubes. Understanding vertex/edge counts and subcube embeddings is essential to evaluate exponential length possibilities. Quick check: How many directed edges does Q_n have? (Answer: n·2^{n-1}.) How many distinct STRIPS₁₁ actions exist for n variables? (At most 4n², reduced to 4n²–4n when pre and eff cannot share a variable.)
- **STRIPS₁₁ Semantics:** An action ⟨pre, eff⟩ can be applied only if the precondition literal holds; the state update flips the effect literal. Misunderstanding these leads to incorrect modeling. Quick check: Can an action with pre=¬v and eff=v be applied in a state where v is true? (Answer: No, because pre ¬v does not hold.)
- **Strongly Connected Components (SCCs) in Directed Graphs:** SCCs group nodes mutually reachable; they are used to argue that subplans after divergence stay within an SCC. Quick check: If an SCC has 5 nodes and 6 edges, what is the maximum number of edges in any subgraph induced by a subset of these nodes? (Answer: ≤6, as the SCC contains all edges among its nodes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (plain text): Problem Generator -> SAT Encoder -> Literal Graph Analyzer -> Bidirectional Edge Counter -> Plan Extractor

Critical path: For a given n, generate a STRIPS₁₁ instance, check if it is “good”; if good, compute the bidirectional edge bound (Theorem 1); otherwise, compute the literal graph and its SCCs. Compare the derived theoretical bounds with empirical results from the SAT solver for n=5,6 to validate.

Design tradeoffs: SAT solving provides exact optimal lengths but scales poorly (n=6 took weeks). Theoretical analysis yields general bounds but often requires the “good” assumption. The “overlap tree” model is illustrative but not algorithmic. Relying on the good‑instance condition simplifies proofs but may not cover all cases.

Failure signatures: (1) SAT returns UNSAT for length L but SAT for L‑1 → L‑1 is optimal (as used for n=5,6). (2) Literal graph SCC size exceeds O(n²) → the O(n³) bound may fail. (3) Bidirectional edge count < n·2^{n-3} → instance is not good; Theorem 1 does not apply.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Is the STRIPS₁₁ plan existence problem in NP?** Equivalently, does every solvable STRIPS₁₁ instance have a shortest plan whose length is polynomial in n? The paper finds partial evidence but no proof, leaving NP‑completeness status unresolved.
2. **Can an O(n³) upper bound be proven for all STRIPS₁₁ instances?** An LLM‑generated proof attempt proposed such a bound but contained errors (e.g., an incorrect claim about overlap tree height). Whether a correct polynomial bound exists remains open.
3. **What is the complexity of reachability in conservative safe Petri nets?** While general safe Petri net reachability is PSPACE‑complete, the effect of conservativity (total token count invariant) is unknown; it might reduce the problem to NP‑complete.

## Limitations
- Theoretical bounds (e.g., ≤3·2^{n-2}) apply only to “good” instances; many practical or random instances may not satisfy this condition.
- The subplan‑disjointness property underpinning the O(n³) bound lacks a general proof; the failed LLM attempt highlights its fragility.
- SAT‑based exhaustive search was limited to n≤6 due to combinatorial explosion; larger n rely on unproven theoretical arguments.
- The SAT encoding details for the BFS reachability variables are not fully specified in the paper, hindering faithful reproduction and extension.
- Constructed families (Pₙ, Qₙ, Rₙ) show only quadratic growth but do not rule out super‑polynomial worst‑case behavior.
