# Trade-offs Between Individual and Group Fairness in Machine Learning: A Comprehensive Review

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.00094
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00094
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Survey finds no method fully reconciles group and individual fairness under standard definitions; 60% of joint approaches are in-processing with regularization dominant; evaluations rely heavily on Adult/COMPAS; only 56% release code; computational costs rarely analyzed; most assume sensitive attributes available.

## Executive Summary
This comprehensive review surveys methods that jointly address group fairness (GF) and individual fairness (IF), two objectives known to be mathematically incompatible under standard definitions. Analyzing 40 papers, the authors introduce a taxonomy that organizes hybrid approaches by fairness mechanism (regularization, adversarial learning, thresholding) rather than pipeline stage. Key findings reveal that in-processing methods dominate (60% of the corpus), with regularization the most common technique. Empirical evaluations concentrate on a small set of tabular datasets (Adult, COMPAS, German Credit), and reproducibility is limited, with only 56% of methods providing code. Moreover, computational costs are seldom reported, and most approaches rely on strong assumptions, such as full access to sensitive attributes during training. The survey concludes that while trade-offs between GF and IF can be managed, no existing method achieves full reconciliation without context-specific constraints, highlighting the need for more scalable, domain-diverse, and principle-driven fairness frameworks.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a systematic literature review targeting machine learning research that explicitly combines group and individual fairness. They compiled a corpus of 40 relevant papers and developed a novel taxonomy classifying methods by their core fairness mechanism—regularization, adversarial learning, or thresholding—instead of by the traditional preprocessing/in-processing/post-processing stages. For each paper, information was extracted on the fairness definitions employed, algorithmic details, empirical evaluation (datasets, fairness and accuracy metrics), code availability, and computational considerations. This enabled quantitative analysis of prevalence trends (e.g., the share of in-processing methods), identification of common benchmark datasets, and a critical assessment of methodological gaps such as limited computational reporting and strong assumptions about sensitive attribute availability.

## Key Results
- Group and individual fairness are mathematically incompatible under standard definitions; no method fully reconciles them without context-specific constraints.
- 60% of joint GF-IF methods are in-processing, with regularization being the most common mechanism.
- Evaluation heavily relies on a few tabular benchmarks (Adult, COMPAS); only 56% of methods release code; computational costs are rarely analyzed; and most assume access to sensitive attributes during training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Regularization-based in-processing
- **Claim**: Regularization-based in-processing methods can manage GF–IF trade-offs by jointly penalizing group disparity and individual inconsistency.
- **Mechanism**: Augment the standard loss with separate penalty terms: a group fairness term (e.g., covariance between predictions and sensitive attribute) and an individual fairness term (e.g., Lipschitz penalty on prediction differences across similar instances). The optimization navigates the Pareto frontier via weighting hyperparameters λ<sub>GF</sub>, λ<sub>IF</sub>.
- **Core assumption**: Sensitive attributes are available during training; a suitable similarity metric d<sub>F</sub> for individual fairness is defined and computable.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states "60% of methods are in-processing, with regularization being most common." Section 3.1 describes regularization as augmenting the loss with group and individual penalties. Corpus includes works like DualFair [204758] that use contrastive regularization.
- **Break condition**: The regularization weights induce excessive smoothing that collapses class boundaries, harming accuracy; or the Lipschitz constant is poorly matched to data density, causing under-penalization of unfair pairs.

### Mechanism 2: Adversarial learning
- **Claim**: Adversarial learning with an auxiliary adversary can promote group fairness (by removing S-information from representations) and, when augmented with pairwise/counterfactual losses, also promote individual fairness.
- **Mechanism**: A predictor f is trained to minimize prediction loss while maximizing an adversary g's inability to predict S from f(X). For IF, add a term that enforces similar outputs for counterfactual pairs (X, S) and (X, ¬S) or for neighbors in feature space.
- **Core assumption**: The adversary is sufficiently expressive and trained to convergence; the predictor's architecture can learn S-invariant representations without catastrophic loss of predictive signal.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions adversarial learning as a fairness mechanism. Section 3.1 details adversarial learning and extensions for IF. Corpus includes Liu & Wong 2024 (paper [39]) that combines adversarial learning with IF/GF losses.
- **Break condition**: The adversary fails to converge or becomes too weak, failing to enforce S-independence; or the added IF penalties dominate, leading to degenerate constant outputs.

### Mechanism 3: Post-processing thresholding
- **Claim**: Post-processing thresholding with group-specific thresholds can enforce group fairness (e.g., equalized odds) and, when combined with similarity-based smoothing, can partially satisfy individual fairness.
- **Mechanism**: After training a score-producing model f, learn group-specific thresholds τ<sub>s</sub> to satisfy group constraints (e.g., equal TPR/FPR across S). For IF, adjust predictions within a reject option band [α, β] using a rule that also respects local similarity (e.g., smoothing predictions of nearby instances).
- **Core assumption**: The base model's score distribution is well-calibrated and sufficiently expressive to allow threshold adjustments without large accuracy loss; a reliable local similarity measure exists.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract lists thresholding as a fairness mechanism. Section 3.1 explains thresholding with group-specific thresholds and notes extensions for IF via similarity metrics. Section 2.4 describes post-processing methods like calibrated equalized-odds adjustments.
- **Break condition**: Score distributions of different sensitive groups do not overlap sufficiently, causing large accuracy drops when equalizing error rates; or the local similarity metric is noisy, leading to inconsistent adjustments that violate IF.

## Foundational Learning
### Group vs. Individual Fairness Definitions
- **Why needed**: Understanding the precise definitions of group fairness (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds) and individual fairness (e.g., Lipschitz condition, counterfactual fairness) is essential because these are the incompatible objectives being traded off. Clear definitions prevent conflation of metrics and misdiagnosis of trade-off sources.
- **Quick check**: Does satisfying equalized odds guarantee that similar individuals receive similar predictions? Why or why not?

### Trade-off Incompatibility Theorems
- **Why needed**: Formal incompatibility theorems (e.g., the impossibility result that a predictor cannot simultaneously satisfy demographic parity and individual consistency) provide the theoretical grounding that GF and IF cannot both be perfectly satisfied under standard definitions. This justifies the need for hybrid methods that manage trade-offs.
- **Quick check**: Under what trivial conditions (e.g., data distributions) could both GF and IF be perfectly achieved?

### Pipeline Stages (PRE/IN/POST)
- **Why needed**: The choice of preprocessing, in-processing, or post-processing determines where the fairness mechanism is inserted, impacting practical assumptions (e.g., access to sensitive attributes) and computational profile.
- **Quick check**: What is a key advantage and disadvantage of post-processing versus in-processing for joint GF–IF methods?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data Input (X, Y, S) → Fairness Mechanism (hybrid method) → Base Model (predictor) → Output (predictions Ŷ, fairness metrics)

### Critical Path
- **In-processing**: Load data → define model with fairness-augmented loss → train with multi-objective optimization → evaluate GF/IF on holdout.
- **Post-processing**: Train base model → fit fairness adjustment module (e.g., thresholds) on validation set → apply to test predictions.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Computational cost**: In-processing (especially adversarial/representation learning) increases training time and memory; post-processing adds a lightweight calibration step.
- **Assumption strength**: In-processing typically requires S during training; some post-processing methods only need S at adjustment time.
- **Flexibility vs. guarantees**: Regularization offers smooth trade-off control via λ weights; thresholding gives exact group fairness but may hurt IF more.

### Failure Signatures
- Accuracy collapses while fairness metrics improve only marginally → λ weights too high or IF penalty too strong.
- Group fairness constraint not met despite high λ<sub>GF</sub> → mechanism mismatch (e.g., using a regularizer for a separation-based notion) or poor optimizer convergence.
- Individual fairness metric (e.g., consistency on nearest neighbors) degrades → similarity metric d<sub>F</sub> poorly chosen or misaligned.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Sanity check on Adult dataset**: Implement a baseline logistic regression; apply a simple in-processing regularization (covariance penalty for demographic parity + Lipschitz penalty). Plot accuracy vs. ΔDP and vs. individual inconsistency rate.
2. **Mechanism comparison**: For the same λ values, compare three mechanisms (regularization, adversarial, post-processing thresholding) on COMPAS. Measure the Pareto frontier between equalized odds difference and local pairwise consistency.
3. **Assumption stress test**: For an in-processing method requiring S at training, simulate 30% missing sensitive attributes. Compare performance drop against a method designed for partial sensitive attribute observation (e.g., [37]).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**  
  - *Question*: How should evaluation protocols be standardized to jointly quantify fairness-accuracy-computation trade-offs?  
  - *Basis*: [explicit] "need for more systematic evaluation protocols that assess fairness trade-offs alongside computational cost and deployment constraints" (Section 4).  
  - *Why unresolved*: Computational efficiency is rarely treated as a first-order concern; most works report costs qualitatively or not at all, preventing cross-method comparisons.  
  - *What evidence would resolve*: Comprehensive benchmarks measuring runtime, memory, and scalability across diverse datasets and model architectures, correlated with Pareto frontiers of fairness and accuracy.

- **Open Question 2**  
  - *Question*: To what extent do joint GF–IF methods generalize beyond standard tabular benchmarks to high-dimensional or domain-specific settings?  
  - *Basis*: [explicit] "expanding empirical studies to more diverse application domains would improve understanding [...] beyond standard tabular benchmarks" (Section 4).  
  - *Why unresolved*: Over-reliance on Adult, COMPAS, and German Credit; underrepresentation of CV, NLP, or graph domains limits generalizability.  
  - *What evidence would resolve*: Systematic empirical evaluation across multiple data modalities (images, text, networks) with domain-appropriate fairness metrics and validation protocols.

- **Open Question 3**  
  - *Question*: How can causal, legal, and human-centered perspectives be formally integrated to determine context-appropriate fairness trade-offs?  
  - *Basis*: [explicit] "closer integration with causal, legal, and human-centered perspectives could help ground trade-off decisions in contextual and normative considerations" (Section 4).  
  - *Why unresolved*: Current methods reduce trade-offs to technical optimization; lack guidance on aligning parameters with legal standards or stakeholder values.  
  - *What evidence would resolve*: Case studies deriving fairness constraints from legal frameworks or participatory design; user studies assessing acceptability of different trade-off decisions.

- **Open Question 4**  
  - *Question*: How can fairness methods be made practical when sensitive attributes are partially observed or privacy-protected?  
  - *Basis*: [explicit] "most approaches assume access to fully labeled sensitive attributes during training, a strong assumption that is rarely met in real-world applications" (Section 4).  
  - *Why unresolved*: Deployment often lacks complete sensitive labels; only a few works (e.g., [37]) address censored data.  
  - *What evidence would resolve*: Methods provably achieving fairness guarantees with incomplete sensitive supervision, using complementary supervision, bounds, or distributional assumptions.

- **Open Question 5**  
  - *Question*: Is the conflict between group and individual fairness mathematically fundamental, or can it be avoided through alternative formulations?  
  - *Basis*: [inferred] from contrast between theoretical incompatibility results (e.g., [10]) and claims that conflicts may stem from biased data or inappropriate metrics (Section 4, referencing [42]).  
  - *Why unresolved*: It remains unclear under which conditions (e.g., similarity metrics, data distributions, group definitions) joint feasibility is possible and whether apparent trade-offs are inherent or artifacts.  
  - *What evidence would resolve*: Theoretical characterization of sufficient conditions for simultaneously satisfying GF and IF; empirical studies measuring conflict severity across dataset properties and demonstrating regimes of Pareto improvement.

## Limitations
- Survey corpus may be incomplete, especially for recent preprints or non-core venues.
- Over-reliance on Adult and COMPAS datasets threatens domain generalizability due to known biases and limited diversity.
- Computational cost analysis relies on absence of reporting, which might be due to space constraints, leading to uncertain conclusions.
