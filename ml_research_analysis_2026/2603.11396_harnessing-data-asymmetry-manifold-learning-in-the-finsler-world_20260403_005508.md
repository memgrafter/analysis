# Harnessing Data Asymmetry: Manifold Learning in the Finsler World

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11396
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11396
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes Finsler manifold learning to preserve data asymmetry, achieving improved clustering on 16 benchmarks over Euclidean methods.

## Executive Summary
Traditional manifold learning methods (e.g., t‑SNE, UMAP) force symmetric dissimilarities and Euclidean embeddings, discarding asymmetric information arising from non‑uniform sampling or local metric variations. This work adopts Finsler geometry—an asymmetric extension of Riemannian geometry—to retain such information. The authors construct asymmetric pairwise affinities from locally scaled Riemannian metrics without symmetrisation, embed the data into a canonical Randers space, and derive Finsler analogues of t‑SNE and UMAP by replacing Euclidean distances with asymmetric Finsler distances. Explicit gradient formulas enable efficient optimization. Synthetic experiments show that Finsler embeddings expose density hierarchies hidden to symmetric methods. Across 16 classification benchmarks (MNIST, CIFAR‑10, ImageNet), Finsler‑UMAP and Finsler‑t‑SNE consistently yield higher clustering quality (AMI, ARI, NMI) than their Euclidean counterparts, demonstrating superior manifold preservation and the discovery of additional structural information.

## Method Summary
The proposed pipeline modifies each stage of manifold learning: (1) Asymmetric dissimilarities are built by computing local scales σ_i for each point via k‑nearest neighbours and forming directed affinities p_{ij} ∝ exp(-||x_i‑x_j||²/(2σ_i²)) without symmetrisation. (2) The embedding space is equipped with a canonical Randers metric F^C_x(u)=||u||_2+ω^⊤u, which induces an asymmetric distance d_{F^C}(x,y)=||x‑y||+ω^⊤(x‑y). (3) For t‑SNE and UMAP, the Euclidean distances in the objective are replaced by these Finsler distances; the authors derive closed‑form gradients (Theorem 1, 2) to enable gradient‑based optimization. Initialization uses a spectral embedding (PCA/Isomap) in ℝ^m, then pads with a zero coordinate and rotates so that ω aligns with the new axis. The approach generalizes to Finsler‑MDS via a gradient‑descent variant, preserving asymmetry and allowing the extra dimension to encode density variations.

## Key Results
- On synthetic planar disk and Swiss roll datasets, Finsler embeddings reveal density hierarchies that Euclidean methods blend into uniform representations.
- Across 16 classification benchmarks (MNIST, CIFAR‑10, ImageNet, etc.), Finsler‑UMAP and Finsler‑t‑SNE systematically improve clustering metrics (Adjusted Mutual Information, Adjusted Rand Index, Normalized Mutual Information) over their Euclidean versions.
- The paper provides efficient gradient updates for Finsler‑based objectives, making the methods practical for medium‑scale data (up to ~30k points).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Asymmetric dissimilarities capture sampling‑induced information
Traditional manifold learning approximates Riemannian distances using locally scaled metrics (R_x(u)=||u||/σ_x). Combined with a k‑NN graph, this yields asymmetric affinities p_{ij}=h(R_{x_i}(x_j‑x_i)) because σ_i≠σ_j. Conventional pipelines then symmetrise these values, discarding directional cues about density. By retaining p_{ij} as‑is, the algorithm preserves information about local sampling variations and directed relationships. This works when non‑uniform sampling is meaningful; if the data are uniformly sampled or the manifold is truly Riemannian, the asymmetry may be negligible.

### Mechanism 2: Finsler geometry enables principled embedding of asymmetric distances
The canonical Randers metric F^C_x(u)=||u||_2+ω^⊤u (with ||ω||_{M^{‑1}}<1) defines an asymmetric distance d_{F^C}(x,y)=||x‑y||+ω^⊤(x‑y). By replacing Euclidean distances in t‑SNE/UMAP objectives with this Finsler distance, the methods directly minimise asymmetric divergence without forcing symmetry. The drift vector ω introduces a preferred direction, allowing the embedding to separate points based on density: sparse regions tend to be placed further along the ω‑axis. The authors derive explicit gradients (Theorem 1 for t‑SNE, Theorem 2 for UMAP) that incorporate the asymmetry. This generalisation is mathematically sound and reduces to the Euclidean version when ω→0. The approach assumes a single asymmetry direction; more complex asymmetric structures might require more general Finsler metrics.

### Mechanism 3: Asymmetric embeddings expose density hierarchies and improve clustering
In Finsler embeddings, the extra dimension (introduced by the constant drift ω) encodes local density differences: points with large σ (sparse regions) are pushed to higher values along the ω axis, creating a visible hierarchy. This additional structural information aids downstream tasks such as clustering. Empirically, on 16 classification benchmarks, Finsler‑UMAP and Finsler‑t‑SNE achieve higher AMI, ARI, and NMI than Euclidean methods, confirming that preserving asymmetry yields superior manifold preservation. The gains are consistent across datasets, suggesting that real‑world data often contain meaningful asymmetric cues from sampling or metric variations. However, if the data lack informative asymmetry, the extra dimension may introduce noise or distort spherical cluster shapes.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Asymmetric dissimilarity builder → Finsler distance module → Objective & optimiser (with initialisation)

**Critical path:** 1. Build asymmetric affinities (kNN graph, local scales σ_i, compute directed p_{ij}). 2. Initialise embedding in ℝ^{m+1} via spectral method, pad with zero, rotate so ω aligns with new axis. 3. Compute pairwise Finsler distances q_{ij}^{F} = ||y_i‑y_j|| + ω^⊤(y_i‑y_j) and transform (e.g., Student‑t). 4. Compute gradient using derived formulas (Theorem 1 for t‑SNE, Theorem 2 for UMAP). 5. Update embedding (Adam/SGD) and repeat until convergence.

**Design tradeoffs:** 
- Finsler metric choice: Canonical Randers is simple but limited to one asymmetry direction; more complex metrics may capture multi‑directional asymmetry but increase optimisation complexity.
- Embedding dimension: Uses ℝ^{m+1} for an m‑dimensional manifold; extra dimension encodes density but must be used carefully to avoid distortion.
- Sparsity vs densification: t‑SNE uses sparse affinities for local focus; UMAP extends distances for global consistency; choice affects trade‑off between local and global structure.
- Computational cost: Finsler distances and gradients add overhead relative to Euclidean; Finsler‑MDS (SMACOF) scales poorly (O(N⁴) memory, O(N³) time), while gradient‑descent variants are more feasible but still heavier than t‑SNE/UMAP.

**Failure signatures:** 
- Numerical instability: In Finsler‑SMACOF, pseudoinverse becomes ill‑conditioned for large N; solution is to use gradient‑descent implementation.
- Poor alignment: If asymmetric affinities are nearly symmetric (σ_i≈σ_j), ω magnitude converges to near zero and embedding reduces to Euclidean, indicating asymmetry not beneficial.
- Optimisation divergence: Large ω or high learning rates cause overshooting; remedies include conservative learning rates or cosine annealing.

**First 3 experiments:** 
1. Synthetic planar disk: Non‑uniform density dataset; Finsler embedding shows the extra dimension correlates with density, while Euclidean methods blend densities.
2. Swiss roll: Curved manifold with density variations; Finsler embedding preserves band structure and reveals hierarchy.
3. Classification benchmarks (MNIST, CIFAR‑10, ImageNet): Quantify clustering via AMI/ARI/NMI; Finsler‑UMAP/t‑SNE consistently outperform Euclidean baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can Finsler manifold learning be extended to multiple independent asymmetry directions beyond the single ω in canonical Randers space? (Current method limited to one direction.)
2. How to construct asymmetric dissimilarities using feature semantics rather than only sampling density? (Only density‑based asymmetry currently.)
3. Can we design scalable Finsler MDS avoiding current O(N⁴) memory and O(N³) time complexity? (Current SMACOF unscalable.)
4. What theoretical guarantees exist for Finsler embeddings' convergence, distortion, and manifold recovery? (Lacks analysis.)
5. How does Finsler metric choice affect embedding quality, and can we develop metric selection criteria? (Only canonical Randers tested.)

## Limitations
- Assumes asymmetry is always beneficial; for uniformly sampled data, the extra ω dimension may add noise or distort structure.
- Relies on the canonical Randers metric, which models only one directional drift; complex multi‑directional asymmetry may not be captured.
- Lacks theoretical guarantees (convergence, distortion bounds) and scalability analysis; computational overhead is not fully benchmarked at large scale.
