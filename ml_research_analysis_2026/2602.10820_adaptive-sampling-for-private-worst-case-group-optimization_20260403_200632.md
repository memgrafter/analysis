# Adaptive Sampling for Private Worst-Case Group Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10820
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10820
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ASC achieves substantially higher worst-case group accuracy (e.g., 59.2% on Unbalanced MNIST) than DP-SGD (20.4%) and prior private DRO methods (0.8%) without sacrificing average accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Differential privacy (DP) and worst-case group fairness often conflict: standard DP-SGD tends to perform poorly on minority groups because the unequal weighting required for fairness amplifies DP noise. This paper introduces ASC (Adaptively Sampled and Clipped Worst-case Group Optimization), which resolves this conflict by introducing group-specific adaptive sampling rates and clipping thresholds. ASC increases sampling for groups with higher estimated loss and adjusts clipping so that each group’s contribution satisfies the same per-step Rényi DP guarantee. Empirically, ASC boosts worst-group accuracy across three benchmarks while maintaining average accuracy. Theoretically, ASC reduces gradient sampling variance relative to prior private DRO methods, enabling more stable convergence.

## Method Summary
ASC is a private distributionally robust optimization algorithm that minimizes the maximum loss across predefined groups. It modifies DP-SGD in two key ways: (1) adaptively setting per-group batch sizes \(m_g\) using private loss estimates via exponential reweighting, and (2) computing per-group clipping thresholds \(\xi_g = \sigma / \kappa_{\alpha}(m_g/n_g, \varepsilon_s)\) to ensure each group’s gradient update meets the same per-step Rényi DP guarantee \((\alpha, \varepsilon_s)\). The adaptive sampling runs every \(k\) steps: an auxiliary private loss release (subsampled Gaussian mechanism) estimates each group’s loss, which then scales its batch size. Clipping balances the increased contribution from larger batches. This reduces the variance of gradients compared to fixed or proportional sampling, leading to improved worst-group accuracy under the same privacy budget without harming average performance.

## Key Results
- On Unbalanced MNIST, ASC reaches **59.2% worst-case group accuracy** versus 20.4% for DP-SGD and 0.8% for aZB, with average accuracy around 84.5%.
- ASC theoretically reduces gradient sampling variance versus prior private DRO methods (Theorem 2), enabling more stable convergence.
- Across three benchmarks (Unbalanced MNIST, CivilComments, Bank Fraud), ASC consistently outperforms DP-SGD and aZB variants on worst-group accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Adaptive Group Sampling
- **Claim:** ASC adaptively increases the batch size for groups with higher estimated loss, thereby focusing training on the worst-performing groups.
- **Mechanism:** Every \(k\) steps, ASC samples a small auxiliary batch from each group to estimate its average loss (with clipping and Gaussian noise). These losses are used in exponential weighting: current batch size \(m_g\) is multiplied by \(\exp(\eta L_g)\), then normalized and rounded to sum to total \(M\). Groups with larger \(L_g\) get larger \(m_g\).
- **Core assumption:** The exponential reweighting gradually amplifies groups with higher loss, and the loss estimates are accurate enough despite noise and clipping.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [Abstract]: "It adaptively controls both the sampling rate and the clipping threshold of each group."  
  [Section 3]: "Adaptive Sampling... allows harder-to-learn groups to be sampled more often."  
  [corpus: "On the Fairness of Privacy Protection" (arXiv:2510.09114) highlights group privacy disparities, motivating the need for such adaptation; however, it does not propose adaptive sampling, so direct corpus evidence is limited.]
- **Break condition:** If noise \(\tau\) in loss estimates is too high or learning rate \(\eta\) poorly chosen, batch sizes may oscillate. Rounding to integers may force a group’s \(m_g\) to zero, removing it from training. Also, if a group’s dataset is extremely small, the adaptive mechanism might not be able to give it sufficient samples without violating privacy.

### Mechanism 2: Group-Specific Clipping Thresholds
- **Claim:** ASC sets per-group clipping thresholds \(\xi_g = \sigma / \kappa_{\alpha}(m_g/n_g, \varepsilon_s)\) to ensure each group’s model update satisfies the same per-step Rényi DP guarantee \((\alpha, \varepsilon_s)\), even when batch sizes differ.
- **Mechanism:** For each group \(g\), after sampling a batch of size \(m_g\), gradients are clipped to \(\xi_g\). The noise multiplier \(\kappa = \sigma/\xi_g\) is chosen such that the subsampled Gaussian mechanism with sampling rate \(\gamma_g = m_g/n_g\) achieves exactly \(\varepsilon_s\) RDP. This balances the increased contribution from larger \(m_g\).
- **Core assumption:** The function \(\kappa_{\alpha}(\gamma, \varepsilon)\) is correctly computed; group sizes \(n_g\) are known and fixed.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [Abstract]: "adaptively controls ... the clipping threshold."  
  [Section 3, Algorithm 2, line 6]: explicit use of \(\kappa_{\alpha}(m_g/n_g, \varepsilon_s)\) in clipping.  
  [Section 3.1, Lemma 1]: proof that the model update step is \((\alpha, \varepsilon_s)\)-RDP.  
  [corpus: "Avoiding Pitfalls for Privacy Accounting of Subsampled Mechanisms under Composition" (arXiv:2405.20769) discusses correct application of subsampled mechanisms, supporting the underlying privacy accounting.]
- **Break condition:** Requires accurate \(n_g\); underestimation leads to weaker privacy. If \(m_g/n_g \to 1\), \(\kappa_{\alpha}\) becomes very small, making \(\xi_g\) very large, which reduces clipping’s variance benefit and may cause large, unstable updates. Also, if groups have highly varying sizes, rounding \(m_g\) may cause some groups to be undersampled.

### Mechanism 3: Private Exponential Reweighting
- **Claim:** ASC uses a private loss release mechanism to adjust group batch sizes without leaking information, ensuring the entire algorithm remains differentially private.
- **Mechanism:** Every \(k\) steps, for each group \(g\), an auxiliary batch of size \(\lfloor \gamma_\ell n_g \rfloor\) is drawn. The average clipped loss \(\zeta\) plus Gaussian noise (variance \(\tau^2\)) yields \(L_g\). These \(L_g\) are then used in exponential weighting: \(\tilde{m}_g = m_g \exp(\eta L_g)\). The loss release is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism contributing \((\alpha, \varepsilon_{\alpha}(\gamma_\ell, \tau/\zeta))\)-RDP; post-processing preserves DP.
- **Core assumption:** \(\zeta\) and \(\tau\) are chosen so that the loss release meets its DP budget; exponential weighting is stable under post-processing.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [Section 3, Algorithm 2, lines 12–15]: private loss computation and reweighting.  
  [Section 3.1, Lemma 2]: privacy of the batch size update step.  
  [Section 3.1, Theorem 1]: overall composition.  
  [corpus: "Optimizing Privacy and Utility Tradeoffs for Group Interests Through Harmonization" (arXiv:2404.05043) also considers group-specific privacy, though not for DRO.]
- **Break condition:** If \(\gamma_\ell\) is too low, loss estimates are too noisy; if \(\tau/\zeta\) too small, privacy cost exceeds budget; extreme loss values may cause numerical issues despite clipping. Excessive \(\eta\) may cause unstable batch size changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** D_g -> Adaptive batch size manager -> {Gradient module, Clipping threshold calculator} -> {Sum, Noise injector} -> θ; Loss estimator -> Adaptive batch size manager; Privacy accountant tracks all DP events.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Initialize θ and m_g (e.g., uniform or proportional to n_g).
  2. For iteration t = 1 to T:
     a. For each group g: sample B_g of size m_g; compute u_g = Σ_{z∈B_g} clip(∇ℓ(z, θ_t), ξ_g).
     b. Sum all u_g, add Gaussian noise N(0, σ² I), and update θ_{t+1} = θ_t - η/M (sum + noise).
     c. If t mod k == 0:
        i. For each group g: sample auxiliary batch B^ℓ_g of size ⌊γ_ℓ n_g⌋; compute L_g = (1/|B^ℓ_g|) Σ clip(ℓ(z, θ_t), ζ) + N(0, τ²).
        ii. For each g: set ˜m_g = m_g exp(η L_g).
        iii. Normalize: m ← round_M( M * ˜m / ‖˜m‖_1 ) (ensuring integer batches summing to M).
  3. Return final θ.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Adaptive vs. fixed batch sizes:* Adaptive sampling (ASC) can improve worst-group accuracy but introduces hyperparameters (η, k, γ_ℓ, τ/ζ) that need tuning. Fixed proportional sampling (aZB-prop) is simpler but yields higher variance (Theorem 2) and may not focus enough on hard groups.
  - *Clipping threshold adaptation:* Balancing privacy by varying ξ_g with m_g keeps per-group privacy equal. However, larger ξ_g for groups with larger m_g reduces the variance reduction from clipping, potentially increasing gradient variance. ASC mitigates this by using group-mixed batches (all groups contribute to the same update) which lowers the between-group variance term.
  - *Frequency of reweighting (k):* Smaller k makes the algorithm more responsive to changes in group performance but increases the privacy cost (more loss releases) and computational overhead. Larger k saves privacy budget but may lag in adaptation.
  - *Noise allocation:* The noise multiplier for loss estimates (τ/ζ) can be much larger than for model updates (σ/ξ) because the loss values contribute less to model stability; this allows cheaper privacy accounting for reweighting.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Stagnant worst-group accuracy:* If WGA does not improve over epochs while average accuracy rises, the reweighting may be ineffective—check if η is too small or noise τ too high.
  - *Privacy budget exhaustion:* If the accumulated ε from the accountant exceeds the target before T steps, the sampling rates m_g/n_g may be too high; consider reducing batch sizes or increasing noise.
  - *Unstable batch sizes:* If some m_g drop to zero or oscillate wildly, the rounding or exponential weighting may be too aggressive; try a smaller η or add a floor to m_g.
  - *Large gradient norms:* Monitor the norm of the aggregated gradient before noise; if it frequently exceeds ξ_g (the effective clipping bound), the clipping thresholds may be set too low, causing excessive clipping bias.
  - *Per-group privacy discrepancy:* If the accountant reveals that implied ε_g differ significantly across groups, the n_g values might be inaccurate or the κα function misapplied.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. *Reweighting unit test:* On a synthetic dataset with two groups having known constant losses L1 < L2, verify that after one reweighting step, m_2 increases and m_1 decreases (given appropriate η). Also check that the sum of m_g remains M after rounding.
  2. *Privacy accounting test:* Using a small real dataset, set target per-step ε_s and run ASC for a few steps. Independently compute the total ε by composing the per-step RDP using Theorem 1 and compare to the library’s accountant output.
  3. *Benchmark replication:* Run ASC on Unbalanced MNIST with target (ε=1, δ=1/(2n)) and the hyperparameters from Table 1 (or Appendix E). Confirm that the achieved worst-case group accuracy is around 59.2% and average accuracy around 84.5% (± standard deviation). This ensures the implementation matches the paper’s results.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can ASC be generalized to other distributionally robust optimization settings, such as multi-task or meta-learning, where tasks have varying dataset sizes and privacy requirements?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the conclusion: "In future work, we plan to generalize the mechanism to other forms of distributionally robust optimization algorithms, e.g. in the context of multi-task or meta-learning..."
- **Why unresolved:** ASC is currently designed for a single learning task with fixed groups; extending it to multiple tasks or meta-learning requires handling interactions between tasks' privacy budgets and group weights, and possibly modifying the adaptive sampling and clipping mechanisms.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis showing how to allocate privacy budgets across tasks, updated algorithms that jointly optimize group objectives with privacy constraints, and empirical validation on multi-task benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can ASC handle scenarios where group membership is unknown, noisy, or must be inferred from data, rather than being provided as ground truth?
- **Basis in paper:** ASC assumes that each data point has a known group label at training time (Section 2: "each data point is also annotated with a group label at training time"). This assumption may not hold in practice, as group membership might be sensitive or missing.
- **Why unresolved:** Without explicit group labels, the adaptive sampling and clipping based on per-group statistics cannot be directly applied. Extending ASC would require techniques for group identification or using proxy variables.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm that jointly learns group assignments or uses soft group memberships while preserving differential privacy, along with experiments demonstrating robustness to label noise or missing group information.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the full convergence rate of ASC under standard SGD assumptions, considering both the sampling variance reduction and the DP noise?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper analyzes sampling variance (Section 5) and shows ASC has lower variance than prior methods, but does not provide a convergence theorem that accounts for DP noise and adaptive sampling dynamics.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a convergence guarantee, it is unclear under what learning rates and privacy parameters ASC will converge to a stationary point, especially given the periodic batch size updates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A convergence proof for ASC under smoothness and bounded variance assumptions, possibly bounding the gradient bias due to adaptive sampling, and empirical validation of predicted convergence rates on synthetic and real datasets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does ASC scale to very large datasets and high-dimensional models, and what are the computational overheads of adaptive sampling and clipping?
- **Basis in paper:** Experiments are on moderately sized datasets (MNIST, CelebA subset, Bank Fraud). The algorithm requires per-group batch sampling and loss estimation every k steps, which could become expensive with many groups or large datasets.
- **Why unresolved:** The computational complexity per iteration depends on the number of groups and their sizes. For thousands of groups or massive datasets, the overhead might negate the accuracy benefits.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Scalability experiments with increasing group counts, model sizes (e.g., ResNet-101, large language models), and dataset sizes; analysis of time and memory complexity; possible optimizations like approximate sampling or caching.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to three benchmarks; generalizability to more complex, high‑dimensional, or many‑group scenarios remains unknown.
- ASC assumes known group labels and exact group sizes; inaccurate sizes weaken privacy guarantees, and rounding may drop small groups entirely.
- The adaptive mechanism introduces several hyperparameters (η, k, γ_ℓ, τ/ζ) that require careful tuning; poor choices lead to unstable batch sizes or privacy budget overruns.
