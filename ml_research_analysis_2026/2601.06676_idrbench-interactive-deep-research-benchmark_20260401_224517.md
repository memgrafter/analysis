# IDRBench: Interactive Deep Research Benchmark

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06676  
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06676  
- **Reference Count:** 24  
- **Primary Result:** Interactive deep research agents consistently produce higher-quality, better-aligned reports (+10.96 average score gain) but at a measurable increase in token cost and interaction turns.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces IDRBench, the first benchmark designed to evaluate interactive deep research agents. It addresses a key limitation of existing static benchmarks: they fail to capture the underspecified, evolving nature of real-world research queries. The core contribution is a modular multi-agent framework augmented with an on-demand interaction mechanism, a scalable reference-grounded user simulator, and an evaluation suite that measures both the benefits (report quality, user alignment) and costs (dialogue turns, token usage) of interaction. Experiments across seven large language models show that enabling interaction reliably improves report quality and robustness, often compensating for lower model capacity, though this comes with increased operational overhead.

## Method Summary
IDRBench constructs a test set of 100 high-quality (query, reference document) pairs from DeepResearch Bench. Queries are compressed by 10–90% via LLM-based summarization to simulate underspecification. The evaluation framework employs a modular multi-agent pipeline (Planner, Supervisor, Researcher(s), Reporter) enhanced with three interaction modules: an **Evaluator** (decides when to ask for clarification based on phase-specific guidelines and a remaining interaction budget), a **Questioner** (formulates 1–2 focused questions), and a **User Simulator** (uses the reference document as an oracle to generate goal-oriented, human-like responses). Performance is measured via *Interaction Benefits* (Report Similarity, Multi-Granularity F1, LLM Aspect Coverage Score) and *Interaction Costs* (Interaction Turns, Interaction Tokens, estimated API cost).

## Key Results
- Interaction yields consistent quality improvements across all seven evaluated LLMs, with average LLM-ACS score gains up to +10.96.
- The benefits are robust across different simulator models, validating the simulator's role in standardizing evaluation.
- Interaction incurs measurable costs, including increased token usage (e.g., +$0.113 to +$1.233 API cost per report) and variable turn counts, revealing a trade-off between alignment and efficiency.
- Early-stage (Planning) interaction provides the largest gains, highlighting the importance of initial alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Intentional ambiguity in user queries increases the necessity and potential benefit of agent-user interaction.
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark introduces *Ambiguity Injection* by compressing detailed reference queries (10–90% shorter), removing specific constraints and details while preserving core intent. This creates underspecified initial conditions, forcing the interactive agent to resolve uncertainty through clarification rather than executing a fully specified plan.
- **Core assumption:** Real-world research queries are often underspecified, and a system's ability to recover the intended scope via interaction is a key capability gap in autonomous agents.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...queries are often underspecified and evolve during exploration, making sustained interaction essential... IDRBench addresses... static benchmarks fail to capture."
  - [section 3.2] "To better reflect real-world underspecification, we introduce an Ambiguity Injection process... intentionally removing detail while preserving core intent."
- **Break condition:** If compressed queries lose recoverable core intent (e.g., become nonsensical or invert the goal), the interaction benefit measurement becomes invalid. The paper assumes the original detailed query provides a recoverable ground truth.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A reference-grounded, idealized user simulator provides consistent, goal-oriented feedback that isolates the agent's interactive capability from user variability.
- **Mechanism:** The User Simulator uses the reference document (the ideal report) as an oracle. Given an agent's clarification question, it generates concise, first-person responses that steer the agent toward the reference's major focus, reject misaligned options, and avoid hallucinating new requirements. This decouples evaluation from human annotator cost and noise.
- **Core assumption:** Standardizing user feedback to be consistent, correct, and goal-oriented is necessary for reproducible, objective measurement of the *agent's* interactive reasoning skill.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1.2] "The User Simulator enables scalable evaluation... acts as a proxy for user feedback... treats the reference document as the source of oracle knowledge... generates responses under three guiding constraints: Human-like Behavior, Macroscopic Guidance, Corrective Behavior."
  - [section 4.4] "Performance remains largely invariant to the simulator choice... IDRBench primarily measures the intrinsic interactive capability of research agents."
  - [Limitations] "We acknowledge that the reference-grounded User Simulator induces an idealized interaction setting... This stability is a necessary design choice for rigorous and reproducible benchmarking."
- **Break condition:** If the reference document itself is low-quality, ambiguous, or does not represent a coherent goal, simulator feedback becomes corrupted. The method assumes high-quality reference documents exist.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An explicit, stage-aware interaction mechanism (Evaluator + Questioner) allows agents to dynamically decide *when* and *what* to clarify, balancing alignment gain against interruption cost.
- **Mechanism:** At key decision points (Planning, Research Loop, Generation), the Evaluator assesses current research context against phase-specific guidelines (scope, completeness, structure) and a remaining interaction budget to decide if clarification is warranted. If yes, the Questioner formulates 1–2 focused, high-level questions adapted to the user's language style.
- **Core assumption:** Effective interaction is not just about asking questions, but about strategically timing them and formulating them to address high-leverage uncertainties unique to each research phase.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1.2] "To bridge autonomous execution and evolving user intent, we introduce an interaction mechanism... contains an Evaluator and a Questioner... determines whether interaction is necessary... formulates targeted inquiries."
  - [section 4.3.1] "Interaction frequency varies systematically across research stages... During Planning, all models frequently seek clarification... In the Research Loop... interaction frequency differs markedly..."
  - [Appendix C] Provides the detailed prompt for both Evaluator and Questioner, showing phase-specific guidelines and constraints.
- **Break condition:** If the Evaluator's decision rule is misaligned with true information gain (e.g., triggers on irrelevant metrics) or the Questioner produces off-topic/trivial questions, interaction becomes costly noise rather than beneficial alignment. The paper assumes the heuristics (scope, completeness) are valid proxies for uncertainty.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input (Vague Query + Ref Doc) -> Planner -> Supervisor -> [Research Loop: Researcher -> Evaluator -> (if ask: Questioner -> User Simulator -> feedback) - repeat until coverage/iterations] -> Reporter -> Evaluation Suite
- **Critical path:** Query → Planner (brief) → Supervisor (decompose) → Research Loop (parallel retrieval/summarization with periodic Evaluator checks) → (if interaction triggered: Questioner asks, Simulator responds) → (loop continues) → Reporter (synthesize) → Metrics calculation (Similarity, F1, LLM-ACS, Costs).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Simulator Fidelity vs. Reproducibility:** Using an oracle-grounded simulator ensures consistent, noise-free feedback for comparative evaluation but may not capture real human volatility, misconceptions, or shifting intent (acknowledged in Limitations).
  - **Interaction Budget Caps:** Limiting turns (1 planning, 3 loop, 1 generation) creates a realistic constraint but may artificially suppress potential benefits from longer, more natural dialogues.
  - **Metric Granularity:** Chunk-level F1 with τ=0.8 is strict to avoid false positives but might penalize valid paraphrasing. LLM-ACS relies on the LLM-as-judge paradigm.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High interaction cost, low benefit gain:** Model asks many questions but shows minimal improvement in LLM-ACS or F1 scores. Indicates poor question formulation or inability to utilize feedback.
  - **Stagnant distribution:** Interaction does not raise the performance floor. Model fails to recover from early mistakes even with feedback.
  - **Phase imbalance:** Over-reliance on planning-stage interaction but poor loop/generation adaptation, or vice-versa. Suggests misalignment between Evaluator's heuristics and actual uncertainty points.
  - **Simulator sensitivity:** Large performance swings when User Simulator model is changed. Indicates agent is overfitting to simulator quirks, not learning robust strategies.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Comparison (Autonomous vs. Interactive):** Run the full pipeline for each of the 7 LLMs in both modes. Compare average scores and cost increases. *Hypothesis test:* Does interaction yield a statistically significant average score increase?
  2. **Interaction Timing Ablation:** For a selected model (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro), run conditions: (a) No interaction, (b) Interaction only in Planning, (c) only in Research Loop, (d) only in Generation, (e) All phases. *Hypothesis test:* Does early-stage (Planning) interaction yield larger gains than late-stage?
  3. **User Simulator Robustness Check:** For a selected research agent (e.g., DeepSeek-V3.2), run experiments with three different User Simulator LLMs. *Hypothesis test:* Are the main benefit metrics invariant across simulator choices?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the reference-grounded User Simulator be extended to model realistic, dynamic user behaviors such as shifting intents, contradictory feedback, or evolving goals over long research horizons?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations section states: "We acknowledge that the reference-grounded User Simulator induces an idealized interaction setting that may not fully capture real-world user behavior, such as volatility, ambiguity, or shifting intent over time."
- **Why unresolved:** The current simulator provides stable, oracle feedback to isolate agent capability, but this decouples evaluation from the noise and adaptability required in real human-AI collaboration.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A study comparing agent performance when interacting with the current simulator versus a stochastic/user-model-based simulator that introduces intent drift, followed by correlation analysis with actual human-user studies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Beyond underspecification, how do other ambiguity types (e.g., factual inaccuracies in the query, user misconceptions, polysemy) affect an interactive agent's ability to clarify, correct, and maintain alignment?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations section notes: "Our current ambiguity injection strategy focuses on underspecification... real-world ambiguity is more diverse, arising from user misconceptions, polysemy, or domain-specific errors."
- **Why unresolved:** The benchmark only tests recovery from omitted detail; it does not evaluate robustness when the user's initial premise contains errors or multiple conflicting interpretations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Constructing curated test cases where queries contain factual errors or ambiguous terms, and measuring whether agents can detect and guide users toward correction without derailing the research objective.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the observed benefits of interaction consistent across non-LLM-based multi-agent architectures (e.g., symbolic planners, retrieval-specialized modules), or are they specific to end-to-end LLM-driven pipelines?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework and all evaluated models are LLM-centric. The Related Work mentions multi-agent decomposition but the benchmark's modular agents (Planner, Supervisor, Researcher, Reporter) are all implemented via LLMs. No experiments vary the underlying agent technology.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear if interaction gains are an emergent property of LLM reasoning or a general principle of modular systems. This affects how future systems should be architected.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-implementing the IDRBench framework with alternative agent technologies (e.g., neuro-symbolic planners for the Supervisor, specialized retrieval agents) and comparing interaction benefit patterns against the LLM-only baseline.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what conditions does interaction *harm* research outcomes (e.g., increased cognitive load leading to worse decisions, or over-correction to user feedback causing loss of important tangential information)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper frames interaction as universally beneficial for alignment, showing consistent quality gains. Cost is measured in tokens/turns, but negative qualitative impacts (e.g., scope narrowing, distraction) are not analyzed. The interaction mechanism is triggered by an LLM Evaluator, which itself may have biases or thresholds that lead to unnecessary or misdirected questions.
- **Why unresolved:** The evaluation suite measures alignment against a reference but does not assess whether interactive trajectories introduce novel, valuable insights not present in the reference (a form of overfitting) or cause the agent to abandon fruitful exploratory paths due to premature user redirection.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyzing failed cases where interaction lowered quality metrics; qualitative analysis of interaction logs to identify patterns of harmful feedback loops; A/B testing with different Evaluator thresholds to see if reduced interaction frequency ever improves final report quality for certain query types.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the optimal interaction policy (i.e., when to ask, what to ask) be learned and personalized per user or query type, rather than using the fixed Evaluator/Questioner design?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The interaction mechanism is rule-based with an LLM-driven Evaluator. The paper reports aggregate turn/token costs but does not analyze the *efficiency* of different questioning strategies across models or query complexities. Table 4 shows performance varies by module (Planning vs. Research Loop), but there is no adaptive policy.
- **Why unresolved:** The current "one-size-fits-all" interaction elicitation may be suboptimal. A learned policy could maximize benefit-per-token or adapt to user patience levels.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training a reinforcement learning policy or fine-tuning the Evaluator on interaction outcome signals (e.g., change in LLM-ACS per question asked) and demonstrating improved benefit-cost trade-offs over the static policy on a held-out set.

## Limitations
- **Ambiguity Injection Validity:** The compression-based method assumes core intent remains recoverable after 10–90% shortening. No human validation confirms compressed queries are plausibly underspecified yet solvable.
- **Simulator Fidelity Gap:** The oracle-grounded simulator provides reproducibility but departs from human unpredictability (e.g., asking irrelevant questions, changing goals). Real user studies are absent.
- **Aspect Generator Opaqueness:** The LLM responsible for generating LLM-ACS aspects is unspecified; sensitivity to this choice is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Interaction Benefit:** **High**. Consistent improvements across 7 LLMs and rigorous ablation (Table 4) support the core benefit.
- **Simulator Stability:** **Medium**. Table 3 shows invariance across simulator models, but this tests *consistency*, not *fidelity*.
- **Mechanism Design:** **Medium-Low**. Phase-aware interaction logic is described, but no direct ablation isolates the Evaluator/Questioner's contribution beyond full vs. no interaction.
