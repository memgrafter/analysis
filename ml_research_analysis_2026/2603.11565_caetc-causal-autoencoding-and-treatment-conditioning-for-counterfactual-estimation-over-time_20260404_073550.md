# CAETC: Causal Autoencoding and Treatment Conditioning for Counterfactual Estimation over Time

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.11565  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11565  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: State‑of‑the‑art counterfactual estimation with significant gains (e.g., MIMIC‑III semi‑synthetic RMSE 0.535 vs 0.578 for CRN) and robustness under high confounding.

## Executive Summary
CAETC tackles the challenge of predicting counterfactual outcomes over time from longitudinal observational data plagued by time‑dependent confounding. It introduces a causal autoencoder that learns a partially invertible, treatment‑invariant representation. This representation is then conditioned via FiLM on planned future treatments to generate outcome forecasts. An entropy‑maximizing adversarial game enforces balance across treatment distributions, while a temporal cutoff mechanism gracefully handles missing covariates. The architecture is agnostic to the sequence encoder; experiments use LSTM and TCN backbones. Comprehensive evaluations on NSCLC synthetic, MIMIC‑III semi‑synthetic, and real MIMIC‑III data show consistent and substantial improvements over strong baselines. Ablations confirm the contribution of each component, and the method demonstrates resilience even when confounding is severe.

## Method Summary
CAETC employs an architecture‑agnostic backbone (LSTM or TCN) to encode the observed history \( H_t \) into a latent representation \( \Phi(H_t) \). The model is trained with a composite objective:
- **Partial autoencoding**: Reconstruct the factual treatment \( A_t \), outcome \( Y_t \), and optionally time‑varying covariates \( X_t \) from \( \Phi(H_t) \).
- **FiLM conditioning**: For a planned future treatment \( A_{t+1} \), a FiLM layer modulates \( \Phi(H_t) \) to predict the counterfactual outcome \( Y_{t+1} \). Training uses only factual outcomes, but the conditioning is applied for all possible treatments via a treatment‑specific head.
- **Adversarial entropy maximization**: A discriminator \( F_B \) classifies \( \Phi(H_t) \) by treatment; its loss is minimized while the encoder maximizes the entropy of the discriminator’s output, encouraging treatment invariance.
- **Temporal cutoff**: During training, future \( X \) steps are randomly dropped and replaced with a learnable vector \( M \) to prevent the model from relying on unavailable information at deployment.
The total loss combines reconstruction (\( L_R \)), next‑outcome (\( L_Y \)), treatment conditioning (\( L_C \)), and adversarial entropy (\( L_E \)) terms, weighted by hyperparameters \( \delta_A, \delta_X, \delta_E \). The adversarial weight \( \delta_B \) is increased gradually. Optimization uses Adam and teacher forcing.

## Key Results
- On MIMIC‑III semi‑synthetic data, CAETC‑LSTM achieves an average RMSE of **0.535**, compared to **0.578** for CRN and **0.684** for CT.
- Under increasing confounding in the NSCLC synthetic task (\( \gamma=8 \)), CAETC‑LSTM maintains an RMSE of **7.74**, while CRN degrades to **8.01** and CT to **8.61**.
- On real MIMIC‑III, CAETC‑LSTM obtains **7.45** vs. CT’s **7.56**.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core innovation lies in learning a representation that captures the underlying confounded state yet is invariant to treatment assignments. The adversarial entropy maximization forces the encoder to produce features that the discriminator cannot reliably map to treatments, mimicking a randomized trial. This invariant representation enables fair counterfactual comparisons via FiLM conditioning, which injects the planned treatment without altering the underlying state. The autoencoder component preserves predictive information about outcomes and covariates, while the temporal cutoff prevents leakage from future covariates, ensuring the model remains applicable in practical settings where data are missing. Together, these elements address time‑dependent confounding and yield accurate, robust counterfactual forecasts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Counterfactual estimation over time**: Predict outcomes under hypothetical treatment sequences. *Why needed*: Enables personalized decision support. *Quick check*: Model should produce varying predictions when conditioning on different treatments.
- **Time‑dependent confounding**: Variables influencing both future treatments and outcomes. *Why needed*: Ignoring them biases causal estimates. *Quick check*: Strong correlation between past confounders and future treatments in data.
- **Treatment‑invariant representation**: Latent encoding independent of treatment. *Why needed*: Allows unbiased counterfactual comparison. *Quick check*: Classifier accuracy on treatments from representation ≈ random chance.
- **Adversarial entropy maximization**: Game that balances treatment distributions. *Why needed*: Enforces invariance without collapsing representation. *Quick check*: Discriminator loss near maximum entropy; classification accuracy low.
- **FiLM conditioning**: Treatment‑specific modulation of features. *Why needed*: Generates outcome predictions for any planned treatment from a shared representation. *Quick check*: Changing conditioning treatment alters predicted outcome meaningfully.
- **Temporal cutoff**: Randomly dropping future covariates during training. *Why needed*: Prevents reliance on unavailable information. *Quick check*: Model performance remains stable when future covariates are missing at test time.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input (static V, time‑varying X_t, A_t, Y_t) → Backbone (LSTM/TCN) → Representation Φ(H_t) → Branches:  
  • Autoencoding heads: F_A (reconstruct A_t), F_Y (reconstruct Y_t), F_X (reconstruct X_t)  
  • FiLM conditioning head: F_C (modulate Φ with A_{t+1} to predict Y_{t+1})  
  • Adversarial branch: F_B (classify treatment from Φ)  
Outputs: Reconstructions, counterfactual predictions, treatment distribution.

**Critical path**: For each timestep \( t \), compute \( \Phi(H_t) \). Use autoencoding heads to reconstruct factual variables. For next‑outcome prediction, take planned \( A_{t+1} \), apply FiLM to \( \Phi(H_t) \), and predict \( Y_{t+1} \). Simultaneously, train \( F_B \) on true \( A_{t+1} \) and update the encoder to maximize entropy of \( F_B \)’s output (often via gradient reversal). Temporal cutoff randomly masks future \( X \) steps in the input sequence during training.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- LSTM vs. TCN: LSTM handles variable‑length dependencies but is sequential; TCN parallelizes but may require larger receptive fields.  
- Adversarial scheduling: Aggressive increase of \( \delta_B \) causes instability; too slow slows convergence.  
- Conditioning protocol: Using all counterfactual treatments per sample improves coverage but increases cost; subsampling trades efficiency for variance.  
- Temporal cutoff vs. imputation: Cutoff is simpler and model‑agnostic but may discard more information than a learned imputation module.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High treatment classification accuracy (>0.8 for 3 treatments) → adversarial balance failed.  
- Reconstruction loss significantly worse than a non‑adversarial baseline → representation collapse.  
- Predictions insensitive to conditioning treatment → FiLM not learning.  
- Oscillating losses or exploding gradients → unstable adversarial training.  
- Large performance drop on a no‑confounding test set → invariant representation not learned.

**Three first experiments**:  
1. **Reconstruction sanity check**: On a small fully observed dataset, verify that autoencodingheads reconstruct A_t, Y_t, X_t with low error.  
2. **Treatment invariance check**: After training, measure classification accuracy of F_B on validation data; it should be near random (e.g., ~0.33 for 3 treatments).  
3. **Counterfactual prediction check**: On a synthetic dataset with known treatment effects, condition on different treatments and confirm that predicted outcome differences match the ground‑truth effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1** – *How can we ensure stable convergence to the equilibrium in the entropy maximization adversarial game?*  
The paper acknowledges (Section 4.4) that achieving equilibrium is difficult in practice. Adversarial training is notoriously unstable; without guarantees, the method may fail on complex real‑world data. Evidence to resolve: theoretical analysis of game dynamics under specific architectures or empirical comparison of optimization schemes (e.g., alternative gradient methods, smoother schedules).

**Open Question 2** – *Under what realistic assumptions can the constant S in the error bound (Theorem 4.2) be bounded, and how does that affect practical applicability?*  
Section 4.2/4.4 notes it is difficult to bound S without extra assumptions. The bound requires S to be bounded, but outcomes like blood pressure may have heavy tails, making S large and the bound loose. This undermines theoretical guarantees for medical applications. Evidence: derive tighter bounds under common assumptions (e.g., sub‑Gaussian outcomes, Lipschitz loss) or provide empirical estimates of S across diverse datasets.

**Open Question 3** – *Does training the conditioning layer on synthetically generated counterfactuals introduce bias, and can alternative objectives mitigate this?*  
Section 4.5 admits that only factual outcomes are observed; counterfactuals are synthetically generated by applying the same outcome model, potentially amplifying model misspecification bias. The paper does not analyze this bias or compare alternatives. Evidence: theoretical bias analysis under correct model specification or empirical evaluation on semi‑synthetic data with known ground truth.

**Open Question 4** – *How does the temporal cutoff mechanism for missing covariates compare to other imputation or encoder‑decoder strategies?*  
Section 4.6 introduces temporal cutoff as a simple dropout‑like alternative but provides no comparison or theoretical justification. Missing data are ubiquitous in EHR; it is unclear when cutoff works best or its effect on causal identification. Evidence: systematic experiments comparing cutoff to multiple imputation, forward‑filling, and encoder‑decoder approaches across datasets with varying missingness, plus theoretical analysis of its impact on treatment invariance.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction barriers**: Critical hyperparameters and exact simulation parameters for semi‑synthetic data are not disclosed, preventing faithful recreation of reported numbers.
- **Ablation sensitivity**: The exact protocol for the treatment conditioning loss (e.g., use of all counterfactuals vs. subsampling, label smoothing) is unspecified; this could alter quantified component contributions.
- **Theoretical assumptions**: Error bounds rely on potentially unrealistic assumptions (e.g., bounded S), limiting their practical interpretability.
