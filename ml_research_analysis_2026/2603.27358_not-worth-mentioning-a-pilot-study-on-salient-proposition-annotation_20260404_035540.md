# Not Worth Mentioning? A Pilot Study on Salient Proposition Annotation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27358  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27358  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** A summary-based method operationalizes graded proposition salience in EDUs, yielding moderate-to-substantial human agreement (κ=0.65–0.84) and a descending score distribution, with RST centrality as a weak predictor (marginal r² = 0.128).

## Executive Summary
This pilot study tackles the lack of an operational definition for graded proposition salience in natural texts. It adapts an entity-salience methodology—using multiple human summaries—to Elementary Discourse Units (EDUs). Each EDU receives a salience score equal to the number of summaries (out of five) in which it is identified, with alignment categories allowing exact, approximate, component, or duplicate matches. Applied to 32 multi-genre documents (~3,800 EDUs), the method achieves inter-annotator agreement ranging from moderate to substantial under lenient matching scenarios. Salience scores follow a descending distribution, and while RST centrality (graph distance from the discourse root) shows a significant negative correlation, it explains only a small portion of variance in a mixed-effects model.

## Method Summary
The authors operationalize graded proposition salience by treating human summarization as a salience judgment task. For each document in the GUM corpus (v12 test set), five human summaries are collected. Annotators use a web interface to highlight EDUs in each summary and categorize alignment to the source document’s EDUs as exact, approximate (normal or component), or duplicate. An EDU’s salience score is the count of summaries where it is marked (exact or approximate). Agreement is measured via Cohen’s kappa under four increasingly lenient scenarios (exact, strict literal, strict match, duplicates OK). The dataset’s pre-existing RST trees enable analysis of salience against discourse centrality (graph distance from root) via a mixed-effects model controlling for EDU length, position, and relation type.

## Key Results
- Inter-annotator agreement reaches κ=0.65 (exact) to κ=0.84 (duplicates OK), with component approximate matches causing the most disagreement (8.6% micro κ impact).
- Salience scores descend sharply: most EDUs appear in 0–1 summaries, contrasting with the U-shaped distribution seen for entity salience.
- RST centrality is the strongest but weak predictor in a mixed-effects model (marginal r² = 0.128, p < 0.0001), explaining only ~13% of variance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The summary-based approach works because human summarizers implicitly select information they deem salient. Aggregating across multiple independent summaries provides a gradient signal that approximates consensus salience, avoiding binary judgments. Allowing approximate and component alignments captures semantic equivalence and informative generalization, which are common in summarization, though they introduce variability that requires careful guideline definition. The method’s feasibility is demonstrated by achieving moderate-to-substantial agreement on a diverse corpus, suggesting the operationalization is practical for pilot annotation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Elementary Discourse Units (EDUs):** Minimal discourse units serving as annotation targets. *Why needed:* They provide a fine-grained, theoretically grounded unit for proposition-level analysis. *Quick check:* Verify a document’s EDUs are pre-segmented and span single semantic thoughts (e.g., “The cat sat on the mat.”).
- **Summary-based salience annotation:** Using multiple human summaries to derive a salience score per unit. *Why needed:* Leverages summarization as a natural salience judgment task. *Quick check:* Confirm each summary is independent and produced without salience guidelines.
- **RST discourse trees:** Rhetorical Structure Theory trees representing hierarchical discourse structure. *Why needed:* Enables graph-based centrality measures (e.g., distance from root) as potential predictors. *Quick check:* Ensure trees are available and well-formed for all documents.
- **Inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ):** Metric for annotator consistency. *Why needed:* Validates the operationalization’s reliability. *Quick check:* Compute κ under the four reported leniency scenarios on a pilot subset.
- **Graded salience:** Continuous score (0–5) based on summary mentions. *Why needed:* Captures nuance beyond binary important/unimportant. *Quick check:* Inspect score distribution for descending pattern.

## Architecture Onboarding
The annotation pipeline consists of: **GUM corpus (with EDUs & RST trees) → Multiple human summaries → Web-based alignment interface → Salience score computation → Agreement & distribution analysis → Mixed-effects modeling with centrality**. The critical path is the alignment step, where annotators match summary spans to source EDUs, as this subjective judgment drives all downstream scores. A key design tradeoff is between strict matching (higher precision, lower recall) and lenient matching (higher coverage, more disagreement); the study adopts a middle ground with approximate/component allowances. Failure signatures include: (1) κ dropping below 0.6 for exact matches, (2) non-descending score distribution, (3) mixed-effects model marginal r² < 0.05. First experiments to run: (1) Replicate the four κ scenarios on 5 documents, (2) Plot salience score histograms per genre, (3) Re-run the mixed-effects model with additional graph centrality metrics (e.g., betweenness).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. To what extent does the summary-based proposition salience annotation methodology generalize beyond contemporary English and the GUM corpus?
2. How can the annotation guidelines be refined to improve inter-annotator agreement, particularly for "component" alignments which cause the most disagreements?
3. What additional linguistic or discourse features beyond RST centrality, EDU length, position, and relation type can substantially improve prediction of graded proposition salience?
4. Why does the distribution of graded proposition salience scores descend (most EDUs in few summaries), contrasting with the U-shaped distribution noted for entity salience in prior work?

## Limitations
- The annotation guidelines, interface code, and annotated dataset are referenced via an anonymized placeholder URL, hindering exact replication.
- The pilot dataset is small (32 documents) and genre-diverse but not statistically powered for broad generalization.
- RST centrality and other tested features explain only 13% of salience variance, limiting predictive and theoretical utility.

## Confidence
- **Moderate-to-substantial agreement (κ=0.65–0.84):** Medium (plausible but unverified due to data unavailability).
- **Descending salience score distribution:** High (direct descriptive observation).
- **RST centrality as weak predictor (marginal r²=0.128):** Medium (effect size small; significance may not replicate).
