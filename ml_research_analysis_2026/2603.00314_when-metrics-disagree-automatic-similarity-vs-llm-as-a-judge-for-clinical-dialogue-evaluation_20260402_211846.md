# When Metrics Disagree: Automatic Similarity vs. LLM-as-a-Judge for Clinical Dialogue Evaluation

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.00314
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00314
*   **Reference Count:** 28
*   **Primary Result:** Fine-tuning Llama-2-7B with LoRA on a clinical dialogue dataset significantly improves lexical similarity metrics (Track A) but is rated slightly lower by GPT-4 (Track B), revealing a critical disagreement between automatic and LLM-based evaluation paradigms.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the evaluation of clinical dialogue systems by comparing two distinct paradigms. A Llama-2-7B model was fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) on a synthetic medical dialogue corpus (GenMedGPT-5k). Evaluation revealed a profound conflict: the fine-tuned model achieved large, statistically significant improvements on standard automatic similarity metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR) compared to the baseline. In contrast, evaluation using GPT-4 as a judge across dimensions of Accuracy, Comprehensiveness, Helpfulness, and Relevance slightly favored the baseline model, though these differences were not statistically significant. The findings demonstrate that automatic metrics, which reward surface-level lexical overlap, can diverge sharply from assessments of semantic and conversational quality, underscoring the fragility of relying solely on automated scores for high-stakes medical LLM deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors performed Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) using LoRA on the `llama-2-7b-chat-hf` model. The training data was the GenMedGPT-5k dataset, a synthetic collection of 5,000 patient-physician conversations generated by ChatGPT. Training used a standard causal language modeling objective with specific hyperparameters (learning rate 3e-5, 1 epoch, sequence length 256). Evaluation was conducted in two separate tracks: Track A computed lexical similarity scores (BLEU, ROUGE variants, METEOR) against reference responses for a set of 84 "well-formed" dialogues. Track B used the GPT-4 API with a rubric prompt to score model outputs on four clinical utility dimensions for a randomly sampled set of 20 dialogue pairs.

## Key Results
*   **Track A (Automatic):** The fine-tuned model showed dramatic, statistically significant improvements: BLEU increased by 548.99%, ROUGE-2F by 84.35% (p < 0.05).
*   **Track B (LLM-as-a-Judge):** The baseline model was consistently scored higher by GPT-4 across Accuracy, Comprehensiveness, Helpfulness, and Relevance. However, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), partly due to a small evaluation sample size (N=20).
*   **Core Finding:** A major disagreement exists between evaluation paradigms; optimizing for lexical similarity does not guarantee better perceived clinical utility as judged by a large language model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: LoRA's Efficient Adaptation
*   **Claim:** LoRA enables effective domain adaptation with minimal catastrophic forgetting.
*   **Mechanism:** LoRA freezes the pre-trained Llama-2 weights and injects low-rank trainable matrices (`B` and `A`) into the Transformer attention layers. The update `ΔW = BA` (where rank `r << min(d,k)`) allows the model to learn task-specific features while preserving the base model's general language knowledge.
*   **Core assumption:** The intrinsic dimension of weight updates for downstream adaptation is low.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...injecting trainable low-rank matrices into the Transformer layers, we efficiently adapted the model... while preserving the foundational knowledge of the base model."
    *   [section 3.2] Full mathematical formulation of `W = W₀ + ΔW = W₀ + BA`.
*   **Break condition:** If the domain shift requires high-intrinsic-rank updates, LoRA's capacity would be insufficient, causing underfitting.

### Mechanism 2: Lexical vs. Semantic Metric Divergence
*   **Claim:** The disagreement stems from Track A and Track B measuring fundamentally different quality aspects.
*   **Mechanism:** Track A metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) measure surface-level n-gram overlap with a reference. Track B (GPT-4) evaluates semantic properties like fluency, coherence, and helpfulness based on its internal knowledge. The fine-tuned model, optimized for lexical similarity on synthetic data, may replicate specific training phrases, diverging from the baseline's general conversational flow that GPT-4 prefers.
*   **Core assumption:** Lexical similarity is a poor proxy for clinical utility and semantic quality in medical dialogue.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...traditional automated scores may not fully reflect clinical utility."
    *   [section 5.3] "BLEU and ROUGE metrics... blindly reward the replication of clinical vocabulary but fail to capture semantic coherence... GPT-4... may prioritize generalized conversational flow."
*   **Break condition:** If fine-tuning successfully aligns lexical precision with semantic and conversational quality, the metrics would converge.

### Mechanism 3: Synthetic Data Artifacts
*   **Claim:** The synthetic GenMedGPT-5k dataset's structure likely encouraged the fine-tuned model to optimize for surface pattern matching rather than robust clinical reasoning.
*   **Mechanism:** The machine-generated, noise-free, template-based dataset creates a homogeneous distribution. The model likely learned to replicate high-frequency phraseological patterns from this curated data, which boosts lexical metrics but may not generalize to the variability and nuanced reasoning required in real clinical dialogue.
*   **Core assumption:** Synthetic data does not fully capture the complexity of authentic patient-physician interactions.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 4.1] "...GenMedGPT-5k corpus. This smaller, highly structured dataset provides a noise-free environment..."
    *   [section 7.3] Risk identified: "The primary concern is the model’s dependency on the quality and diversity of the training data."
*   **Break condition:** Training on a large, diverse corpus of *real* clinical dialogues could reduce this gap.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Decoder-Only Transformer Architecture (e.g., Llama-2)
    *   *Why needed here:* The entire study builds on adapting this architecture. Understanding self-attention, causal masking, and autoregressive generation is prerequisite to interpreting LoRA's injection points and the model's generative behavior.
    *   *Quick check question:* How does Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) differ from absolute positional embeddings, and why might it be important for long clinical dialogues?
*   **Concept:** Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) & Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
    *   *Why needed here:* The core intervention is LoRA. One must grasp the rationale for freezing base weights, the mathematics of low-rank decomposition, and the trade-off between trainable parameters and performance to evaluate the method's suitability.
    *   *Quick check question:* What is the intuition behind the assumption that weight updates for a new task have a low "intrinsic rank," and what would a counterexample look like?
*   **Concept:** Evaluation Metrics for Text Generation (Lexical vs. Semantic)
    *   *Why needed here:* The paper's central finding is a conflict between these paradigms. A learner must understand what BLEU/ROUGE/METEOR compute (n-gram overlap) versus what an "LLM-as-a-Judge" assesses (semantic alignment, utility).
    *   *Quick check question:* Why is a high BLEU score not sufficient to guarantee that a clinical response is factually accurate or helpful to a patient?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Base Model (llama-2-7b-chat-hf) -> LoRA Adapters (injected into q_proj, v_proj) -> Training Loop (Causal LM loss) -> Inference (merged or PEFT model) -> Evaluation Track A (BLEU/ROUGE/METEOR) & Track B (GPT-4 API)
*   **Critical path:**
    1.  Load base model & tokenizer.
    2.  Prepare LoRA configuration.
    3.  Load & format GenMedGPT-5k dataset into instruction format.
    4.  Train with Trainer (e.g., Hugging Face TRL), saving LoRA adapters.
    5.  Generate responses for Track A eval set (N=84) and Track B sample (N=20).
    6.  Compute Track A metrics against references.
    7.  Format prompts for GPT-4 judge and collect scores for four dimensions.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **LoRA Rank (`r`):** Higher rank = more capacity/risk of overfitting on small `5k`. Lower rank = less expressive/potential underfitting. Paper does not report chosen `r`.
    *   **Metric Choice:** Track A is cheap/fast/reproducible but clinically irrelevant. Track B is expensive/less reproducible but correlates better with human judgment. Small `N=20` for Track B risks high variance.
    *   **Dataset:** Synthetic `GenMedGPT-5k` offers data purity but limits generalizability to real-world clinical nuance.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Track A high, Track B low:** Classic overfitting to surface patterns in training data (observed).
    *   **No statistical significance in Track B:** Sample size (`N=20`) likely too small to detect subtle differences.
    *   **Low absolute BLEU/ROUGE:** Despite large % gains, absolute scores are very low (e.g., BLEU=0.0033 baseline), indicating poor absolute performance.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Replicate & Stress-Test LoRA:** Repeat fine-tuning with ranks `r=4,8,16` and learning rates `1e-5, 3e-5`. Monitor both Track A and a small human eval subset (`N=50`) to see if disagreement pattern holds and if any hyperparameter reduces it.
    2.  **Synthetic vs. Real Data Probe:** Fine-tune a separate model on a small subset (`1k`) of *real* clinical dialogue (e.g., HealthCareMagic). Compare its metric disagreement profile to the synthetic-data model.
    3.  **GPT-4 Judge Calibration:** Run a calibration test: have human clinicians score `N=50` model outputs, then correlate human scores with GPT-4 scores on the same set to quantify GPT-4's reliability as a proxy for clinical judgment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** What are the specific linguistic or semantic features that cause automatic similarity metrics and GPT-4-as-a-judge to disagree?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The paper documents a "profound disagreement" but does not investigate *why*. It hypothesizes about memorization versus fluency but lacks an ablation or qualitative error analysis.
    *   *Why unresolved:* No formal analysis of feature importance for each metric's scoring.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* A detailed qualitative analysis of outputs with the strongest metric disagreement, extracting features (exact terminology, coherence, hallucinations) and correlating them with each metric's score.
*   **Open Question 2:** To what extent is the GPT-4 evaluator's preference an artifact of its training/prompting versus genuine higher clinical utility?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The paper uses GPT-4 as a judge and acknowledges it evaluates on "probabilistic language generation rather than rigorous medical training."
    *   *Why unresolved:* No validation of GPT-4's scores against ratings from actual medical professionals for this specific clinical context.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* A study comparing GPT-4 scores to those from a panel of licensed clinicians on the same model outputs (baseline and fine-tuned).
*   **Open Question 3:** How does the synthetic GenMedGPT-5k dataset limit the validity and generalizability of the observed metric disagreement?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The paper acknowledges the dataset is a "controlled, noise-free environment" and suggests future work with real-world data.
    *   *Why unresolved:* The entire fine-tuning and evaluation loop occurs on LLM-generated data, creating a circular evaluation that may not reflect real clinical dialogue complexity.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Re-running the pipeline on a large corpus of authentic patient-physician transcripts and comparing the pattern of metric disagreement.
*   **Open Question 4:** Can the fine-tuning process be adjusted to improve performance on *both* lexical similarity and LLM-as-a-judge criteria simultaneously?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The results show a clear trade-off. The paper speculates fine-tuning led to memorization of specific phrasing at the expense of fluent conversation.
    *   *Why unresolved:* No experimentation with different objectives, data mixtures, or regularization to attempt bridging the gap.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* An ablation study varying training data ratios, LoRA parameters, and regularization losses, measuring impact on both Track A and Track B.

## Limitations
*   **Unreported Hyperparameters:** The exact LoRA configuration (rank `r`, alpha, target modules) is missing, preventing precise replication of adaptation capacity.
*   **Ambiguous Evaluation Sets:** Criteria for the Track A set (N=84 "well-formed") and sampling method for Track B (N=20) are unspecified, risking selection bias.
*   **Synthetic Data Dependency:** Using a homogeneous, machine-generated dataset for both training and reference texts may have created a circular evaluation that inflates lexical metrics without reflecting real-world clinical utility.

## Confidence
*   **LoRA enables effective adaptation for this task:** **Medium**. Theoretically sound and described, but lacking ablation evidence (rank sensitivity) for this specific clinical task.
*   **Metric disagreement reflects a lexical vs. semantic divide:** **Medium**. Plausible and consistent with literature, but Track B's small `N` and uncalibrated GPT-4 judge limit certainty.
*   **Synthetic data encouraged surface optimization:** **Low-to-Medium**. Supported by risk acknowledgment and corpus signals, but not empirically isolated (no real-data comparison).

## Next Checks
1.  **Hyperparameter Ablation & Metric Disagreement Robustness:** Re-run LoRA fine-tuning with ranks `r ∈ {4, 8, 16}` and Learning Rates `[1e-5, 3e-5]`. For each, compute both Track A and Track B (with `N≥100`, `temperature=0`). Test if disagreement persists across capacity regimes.
2.  **GPT-4 Judge Calibration Against Clinicians:** Collect human expert scores (1–5) on the same four dimensions for a fixed subset (`N=50`) of model outputs. Compute correlation between human and GPT-4 scores. A correlation `r < 0.6` would question Track B's validity.
3.  **Synthetic vs. Real Data Fine-tuning Comparison:** Fine-tune a separate model on a small (`~1k`) authentic clinical dialogue corpus (e.g., HealthCareMagic). Compare its Track A/Track B discrepancy profile against the GenMedGPT-5k model.
