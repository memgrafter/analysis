# AI Gamestore: Scalable, Open-Ended Evaluation of Machine General Intelligence with Human Games
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.17594
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17594
- **Reference count**: 25
- **Primary result**: Frontier vision-language models achieved geometric mean scores below 10% of human median on most games and were 12–18× slower at gameplay, with key deficits in memory, planning, and world-model learning.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AI GameStore, a platform for evaluating general artificial intelligence by tasking models with playing a diverse set of human-designed games. By sourcingpopular games from digital marketplaces (Apple App Store, Steam) and converting them into standardized p5.js implementations via a hybrid LLM–human pipeline, the authors assembled a benchmark of 100 games. In a proof-of-concept evaluation, seven state-of-the-art vision-language models performed substantially worse than 106 human participants, rarely exceeding 10% of the human median score on most games. Performance was bimodal, with partial progress (10–30%) on two-thirds of games and near-complete failure (<1%) on the remaining third. Models also required 12–18 times more time than humans to complete identical 120-second sessions. The work argues that open-ended evaluation via a "multiverse of human games" better probes general intelligence than narrow benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The evaluation task required models to play 100 generated games in a p5.js environment over a fixed 120‑second period. Games were sourced from top charts of the Apple App Store and Steam, filtered by popularity (≥10 000 reviews, ≥4.5/5 rating) and suitability via an LLM scorer that ensured games could be played in minutes, expressed in p5.js, and had quantifiable metrics. For each selected game, an LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.5) generated an initial codebase, which underwent automated testing and then iterative human refinement (≈4.7 rounds) to ensure playability and alignment with the source game’s core mechanics. To evaluate models, a harness paused gameplay every second, provided a prompt containing the game description, a scratchpad, previous actions, and a screenshot, and requested five action segments (each 0.2 s) from the vision-language model. Models were run three times each, and their scores normalized to human medians; runtime (model decision time vs. human playtime) was also measured.

## Key Results
- Frontier VLMs achieved geometric mean normalized scores below 10% of the human median on most games; performance was bimodal: 10–30% of human level on two‑thirds of games and <1% on 30–40%.
- Capability annotations revealed consistent bottlenecks across models in memory, planning, and world‑model learning.
- Models took 12–18 times longer than humans to complete the same 120‑second gameplay sessions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1 – Diverse sourcing via popularity and suitability filters**
Harvesting games from major marketplaces and applying a dual‑filter (popularity/rating thresholds plus an LLM‑driven suitability scorer) yields a finite, representative sample of "human games" that spans a broad spectrum of cognitive demands. The underlying assumption is that popular, well‑rated games are valid proxies for the cognitive skills humans develop through play. If the suitability filter rejects genuinely rich games or the final set shows low diversity, this mechanism fails.

**Mechanism 2 – Hybrid LLM generation and human refinement**
An LLM generates a functional p5.js draft from a game description, which is then polished via automated testing and iterative human feedback. This balances scalability (LLM code production) with quality control (human assessment of fun and mechanics), producing standardized variants that retain core mechanics while being suitable for rigorous evaluation. If refinement steps grow super‑linearly with complexity or humans judge refined variants as less authentic, the mechanism does not scale.

**Mechanism 3 – Cognitive demand annotation for diagnostic analysis**
Expert annotators rate each game on six cognitive capabilities (e.g., memory, planning) using structured rubrics, yielding a multi‑dimensional profile. Grouping games by these profiles allows identification of consistent performance bottlenecks across models. If inter‑annotator agreement is low or capability‑based groupings show high variance in model scores, the profiling lacks reliability for diagnosis.

## Foundational Learning

- **Human games as a cognitive distribution**  
  *Why needed*: The benchmark’s validity relies on the assumption that the space of human‑designed games samples a meaningful distribution of real‑world cognitive skills. Understanding this "training ground" analogy is foundational to interpreting results.  
  *Quick check*: If a game is trivial for humans but extremely hard for an AI, does that automatically indicate a general cognitive deficit, or could it be a narrow, implementation‑specific failure?

- **Human-in-the-loop iterative refinement**  
  *Why needed*: The pipeline’s feasibility depends on efficiently transforming raw game concepts into standardized, playable environments. The hybrid LLM + human loop is a core architectural innovation.  
  *Quick check*: At what point does human refinement introduce so much variance that the "standardized" nature of the benchmark is compromised?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Sourcing Module (scrapes marketplaces, applies popularity filter, LLM suitability scorer) → Generation Module (LLM produces initial p5.js code) → Refinement Module (automated test script executor, human feedback interface, iterative LLM patching, variant generator) → Annotation Module (human annotators apply cognitive rubrics, produce demand vectors) → Evaluation Harness (pauses game, captures screenshot, sends prompt to VLM, receives and applies action lists).

**Critical path**  
Sourcing → Generation (V0) → Automated Test → Human Refinement (to V1) → Annotation → Model Evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Scalability vs. Human Fidelity: More refinement steps improve game quality but limit throughput; current average ~30 min/game is a fixed cost.
- Generality vs. Performance: The p5.js constraint enables a universal interface but restricts genre fidelity (e.g., fast‑paced 3D action), biasing the corpus.
- Annotation Depth vs. Breadth: Detailed 6‑dimension rubrics provide diagnostic power but require three annotators per game; a coarser scale would allow more games but less insight.

**Failure signatures**
- Sourcing: LLM suitability filter outputs low‑variance scores, or final game set under‑represents certain genres/capabilities from source platforms.
- Generation: High percentage of initial (V0) games failing basic automated tests; refinement loop failing to converge (e.g., >10 steps without human satisfaction).
- Evaluation: Model performance distributions (Figure 6) showing no bimodality (all games equally hard/easy) could indicate a flawed or homogenized game set.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Ablate the Human Refinement**: Run the pipeline for 20 games with only automated testing (no human feedback). Measure playability (crash rate), fun ratings (from an independent human cohort), and resulting cognitive profile diversity vs. the full 100‑game set.
2. **Validate Cognitive Annotations**: Correlate the human‑annotated demand scores (e.g., Memory: 0–5) with actual human player performance metrics (e.g., scores under time pressure). Low correlation would suggest invalid rubrics.
3. **Test the Harness Sensitivity**: Run a single capable VLM (e.g., GPT‑5.2) on the 10 public games with three different harness frequencies (query every 0.5 s, 1.0 s, 2.0 s). If performance changes drastically, the evaluation is overly sensitive to the arbitrary thought‑frequency parameter.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
*How can the AI GameStore platform automatically generate and integrate games featuring non‑player characters (NPCs) that employ theory of mind—such as belief attribution and intention reading—to create adaptive social challenges for evaluating AI social reasoning?*  
The current suite largely lacks sophisticated social interaction scenarios, leaving a gap in assessing AI’s capacity for recursive mentalizing. Future work could draw inspiration from frameworks like Melting Pot to build environments that require complex multi‑agent interaction.

**Open Question 2**  
*What procedural generation techniques can create long‑horizon, complex game scenarios (e.g., multi‑hour gameplay with evolving narratives) that consistently engage humans while imposing severe memory and planning demands on AI systems?*  
Existing games are short and simple, failing to probe AI’s ability to integrate information over extended periods. An algorithmic system that generates games with calibrated temporal depth (e.g., missions requiring dozens of sequential decisions) would force models to maintain state across vast temporal windows and differentiate between short‑ and long‑term reasoning deficits.

**Open Question 3**  
*How can level generation within the AI GameStore be fully automated to produce levels that are both playable and precisely tuned to specific cognitive demand profiles (e.g., exact memory load or planning depth), enabling scalable, fine‑grained evaluation?*  
The present human‑in‑the‑loop level refinement is a bottleneck; models often generate trivial or impossible levels. A fully automated level generator that accepts a game specification and target cognitive demand (verified by human annotations and model performance gradients) would greatly improve scalability and diagnostic power.

**Open Question 4**  
*What methodological frameworks can disentangle composite cognitive skills in sequential game‑playing to isolate specific capabilities (e.g., distinguishing memory failures from planning deficits), given that a single game often taxes multiple integrated abilities?*  
Relying on coarse cognitive annotations and aggregate scores cannot pinpoint the underlying reasons for model failures. Adapting techniques such as measurement layouts or ADeLe to interactive settings could produce per‑game, per‑capability scores that better explain performance and guide architectural improvements.

## Limitations
- **Transparency**: The evaluation harness code is not released; only 10 of 100 games are public; per‑game human performance medians are unavailable, hindering full replication.
- **Pipeline biases**: The suitability LLM filter may exclude games with high cognitive demand but low popularity; the p5.js requirement biases the corpus toward 2D, slow‑paced games, omitting genres like real‑time action or 3D exploration.
- **Human refinement variance**: Iterative human feedback could alter the original game’s mechanics or difficulty, and the required 4.7 steps may not scale linearly with complexity.

## Confidence
- **Sourcing and generation pipeline yields a diverse, standardized game set**: Medium (supported by internal diversity metrics but lacking external validation of the suitability filter or genre representation).
- **Cognitive bottleneck diagnosis (memory, planning, world‑model) is accurate**: Medium‑High (results are consistent across seven models and correlate with intuitive difficulty, yet rely on unverified rubrics).
- **Models are 12–18× slower than humans**: Low‑Medium (heavily dependent on harness configuration; without sensitivity analysis, the number may be an artifact of the evaluation design).

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation of human refinement**: Re‑run the generation pipeline for a subset (e.g., 20 games) without human feedback. Compare automated‑only games to the refined ones on crash rate, fun ratings, and cognitive profile diversity.
2. **Validate cognitive annotations**: Compute correlations between human‑annotated demand scores (e.g., Memory: 0–5) and actual human player performance (scores, completion rates). Low correlation indicates poor rubric validity.
3. **Harness sensitivity analysis**: For the 10 public games, vary the model query interval (0.5 s, 1 s, 2 s) and measure changes in normalized scores and runtime ratios; report the performance range to bound robustness.
