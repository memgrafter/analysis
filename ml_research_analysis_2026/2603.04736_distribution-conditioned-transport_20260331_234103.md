# Distribution-Conditioned Transport

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.04736  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04736  
- **Reference Count**: 40  
- **Primary Result**: DCT conditions transport maps on distribution embeddings, enabling generalizable any-to-any transport and superior performance on out-of-distribution biological tasks.

## Executive Summary
Transport models traditionally learn a map for a specific source-target pair, making them brittle when applied to new distributions. This is especially problematic in scientific domains like single-cell genomics, where data come from many related but distinct conditions and paired observations are scarce. Distribution‑Conditioned Transport (DCT) addresses this by learning embeddings that characterize entire distributions and conditioning a transport mechanism (e.g., flow matching or MMD) on these embeddings. As a result, a single model can generate transport maps for any source‑target pair, even unseen ones, and can incorporate “orphan” marginals observed at only a single condition. Evaluations on synthetic Gaussian benchmarks and four biological applications—scRNA‑seq batch transfer, mass cytometry drug perturbation prediction, clonal dynamics, and TCR sequence forecasting—show consistent improvements over baselines, often matching oracle performance in out‑of‑distribution scenarios.

## Method Summary
DCT first uses an encoder network to map samples from a marginal distribution into a low‑dimensional distribution embedding that captures global statistical properties (e.g., mean, covariance, higher moments). These embeddings serve as conditioning variables for a transport estimator, which can be any standard mechanism such as continuous normalizing flows via flow matching or a kernel‑based MMD loss. During training, the model is given pairs of source‑target marginals; the encoder produces embeddings for each, and the transport estimator learns to predict a map that transports source samples to align with the target. Because the transport map is a function of the embeddings rather than the raw data, the same trained conditional estimator can be applied to any new source‑target pair simply by computing their embeddings. Orphan marginals (distributions observed without a partner) are still embedded, allowing the model to perform semi‑supervised training by leveraging the shared embedding space. The framework is agnostic to the specific transport mechanism and makes efficient use of all available marginals, even when paired data are limited.

## Key Results
- **scRNA‑seq batch transfer**: DCT reduced MMD distance by a factor of 2–3 compared to traditional K‑to‑K models.  
- **Mass cytometry drug perturbation (OOD patients)**: Sliced Wasserstein distance dropped from 2.59 to 1.97, matching oracle performance by utilizing unpaired data.  
- **Clonal dynamics & TCR forecasting**: Conditioning on both source and target embeddings significantly improved prediction accuracy over supervised models trained on source data alone.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Distribution embeddings provide a compact, continuous representation of a marginal’s location, shape, and higher‑order statistics. By conditioning the transport map on these embeddings, DCT learns a *family* of maps parameterized by the source and target embeddings rather than a single static map. This parametrization generalizes smoothly: embeddings that are similar (e.g., two Gaussians with nearby means) yield similar transport behaviors, so unseen pairs whose embeddings lie within the training manifold are handled naturally. Orphan marginals are accommodated because the embedding function is trained on all observed marginals, and the conditional estimator can be paired with a default or inferred counterpart, effectively leveraging the learned manifold structure to impute missing information.

## Foundational Learning
- **Optimal Transport (OT)**: Provides the mathematical foundation for mapping one distribution to another at minimal cost. Many transport estimators are derived from OT.  
  *Quick Check*: Implement the Sinkhorn algorithm to compute an approximate OT plan between two empirical distributions.

- **Flow Matching**: A simulation‑free method for training continuous normalizing flows by matching a conditional probability path, yielding a differentiable transport map. Used as a conditional estimator in DCT.  
  *Quick Check*: Train a flow matching model to transform a standard normal distribution into a 2‑D Gaussian.

- **Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)**: A kernel‑based distance between distributions; serves as an alternative training objective for transport and as an evaluation metric.  
  *Quick Check*: Compute MMD between two sets of samples using a Gaussian (RBF) kernel.

- **Distribution Embeddings**: Mapping an entire distribution to a fixed‑dimensional vector (e.g., via moments or neural networks). Core to DCT’s conditioning on whole distributions.  
  *Quick Check*: Train an encoder to produce embeddings for Gaussians with varying means and covariances; verify that embeddings cluster by true parameters.

- **Conditional Transport**: Extends OT so that the transport map depends on additional context (here, source and target embeddings). Enables generalization across pairs.  
  *Quick Check*: Build a conditional OT model that takes a context vector and applies it to transform a source distribution.

- **Orphan Marginals**: Marginals observed at only one condition (source or target). DCT’s semi‑supervised setting leverages these unpaired distributions.  
  *Quick Check*: Simulate a dataset containing both paired and orphan marginals; train a model that uses all data and evaluates transport on held‑out pairs.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Raw marginals (paired or orphan) → Encoder Networks → Distribution Embeddings (z_s, z_t) → Conditional Transport Estimator (e.g., Flow Matching or MMD) → Transport Map (velocity field / coupling) → Transformed Samples  

**Critical Path**  
1. Encode each marginal (source/target) to obtain embeddings.  
2. For paired data, train the conditional transport estimator to map from (z_s, z_t) to a transport operator.  
3. At inference, compute embeddings for a new source‑target pair and apply the learned operator to the source samples.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Embedding capacity vs. data efficiency*: Larger encoders capture finer details but need more data to avoid overfitting.  
- *Transport mechanism choice*: Flow Matching offers differentiable trajectories and efficient training; MMD provides a simpler distributional loss but may be less flexible.  
- *Orphan marginal handling*: Including them via semi‑supervised loss weights improves data usage but can introduce noise if embeddings are unreliable.  
- *Scalability*: High‑dimensional data may require dimensionality reduction or approximations (e.g., random features for MMD) to keep memory and compute in check.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Poor embedding quality (small sample size, large shift) → mis‑conditioned maps and bad alignment.  
- Overfitting to training pairs → large train/validation performance gap on unseen pairs.  
- Numerical instability in transport estimation (e.g., diverging flow matching loss) → degenerate outputs.  
- Memory or time blow‑up when naively scaling to very high dimensions.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Synthetic Gaussian transport**: Validate basic generalization on 2‑D Gaussians with varying means/covariances.  
2. **scRNA‑seq batch correction**: Apply DCT to a public batch‑effect dataset and compare against standard methods.  
3. **Embedding dimension ablation**: Vary embedding size and measure impact on a held‑out biological task to find optimal capacity.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility**: Code and data availability are not reported, hindering independent verification.  
- **Scalability**: Computational complexity and performance on high‑dimensional data remain unexplored.  
- **Embedding sensitivity**: The model’s reliance on quality distribution embeddings is not analyzed; performance may degrade with small samples or noisy marginals, and no theoretical guarantees for generalization or robustness are provided.
