# SLO-Conditioned Action Routing for Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Objective Ablation and Failure Modes

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.00841  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00841  
- **Reference count:** 19  
- **Primary result:** Learned policies provide only marginal reward improvement under a quality-focused SLO but collapse into near-total refusal (>94%) under a cost-focused SLO, while a strong fixed baseline (k=2, guarded) is consistently optimal.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates a practical control problem in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): selecting per-query retrieval depth and generation mode to meet service-level objectives (SLOs). The task is formulated as discrete action selection from a small set (retrieval k ∈ {2,5,10}, guarded/auto generation, or refuse). Using an offline dataset logged from SQuAD 2.0 with a BM25 retriever and GPT-4.1-nano, the authors learn policies via two simple objectives: supervised best-action classification (Argmax-CE) and a reward-weighted variant (Argmax-CE-WT). The core finding is that a single conservative fixed action (retrieve 2 passages with guarded generation) is often empirically optimal. While Argmax-CE yields modest reward gains under a quality-weighted SLO, policies trained under a cost-weighted SLO catastrophically collapse to refusing nearly all queries, producing near-zero accuracy. The reward-weighted objective frequently overfits, increasing operational cost without improving the composite SLO reward.

## Method Summary
The problem is cast as an offline contextual bandit: a policy π(a|s) maps a query state s (derived from question embedding and metadata) to one of five actions. The scalar reward r(q,a) linearly combines accuracy, token cost, hallucination penalty, and refusal reward with SLO-specific weights (quality-first vs. cheap). A logged dataset is created by executing all actions on a sample of 200 SQuAD 2.0 questions, recording metrics and rewards. Policies are trained to mimic the best-action per state from this log, using either standard cross-entropy (Argmax-CE) or cross-entropy weighted by the reward margin between the best and second-best action (Argmax-CE-WT). Evaluation compares learned policies against fixed-action baselines, particularly the best fixed action on the evaluation set.

## Key Results
- A fixed conservative action (k=2, guarded) is the best fixed baseline across all SLO conditions.
- Under a quality-focused SLO, Argmax-CE improves average reward (−0.0287 vs. −0.0419) and accuracy (0.275 vs. 0.250) over the best fixed baseline, but at a higher token cost.
- Under a cost-focused SLO, both Argmax-CE and Argmax-CE-WT collapse to refusing >94% of queries, yielding near-zero accuracy and demonstrating a severe failure mode of reward misalignment.
- The reward-weighted objective (Argmax-CE-WT) often overfits, increasing retrieval cost (e.g., higher k) without achieving a corresponding reward gain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Reward-weighted learning (Argmax-CE-WT) can overfit to actions that increase operational costs without improving the SLO-weighted reward.
- **Mechanism:** Weighting the cross-entropy loss by the margin (best minus second-best reward) amplifies the influence of confident training examples. If the reward proxies (hit-rate, token cost) are imperfectly aligned with true utility, the policy may select expensive, high-coverage actions (e.g., k=10, auto) that increase cost but not the composite reward.
- **Core assumption:** The logged per-action rewards accurately reflect the SLO components and the margin is a valid confidence measure.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "reward-weighted objective often overfits, increasing cost without reward gain."; [section 6.3] Argmax-CE-WT under quality first shifts heavily toward expensive actions, achieving higher retrieval hit-rate but worse reward than best fixed baseline.
- **Break condition:** The reward model (using hit-rate and cost as proxies) is poorly correlated with true SLO determinants like answer correctness.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under a cost-focused SLO, the learned policy can collapse to near-total refusal, collapsing accuracy to near zero.
- **Mechanism:** The reward includes +w_ref·Ref (reward for correct refusal). If refusal is the cheapest action (minimal tokens, no hallucination risk) and w_cost is high, the best per-state action in the logged data becomes "refuse" for most queries. The supervised objective then learns to predict this degenerate "best action."
- **Core assumption:** The SLO-weighted reward correctly quantifies trade-offs and the offline dataset accurately captures refusal outcomes.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "under a cheap SLO, policies collapse to near-total refusal (>94%) and near-zero accuracy."; [section 6.2] "Under cheap, Argmax-CE collapses to refusal (94.5%), yielding near-zero accuracy."; [section 7.1] "optimizing for risk/cost without a calibrated abstention constraint often yields 'always abstain' solutions."
- **Break condition:** The reward lacks a hard constraint or strong negative weight on unnecessary refusals (e.g., on answerable questions).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A strong, conservative fixed action (low retrieval depth, guarded generation) is often empirically optimal or near-optimal, making learned adaptive routing provide only marginal gains under specific SLOs.
- **Mechanism:** The reward landscape from the specific retriever (BM25) and generator (GPT-4.1-nano) may have a shallow basin where a single action performs well across most states. The marginal accuracy gains from deeper retrieval are outweighed by cost and hallucination risk for most SQuAD queries.
- **Core assumption:** The state features (question embedding, length, simple uncertainty) are not sufficiently discriminative to identify a large subset of queries where a more expensive action yields a meaningful SLO improvement.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "a strong fixed baseline (k=2, guarded) is consistently best."; [section 6.1] "Across all conditions, the best fixed action is action 0 (retrieve k=2 with guarded generation)… Under quality first, Argmax-CE improves average reward from −0.0419 (best fixed) to −0.0287."; [table 1] Best fixed reward is consistently higher or comparable to learned policies except modest gain in quality-first Argmax-CE.
- **Break condition:** The dataset contains a long-tail of complex queries (multi-hop, ambiguous) where deeper retrieval or auto-generation provides a clear reward advantage not captured by average-case performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Offline Contextual Bandit Learning from Logged Feedback**  
  *Why needed here:* The controller must learn a policy π(a|s) from a pre-recorded dataset of (state, action, reward) tuples without environment interaction. This requires understanding pitfalls of distribution shift and using simple objectives (like best-action classification) as policy learning proxies.  
  *Quick check question:* If the logged dataset only contains actions from a fixed, suboptimal behavior policy, what key assumption must hold for a trained policy to outperform the logging policy under a new SLO?

- **Concept: Service-Level Objective (SLO) as a Weighted Linear Reward**  
  *Why needed here:* The routing problem is framed by a scalar reward r(q,a) = w_acc·Acc − w_cost·Cost − w_hall·Hall + w_ref·Ref. Understanding how weight choices (quality-first vs. cheap) reshape the optimal policy is central to analyzing failure modes.  
  *Quick check question:* Why does increasing w_ref (reward for correct refusal) not necessarily improve the system's helpfulness, based on the paper's findings?

- **Concept: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Pipeline Mechanics**  
  *Why needed here:* The state representation and action outcomes depend on the retriever (BM25 on SQuAD), generator (GPT-4.1-nano), and prompt templates (guarded vs. auto). The observed failure modes are tied to this specific instantiation.  
  *Quick check question:* How does the "guarded" prompting mode differ from "auto" mode in terms of refusal incentives, and how might this interact with the SLO reward?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Question Text -> State Encoder (embedding + metadata) -> Policy Network (MLP classifier) -> Action Selection (a ∈ {k=2/5/10, guarded/auto, refuse}) -> Action Executor (retrieval + LLM generation) -> Metrics (Acc, Cost, Hall, Ref). Offline Logger precomputes (s, a, r, metrics) for all actions per question. Reward Computer applies SLO weights to metrics to produce r.
- **Critical path:** State Encoder → Policy Network → Action Selection. The Action Executor runs only during offline logging; online inference uses only the first two steps.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Action space size (5 actions) vs. expressivity: small space enables stable offline learning but limits fine-grained control.  
  - State feature richness vs. generalization: simple features (length, BM25 scores) may not capture query difficulty; more features risk overfitting to logged distribution.  
  - Reward linearity vs. fidelity: Linear SLO weights are transparent but may not capture non-linear trade-offs (e.g., hard refusal rate constraints).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Refusal collapse: Policy outputs action=refuse for >90% of queries under cost-heavy SLO.  
  - Cost explosion: Policy (e.g., Argmax-CE-WT) selects high-k (k=10) and auto mode excessively, inflating token cost.  
  - No improvement: Learned policy's reward/accuracy is within noise of best fixed action across all SLOs.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Establishment:** Evaluate all fixed actions (0–4) on a validation set under each SLO to identify the "best fixed" baseline. This sets the performance bar for any learned policy.  
  2. **Quality-First Policy Training:** Train Argmax-CE with quality-first SLO weights. Evaluate on held-out set. Expectation: modest reward/accuracy gain at higher cost. Check whether action distribution remains conservative or diversifies.  
  3. **Cheap SLO Policy Training:** Train Argmax-CE with cheap SLO weights. Critical test: observe refusal rate and accuracy. Expectation: near-total refusal collapse. This confirms the core failure mode and tests if mitigation (e.g., adding a minimum accuracy constraint) is needed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can SLO reward functions be designed to avoid refusal collapse while still enforcing cost constraints in RAG routing?  
  *Basis in paper:* The cheap SLO profile causes policies to refuse >94% of queries (Section 6.2), and Section 7.1 attributes this to optimizing per-query cost without abstention constraints, a known issue in selective prediction.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper does not propose or evaluate alternative reward formulations that balance cost and utility, leaving a gap in practical SLO design.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative experiments with modified rewards (e.g., adding refusal rate penalties, using constrained optimization) showing stable policies that reduce cost without catastrophic refusal.

- **Open Question 2:** What causes the performance degradation of reward-weighted policy learning (Argmax-CE-WT) in quality-focused settings, and how can its instability be mitigated?  
  *Basis in paper:* Under quality-first SLO, Argmax-CE-WT increases token cost significantly and yields worse reward than both best fixed baseline and Argmax-CE (Section 6.3, Table 1), despite aiming to focus on high-margin examples.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper suggests overfitting to actions that increase retrieval coverage but lacks diagnostic analysis; the interaction between margin estimation, reward noise, and action space is not understood.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Error analysis decomposing reward contributions, studying margin distributions, and testing alternative confidence estimates or regularization to prevent overemphasis on expensive actions.

- **Open Question 3:** To what extent do offline-learned RAG routing policies generalize across changes in retrieval or generation components, and what are effective transfer methods?  
  *Basis in paper:* Limitation 8.2 warns that policies may not transfer when the underlying retriever or generator changes, as the logged dataset is tied to a specific configuration (BM25 retriever, GPT-4.1-nano).  
  *Why unresolved:* The study only evaluates the static setup; real-world systems require adaptation to model updates or corpus shifts, but no transfer experiments are conducted.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic transfer studies where either the retriever (e.g., switching to dense vectors) or generator (e.g., different prompts or LLMs) is altered, measuring policy degradation and evaluating techniques like re-weighting logged data or few-shot adaptation.

## Limitations
- State feature engineering (exact embedding model and metadata computation) is unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Precise SLO weight coefficients (w_acc, w_cost, etc.) are unreported, obscuring the exact reward shaping that drives policy behavior.
- The offline log uses only 200 SQuAD 2.0 questions, raising concerns about dataset scale, representativeness, and result variance.

## Confidence
- Fixed baseline superiority, refusal collapse, reward-weighted overfitting: High  
- Mechanistic explanations (e.g., margin-weighted overfitting, shallow reward landscape): Medium  
- Generalizability beyond SQuAD 2.0/BM25/GPT-4.1-nano: Low
