# Automated univariate time series forecasting with regression trees
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.00077
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00077
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: An automated regression tree-based forecasting method using additive trend transformation and random forests achieves median MASE 0.929 on M4 yearly (vs ARIMA 0.895) and 0.929 on M4 quarterly (vs ETS 0.886), requiring no parameter tuning via the `utsf` R package.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents an off‑the‑shelf solution for univariate time series forecasting based on regression trees and ensemble methods. The core approach automatically handles trends via an additive transformation, selects relevant autoregressive lags based on seasonal period or partial autocorrelation, and employs recursive multi‑step forecasting. Implemented in the publicly available `utsf` R package, the method requires no manual parameter tuning yet yields accuracy comparable to established statistical models on M4 competition data: random forests achieve median MASE 0.929 on yearly series (ARIMA: 0.895) and 0.929 on quarterly series (ETS: 0.886). These results demonstrate that tree‑based ensembles can serve as robust, fully automated forecasting tools.

## Method Summary
The method converts a univariate time series into a supervised regression problem by constructing lagged features. It automatically determines the lag order: for seasonal series, all lags up to the seasonal period are included; for non‑seasonal series, significant partial autocorrelation lags are selected, defaulting to lags 1–5. To address trends, an additive transformation subtracts the mean of the lag vector from both the target and (optionally) the features, thereby stabilizing the series around zero. A random forest regression model (default 500 trees, `mtry = floor(p/3)`) is trained on this transformed dataset. Multi‑step forecasts are generated recursively: at each step, the latest observations (including prior forecasts) are used to construct the input, the model predicts the transformed value, and the stored mean is added back to produce the final forecast. The entire pipeline is encapsulated in the `utsf` R package, which automates all steps and requires no user‑specified hyperparameters.

## Key Results
- On M4 yearly data, random forests achieve a median MASE of 0.929; ARIMA achieves 0.895.
- On M4 quarterly data, random forests achieve a median MASE of 0.929; exponential smoothing achieves 0.886.
- The methodology is fully automated and available in the `utsf` R package, requiring no parameter tuning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Additive transformation of features and targets enables regression trees to accurately forecast trending series.
- **Mechanism**: The transformation subtracts the mean of the lag vector from each target and (optionally) each feature, making the series stationary around zero. During forecasting, the mean of the input feature vector is added back to the prediction, restoring the trend.
- **Core assumption**: The trend is additive (linear) and the mean of the input vector accurately reflects the current level.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] Mentions "additive transformation of features and targets".
  - [section 5] Details the transformation and reports experimental results on M4 yearly: additive transformation yields median MASE 2.468, best among strategies.
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers focus on neural architectures and do not evaluate such transformations; evidence is specific to this work.
- **Break condition**: Fails for multiplicative trends where multiplicative transformation might be needed but was less effective in experiments.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Including autoregressive lags up to the seasonal period captures seasonal patterns effectively.
- **Mechanism**: For seasonal series with period s, including lag s (and often lags 1..s) provides the model with the previous season's value, allowing regression trees to split on it and differentiate seasons.
- **Core assumption**: Seasonality is deterministic and repeats every s periods.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "automatically selects autoregressive lags based on seasonal period or partial autocorrelation".
  - [section 6] Artificial quarterly example: using lag 4 forecasts correctly; using lag 1 fails. Experiments on M4 quarterly show adding lags 1 to 4 reduces median MASE from 1.165 (lag1) to 1.032.
  - [corpus] Related works (e.g., SutraNets) use autoregressive networks but do not explicitly address lag selection for seasonality.
- **Break condition**: If seasonality is non-integer, evolving, or not aligned with a fixed lag, simple lag s may not suffice.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Random forests improve accuracy by reducing variance of single regression trees without requiring tuning.
- **Mechanism**: Bootstrap aggregation builds many trees on different subsets; random forests further randomize feature selection at splits. Averaging predictions reduces variance, especially for high‑variance base learners.
- **Core assumption**: Individual regression trees are high‑variance; aggregating decorrelated trees lowers expected error.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "ensemble methods (bagging, random forests)".
  - [section 4] Explains bagging and random forests, noting they "tend to offer a good performance with a default configuration".
  - [section 7] Table 6: Random forests achieve median MASE 0.929 on M4 quarterly, better than single regression tree (median MASE 1.032) and close to exponential smoothing (0.886).
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; neighboring papers explore different ensemble architectures but not random forests for time series.
- **Break condition**: Very short series may lead to underfitted bootstrap trees; computational cost scales with number of trees.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- **Series input**: Univariate time series (e.g., `ts` object).
- **Automatic lag selector**: Determines seasonal period from frequency or uses PACF to pick lags; defaults to lags 1..s if seasonal, else lags 1..5 if no significant PACF.
- **Trend transformer**: Applies additive transformation (subtract mean of lag vector from target and features) to training data; stores mean for back‑transformation.
- **Model trainer**: Fits random forest (default: 500 trees, `mtry = floor(p/3)`) using regression trees as base learners; alternatives: bagging or single tree.
- **Recursive forecaster**: Generates multi‑step forecasts by iteratively feeding predictions back as lag features.
- **Output**: Forecast time series with point predictions.

### Critical path
1. Input series and horizon h.
2. Determine seasonal period s: if frequency > 1, s = frequency; else treat as non‑seasonal.
3. Select lags: if seasonal, use 1..s; else compute PACF, select significant lags, fallback to 1..5.
4. Apply additive transformation: for each training example, compute μ = mean(features). Transform target: y' = y – μ; optionally transform features: X' = X – μ. Store μ.
5. Build lagged feature matrix.
6. Train random forest on transformed data.
7. For i = 1 to h: construct input from latest observations (including forecasts), compute μ, predict transformed value, add μ, record forecast, update series.
8. Return forecast object.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Additive vs. differencing**: Additive is simpler and performed best on M4 yearly, but differencing may be more robust for non‑linear trends. The default is additive.
- **Lag set size**: Many lags provide completeness but add noise; regression trees filter irrelevant features, but random forests may suffer with high‑dimensional noise. Recommendation: use generous lags when unsure.
- **Ensemble size**: More trees (e.g., 500) improve accuracy but increase compute; for large data one might reduce ntree.
- **Feature transformation**: Transforming features (subtracting mean) helps with level shifts but adds computational cost; paper found it beneficial.
- **Automation vs. control**: Full automation is the goal, but exposing parameters (e.g., `trend`) allows expert intervention for problematic series.

### Failure signatures
- **Constant forecast**: Likely `trend="none"` on a trending series; check transformation setting.
- **Missing seasonality**: Likely seasonal lags not used (e.g., lag 1 for quarterly); verify lag selection.
- **Overfitting/Oscillating forecasts**: Overly deep trees or insufficient data; check `minsplit` and tree depth.
- **Training error**: "insufficient data" if series too short for chosen lags; ensure length > max(lag)+1.
- **Poor performance on outliers**: Series with extreme outliers may distort transformation; consider robust scaling or outlier detection.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Trend test**: Forecast a linear trend series (e.g., `ts(1:100)`) with default (additive) and `trend="none"`. Default should produce an increasing line; `none` should be flat. Compute holdout MASE to confirm drastic improvement.
2. **Seasonal lag test**: Use artificial quarterly series (5,5,5,10 repeating). Train with `lags=1` and `lags=1:4`. The former yields constant forecast ~6.25; latter captures the 10 spike. Visualize.
3. **Ensemble benefit**: On USAccDeaths data, compare single tree (`method="rt"`) and random forest (`method="rf"`) with same lags and additive transformation. Expect RF to have lower validation MASE and smoother forecasts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** What is the precise computational cost and accuracy trade‑off between using bagging versus random forests for automated time series forecasting?

**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state random forests achieve better median MASE (0.929) than bagging (0.957) on M4 yearly data but note "random forests demand more computational time." They do not quantify this trade‑off or provide a guideline for when the accuracy gain justifies the extra cost.

**Why unresolved:** The paper presents results for both methods but does not analyze their relative efficiency or provide a framework for selecting between them in an automated tool where computational resources may be constrained.

**What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic benchmark measuring runtime and memory usage against accuracy (e.g., MASE) across diverse series characteristics (length, frequency, trend strength) to establish a cost‑benefit curve.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How robust and generally applicable is the proposed automated lag selection strategy (using seasonal period or partial autocorrelation) across different time series domains and noise levels?

**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The methodology automatically selects lags: "If the series is seasonal, use lags from 1 to the length of the seasonal period... If the series is not seasonal, use the significant autocorrelated lags." This is a heuristic not validated outside the M4 competition data.

**Why unresolved:** The effectiveness is inferred from good M4 results but not tested on series with weak/irregular seasonality, high noise, or non‑standard seasonal patterns. The assumption that including the seasonal period is always sufficient is not proven.

**What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical evaluation on diverse external datasets (e.g., retail, energy, web traffic) measuring forecast accuracy when the automated lag selection deviates from a manually identified optimal set.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Does the additive transformation (applied to features and targets) effectively stabilize variance and remove trends for series with complex structures, such as multiple seasonal periods or structural breaks?

**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The additive transformation is the recommended default strategy for "dealing with trending series and, in general, with level shifts." Its success is demonstrated on M4 yearly series, many of which have trends.

**Why unresolved:** The transformation is a simple global mean adjustment. It may fail for series with non‑additive trends (e.g., exponential), time‑varying volatility, or multiple level shifts not captured by a single global mean. The paper does not test its limitations.

**What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative experiments on synthetic and real series with known structural breaks or non‑linear trends, measuring forecast accuracy before and after transformation, to identify failure modes.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Is the recursive forecasting strategy, inherent to the autoregressive approach with regression trees, optimal for multi‑step forecasts compared to direct or hybrid strategies?

**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper adopts recursive forecasting because it is "simple and effective" and uses it exclusively in all experiments. No comparison to direct (train separate models for each horizon) or multi‑output strategies is provided.

**Why unresolved:** Recursive forecasts can accumulate errors for long horizons. The choice is presented as default without justification, leaving open whether a more complex strategy would yield significant improvements for tree‑based models.

**What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled study comparing recursive, direct, and perhaps DirRec (direct‑recursive hybrid) strategies using the same regression tree/ensemble base learner on datasets with varying forecast horizons and series properties.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How sensitive are the reported results to the default hyperparameter settings of the regression tree ensembles (e.g., `mtry` in random forests, tree depth)?

**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The methodology emphasizes "no parameter tuning" and uses default values (e.g., random forests with "one third of the features" and 500 trees). While touted as an advantage, the paper does not investigate whether tuning could substantially improve accuracy beyond the already competitive results.

**Why unresolved:** Claiming a method works well "without tuning" is a strong statement that requires evidence of insensitivity. It is possible that modest tuning could close the gap with ARIMA/ETS on quarterly data. The default‑centered evaluation leaves this unknown.

**What evidence would resolve it:** A sensitivity analysis on a representative subset of the M4 data, varying key parameters (e.g., `mtry`, number of trees, minimum node size) and measuring the distribution of accuracy improvements to assess the potential gains from tuning.

## Limitations
- Additive trend: Only handles additive trends; multiplicative or nonlinear trends degrade performance.
- Seasonality: Requires fixed integer seasonal period; fails for non‑integer or evolving patterns.
- Data length: Series must be longer than max lag; short series cause errors.
- Outlier sensitivity: Mean‑based transformation distorted by extremes.
- Undocumented parameters: PACF significance level and `rpart` control (e.g., `minsplit`) are not specified, hindering replication.
- Benchmark scope: Evaluated mainly on M4; unknown on other datasets.

## Confidence
- Additive transformation: Medium (good on M4 yearly; not tested beyond linear trends).
- Seasonal lag capture: High for integer seasonality; Low for irregular patterns.
- Random forest gains: High (empirical and theoretical support).
