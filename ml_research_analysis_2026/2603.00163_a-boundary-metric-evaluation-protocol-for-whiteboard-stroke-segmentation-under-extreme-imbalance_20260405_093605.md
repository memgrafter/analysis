# A Boundary-Metric Evaluation Protocol for Whiteboard Stroke Segmentation Under Extreme Imbalance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00163
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00163
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Overlap-based losses (Dice, Tversky) improve F1 by over 20 points versus cross-entropy under ~1.8% foreground imbalance, while a boundary-aware evaluation protocol exposes hidden trade-offs in contour fidelity and model consistency.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses whiteboard stroke segmentation, where stroke pixels are extremely sparse (~1.8% foreground). Standard region-based metrics like F1 and IoU can mask failures on thin strokes. The authors propose a comprehensive evaluation protocol that combines region metrics (F1, IoU) with boundary-aware metrics (BF1, B-IoU), analyzes performance on core vs. thin stroke subsets, and assesses per-image robustness via statistics like median and interquartile range under multi-seed training. Using DeepLabV3-MobileNetV3 and five loss functions on a dataset of 34 high-resolution images, they find that Dice-family losses (Dice, Tversky, Dice+Focal) dramatically outperform distribution-based losses (cross-entropy, Focal) in F1 and narrow the core-thin performance gap. Classical Sauvola binarization achieves a higher mean F1 but suffers from far worse worst-case performance, revealing a consistency-accuracy trade-off. Doubling the training resolution yields a further significant boost.

## Method Summary
The study uses a dataset of 34 whiteboard images (native resolution ~3712×2784 or 3968×2232) with pixel-level stroke masks. Data augmentation includes weak (brightness, contrast, gamma shifts, blur, noise) and strong (directional glare, shadow bands) transformations, with gentler augmentations for small-stroke images. Models are trained on a fixed training/validation split (22 images, 80/20 alphabetical split) and evaluated on 12 held-out test images, split into "core" and "thin" subsets. The core architecture is DeepLabV3 with a MobileNetV3-Large backbone (ImageNet-pretrained), an ASPP neck, and a 1x1 convolution head for binary classification. Five loss functions are compared: Cross-Entropy (CE), Focal, Dice, Dice+Focal, and Tversky. Models are trained with AdamW (lr=1e-4, weight decay=1e-4), cosine annealing, batch size 2, and early stopping, across three random seeds. Evaluation employs a multi-faceted protocol: mean F1/IoU (threshold 127), boundary metrics (BF1 with τ scaling, B-IoU on a 2% diagonal band), subset-specific scores (core/thin), and per-image robustness statistics (median, IQR, minimum). Statistical significance is tested with Wilcoxon signed-rank (α=0.005). Classical baselines (OpenCV adaptive, Otsu, Sauvola) are evaluated at native resolution for comparison.

## Key Results
- Overlap-based losses (Dice, Tversky, Dice+Focal) increase mean F1 by over 20 points compared to cross-entropy (0.663 vs. 0.438, p<0.001) and halve the performance gap between core and thin stroke subsets.
- Boundary metrics (BF1, B-IoU) expose a trade-off: Dice+Focal achieves the highest BF1 (0.676) for crisper boundaries, while Tversky leads in F1 and B-IoU—a distinction masked by region metrics alone.
- Classical Sauvola binarization attains a higher mean F1 (0.787) but a substantially lower worst-case F1 (0.452) than the best learned model (Tversky, worst-case F1=0.565), highlighting a consistency-accuracy trade-off.
- Doubling the training resolution from 1024×768 to 1536×1152 raises mean F1 by 12.7 points and BF1 by ~19 points, indicating resolution is a key bottleneck for thin strokes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Claim: Overlap-based losses (Dice, Tversky) outperform distribution-based losses (cross-entropy, Focal) under extreme class imbalance because their gradients remain significant for the minority class, whereas cross-entropy gradients for rare stroke pixels vanish due to dominance by easy negative background pixels. The Dice loss gradient is normalized by the sum of prediction and ground truth volumes, not total pixels, keeping the learning signal focused on foreground-background overlap even when the foreground is ~1.8% of the image. Core assumption: The model's decision boundary is initially poor for the minority class, making gradient scale at early training critical. Evidence: The paper reports a >20-point F1 improvement for Dice over CE (p<0.001) and explains the gradient behavior in Section 6.1. Break condition: If the foreground fraction rises substantially (e.g., >20%), the gradient advantage of Dice may diminish as CE gradients become meaningful for the minority class.

### Mechanism 2
Claim: Boundary-aware metrics (BF1, B-IoU) reveal performance differences in contour fidelity that region metrics (F1, IoU) mask, particularly critical for thin strokes where precise localization is key. Mechanism: Region metrics aggregate all correctly classified interior pixels, allowing large interior fills to compensate for poor boundaries. Boundary metrics restrict evaluation to a narrow morphological or distance-based band around the ground-truth contour. Thin strokes have a high boundary-to-interior ratio, so errors in that band disproportionately affect boundary metrics. Core assumption: For thin-stroke applications (e.g., digitization), utility depends more on contour accuracy than interior fill; a thick, blurry prediction is less useful than a thin, precise one. Evidence: The paper states that standard metrics can mask thin-stroke failures and shows in Section 6.3 that while Dice-family losses rank similarly by F1, boundary metrics expose a trade-off (Dice+Focal highest BF1). Break condition: If all strokes become very thick (high area-to-perimeter ratio), the differential between boundary and region metrics may shrink.

### Mechanism 3
Claim: The consistency–accuracy trade-off arises because classical local thresholding (Sauvola) excels on favorable (high-contrast, well-lit) images but fails catastrophically on low-contrast or unevenly lit boards, while learned models sacrifice some mean accuracy for tighter per-image reliability. Mechanism: Classical methods rely on local intensity statistics within a fixed window; these statistics become unreliable under illumination variations, causing image-specific errors. Learned models, trained on diverse augmentations (including glare and shadows), learn features more robust to such variations, leading to higher worst-case performance but sometimes lower mean because they miss easy cases classical methods handle perfectly. Core assumption: The training augmentation improves robustness to lighting variations present in the test distribution. Evidence: Sauvola achieves higher mean F1 (0.787) but much worse worst-case (0.452) than Tversky (0.565); Sauvola wins on 10/12 images in absolute F1 but loses on low-contrast boards (Section 5.6). Break condition: If the test distribution shifts to only high-contrast, well-lit boards, classical methods may dominate on both mean and worst-case. If the learned model is trained at native resolution, the trade-off may narrow.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Extreme Class Imbalance**  
  Why needed: Stroke pixels are only ~1.8% of the image. Standard losses like cross-entropy are dominated by the background class, making gradients for strokes negligible and causing the model to ignore the minority class.  
  Quick check: If a binary segmentation problem has 99% background pixels, why would a model trained with cross-entropy likely ignore the foreground even if it's the class of interest?

- **Concept: Boundary-Aware Evaluation**  
  Why needed: For thin strokes, primary errors are often along the contour (jagged, broken edges). Region metrics like F1 can be high if the interior is filled, even with poor boundaries, masking failures critical to the application.  
  Quick check: How might a prediction that slightly dilates all strokes achieve a decent F1 score but still be considered poor for a whiteboard scanner application?

- **Concept: Loss Function Design for Imbalance**  
  Why needed: Different losses reweight pixel influence differently. Dice weights by the inverse of predicted+true volume, making it sensitive to small foregrounds; Focal down-weights easy examples. Understanding these mechanisms explains the observed ranking (Dice-family > CE/Focal).  
  Quick check: What is the key mathematical difference between Dice loss and cross-entropy that makes Dice less sensitive to the number of background pixels?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (resized image) -> Backbone (MobileNetV3-Large, ImageNet-pretrained) -> Neck (ASPP for multi-scale context) -> Head (1x1 convolution to 2 classes) -> Output (softmax segmentation map)
- **Critical path:** The resolution at which features are computed is the primary bottleneck for thin stroke preservation; the ASPP module's fixed atrous rates do not adapt to stroke thinness.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - MobileNetV3 is lightweight (~11M params) for real-time deployment but has lower capacity to preserve fine details versus larger backbones, capping absolute performance.  
  - Fixed resolution downsampling (to 1024×768 or 1536×1152) erodes thin strokes (1-2px at native); higher resolution helps but increases memory/computation.  
  - No boundary-specific loss is used; boundary-aware losses (e.g., clDice) could further improve BF1 but were left for future work.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - CE/Focal: Fragmented thin strokes, high false-negative rate on thin subset, large per-image variance (high IQR), worst-case F1 as low as 0.230.  
  - All models: Systematic failures on extremely low-contrast strokes under heavy glare; very fine strokes (<8px at training resolution) eroded after downsampling.  
  - Sauvola baseline: High mean F1 but catastrophic drops (F1=0.452) on low-contrast, unevenly lit boards.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate loss ablation: Train DeepLabV3-MobileNetV3 with CE, Focal, Dice, Dice+Focal, Tversky at 1536×1152 for 3 seeds. Verify >20pt F1 gap between CE and Dice-family and halved core-thin gap.  
  2. Evaluate boundary metrics: Compute BF1 and B-IoU for the same models. Confirm Dice+Focal has higher BF1 than Tversky despite similar F1.  
  3. Resolution study: Train Dice+Focal at 1024×768 vs. 1536×1152. Quantify F1 gain (~13pt) and BF1 gain (~19pt).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Does training at native resolution (≈3700×2200) or via patch-based pipelines close the performance gap between deep models and classical baselines like Sauvola? Basis: Resolution is identified as a critical bottleneck; classical baselines were evaluated at native resolution while deep models were capped by GPU limits. Why unresolved: Native-resolution training was computationally infeasible; only two lower resolutions were tested. Evidence needed: Train the best model (Tversky) patch-wise at native resolution and compare mean/worst-case F1 against native-resolution Sauvola.
- **Question 2:** How do boundary-optimized losses (e.g., clDice, InverseForm, Boundary Loss) perform under the proposed protocol? Do they further reduce core–thin equity gap or improve worst-case robustness? Basis: Evaluation of boundary-aware losses is explicitly listed as future work (Section 7). Why unresolved: Loss ablation compared only region-based losses. Evidence needed: Train with boundary losses, compute all metrics, and statistically compare worst-case F1 and core–thin gap against best Dice-family model.
- **Question 3:** Does the loss-function ranking and consistency–accuracy trade-off generalize to modern architectures (SegFormer, Mask2Former)? Basis: Authors fixed architecture to isolate loss effects; modern designs may interact differently. Why unresolved: All experiments used a single lightweight CNN. Evidence needed: Replicate loss ablation with SegFormer-B0 and Mask2Former-Small; check if F1 gap and core–thin trends persist.
- **Question 4:** How does inter-annotator variability at thin-stroke boundaries affect evaluation metrics, especially BF1/B-IoU? Basis: Paper notes a limitation: all masks were created by a single annotator; boundary uncertainty is unmeasured. Why unresolved: No multiple annotations exist. Evidence needed: Have multiple annotators label a subset of test images, compute annotation disagreement, and re-evaluate top models to measure metric variability.
- **Question 5:** What is the mechanistic cause of the consistency–accuracy trade-off? Is it due to local thresholding's sensitivity to illumination gradients or the deep model's context aggregation? Basis: Trade-off is observed empirically; Sauvola fails on low-contrast boards, but the precise factor isn't isolated. Why unresolved: No controlled experiment varying illumination/contrast. Evidence needed: Synthetically vary illumination on test images, run Sauvola and Tversky, and correlate performance drop with local intensity gradient magnitude.

## Limitations
- Exact augmentation parameters (glare, shadow bands) and the alphabetical train/val split are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- The dataset is small (34 images), raising generalizability concerns to larger or more diverse whiteboard collections.
- Fixed-resolution downsampling likely erodes the thinnest strokes; native-resolution training remains unexplored.
- Boundary-specific losses were not evaluated; they could further improve boundary metrics.
- All masks were annotated by a single person; inter-annotator variability at thin boundaries is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Overlap-based losses outperform under extreme imbalance:** High – results are robust across seeds with statistical significance (p<0.001).
- **Boundary metrics reveal hidden trade-offs:** Medium – observations are consistent but lack independent validation.
- **Consistency–accuracy trade-off between classical and learned methods:** Medium – experiment-based but may not hold beyond current test distribution.
- **Mechanistic explanations (gradient invariance, boundary ratio, robustness):** Low – plausible reasoning not directly tested.

## Next Checks
1. **Gradient magnitude comparison:** Track gradient norms for stroke pixels during early training for CE vs. Dice losses to confirm Dice yields larger gradients when foreground ~1.8%.
2. **Human alignment of boundary metrics:** Conduct a small user study rating stroke boundary quality; compute correlation with BF1/B-IoU versus region metrics to evaluate perceptual relevance.
3. **Native-resolution training:** Implement a patch-based training scheme at original resolution (~4K×3K) and measure gains in thin-stroke F1 and BF1 to quantify the resolution bottleneck.
