# Revisiting the Seasonal Trend Decomposition for Enhanced Time Series Forecasting
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18465
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18465
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Reduces MSE by 9.46% and MAE by 8.89% on benchmark datasets; dual-MLP models achieve ~7% MSE reduction on USGS hydrological data while maintaining linear O(L) time complexity.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles ineffective seasonal handling and computational inefficiency in time series forecasting by proposing a decomposition-based framework. The method splits a multivariate series into trend and seasonal components using a moving average. The trend is processed with reversible instance normalization (RevIN) followed by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), while the seasonal component is fed into either existing backbone models (e.g., iTransformer) with all normalization layers removed or into new dual-MLP architectures (RMSM, RMM). The final forecast is the sum of the two branches. Experiments on benchmark datasets show an average reduction of 9.46% in MSE and 8.89% in MAE across four state-of-the-art models. On real-world USGS hydrological data, the dual-MLP variants achieve approximately 7% MSE reduction with linear computational complexity, outperforming Transformer-based models in efficiency.

## Method Summary
The framework operates on multivariate input series \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times C}\) with fixed input length \(L=96\) and prediction horizons \(H \in \{96,192,336,720\}\). First, a moving average (AvgPool1d with padding) decomposes \(X\) into a smoothed trend \(X_T\) and a seasonal residual \(X_S = X - X_T\). The trend branch applies RevIN to normalize each instance, processes it through a three-layer MLP (model dimension 512), and then denormalizes to produce \(Y_T\). The seasonal branch has two alternatives: (a) a backbone model (iTransformer, PatchTST, TimesNet, or DLinear) with all instance/layer normalization removed, or (b) a dual-MLP architecture (RMSM or RMM) that operates directly on \(X_S\) without normalization. The final forecast is \(Y = Y_T + Y_S\). Training uses the Adam optimizer (learning rate \(1^{-4}\)), batch size 32, and MSE loss, with results averaged over three runs. Datasets include Electricity, ETT (ETTm2/ETTh2), Weather, and USGS hydrological data, split chronologically 7:2:1 into train, validation, and test sets.

## Key Results
- Average reduction of 9.46% in MSE and 8.89% in MAE across four state-of-the-art models on benchmark datasets.
- Dual-MLP models (RMSM/RMM) achieve approximately 7% MSE reduction on USGS hydrological data.
- Dual-MLP models maintain \(O(L)\) time complexity, offering superior efficiency over Transformer-based counterparts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Decomposing the time series into separate trend and seasonal components enables specialized, more accurate processing for each.
- **Mechanism**: A moving average filter (AvgPool1d with padding) extracts a smoothed trend component \(X_T\). The seasonal component \(X_S\) is obtained by subtraction: \(X_S = X - X_T\). This separation allows different modeling strategies for components with distinct statistical properties.
- **Core assumption**: Trend and seasonal components have inherently different characteristics (e.g., stationarity, predictability) such that a single model architecture and normalization strategy are suboptimal.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states decomposition and selective RevIN; Section 2.2 details moving average equations; neighboring papers (DMamba, DSAT-HD, ST-MTM) also explore decomposition.
- **Break condition**: If the series lacks a clear stable seasonal pattern or the trend is not smooth enough for moving average, decomposition may introduce artifacts or fail.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Applying reversible instance normalization (RevIN) exclusively to the trend component stabilizes training and improves trend forecast accuracy, while it harms performance if applied to the seasonal component.
- **Mechanism**: RevIN normalizes each series instance to zero mean and unit variance before processing the trend through an MLP, then denormalizes the output. This mitigates non-stationarity primarily in the trend. The seasonal component bypasses normalization.
- **Core assumption**: The majority of distribution shift (non-stationarity) in multivariate time series resides in the trend component, not in the seasonal/remainder component.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 2.3 explicitly state RevIN is used only for trend; prior work [22] supports RevIN for trend-like signals.
- **Break condition**: If the seasonal component exhibits significant non-stationarity (changing amplitude/phase), omitting normalization could degrade performance. If the trend is already stationary, RevIN may be unnecessary overhead.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Removing instance normalization from backbone models when processing the seasonal component preserves its intrinsic structure, leading to better seasonal predictions compared to using normalized backbones.
- **Mechanism**: The authors take existing SOTA backbones (iTransformer, PatchTST, etc.) and remove their built-in instance normalization layers. This raw seasonal signal \(X_S\) is fed directly into the backbone. Alternatively, they replace the backbone with simple MLPs (RMSM, RMM) that also operate without normalization on \(X_S\).
- **Core assumption**: Seasonal patterns are relatively stable and meaningful in their original scale; normalization distorts amplitude and phase relationships critical for accurate forecasting.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states elimination of normalization increases accuracy; Section 2.4 describes removal and its benefit.
- **Break condition**: If the seasonal component has large inter-variable scale disparities or significant distribution shift, omitting normalization could cause training instability or poor generalization for backbones expecting normalized inputs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Classical Time Series Decomposition (Moving Average)**
  - **Why needed**: The entire method hinges on decomposing a series into trend and seasonal parts. Moving average is a simple low-pass filter that extracts a smoothed trend, but has limitations like boundary effects and assumption of stable seasonality.
  - **Quick check**: What are key differences between moving average and STL? Why might moving average be preferred for computational efficiency?

- **Reversible Instance Normalization (RevIN)**
  - **Why needed**: RevIN is the key normalization technique applied selectively to the trend. Understanding its learnable affine transforms and reversibility is essential.
  - **Quick check**: How does RevIN differ from batch/instance normalization in parameter sharing and reversibility? Why are these properties suitable for time series forecasting?

- **Model Complexity and Computational Scaling (O(L) vs. O(L²))**
  - **Why needed**: The paper's efficiency claim rests on dual-MLP models achieving linear complexity versus quadratic complexity of attention-based Transformers.
  - **Quick check**: For input length L=96 and horizon H=720, what are the theoretical computational orders of Transformer self-attention vs. the proposed MLP-based seasonal processor? How does this impact training/inference time on long horizons?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1. Input: Multivariate series \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times C}\).
  2. Decomposition Block: Moving Average (AvgPool1d with padding) → Trend \(X_T\); Seasonal \(X_S = X - X_T\).
  3. Trend Processor: RevIN → 3-layer MLP → RevIN (denorm) → \(Y_T\).
  4. Seasonal Processor (two alternatives):
     - Backbone Path: \(X_S\) → SOTA Backbone (iTransformer/PatchTST/etc.) **with normalization layers removed** → \(Y_S\).
     - Dual-MLP Path: **RMSM**: \(X_S\) → Linear+ReLU → Concat(\(X_S\), original \(X\)) → Linear+ReLU → Linear+ReLU → \(Y_S\). **RMM**: \(X_S\) → 3-layer MLP (no concat) → \(Y_S\).
  5. Recombination: \(Y = Y_T + Y_S\).

- **Critical path**:
  - Decomposition must precede both Trend and Seasonal processing.
  - The two branches (Trend, Seasonal) are parallel and independent.
  - Their outputs must be summed for the final prediction.
  - Selection of Seasonal Processor (Backbone vs. RMSM/RMM) is a top-level architectural choice.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Simplicity vs. Backbone Expressiveness**: Simple MLPs (RMSM/RMM) sacrifice representational power but gain efficiency (O(L) vs. O(L²)) and often better accuracy per paper.
  - **Normalization Choice**: Removing normalization from backbones is high-risk/high-reward; may break training for models expecting normalized inputs but preserves seasonal structure.
  - **Decomposition Method**: Moving average is simplest and fastest (O(L)) but may be insufficient for complex seasonality; MoE/FD offer marginal gains at higher cost (O(L) or O(L log L)).

- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Decomposition failure**: If \(X_T\) is too jagged or \(X_S\) retains trend-like patterns, separation is poor. Check residual autocorrelation.
  - **RevIN misuse**: Applying RevIN to \(X_S\) will likely degrade performance; verify it's only on the trend branch.
  - **Backbone incompatibility**: Removing normalization from a backbone not designed for unnormalized inputs may cause training divergence (loss explosion, NaNs).
  - **Seasonal processor mismatch**: Using a linear-only processor (RMM) for highly complex seasonal dynamics may underfit.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline replication**: Implement a chosen SOTA backbone (e.g., iTransformer) *without any decomposition or RevIN* on a benchmark dataset (e.g., Weather) to establish baseline MSE/MAE.
  2. **Add decomposed trend processing**: Implement the full framework with moving average decomposition, applying RevIN+MLP to the trend, and the *original* (normalized) backbone to the seasonal component. This isolates the benefit of trend-specific processing.
  3. **Test proposed seasonal processors**: Replace the normalized seasonal backbone with: (a) the same backbone *with normalization removed*, and (b) the new RMSM and RMM models. Compare metrics, training time, and memory usage. This verifies the core claim that normalization removal and simple MLPs improve accuracy and efficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: For which types of time series data (e.g., length, dimensionality, seasonality strength, noise level) does moving average decomposition achieve comparable or superior performance relative to more complex decomposition methods like frequency-based or MoE decomposition? The paper shows MA often outperforms FD and MoE, but does not characterize when FD/MoE might be preferable.
- **Open Question 2**: Does the absence of normalization in the seasonal component processing universally improve forecasting accuracy, or are there specific scenarios where including normalization (e.g., RevIN, BatchNorm) could be beneficial? The claim is based on empirical results with a few datasets; it might not hold for all characteristics.
- **Open Question 3**: Can dual-MLP architectures (RMSM/RMM) maintain efficiency and accuracy advantages over Transformer-based models when faced with very long input sequences (e.g., L > 1000) and high-dimensional multivariate data? The paper demonstrates O(L) complexity and speedups for L=96 but does not evaluate scaling to longer sequences.
- **Open Question 4**: How does the decomposition-based approach perform on time series with multiple seasonal patterns (e.g., daily and weekly) or non-integer seasonal periods, where moving average decomposition may be non-trivial? The paper applies moving average with a single seasonal period and does not address multiple seasonality.

## Limitations
- **Uncertain procedure for normalization removal**: Exact steps for removing normalization from SOTA backbones (iTransformer, PatchTST, etc.) are unspecified; requires inspecting original code.
- **Incomplete dual-MLP specifications**: Hidden layer dimensions and concatenation details for RMSM/RMM are ambiguous, hindering faithful implementation.
- **Limited evidence for core assumptions**: The selective RevIN hypothesis (trend-only normalization) and the benefit of removing normalization from seasonal backbone lack corpus support; break conditions (e.g., seasonal non-stationarity) are untested.

## Confidence
- **Decomposition benefit**: Medium
- **Selective RevIN (trend only)**: Low
- **Normalization removal for seasonal backbone**: Low-Medium
- **Dual-MLP efficiency (O(L))**: High
