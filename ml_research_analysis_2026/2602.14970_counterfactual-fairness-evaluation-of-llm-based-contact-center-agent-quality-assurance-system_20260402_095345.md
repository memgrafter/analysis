# Counterfactual Fairness Evaluation of LLM-Based Contact Center Agent Quality Assurance System
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14970
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14970
- **Reference count:** 40

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates fairness risks in automated contact center quality assurance (QA) systems powered by large language models (LLMs). The authors introduce a counterfactual fairness evaluation framework that systematically alters sensitive attributes (e.g., agent name, gender, contextual cues) in real customer service transcripts to measure evaluative consistency. Evaluating 18 LLMs on 3,000 transcripts, they find significant disparities: counterfactual flip rates (CFR) reach up to 13.0%, with contextual priming (e.g., past performance labels) inducing the highest bias (CFR up to 16.4%). While larger, more aligned models show lower unfairness, fairness does not correlate with accuracy on the primary QA task. Explicit fairness prompting yields only modest improvements, highlighting the need for rigorous, standardized fairness audits before deploying such systems.

## Method Summary
The authors create counterfactual variants of each transcript by modifying agent identity cues (name, pronouns), injecting contextual metadata (e.g., past performance labels), or altering stylistic markers (e.g., politeness phrases). Semantic equivalence of variants is validated using Claude-4-Sonnet. Each original and transformed transcript is evaluated by target LLMs using a detailed Auto-QA prompt that produces binary judgments, confidence scores, and coaching notes. Two fairness metrics are computed: Counterfactual Flip Rate (CFR), the frequency of judgment reversals between a counterfactual pair, and Mean Absolute Score Difference (MASD), the average absolute difference in confidence scores. These are aggregated across 13 bias dimensions (e.g., gender, ethnicity, contextual priming) to assess evaluative consistency.

## Key Results
- CFR ranged from 5.4% to 13.0% across models, with contextual priming (e.g., "high performer" label) reaching CFR up to 16.4%, indicating severe bias from past-performance cues.
- Larger, more strongly aligned models exhibited lower unfairness, but fairness did not correlate with answer accuracy on the original task.
- Explicit fairness prompting (e.g., "evaluate evidence without considering identity") produced only modest reductions in CFR/MASD.
- Deterministic model outputs (temperature=0) still showed systematic disparities, suggesting bias is embedded beyond stochastic decoding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The counterfactual approach isolates evaluative bias by holding conversation content constant while flipping sensitive attributes. A significant CFR or MASD indicates the model’s judgment depends on those attributes rather than the customer interaction’s substantive qualities. This method uncovers hidden associations (e.g., linking certain names or past-performance labels to higher quality scores) that would be invisible in aggregate accuracy metrics. By testing across 13 dimensions, it provides a multidimensional fairness audit tailored to contact center QA risks. The semantic validation step ensures that observed differences stem from attribute changes rather than meaning drift, strengthening causal inference about bias.

## Foundational Learning
- **Counterfactual Fairness:** A paradigm for fairness auditing that tests model consistency under minimal input changes to protected attributes. *Why needed:* Detects subtle biases that disappear in aggregate metrics. *Quick check:* Apply to a model known to associate certain names with sentiment; expect high CFR on name-dimension pairs.
- **CFR / MASD:** Metrics quantifying output instability between counterfactual pairs. *Why needed:* Provide dimension-specific, interpretable fairness scores. *Quick check:* On a deterministic model with no attribute bias, CFR should approximate zero across random attribute flips.
- **Semantic Validation:** Verification that counterfactual edits preserve the task’s critical meaning. *Why needed:* Prevents false positives from transformation drift. *Quick check:* Manually audit 20 validated pairs; nearly all should retain resolution outcomes and sentiment.
- **Fairness Prompting:** Instruction-based intervention to mitigate bias. *Why needed:* Tests if alignment alone suffices for fairness. *Quick check:* Compare base vs. fairness prompt CFR on 10 transcripts; modest reduction expected per results.
- **Contextual Priming:** Bias arising from non-identity metadata (e.g., agent history). *Why needed:* Captures real-world QA risks like recency or performance labels. *Quick check:* Prepend "high performer" vs. "low performer" to identical transcripts; observe score divergence.
- **Scale–Alignment Trade-off:** Observation that larger, aligned models fairer, but not more accurate. *Why needed:* Informs model selection for fairness-critical deployment. *Quick check:* Plot CFR vs. parameter count for evaluated models; expect negative correlation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Original Transcript → Counterfactual Generator → LLM Evaluator → Metrics Calculator → Dimension Aggregation

**Critical Path:**  
1. Input parsing and attribute identification (names, pronouns, structure).  
2. Counterfactual transformation per dimension (turn rewrite, context injection, metadata append).  
3. Semantic equivalence validation.  
4. LLM inference with Auto-QA prompt for original and all variants.  
5. Pairwise comparison for CFR and MASD computation per dimension.  
6. Aggregation and statistical analysis across models and dimensions.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Transformation strength vs. semantic drift:* Aggressive edits increase sensitivity but risk invalidation.  
- *Validator choice:* Using Claude-4-Sonnet for validation introduces a proprietary, potentially biased oracle.  
- *Metric sensitivity:* CFR/MASD can be inflated by model stochasticity; requires controlled generation settings (temperature=0) and stability baselines.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- High semantic validation rejection rates (>20% for Ethnicity/Religion), indicating transformation policy failure.  
- CFR within model’s intrinsic stochasticity baseline (from repeated runs), suggesting no systematic bias.  
- Inconsistent output formatting from LLMs breaking automated parsing.  
- Mispaired counterfactuals (e.g., mixing different dimension variants) yielding nonsense metrics.

**First Experiments:**  
1. **Pilot Scope Test:** Run counterfactual pipeline on 20 transcripts across 5 dimensions; measure CFR variance and validation rejection rates.  
2. **Stability Baseline:** Evaluate 10 transcripts 5 times each with temperature=0; compute minimum detectable CFR above random flips.  
3. **Prompt Fidelity Check:** Test exact Auto-QA prompt on 3 models; verify step-by-step reasoning and score format adherence before scaling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
What specific demographic or contextual bias dimensions are missing from the proposed 13-dimension taxonomy, and how might they manifest in real-world contact center QA evaluations?
- *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The authors state in the limitations that their taxonomy "may not be exhaustive and may not cover all potential sources of bias."
- *Why unresolved:* An incomplete taxonomy could leave critical fairness risks undetected in deployment audits.
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic discovery of new bias dimensions via causal analysis of model errors on diverse, global conversation datasets, followed by empirical validation using the counterfactual framework.

### Open Question 2
Why does explicit fairness prompting yield only modest improvements in evaluative consistency, and what alternative intervention strategies (e.g., fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, or architectural changes) could more effectively mitigate the identified biases?
- *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors analyze fairness-aware prompting and find it yields "only modest improvements," calling it a "preliminary intervention rather than a comprehensive solution."
- *Why unresolved:* Without understanding the limits of prompting, efforts to operationalize fairness may rely on ineffective methods.
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments comparing a broader suite of interventions (prompt engineering, supervised fine-tuning on counterfactually balanced data, adversarial debiasing) across diverse model architectures, measuring both fairness gains and any accuracy trade-offs.

### Open Question 3
To what extent do the observed fairness disparities stem from the LLM's internal representations (e.g., embedding-space associations) versus its decoding/instruction-following processes, and can these be disentangled?
- *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper uses output-based metrics (CFR, MASD) but does not probe model internals. The variation in "fairness–robustness profiles" (e.g., deterministic vs. stochastic biased models) suggests different underlying failure modes.
- *Why unresolved:* Attributing bias to specific components is necessary for targeted mitigation (e.g., representation cleansing vs. output calibration).
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Analysis of activation patterns, attention maps, or probing classifiers on counterfactual pairs to isolate where in the forward pass attribute-sensitive information influences the final evaluation judgment.

### Open Question 4
What is the causal relationship between model scale/alignment and fairness in this task—does increased scale reduce bias intrinsically, or does alignment for helpfulness/harmlessness inadvertently improve consistency on counterfactual probes?
- *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors find "larger, more strongly aligned models show lower unfairness, though fairness does not track accuracy," and note fairness "tends to emerge as a byproduct of scale and alignment rather than accuracy alone."
- *Why unresolved:* The correlation is observed but not causally explained, making it unclear how to proactively design for fairness.
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies on models at different scales, comparing checkpoints pre- and post-alignment (e.g., RLHF), and analyzing whether alignment objectives that explicitly penalize inconsistency on counterfactuals improve fairness more than standard objectives.

### Open Question 5
How stable and generalizable are the counterfactual fairness metrics (CFR, MASD) across different QA question types (objective vs. subjective), domains, and languages, and do they reliably predict real-world unfair impact (e.g., on agent promotions or turnover)?
- *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The study uses 30 balanced questions across 12 domains but only English transcripts. The metrics measure output consistency but are not validated against downstream HR/operational outcomes.
- *Why unresolved:* Without external validation, it's uncertain whether reduced CFR/MASD translates to meaningful fairness in actual workforce decisions.
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Multi-domain, multi-lingual replication studies; longitudinal audits linking model CFR/MASD scores to historical agent performance data, compensation changes, and employee satisfaction metrics to establish predictive validity.

## Limitations
- **Data and model opacity:** The proprietary 3,000 transcripts, exact Auto-QA rubric (30 questions), and transformation rules for ethnicity/religion cues are unreleased, blocking faithful replication.
- **Validator dependency:** Semantic equivalence relies on Claude-4-Sonnet, introducing a black-box validator whose own biases may affect counterfactual acceptance.
- **Generalizability gap:** Metrics are validated only on English contact center data; stability across languages, domains, and QA question types remains unknown.
