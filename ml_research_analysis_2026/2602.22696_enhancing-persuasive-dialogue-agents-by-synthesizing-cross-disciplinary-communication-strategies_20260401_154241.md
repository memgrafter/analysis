# Enhancing Persuasive Dialogue Agents by Synthesizing Cross-Disciplinary Communication Strategies

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22696
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22696
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A cross‑disciplinary 31‑strategy framework (ProCoT‑rich‑desc) achieved a success rate of 0.833 on P4G and a 72.5% human win‑rate, with major improvements for low‑intent users.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the limitation of current persuasive dialogue agents that rely on a narrow set of predefined strategies—often as few as 10 from the P4G dataset. The authors construct a cross‑disciplinary framework that synthesizes 31 persuasive strategies drawn from social psychology, behavioral economics, and communication theory. Operationalized via ProCoT (Proactive Chain‑of‑Thought) prompting with GPT‑4o, the agent first analyzes conversational context, infers an appropriate strategy, and then generates a response. Evaluated on the charity donation dataset P4G and the multi‑domain DailyPersuasion dataset, the proposed agent (ProCoT‑rich‑desc) outperforms the P4G‑based baseline, achieving a success rate of 0.833 on P4G and a 72.5% human win‑rate on persuasiveness. Crucially, it significantly boosts outcomes for low‑intent individuals (e.g., initially reluctant and explicit non‑donors), demonstrating the value of a broader, context‑aware strategy toolkit.

## Method Summary
The methodology integrates four main components:  
1. **Strategy Library:** A taxonomy of 31 persuasive strategies grouped into seven categories, grounded in theories such as ELM/HSM, Cialdini’s principles, and behavioral economics concepts.  
2. **ProCoT Engine:** A prompt template that forces the LLM to reason in three steps—context analysis, strategy selection, and response generation—ensuring strategic grounding.  
3. **Persona/Intent Module:** For P4G, it builds persuadee profiles from nine attributes (age, sex, marital status, etc.) and assigns one of five donation intention levels.  
4. **Persuadee Simulator:** An LLM‑based agent that role‑plays the target user, conditioned on persona and dialogue history.  

The system is evaluated automatically (success rate, average turns, average intention improvement) and via human judgments. Ablation studies test variants with/without strategy descriptions and with the original 10‑strategy set. Generalizability is probed on DailyPersuasion’s 35 domains.

## Key Results
- **P4G success rate:** ProCoT‑rich‑desc reaches 0.833, surpassing the P4G‑based agent (0.760).  
- **Low‑intent improvement:** For initially reluctant users (level 4) success rate is 0.811; for explicit non‑donors (level 5) it is 0.500, with an average intention uplift of 1.100 in failed low‑intent dialogues.  
- **DailyPersuasion:** The agent wins 35.1% of automatic evaluations against the P4G‑based agent; human evaluators rate it as more persuasive with a 72.5% win‑rate (realism 3.73/5).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cross‑disciplinary strategy expansion
- **Claim:** Expanding from 10 to 31 strategies improves persuasive efficacy by covering more psychological levers.
- **Mechanism:** The framework systematizes principles from communication theory (ELM/HSM), social psychology (consistency, reciprocity), and behavioral economics (framing, scarcity). This lets the agent pick contextually apt tactics beyond a dataset‑derived subset.
- **Core assumption:** Real‑world persuasion needs a diverse, transferable toolkit.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 describe the 7‑category framework; neighboring papers discuss strategy expansion.
- **Break condition:** Further strategies yield negligible gains or cause coherence/overfitting.

### Mechanism 2: Granular intent modeling
- **Claim:** A 5‑level intent scale enables targeted strategy selection, especially for low‑intent persuadees.
- **Mechanism:** Distinguishing levels 3–5 allows subgroup‑specific tactics (e.g., credibility appeal for level 4). The Average Intention Improvement metric captures uplift in failed dialogues, revealing progress hidden by binary success/failure.
- **Core assumption:** Persuasion dynamics differ across intent gradients; one‑size‑fits‑all fails on resistant users.
- **Evidence anchors:** SR4 = 0.811, SR5 = 0.500; Table 1 and Table 23 show unique movement of level‑5 users.
- **Break condition:** No marginal benefit from per‑subgroup selection.

### Mechanism 3: ProCoT prompting with rich descriptions
- **Claim:** ProCoT with strategy descriptions (`ProCoT‑rich‑desc`) grounds the LLM in the extended framework, improving strategy selection.
- **Mechanism:** The chain‑of‑thought enforces context analysis → strategy inference → response generation. Descriptive labels boost the model’s conceptual grasp versus bare keywords.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs benefit from structured reasoning and semantic definitions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Ablation: `ProCoT‑rich‑desc` > `ProCoT‑rich` in SR and human win‑rate; English experiment shows descriptions help only for the rich set.
- **Break condition:** Adding descriptions consistently fails to improve performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Strategy Library → ProCoT Engine → Agent Generation → Persuadee Simulator → Evaluation Module (Automatic/Human)

**Critical path:**  
1. Define 31‑strategy taxonomy. 2. Engineer ProCoT prompts (with/without descriptions). 3. Generate dialogues using LLM agent and persona‑based simulator. 4. Evaluate success rate, intention improvement, and human ratings. 5. Analyze strategy distributions and subgroup performance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Strategy diversity vs. coherence:* More strategies risk incoherent selections; mitigated by ProCoT reasoning and intent modeling.  
- *Prompt complexity vs. cost/latency:* Descriptions and CoT steps increase token count and API cost, but improve efficacy.  
- *Simulation fidelity vs. scalability:* LLM‑based simulators are scalable but introduce model bias and lack true human unpredictability.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Strategy misalignment: Over‑reliance on high‑frequency strategies (e.g., Emotion appeal) across contexts, especially on first turns.  
- Low‑intent plateau: Difficulty moving level‑5 users; few effective strategies identified for English.  
- Abstract/generic responses: In domains like Law, the agent produces vague statements, losing to simpler baselines.  
- Repetitiveness: Human evaluators noted repetitive expression patterns harming realism.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Coverage & Activation Test:** Run 500 dialogues with `ProCoT‑rich‑desc`; confirm ≥80% of the 31 strategies are used at least once and no single strategy exceeds 50% usage.  
2. **Low‑Intent Stress Test:** Isolate dialogues with initial intent ≤ level 4. Compare `ProCoT‑rich‑desc` vs. `ProCoT‑p4g‑desc` on success rate and AII; expect significant lift.  
3. **Ablation: Descriptions Matter:** For the rich set, compare `ProCoT‑rich` (labels only) vs. `ProCoT‑rich‑desc` on a held‑out set; anticipate higher human win‑rate and success rate for the `-desc` variant.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Dynamic strategy selection:** How can agents choose strategies based on conversational context and persuadee characteristics? The current fixed set lacks adaptation.
2. **Sequential strategy combination:** What is the impact of combining multiple strategies in sequence versus using them in isolation? Real persuasion often relies on strategic sequencing.
3. **Donation amount as a metric:** How does evaluating continuous donation amounts (rather than binary success) change strategy optimization? Current metrics ignore monetary impact.
4. **Generalization to real humans:** To what extent do improvements hold with real human persuadees across cultures? LLM simulators may not capture human variability; cultural factors (e.g., Japanese vs. English) affect strategy effectiveness.
5. **Harmfulness detection gateway:** Can a real‑time safety layer block risky strategies without hurting persuasiveness? Proposed but not yet implemented.

## Limitations
- Simulation fidelity: LLM‑based persuadee simulators may not accurately reflect human resistance or emotional dynamics.
- Strategy description ambiguity: The exact 31‑strategy descriptions for `ProCoT‑rich‑desc` are not fully specified; reconstruction risks bias.
- Model accessibility: DailyPersuasion evaluation required the `o3‑2025‑04‑16` model (likely unavailable); substitutions may alter win‑rates.
