# A Systematic Evaluation of Sample-Level Tokenization Strategies for MEG Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16626
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16626
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** All tokenizers achieve high reconstruction fidelity (>97% variance explained); learnable tokenizers improve subject fingerprinting while fixed tokenizers offer similar performance on token prediction, generative quality, and downstream decoding.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the design of tokenization methods for transformer‑based MEG foundation models, introducing a learnable autoencoder‑based tokenizer and comparing it to standard non‑learnable baselines (µ‑transform and quantile binning). Using the MEG‑GPT model across three MEG datasets, the authors show that all tokenizers maintain high signal reconstruction fidelity (>97% variance explained). While token prediction accuracy, generative quality, and downstream task decoding are broadly comparable, learnable tokenizers significantly enhance subject fingerprinting and preservation of inter‑subject variability, and µ‑transform tokenizers with larger vocabularies provide a slight edge in token prediction. The findings suggest that simple fixed discretization is generally sufficient for MEG foundation models, with learnable methods offering modest benefits for applications emphasizing individual neural signatures.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates three sample‑level tokenization strategies: a novel learnable autoencoder (with causal and noncausal variants), µ‑transform (a companding transform with uniform binning), and standard quantile binning (SQ). Tokenizers convert continuous MEG source‑level parcel time courses into discrete token sequences. The foundation model MEG‑GPT, a transformer decoder, is trained on Cam‑CAN tokens via next‑token cross‑entropy. Evaluations include reconstruction fidelity (percentage variance explained), token prediction accuracy, generative quality (static PSD and dynamic TDE‑HMM distances), subject fingerprinting (nearest‑neighbor classification on TDE features), and downstream decoding (zero‑shot and fine‑tuned) on the Wakeman‑Henson dataset. Preprocessing follows a standard pipeline (band‑pass, downsampling, ICA, source reconstruction, leakage correction, parcel‑wise z‑scoring). Vocabulary sizes are modest (~100 tokens for the learnable tokenizer, 54–256 for µ‑transform, 108 for SQ). Results consistently show high reconstruction; learnable tokenizers excel in subject fingerprinting.

## Key Results
- All tokenizers achieve >97% variance explained in signal reconstruction.
- Token prediction, generative quality (static/dynamic spectra), and downstream decoding performance are broadly similar across tokenization approaches.
- Differentiators: Learnable tokenizers significantly improve subject fingerprinting and inter‑subject variability preservation; µ‑transform tokenizers with larger vocabularies yield slightly higher token prediction accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Sample-level tokenization achieves high-fidelity reconstruction of continuous MEG signals using modest vocabulary sizes (~100 tokens).
- **Mechanism:** Tokenizers apply affine scaling (e.g., z‑score or max‑absolute) followed by fixed binning (quantile or µ‑law) to map each sample to a discrete token; the learnable autoencoder variant further optimizes reconstruction via learned convolution kernels. This discretization retains most variance because bin boundaries align with the signal amplitude distribution.
- **Core assumption:** MEG amplitude distributions can be effectively captured by a moderate number of bins after standardization/companding; local temporal structure can be reconstructed from token‑level representations.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “all tokenizers achieve high signal reconstruction fidelity (>97% variance explained).”  
  - [section IV‑D]: “Across the Cam‑CAN dataset, all tokenizers achieved high reconstruction accuracy, exceeding 97% PVE on both training and test sets (Fig. 5(a)).”
- **Break condition:** If the signal contains extreme outliers or a distribution far from the expected range, reconstruction fidelity would degrade; also, using an excessively small vocabulary could increase quantization error.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Tokenization converts the continuous time series modeling problem into a categorical next‑token prediction task, enabling the use of cross‑entropy loss and leading to stable, efficient training of transformer‑based foundation models.
- **Mechanism:** The discrete token space allows MEG‑GPT to predict the next token among a finite set of possibilities, framing the problem as multi‑class classification. Cross‑entropy loss provides well‑behaved gradients and avoids the pitfalls of regression on raw amplitudes (e.g., sensitivity to scaling, non‑stationarity).
- **Core assumption:** The discrete tokenization preserves sufficient information for the model to learn temporal dependencies; cross‑entropy optimization remains effective despite the inherent label noise from quantization.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section II‑B]: “Tokenization allows us to employ the cross‑entropy as our loss function for the foundation model, which is known to provide superior numerical stability and more robust convergence behavior during training than regression‑based objectives such as mean squared error (MSE).”  
  - [section IV‑C]: Convergence analysis (Fig. 3) showed all MEG‑GPT models decayed monotonically and reached comparable asymptotic losses.
- **Break condition:** If tokenization discards critical information (e.g., through overly coarse binning), the cross‑entropy objective may still optimize, but the model’s generative or predictive performance will suffer; also, if the vocabulary is too large, classification sparsity could slow convergence.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Learnable tokenization better preserves subject‑specific neural signatures and inter‑subject variability compared to fixed tokenizers, leading to improved subject fingerprinting.
- **Mechanism:** The autoencoder‑based tokenizer adapts its encoder and decoder weights to the training data, potentially capturing idiosyncratic patterns across subjects. This adaptability translates into token assignments that retain individual differences, which are then reflected in the embeddings and outputs of the foundation model. The resulting synthetic data maintain a more faithful inter‑subject similarity structure.
- **Core assumption:** Subject‑specific information is encoded in fine‑grained temporal patterns that a flexible tokenizer can learn; the foundation model propagates these cues through its self‑attention layers and subject embeddings.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “learnable tokenizers significantly improve subject fingerprinting and preservation of inter‑subject variability.”  
  - [section IV‑G]: “Learnable tokenizers achieved significantly higher consistency scores with large effect sizes compared to non‑learnable baselines.” and “The causal tokenizer achieved the highest performance with a statistically significant and large effect size” in top‑1 subject identification.
- **Break condition:** If the tokenizer training is underpowered (e.g., insufficient data, limited capacity) or if the autoencoder collapses to a trivial mapping, subject‑specific information may be lost, making performance similar to non‑learnable methods.

## Foundational Learning

### Tokenization in Neural Time Series
- **Why needed here:** The paper’s core premise is that converting continuous MEG into discrete tokens is a necessary step to apply large language model architectures (like GPT) to neural data. This concept underpins the entire experimental design.
- **Quick check question:** What are the two main categories of tokenization based on temporal granularity, and why does the paper focus exclusively on sample‑level tokenization?

### Autoencoder-Based Learnable Tokenization
- **Why needed here:** The authors propose a novel learnable tokenizer based on an autoencoder, which is central to comparing fixed versus adaptive approaches. Understanding its components (GRU encoder, convolution decoder, annealed relaxation) is key to interpreting results.
- **Quick check question:** How does the annealed relaxation (coefficient κ) enable training of the categorical token assignments, and what changes at inference time?

### Subject Fingerprinting as an Evaluation Metric
- **Why needed here:** While downstream decoding is common, the paper highlights the importance of evaluating whether foundation models preserve individual neural identities. Subject fingerprinting via TDE features and nearest‑neighbor classification measures this directly.
- **Quick check question:** Describe the pipeline used for subject fingerprinting: what features are extracted from generated data, how is similarity measured, and what metric (top‑1, consistency) is reported?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Preprocessed MEG parcel time courses → Tokenizer (learnable autoencoder or fixed baselines) → MEG‑GPT (transformer decoder with subject/channel/position embeddings) → (1) token prediction logits, (2) autoregressive synthetic token sequences, (3) decoder layer features. Downstream: zero‑shot decoding (frozen MEG‑GPT + logistic regression), fine‑tuning (joint classifier and decoder layers), synthetic generation (nucleus sampling + detokenization). Evaluation suite: reconstruction PVE, token prediction PVE, static PSD L2, TDE‑HMM L2, subject fingerprinting (top‑k, consistency), decoding accuracy.

### Critical Path
1. **Preprocessing:** band‑pass (0.5–125 Hz), notch, downsample to 250 Hz, GESD artifact detection, ICA (FastICA) with EOG/ECG removal, LCMV beamformer source reconstruction, parcellation (52 regions), symmetric multivariate leakage correction, parcel‑wise z‑scoring. Split Cam‑CAN: first 50 subjects for tokenizer training, remaining for MEG‑GPT pretraining.
2. **Tokenizer setup:** Learnable tokenizer – train on Cam‑CAN training parcels with Adam (lr=1e‑5, batch 32, seq len 200, 40 epochs, annealed κ); then token refactorization. Baselines – compute scaling/bin edges from Cam‑CAN training; apply to all datasets with specified vocab sizes (µ‑transform: 256, 182, 108, 54; SQ: 108).
3. **MEG‑GPT pretraining:** For each tokenizer, train separate MEG‑GPT on Cam‑CAN tokens (4 layers, 4 heads, embed 400, dropout 0.2, seq len 80) with next‑token cross‑entropy, Adam (lr=1e‑5, batch 8). Fixed random seed; monitor loss convergence.
4. **Evaluation sequence:** (a) reconstruction fidelity, (b) token prediction analysis, (c) synthetic data generation and quality (static PSD, TDE‑HMM), (d) subject fingerprinting, (e) downstream decoding (Wakeman‑Henson, zero‑shot and fine‑tuned, within‑ and new‑subject).
5. **Comparison:** Welch’s t‑test with Bonferroni correction for statistical significance between tokenizers.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Learnable vs. Non‑learnable Tokenizers:** Learnable tokenizers adapt to dataset‑specific patterns, yielding better preservation of subject‑specific information, but require additional training and exhibit run‑to‑run variability. Non‑learnable tokenizers are fast, deterministic, and suffice for many tasks.
- **Vocabulary Size:** Larger vocabularies (e.g., µ‑transform with 256 tokens) slightly improve token prediction accuracy, yet reconstruction fidelity remains high even with as few as 54 tokens. Larger vocabularies increase model size and computational cost.
- **Causal vs. Noncausal Context:** The noncausal tokenizer leverages future samples when assigning tokens, which may introduce temporal leakage but can capture longer‑range structure; it showed better dynamic spectral fidelity in some analyses but is incompatible with truly online applications.
- **Evaluation Priorities:** If the goal is maximal decoding accuracy, tokenization choice is less critical under fine‑tuning. For applications relying on individual neural signatures (e.g., biometrics), learnable tokenizers offer a clear advantage.

### Failure Signatures
- **Tokenizer training divergence or high final loss:** indicates poor hyperparameters or insufficient capacity.
- **Reconstruction PVE < 95%:** suggests vocabulary too small, scaling inappropriate, or tokenizer failing to capture signal structure.
- **MEG‑GPT validation loss increases or plateaus high:** token quality inadequate or architecture mismatch (embedding dim vs. vocab size).
- **Synthetic data lack expected spectral peaks (e.g., alpha, beta):** tokenizer or model failed to preserve frequency information; check preprocessing and model capacity.
- **Subject fingerprinting accuracy near chance:** tokenizer discards subject‑specific cues; consider using learnable tokenizer or increasing its capacity.
- **Downstream decoding accuracy much lower than reported:** verify data splits, feature extraction from decoder outputs, and classifier setup.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Signal Reconstruction Fidelity:** Compute PVE between original MEG signals and detokenized reconstructions across subjects and channels for each tokenizer.
2. **Token Prediction Analysis:** Train MEG‑GPT, predict next tokens on validation set, de‑tokenize predictions, compute channel‑wise PVE against ground‑truth continuous signals.
3. **Generated Data Quality:** Synthesize new MEG recordings via autoregressive sampling from MEG‑GPT (nucleus sampling) followed by detokenization; evaluate static power spectral densities (Welch) and dynamic bursting characteristics (TDE‑HMM) against real data using L2 distances.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do non‑sample‑level tokenization strategies (e.g., patching, time‑frequency transforms, vector quantization) compare to sample‑level tokenization in terms of reconstruction fidelity, generative quality, and downstream task performance for MEG foundation models?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly defer the evaluation of non‑sample‑level tokenizers to future work, stating they focus exclusively on sample‑level tokenization.
- **Why unresolved:** Non‑sample‑level approaches may offer different trade‑offs in compressing temporal structure and capturing multi‑scale dynamics, which could be critical for modeling neural signals.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic comparison mirroring the paper’s evaluation framework across multiple MEG datasets, using the same foundation model architecture for tokenizers like patching, TF transforms, and VQ.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the impact of run‑to‑run stochastic variability in training both the tokenizers and the foundation model on the observed performance differences between tokenization strategies?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors acknowledge limited computational resources prevented multiple runs: “MEG‑GPT models were pretrained and fine‑tuned only once per tokenizer, leaving run‑to‑run variability uncharacterized.”
- **Why unresolved:** Without multiple training runs, it is unclear whether the modest benefits of learnable tokenizers are consistent or within stochastic noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Repeat the entire pipeline (tokenizer training, MEG‑GPT pretraining, downstream tasks) with multiple random seeds per tokenizer, then use mixed‑effects models to assess stability and significance of differences.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does extending the receptive field (temporal context window) of the transformer‑based foundation model alter the relative performance of learnable versus non‑learnable tokenizers, particularly for capturing long‑range dependencies and slow neural dynamics?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes: “the receptive field of MEG‑GPT was limited to 80 samples (320 ms), which may restrict sensitivity to slower neural dynamics.”
- **Why unresolved:** A fixed short context may disadvantage tokenizers that encode longer temporal patterns; a longer receptive field could change the balance, especially for generative quality and dynamic spectral analysis.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train MEG‑GPT models with varying sequence lengths (e.g., 80, 160, 320, 640 samples) for each tokenizer and re‑evaluate all criteria, focusing on metrics sensitive to long‑term structure.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive are the tokenization strategies and the downstream performance of MEG foundation models to variations in preprocessing pipelines (e.g., filtering, downsampling, source reconstruction methods, parcellation schemes)?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: “our analyses relied on source‑reconstructed parcel‑level time courses with fixed preprocessing choices… The impact of alternative preprocessing pipelines on tokenization and model performance warrants further investigation.”
- **Why unresolved:** Preprocessing choices alter signal statistics, which may interact with tokenizer design and affect generalization across datasets.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply alternative preprocessing variants (sensor‑level vs. source‑level, different bandpass filters, no leakage correction) to the same raw MEG data, train tokenizers and MEG‑GPT for each variant, and compare performance across evaluation criteria.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the identifiability problem in learnable tokenizers—where multiple distinct token assignments yield equivalent reconstructions—be mitigated to produce more consistent and interpretable token representations across training runs?
- **Basis in paper:** The discussion notes: “the inferred token representations are not uniquely identifiable… multiple distinct sets of token assignments may reconstruct the data equally well, leading to variability… This non‑uniqueness… may complicate the interpretability and comparability of learned tokens.”
- **Why unresolved:** Variability in token assignments limits the ability to ascribe consistent meaning to tokens, hindering scientific interpretation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce regularization or architectural constraints (e.g., penalizing token entropy, enforcing cluster separation) during tokenizer training, measure token consistency across runs (e.g., adjusted Rand index), and assess whether improved consistency affects downstream performance.

## Limitations
- Token refactorization algorithm underspecified, affecting reproducibility of vocabulary size and token usage.
- TDE‑HMM bursting analysis parameters (embedding dimension, state count, lag structure) not fully reported, hindering replication of dynamic spectral metrics.
- Synthetic data generation protocol (prompt construction, nucleus sampling conditioning) lacks detail, potentially impacting generative quality assessments.
- Evaluation relies solely on MEG data and a single transformer architecture; generalizability unknown.

## Confidence
- **Reconstruction fidelity (>97% PVE):** High confidence
- **Comparable performance (token prediction, generative quality, decoding):** High confidence
- **Learnable tokenizers improve subject fingerprinting:** Medium confidence
- **Simple fixed discretization is largely sufficient:** High confidence

## Next Checks
1. **Token refactorization replication:** Implement the exact pruning (e.g., zero‑count threshold) and relabeling strategy; report resulting vocabulary sizes, reconstruction PVE, and fingerprinting metrics to verify alignment with the original.
2. **Synthetic data generation and evaluation protocol:** Disclose and reproduce prompt length, subject/channel conditioning, nucleus sampling (top‑p), and detokenization transforms; compute static PSD (Welch parameters) and dynamic bursting via TDE‑HMM with fully documented hyperparameters; validate L2 distances.
3. **Learnable tokenizer capacity ablation:** Vary GRU hidden size and convolutional kernel width; retrain on Cam‑CAN and evaluate subject fingerprinting to confirm robustness of observed gains.
