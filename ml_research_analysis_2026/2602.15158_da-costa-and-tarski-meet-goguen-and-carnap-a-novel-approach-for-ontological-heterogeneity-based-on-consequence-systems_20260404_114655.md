# da Costa and Tarski meet Goguen and Carnap: a novel approach for ontological heterogeneity based on consequence systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15158
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15158
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes da Costian-Tarskianism—a syntax-based framework using extended consequence systems—and formally defines four operations (refinement, integration, connection via fibring, and decomposition), with decomposition highlighted as a novel capability relative to Carnapian-Goguenism.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses ontological heterogeneity—the challenge of integrating knowledge from domains with differing ontological assumptions—by introducing **da Costian-Tarskianism**. This framework replaces the institution-semantic foundation of Carnapian-Goguenism with a purely syntactic one based on **consequence systems**. Ontologies are modeled as **extended consequence systems**, which package a logical consequence operator with explicit ontological axioms. Key operations—refinement, integration, connection (via fibring), and decomposition—are defined categorically. The approach emphasizes modularity and flexible semantics but remains entirely theoretical, with no implementation or empirical evaluation provided.

## Method Summary
The methodology formalizes ontologies as pairs `⟨(C, ⊢), (C_o, Γ_o)⟩`, where `⟨C, ⊢⟩` is a consequence system (syntactic inference engine) and `(C_o, Γ_o)` are ontological sub-signature and axioms. Heterogeneous composition is achieved through:
- **Morphism-based links** (refinement, integration) in extended development graphs.
- **Fibring** for connection: combines signatures via translations that rename non-shared symbols to avoid conflicts, then closes under both translated consequence relations.
- **Splitting** for decomposition: uses signature morphisms that map connectives to formulas to form product diagrams in the category of splitting consequence systems.
Correctness is argued via categorical properties (e.g., closure conditions, universal properties). The framework is compared conceptually to Carnapian-Goguenism, highlighting decomposition as a novel syntactic operation.

## Key Results
- Formal definitions for four ontology combination operations: refinement, integration, connection (fibring), and decomposition.
- Decomposition is presented as a novel capability not directly available in the compared institution-based approach.
- Theoretical advantages claimed: non-relational semantics, algorithmic combination potential, and enhanced modularity through clean separation of logical and ontological layers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Syntax-first ontology representation
- **Claim:** Representing ontologies as extended consequence systems allows a semantically flexible foundation for heterogeneity by separating logical inference (`⟨C, ⊢⟩`) from domain knowledge (`Γ_o`).
- **Mechanism:** An ontology is a pair: a base consequence system (satisfying extensivity, monotonicity, idempotence) plus an ontological theory `Γ_o ⊆ L(C_o)` where `C_o ⊆ C`. The consequence operator is purely syntactic, decoupled from any model theory.
- **Core assumption:** The boundary between logical machinery and ontological commitments can be cleanly severed, enabling diverse semantics to be attached to the same syntactic core.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 2 define consequence systems as "devoid of a semantic construct"; Section 3 defines ontologies via Def 3.2 with explicit separation.

### Mechanism 2: Fibring for conflict-free connection
- **Claim:** Ontological connection via fibring combines two ontologies into a minimally stronger, conflict-free union by translating disjoint signature parts into a common language using variable renaming.
- **Mechanism:** Given ontologies `O1, O2`, form a combined signature `C = C1 ∪ C2`. Use translations `τ1, τ2` mapping symbols unique to one signature to fresh propositional variables. The combined consequence `Γ ⊢` is the smallest set containing `Γ` and closed under applying `⊢1` after `τ1⁻¹` and `⊢2` after `τ2⁻¹`. Ontological axioms are included via `τ1⁻¹(Γ1_o) ∪ τ2⁻¹(Γ2_o)`.
- **Core assumption:** Renaming non-shared symbols prevents syntactic collisions (homonymy), ensuring a well-defined mutual closure.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "connection (via fibring)"; Section 4.3 defines translations (Def 4.5), fibring (Def 4.7), and heterogeneous connection (Def 4.8), stating the goal is to join logics in a "conflict-free" language.

### Mechanism 3: Splitting for ontological decomposition
- **Claim:** Ontological decomposition extracts sub-ontologies via splitting signature morphisms that map connectives to formulas, enabling a product construction in the category `sCon`.
- **Mechanism:** A splitting morphism `f: ⟨C, ⊢⟩ → ⟨C', ⊢'⟩` maps each `k`-ary connective `c` to a `k`-ary formula `f(c) ∈ L(C')` (not necessarily a connective), preserving entailment (`Γ ⊢ φ ⇒ ̂f(Γ) ⊢' ̂f(φ)`). Decomposition holds if splitting links form a product diagram in an extended splittable development graph (ESDG).
- **Core assumption:** The syntactic structure of formulas (variable arity) suffices to define a meaningful "projection" of an ontology’s language and rules.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract lists "decomposition" as novel; Section 4.4 defines splitting morphisms (Def 4.10), category `sCon` (Def 4.12), and decomposition via product diagrams (Def 4.14).

## Foundational Learning
- **Consequence System `⟨C, ⊢⟩`**
  - *Why needed:* Atomic unit representing a class of logics via syntactic inference, independent of semantics. All higher structures build upon it.
  - *Quick check:* What does the condition `Γ ⊆ ⊢(Γ)` (extensivity) mandate about the set of consequences?
- **Extended Consequence System / Ontology `⟨C, ⊢, C_o, Γ_o⟩`**
  - *Why needed:* Explicitly separates logical inference engine from domain-specific commitments. Core representation for an ontology.
  - *Quick check:* Why is `ϕ ∈ ⊢(∅)` for all `ϕ ∈ Γ_o` (axiomatic theory) necessary in Def 3.1?
- **Fibring and Splitting**
  - *Why needed:* Primary heterogeneous operations: fibring builds up via union with conflict resolution; splitting breaks down via structured morphisms.
  - *Quick check:* What is the key syntactic difference between a translation `τ` (Def 4.5) and a splitting morphism `f` (Def 4.10)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Signature (C) -> Consequence System ⟨C, ⊢⟩ -> Extended CS / Ontology ⟨C, ⊢, C_o, Γ_o⟩ -> Extended Development Graph (EDG) / Extended Splittable Development Graph (ESDG) -> Operations (refinement, integration, connection, decomposition)
- **Critical path:**  
  (1) Choose/define base consequence systems for relevant logics. (2) For each domain view, pick a base CS and define `C_o, Γ_o` to form an ontology. (3) Place ontologies in an ESDG. (4) Add morphism links (definition, theorem) for refinement/integration. (5) Apply fibring (connection) to combine two nodes. (6) Apply splitting morphisms (decomposition) to break a node into a product of sub-ontologies.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Semantic flexibility vs. decidability: Syntax-first allows any semantics, but fibring may break decidability even for decidable inputs.  
  - Compositional canonicity vs. semantic alignment: Fibring yields minimal syntactic union but does not resolve synonymy/homonymy; requires manual pre-processing.  
  - Modularity vs. expressive power: Separation of `⊢` and `Γ_o` increases modularity but cannot automatically reason about their interaction.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Undecidable fibring: Combined `⟨C, ⊢⟩` may be undecidable despite decidable inputs if signatures overlap.  
  - Broken ontological alignment: Connection `O1 ∪ O2` may suffer from unaddressed homonyms/synonyms without pre-processing morphisms.  
  - Non-product decomposition: Diagram of splitting morphisms fails universal property if not a product in `sCon`.  
  - Non-structural base CS: Decomposition products not guaranteed if base CS lacks structurality (Prop 4.2).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Basic ontology construction:** Define a propositional consequence system `⟨C_prop, ⊢_prop⟩`. Create two ontologies `O1 = ⟨C_prop, ⊢_prop, {P}, {P(a)}⟩`, `O2 = ⟨C_prop, ⊢_prop, {Q}, {Q(a)}⟩`. Validate Def 3.1 conditions.
  2. **Heterogeneous connection via fibring:** Connect `O1` and `O2` from Exp 1. Compute `O1 ∪ O2` per Def 4.8. Verify combined `Γ_o` includes translated axioms and that `⟨C, ⊢⟩` is a consequence system.
  3. **Decomposition via splitting:** Take an ontology with a binary connective `c`. Define a splitting morphism `f` mapping `c(ξ1, ξ2)` to a formula like `(ξ1 ∧ ξ2)`. Build the product diagram in an ESDG. Check entailment preservation and universal property.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can a practical implementation be developed, and does it offer tangible benefits over existing tools like Hets? (Basis: Paper states it is "merely a proposal" with no implementation.)
- Does fibring-based connection preserve decidability and complexity bounds when extended with ontological axioms? (Basis: Paper acknowledges fibring generally breaks decidability; effect of ontological axioms is unanalyzed.)
- Is decomposition via splitting fundamentally different from achieving similar results in Carnapian-Goguenism? (Basis: Paper notes Carnapian-Goguenism could emulate it via universal properties but differences are unexplored.)
- Which alternative combination operations (e.g., graph-theoretic fibring, importing) integrate seamlessly while preserving soundness, completeness, and decidability? (Basis: Paper suggests other fibring operations could be incorporated but does not evaluate them.)
- Do theoretical benefits (non-relational semantics, modularity) translate into measurable improvements in ontology engineering tasks? (Basis: Paper claims advantages but provides no empirical metrics.)

## Limitations
- Entirely theoretical; no implementation or case studies to validate claims of modularity or algorithmic combination.
- Decidability and complexity of fibring remain unclear; undecidability may persist even with disjoint signatures.
- Semantic alignment (synonymy/homonymy) relies on manual pre-processing and is not automatically resolved by the core operations.
- Decomposition requires structural consequence systems to guarantee categorical products, limiting applicability.
- Schema variables `Ξ` are treated abstractly; concrete computational instantiation strategies are unspecified.

## Confidence
- **High:** Formal definitions and operational correctness (properties of consequence systems, morphisms, fibring, splitting).
- **Medium:** Theoretical advantages (flexible non-relational semantics, modularity) are plausible but untested.
- **Medium:** Novelty of decomposition vs. institution semantics is conceptually sound but requires broader comparative confirmation.
- **Low:** Practical applicability, scalability, and empirical benefits (no implementation or user studies).
