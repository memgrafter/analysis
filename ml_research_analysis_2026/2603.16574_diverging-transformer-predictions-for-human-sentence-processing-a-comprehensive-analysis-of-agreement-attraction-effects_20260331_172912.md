# Diverging Transformer Predictions for Human Sentence Processing: A Comprehensive Analysis of Agreement Attraction Effects

## Quick Facts
- arXiv: 2603.16574
- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16574
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Autoregressive transformers replicate human agreement attraction patterns in prepositional phrases but diverge on object-extracted relative clauses, showing no uniform cognitive mechanism across constructions.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the cognitive adequacy of autoregressive transformer language models as models of human sentence processing, focusing on agreement attraction effects. The authors compare surprisal predictions from 11 transformer models (124M–4B parameters) to human self-paced reading times for two syntactic constructions: prepositional phrases and object-extracted relative clauses. For prepositional phrases, models replicate the human pattern, showing facilitatory attraction only in ungrammatical singular subject sentences. For relative clauses, model predictions diverge both from each other and from humans, with all models erroneously exhibiting facilitatory attraction in grammatical plural subject sentences—a pattern never observed in human data. The findings demonstrate that transformers do not provide a unified explanation for morphosyntactic processing and highlight the necessity of comprehensive, construction-specific evaluation.

## Method Summary
The study uses stimuli from Wagers et al. (2009): 192 sentences with prepositional phrase modifiers and 384 with object-extracted relative clauses, fully crossing subject number, grammaticality, and attraction. Eleven autoregressive transformer models (124M–4B parameters) are evaluated in a left-to-right processing paradigm. Surprisal (−log₂ P(token|context)) is computed at the target verb, summing across sub-tokens when necessary. Human reading times are aggregated at the same verb region.

Statistical analyses include within‑model paired t‑tests comparing attraction conditions, linear mixed‑effects regressions with random intercepts for items and models (nested by family where convergent), and a global regression testing fixed effects of grammaticality, subject number, attraction, model size (log₁₀), and all interactions. The primary outcome is the pattern of attraction effects (presence, direction, magnitude) across constructions.

## Key Results
- **Prepositional phrases:** All models show a significant facilitatory attraction effect (mean reduction 2.4 bits) exclusively in ungrammatical singular subject sentences, matching human data.
- **Object-extracted relative clauses:** Model predictions diverge sharply from each other and from humans; none replicate the human asymmetric interference pattern (attraction only in ungrammatical singular subject sentences).
- **Unexpected pattern:** All models except Bloom (560M) exhibit a facilitatory attraction effect in grammatical plural subject sentences, a pattern never observed in human reading times and inconsistent with theoretical expectations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Transformer surprisal can predict human reading time patterns for agreement attraction in simpler syntactic constructions (prepositional phrases).
- **Mechanism:** Autoregressive transformers compute token probabilities left-to-right; surprisal (−log₂ P(word|context)) correlates with processing effort. For prepositional phrases, this yields facilitatory attraction only in ungrammatical singular subject sentences, matching human data.
- **Core assumption:** The next-word prediction objective implicitly captures human-like working memory constraints for these constructions.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “While transformer predictions generally align with human reading time data for prepositional phrase configurations...”
  - Section 3: Surprisal theory directly links model log-probability to reading time.
- **Break condition:** Fails for more complex constructions like object-extracted relative clauses.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Agreement attraction effects arise from interference during memory retrieval; transformers may simulate this via attention patterns over distractor tokens.
- **Mechanism:** In human processing, a plural distractor noun erroneously satisfies the verb’s number feature during retrieval. Transformers might exhibit analogous behavior if attention disproportionately attends to the distractor when predicting verb number.
- **Core assumption:** Self-attention mechanisms functionally resemble human working memory retrieval processes.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Section 1: Agreement attraction is explained by storage and access in working memory.
  - Section 6: Notes that low-level operations may not correspond neatly across architectures.
- **Break condition:** If attention does not mirror interference (e.g., no sensitivity to distractor number), attraction effects will not emerge or will be construction-dependent.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Transformers lack a uniform, cognitively plausible mechanism for agreement attraction across syntactic structures; performance is construction-specific and model-dependent.
- **Mechanism:** No single architectural property consistently yields human-like attraction patterns; outcomes depend on model architecture, training data, and syntactic complexity.
- **Core assumption:** A valid cognitive model must replicate the full human pattern (asymmetric interference) across a comprehensive set of constructions.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “model predictions diverge sharply... no model replicates the human asymmetric interference pattern.”
  - Section 5: Contrasts with Experiment 1, noting divergence in ORC.
  - Corroborated by neighboring papers reporting mixed transformer performance on syntactic phenomena.
- **Break condition:** Comprehensive testing across more constructions/languages will likely reveal further divergences.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Surprisal Theory**
  - **Why needed here:** The analysis hinges on linking token-level surprisal from transformers to human reading time. Without this linking hypothesis, model predictions cannot be compared to behavioral data.
  - **Quick check question:** If a transformer assigns higher surprisal to a verb in a particular context, what does surprisal theory predict about reading time for that verb region?

- **Concept: Agreement Attraction Asymmetry**
  - **Why needed here:** The human effect is highly specific: facilitatory attraction only in ungrammatical sentences with singular subjects. Any model must explain this full pattern, not just pairwise effects.
  - **Quick check question:** In the Wagers et al. materials, which combination of subject number and grammaticality yields the attraction effect in humans?

- **Concept: Autoregressive Processing**
  - **Why needed here:** Transformers are evaluated in a left-to-right, token-by-token prediction setup to mimic self-paced reading. Understanding attention masks and context windows is essential to ensure proper simulation.
  - **Quick check question:** How does an autoregressive transformer’s attention mask differ from a bidirectional model like BERT, and why is the former used here?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Stimulus → Subword tokenization → Transformer forward pass (embedding → attention layers → FFN → output logits) → Token probabilities → Summed surprisal for multi-token verbs → Linear mapping to reading time (via regression).

**Critical path:** Ensure strict left-to-right processing (no future tokens visible); handle multi-token verbs by summing surprisal; align model outputs to human verb region; run within-model paired comparisons and cross-model regressions.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Model selection:* Mid-sized autoregressive models (124M–4B) balance expressivity and reliability; larger models do not consistently align better (global regression shows size effects only in Exp. 2, reducing surprisal).
- *Tokenization:* Frequent words rarely split, but summing surprisal is necessary; tokenization bias could confound comparisons.
- *Generalization:* Monolingual vs. multilingual models show no consistent pattern; corpus differences likely matter.

**Failure signatures:**
1. Spurious facilitatory attraction in grammatical plural subject sentences (observed in Exp. 2 across most models).
2. Complete absence of the human-observed attraction effect (c/d contrast) in either construction.
3. High variance in predictions across models for the same construction (Exp. 2).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate Experiment 1 (prepositional phrases) to confirm alignment for simpler constructions.
2. Replicate Experiment 2 (object-extracted relative clauses) to test for construction-specific divergence.
3. Test a novel construction (e.g., subject-extracted relative clauses) to probe boundary conditions of model failures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do the discrepancies between transformer predictions and human agreement attraction data generalize to languages beyond English, and do multilingual models perform differently from monolingual ones in non-English contexts?
- **Basis:** Explicit – The authors state in the Limitations section: “Future work must investigate whether the observed performance differences between monolingual and multilingual models persist, particularly when modeling sentence processing in non-English languages for which experimental reading time data is available.”
- **Why unresolved:** The study is limited to English stimuli and a specific set of models; cross-linguistic validation is lacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the experiments in multiple languages with established agreement attraction reading time data, comparing predictions from monolingual and multilingual transformer models of similar sizes and training regimens.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the failure of autoregressive transformers to replicate human agreement attraction patterns in object-extracted relative clauses due to the next-word prediction training objective or to specific architectural limitations of the transformer design?
- **Basis:** Explicit – The authors write: “Testing a broader range of models, including strictly monolingual models trained on massive corpora or alternative architectures, is necessary to determine if the failure to capture human-like processing patterns in relative clauses is an inherent limitation of the next-word prediction objective or an artifact of specific architectural choices.”
- **Why unresolved:** Only transformer-based models were evaluated; without comparing to models with different objectives or architectures, the root cause remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate non-transformer models (e.g., recurrent networks, state-space models) and transformers trained with alternative objectives (e.g., masked language modeling, sequence-to-sequence tasks) on the same agreement attraction benchmark.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do transformer models predict facilitatory attraction effects in grammatical plural subject sentences for object-extracted relative clauses, contrary to human data and theoretical expectations?
- **Basis:** Inferred – The results show that “all models but Bloom (560M) predicted a facilitatory attraction effect for grammatical sentences with plural subject (e/f), where theory would predict an inhibitory effect.” This pattern is inconsistent with human performance and unexplained by the authors.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper highlights this as a key divergence but does not provide an analysis of why models exhibit this effect; it may indicate a flaw in how transformers process number agreement in complex syntax.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct detailed analyses of model predictions (e.g., probing attention weights, representation similarity) for these specific configurations to identify the mechanism leading to the unexpected facilitation.

## Limitations
- Exact Wagers et al. (2009) sentence lists are not provided; reconstruction may introduce item mismatches.
- Model checkpoints, tokenizers, and preprocessing details are unspecified, risking reproducibility errors.
- Mixed-effects random effects structure is incompletely described; conclusions may be sensitive to specification.
- The study examines only two English constructions, limiting the generalizability of claims about a uniform mechanism.

## Confidence
- **PP replication (models show human-like attraction pattern):** Medium confidence
- **ORC divergence (models deviate from humans, with unexpected facilitatory attraction):** Medium-High confidence
- **No uniform mechanism (transformer behavior is construction-specific and model-dependent):** Medium confidence
