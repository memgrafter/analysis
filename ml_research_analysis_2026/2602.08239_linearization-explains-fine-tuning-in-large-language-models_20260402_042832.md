# Linearization Explains Fine-Tuning in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08239
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08239
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Parameter-efficient fine-tuning with explicit ℓ2 regularization induces linearized dynamics governed by the neural tangent kernel; the NTK condition number at initialization predicts fine-tuning performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the dynamics of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) in large language models, using LoRA as a primary example. The authors show that adding an explicit ℓ2 regularization term that penalizes deviation from the pretrained parameters constrains the fine-tuning trajectory to a small neighborhood where the model behaves linearly. This linearized regime is mathematically equivalent to kernel regression with the neural tangent kernel (NTK). Theoretical bounds prove that the distance from the linearized solution depends on the regularization strength and Lipschitz constants. The eigenvalue spectrum of the NTK, summarized by the condition number κ(K+σI), is shown to govern the empirical risk: a lower condition number predicts lower training/evaluation loss and higher accuracy on several text classification benchmarks. Moreover, the authors analyze how selecting different layers to train perturbs the NTK spectrum, providing theoretical bounds that lead to an algorithm for choosing trainable layers. Experiments on RoBERTa-base confirm that (i) stronger ℓ2 regularization keeps the model within the linear regime, and (ii) the NTK condition number at initialization is a strong predictor of downstream performance, thereby providing a rigorous foundation for designing and evaluating PEFT methods.

## Method Summary
The method starts from a pretrained model fθ₀. A subset of parameters (e.g., LoRA matrices A, B in selected layers) is chosen for fine-tuning. An ℓ2 regularization term λ/2∥θ−θ₀∥² is added to the loss, encouraging the parameters to stay close to initialization. The NTK matrix K is computed on a small random sample (≈32 examples) from the pretrained model: K = ∇fθ₀(X)∇fθ₀(X)ᵀ, where the gradients are taken only with respect to the trainable parameters. With this regularization, the fine-tuning dynamics are equivalent to kernel gradient descent with the fixed NTK. The condition number κ(K+σI) (with σ a small RKHS regularization) is then used as a predictor of fine-tuning success; lower κ indicates better conditioning and is expected to yield lower risk. To decide which layers to train, the spectral perturbation effect of adding a layer’s contribution S_l = ∇θₗfθ₀(X)∇θₗfθ₀(X)ᵀ is analyzed. The ratio λ_max(K+S+σI)/λ_max(K+σI) approximates the risk increase; thus layers that minimally increase this ratio are selected. During actual training, the selective ℓ2 regularization (which applies the penalty only when the gradient and parameter direction have positive inner product) may be used to ensure monotonic loss decrease.

## Key Results
- Moderate ℓ2 regularization (λ ≈ 1–10) keeps ∥θ_t−θ₀∥ and the KL divergence between the true and linearized models low, confirming that the fine-tuning stays within the linear regime.
- The NTK condition number κ(K+σI) computed at initialization exhibits a strong negative correlation with final evaluation accuracy across GLUE, IMDb, and Yelp tasks; a lower κ predicts better performance.
- Spectral perturbation theory accurately forecasts the effect of adding layers on the NTK spectrum, and an algorithm based on eigenvalue ratios selects layers that minimize predicted risk, which aligns with empirical results.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Explicit ℓ2 regularization toward pretrained parameters induces fine-tuning dynamics that are well-approximated by linearization (first-order Taylor expansion) and governed by the neural tangent kernel (NTK).
- **Mechanism:** The regularizer λ/2∥θ−θ₀∥² constrains the parameter trajectory to stay within a small neighborhood of θ₀ where the model function fθ(x) is approximately linear: fθ(x) ≈ fθ₀(x) + ⟨∇fθ₀(x), θ−θ₀⟩. This makes the training dynamics equivalent to kernel gradient descent with a fixed NTK matrix K = ∇fθ₀(X)∇fθ₀(X)ᵀ.
- **Core assumption:** The model is Lipschitz in parameters around θ₀, and λ is large enough to keep updates small but not so large as to cause underfitting (Theorem 4 defines a threshold λ°).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "By making this explicit, using an ℓ2-distance inductive bias in parameter space, we show that fine-tuning dynamics become equivalent to learning with the positive-definite neural tangent kernel (NTK)."
  - [section 3] Theorem 3 bounds ∥fθₜ(X)−f̄θ̄ₜ(X)∥ in terms of Lipschitz constants and λ, showing that regularization controls deviation from the linearized model.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning for Continual Learning: A Neural Tangent Kernel Perspective") also apply NTK analysis to PEFT, supporting the relevance of linearization, though they do not explicitly induce it via regularization.
- **Break condition:** If λ is too small (e.g., λ→0), ∥θₜ−θ₀∥ may grow large (Theorem 2), violating the linear regime; if λ is too large, the model may remain too close to initialization and underfit (λ > λ° in Theorem 4). The selective regularization scheme (Eq. 8) helps maintain monotonic loss decrease but does not guarantee linearity if λ is inadequate.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The condition number κ(K+σI) of the NTK matrix at initialization predicts subsequent fine-tuning performance (lower training/evaluation loss and higher accuracy).
- **Mechanism:** In the NTK regression formulation (Eq. 14), the closed-form solution yields empirical risk bounds (Theorem 5) that depend on the eigenvalues of K. Specifically, R(θ) is squeezed between terms involving λ_min(K) and λ_max(K). A smaller condition number (λ_max/λ_min) indicates better conditioning, leading to lower risk.
- **Core assumption:** Linearization holds during fine-tuning, and the loss is squared error (theoretical); empirically, cross-entropy also shows correlation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "When linearization is a good model, our findings reveal a strong correlation between the eigenvalue spectrum of the NTK and the performance of model adaptation."
  - [section 4] Theorem 5: (σ∥Y∥/(σ+λ_max(K)))² ≤ R(θ) ≤ (σ∥Y∥/(σ+λ_min(K)))².
  - [section 6] Table 2 and Figure 2 show negative correlation between κ(K+σI) and evaluation accuracy across tasks; lower condition numbers correspond to better performance.
- **Break condition:** If the NTK condition number is very high (ill-conditioned), the bounds become loose and performance may degrade. However, the correlation assumes the linearization assumption is valid; if the fine-tuning trajectory leaves the linear regime (e.g., due to insufficient regularization), the condition number may not reliably predict performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The choice of which layers to fine-tune affects the NTK spectrum via spectral perturbation, and this can be quantified to guide parameter selection for improved performance.
- **Mechanism:** Adding trainable parameters from layer l modifies the NTK by adding a kernel contribution S_l = ∇θₗfθ₀(X)∇θₗfθ₀(X)ᵀ. Theorem 6 bounds the eigenvalue change: λ_i(K+S) ∈ [(1−η)λ_i(K), (1+η)λ_i(K)] with η = ∥K⁻¹/²SK⁻¹/²∥. Theorem 7 then relates the empirical risk ratio to the maximum eigenvalue ratio. Thus, at initialization, computing λ_max(K+S+σI) for candidate layers allows selecting those that minimize the predicted risk.
- **Core assumption:** The base NTK K corresponds to already-selected parameters, and the perturbation S is small enough (η ≪ 1) for the bounds to be tight.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5] Theorem 6 and Theorem 7 provide the spectral perturbation and risk ratio bounds.
  - [section 6] Figure 3 compares predicted eigenvalue ratios with empirical risk ratios for different layer combinations; the trends generally align, validating the bounds.
  - [corpus] Neighbors study layer selection in PEFT but not via NTK spectral perturbation; this paper's Algorithm 2 is a novel application.
- **Break condition:** If adding a layer causes a large perturbation (η large), the bounds become loose and the eigenvalue ratio may not accurately reflect the risk ratio. Also, the analysis fixes the regularization σ; if the number of trainable parameters changes drastically, σ should be adjusted to reflect model complexity (noted as a limitation in the paper).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**
  - *Why needed here:* The NTK is the core theoretical tool that maps fine-tuning to kernel regression; its spectrum determines optimization behavior.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the NTK matrix, and why does it remain constant during linearized fine-tuning?
- **Concept: Linearization (First-order Taylor approximation)**
  - *Why needed here:* Justifies using a linear model to approximate the neural network; enables NTK analysis.
  - *Quick check question:* Under what conditions does fine-tuning stay in the linear regime, and how does regularization enforce this?
- **Concept: Condition number**
  - *Why needed here:* Quantifies sensitivity of the NTK matrix; lower condition number predicts easier optimization and better generalization.
  - *Quick check question:* How does κ(K+σI) influence the empirical risk bound in Theorem 5?
- **Concept: Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)**
  - *Why needed here:* Provides the function space for the kernel regression solution; the representer theorem justifies using kernel expansions.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does the solution to the regularized fine-tuning problem lie in the RKHS generated by the NTK?
- **Concept: Lipschitz continuity**
  - *Why needed here:* Assumption for bounding deviations (Theorems 2–4); ensures model function and gradients do not change too rapidly.
  - *Quick check question:* How do Lipschitz constants of f and ∇f appear in the bound of Theorem 3?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Pretrained model (fθ₀) → Gradient computation on small sample → NTK matrix (K) → Loss with ℓ2 regularization (λ, σ) → AdamW (selective ℓ2) → Fine-tuned parameters (θ)

**Critical path:**
1. At initialization, compute K for baseline tuned parameters (e.g., {Wq, Wv} of layer 0).
2. For each candidate layer addition, compute S_l and then K+S for the union.
3. Compute condition number κ(K+S+σI) or eigenvalue ratio λ_max(K+S+σI)/λ_max(K+σI).
4. Select layers that minimize this ratio (lowest predicted risk).
5. Fine-tune with chosen layers, using moderate λ (e.g., λ=1–10 as in Table 1) to stay in linear regime without underfitting.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- λ: Larger λ keeps closer to linearization but may underfit; too small risks non-linear effects. Theorem 4 suggests λ proportional to Lip(f)∥fθ₀(X)−Y∥ and inversely proportional to the Lipschitz ball radius r.
- σ: Regularization in RKHS; if number of tunable parameters changes, σ should scale with complexity (not addressed in paper, fixed empirically).
- Sample size for NTK: Very small (n=32) suffices (experiments), but samples should be representative; no need for full dataset.
- Layer selection: Adding more layers increases capacity but also changes NTK spectrum; use spectral perturbation to guide.

**Failure signatures:**
- At initialization, κ(K+σI) is very high (e.g., >1000) → likely poor performance.
- During fine-tuning, ∥θₜ−θ₀∥ exceeds the r-ball where Lipschitz assumptions hold (compute bound from Theorem 2) → linearization may break.
- Predicted risk ratio (from eigenvalue ratio) does not match actual empirical risk after fine-tuning → indicates large η or violation of linearity assumption.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Sweep λ and measure ∥θₜ−θ₀∥², KL divergence between fθ and linearized model, and accuracy (Table 1). Verify moderate λ (e.g., 1–10) keeps model close to linearization without significant accuracy drop.
2. For fixed layer choices (e.g., {Wk} of layers {0,5,11}), compute NTK condition number at initialization and correlate with final evaluation accuracy across GLUE/IMDb/Yelp (Table 2, Figure 2). Expect strong negative correlation.
3. For a baseline set θ (e.g., {Wk} of layer 0), add candidate layers ˆθ and compare risk ratio log(R(θ∪ˆθ)/R(θ)) with eigenvalue ratio log(λ_max(K+S+σI)/λ_max(K+σI)) (Figure 3). Consistency supports perturbation bounds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What theoretical bounds exist between standard (non-regularized) fine-tuning and its linearized approximation?** The authors state that establishing such bounds remains outside their work, as they assume explicit ℓ2 regularization.
- **How does the kernel regularization parameter σ affect the NTK condition number as a predictor when selecting layers for fine-tuning?** They fix σ in experiments and note that varying trainable parameter types changes the condition number without adjusting σ, suggesting the need to factor σ into layer selection.
- **Do the NTK condition number and spectral perturbation results generalize to other PEFT methods besides LoRA?** The abstract hints they could, but empirical validation is only on LoRA; different parameterizations might affect the NTK spectrum differently.

## Limitations
- Linearization holds only within a λ‑tuned neighbourhood; λ too small breaks the approximation, λ too large causes underfitting.
- NTK condition number κ is estimated on n=32 samples with fixed σ=1e‑4; this estimate is noisy and does not adjust for parameter count, limiting cross‑configuration comparisons.
- Theory assumes gradient flow; AdamW’s stochasticity and the heuristic selective regularization (Eq.8) may violate key assumptions.

## Confidence
- Regularization induces linearization: Medium
- NTK condition number predicts performance: Medium
- Layer selection via spectral perturbation: Medium
