# From Bias to Balance: Fairness-Aware Paper Recommendation for Equitable Peer Review
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22438
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22438
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Fair-PaperRec achieves up to 42.03% increase in underrepresented-group participation with at most 3.16% utility change.

## Executive Summary
Despite double-blind peer review, systemic demographic biases persist in academic paper selection. This paper presents Fair-PaperRec, a post-review re-ranking system designed to increase equity while preserving selection quality. The system uses a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) trained with a combined loss that includes a differentiable fairness regularizer targeting demographic parity for intersectional attributes (race and country). The authors first validate the approach on synthetic datasets with tunable bias levels, identifying optimal fairness regularization strengths (λ) that improve macro- and micro-diversity metrics while stabilizing utility. They then apply the method to real conference data from SIGCHI, DIS, and IUI, where a configuration with λ=3 yields up to a 42.03% increase in underrepresented-group participation and at most a 3.16% change in utility (measured via weighted h-index). The results demonstrate that fairness regularization can enhance equity without degrading selection quality, particularly in highly biased regimes.

Further analysis reveals that multi-attribute fairness requires careful balancing of fairness weights: race, which exhibits higher initial disparity, benefits from larger λ (≈3), while country performs best at lower λ (≈2.5). Ablation studies confirm that emphasizing the more disadvantaged attribute can improve diversity for both. The work provides a practical, post-hoc intervention that can be integrated into existing peer review workflows to mitigate bias.

## Method Summary
Fair-PaperRec operates as a post-review re-ranking system. It uses a two-hidden-layer MLP to predict acceptance probabilities from input features that include a paper’s weighted h-index and author metadata (career stage, gender, race, country), while deliberately excluding the protected attributes (race, country) during training to prevent direct discrimination. The model is trained on a combined loss: binary cross-entropy for prediction accuracy plus a fairness regularizer λ * L_fairness, where L_fairness penalizes deviations from demographic parity for race and country subgroups using weights W_r and W_c. The total loss is minimized via Adam (learning rate 0.001) for up to 50 epochs. After training, the model scores all submissions, which are then ranked by predicted acceptance probability; the top N_a papers are selected.

The authors first validate on synthetic datasets with controlled demographic skew (high, moderate, fair bias) to map the fairness-utility landscape across λ values. They then transfer the approach to three HCI conferences (SIGCHI, DIS, IUI 2017) where historical acceptance data exhibits real-world bias. This two-stage evaluation identifies λ configurations that yield optimal diversity improvements with minimal utility drop—for instance, λ=3 on the original data provides up to 42% increase in underrepresented-group participation at under 3.2% utility cost.

## Key Results
- Achieves up to 42.03% increase in underrepresented-group participation with ≤3.16% utility loss.
- Identifies optimal fairness regularization strength (λ) around 3 for race and 2.5 for country in real conference data.
- In high-bias synthetic settings, fairness regularization can stabilize or even improve utility while boosting diversity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The system’s fairness-aware training works through three interrelated mechanisms:

**1. Differentiable Fairness Regularization:** The MLP is trained with a total loss combining prediction error (binary cross-entropy) and a fairness loss that penalizes deviations from demographic parity for race and country. By tuning λ, the model learns representations that balance utility (paper quality via weighted h-index) and fairness simultaneously. *Evidence*: Synthetic and real experiments show that appropriate λ values yield substantial diversity gains (up to 42%) with minimal utility impact. However, this relies on demographic parity being a suitable criterion and on bias being present in the training data; if quality distributions differ across groups, parity may conflict with meritocracy.

**2. Synthetic-to-Real Transfer for λ Selection:** Synthetic datasets with controllable demographic skew allow systematic exploration of how λ affects the fairness-utility trade-off. Patterns observed in these simplified settings (e.g., a λ “sweet spot” where utility stabilizes while diversity rises) are then applied to real conference data, where optimal λ values (2.5–3) produce the best trade-offs. *Evidence*: High-bias synthetic data shows peak diversity at λ≈3; real data for race shows stable utility at λ=2.5–3, supporting transferability. The mechanism assumes that synthetic bias models capture essential confounders of real-world peer review; if real biases involve additional factors (e.g., prestige networks), transfer may fail.

**3. Multi-Attribute Weight Balancing:** Since different protected attributes (race, country) have disparate baseline disparities, the combined fairness loss uses weights W_r and W_c. The paper finds that race, with higher initial disparity, requires larger λ (≈3) for optimal trade-off, while country performs best at lower λ (≈2.5). Ablation shows that emphasizing the more disadvantaged attribute (race) can improve diversity for both attributes. *Evidence*: In real data, race’s Macro Gain peaks at λ≈3, country at λ≈2.5; varying W_r and W_c confirms that increasing weight for race yields joint benefits. This mechanism assumes additive contributions of attributes and that their optimal regularization strengths depend primarily on initial disparity levels; if attributes are highly correlated or interact non-additively, the weighting scheme may be suboptimal.

## Foundational Learning
**Demographic Parity**  
*Why needed*: The fairness loss explicitly enforces that acceptance probability should be equal across protected groups, aiming to correct historical underrepresentation.  
*Quick check*: What is a key limitation of demographic parity in selection contexts where quality distributions might differ across groups? (Answer: It forces equal acceptance rates regardless of quality, potentially selecting lower-quality papers from some groups to meet quotas, which conflicts with meritocracy.)

**Differentiable Fairness Loss**  
*Why needed*: Allows fairness to be incorporated as a differentiable component in the end-to-end neural training objective, enabling gradient-based optimization alongside prediction loss.  
*Quick check*: How would you modify the fairness loss in Eq. (3) if you wanted to enforce equality of opportunity instead? (Answer: Replace the parity loss with a loss that penalizes differences in true positive rates (acceptance given quality) across groups.)

**Weighted h-index as Utility Proxy**  
*Why needed*: Utility is measured as the change in the average weighted h-index of selected authors, where weights account for career stage. This attempts to adjust for seniority when evaluating the quality of the selected set.  
*Quick check*: Why might using a weighted h-index still introduce bias into the utility measurement, and how could that affect the reported fairness-utility trade-offs? (Answer: h-index correlates with seniority and field-specific publication practices; weighting may not fully remove these biases, so utility could be overestimated for certain groups, making fairness gains appear smaller.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw data → Pre-processing (one-hot encoding, min-max scaling, stratified split) → MLP forward pass → Losses (prediction + fairness) → Total loss → Backpropagation (Adam) → Parameter update (repeat for epochs) → After training: All papers → Pre-processing → MLP prediction (ŷ) → Ranking by ŷ → Top-N selection.

**Critical path:** For each training epoch: forward pass on a batch → compute binary cross-entropy and batch fairness loss → sum L_total = L_pred + λ * L_fair → backward pass → Adam step. After training completion, forward-pass all papers, sort by ŷ, select top N_a.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- λ sensitivity: Too low yields minimal fairness gains; too high risks utility collapse. Optimal λ depends on underlying bias level and attribute (e.g., ~3 for race, ~2.5 for country).
- Attribute weighting: Joint fairness optimization can dilute gains for one attribute when emphasizing another; initial weights based on group size may not be optimal, necessitating ablation.
- Model simplicity: The MLP is interpretable and efficient but may not capture complex interactions (e.g., author networks, topic effects); more sophisticated architectures (e.g., graph neural networks) could improve performance but reduce transparency.

**Failure signatures:**
- Utility Gain becomes large negative, indicating over-correction and selection of low-quality papers.
- Macro Gain and Micro Gain move in opposite directions, suggesting intervention benefits group-level statistics but harms individuals, or vice versa.
- High variance in results across random seeds for certain λ values, signaling unstable training or a poorly conditioned loss landscape.

**First 3 experiments to run:**
1. **Synthetic regime scan:** For each synthetic dataset (high/moderate/fair bias), train with λ ∈ [1,10] and record Macro Gain, Micro Gain, Utility Gain. Identify λ where Utility Gain ≈ 0 or positive and diversity peaks. Expected: In high-bias data, diversity peaks at λ≈3; in fair/moderate bias, utility drops at larger λ.
2. **Original data single-attribute runs:** Train separate models on race-only and country-only (excluding the other from features) for λ=1,2,2.5,3,5,10. Compare per-attribute Macro/Micro gains and utility. Expected: Race achieves best fairness-utility trade-off at λ≈3; country at λ≈2.5.
3. **Ablation – multi-attribute weights:** Using original data, fix λ (e.g., 2.5) and vary weight pairs: (W_r,W_c) = (0.32,0.68), (2*0.32,0.68), (0.32,2*0.68). Measure combined diversity gain and utility. Expected: Increasing W_r improves both attributes’ diversity; increasing W_c may reduce race diversity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. Generalization of λ from synthetic to real bias regimes.** The paper validates λ transfer via synthetic experiments and three conferences but does not establish a systematic mapping between synthetic bias levels and optimal λ in real settings where additional confounders exist. A meta-analysis across multiple conferences correlating baseline bias severity with optimal λ would resolve this.

**2. Utility metric robustness.** Weighted h-index may encode seniority and field biases; the fairness-utility trade-offs reported might change with alternative quality measures (e.g., normalized citations, expert scores). Re-running experiments with such metrics is needed.

**3. Principled multi-attribute weighting.** The fairness loss uses weights based on protected group proportions, yet ablation shows that emphasizing the more disadvantaged attribute (race) can improve joint diversity. A general weighting strategy for intersectional fairness remains undeveloped; optimization across subgroups is required.

**4. Alternative fairness definitions.** The paper uses demographic parity, but other notions like equality of opportunity may be more appropriate for selection. Comparing losses that enforce conditional parity would clarify trade-offs.

**5. Staged interventions.** Fair-PaperRec is a post-review re-ranker; bias may already be embedded in review scores from reviewer assignment. Extending the approach to earlier stages (e.g., fairness-aware reviewer matching) and comparing holistic pipelines is necessary.

## Limitations
- Synthetic datasets may not capture real-world confounders (e.g., institutional prestige, network effects), questioning the generalizability of λ transfer.
- The weighted h-index utility metric might encode seniority and geographic biases, potentially distorting the measured fairness-utility trade-off.
- Results are based on three HCI conferences; it is unclear how well the approach transfers to other academic fields with different bias patterns.

## Confidence
- Fairness-utility improvement: **Medium** (empirical support from synthetic and real data, but dependent on metrics and datasets).
- Synthetic-to-real λ transfer: **Low‑Medium** (consistent patterns observed but synthetic realism uncertain).
- Multi-attribute balancing: **Medium** (ablation evidence, though domain-specific).

## Next Checks
1. **Synthetic realism audit:** Compare attribute-quality correlations (e.g., h-index vs. race/country) between synthetic and real data; if misaligned, refine synthetic generation and retest λ transfer.
2. **Utility metric robustness:** Replace weighted h-index with alternative quality measures (e.g., reviewer scores, citations) to confirm stability of fairness-utility trade-offs.
3. **Cross-domain validation:** Apply Fair-PaperRec to submissions from at least two additional conferences in different fields (e.g., ACM FAccT, IEEE S&P) to test generalizability of λ optima (~2.5–3) and attribute-specific balances.
