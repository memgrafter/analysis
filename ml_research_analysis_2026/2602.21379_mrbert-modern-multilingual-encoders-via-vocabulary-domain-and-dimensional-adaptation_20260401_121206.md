# MrBERT: Modern Multilingual Encoders via Vocabulary, Domain, and Dimensional Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21379
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21379
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: State-of-the-art performance on Catalan/Spanish benchmarks and domain tasks, with up to 2.4× inference speedup via attention-head pruning.

## Executive Summary

MrBERT introduces a family of encoder models derived from ModernBERT, specialized for specific languages or domains while maintaining efficiency. The paper explores three adaptation strategies: vocabulary specialization to create smaller 150M-parameter models for Catalan and Spanish; continued pre-training on biomedical and legal corpora yielding 300M-parameter domain experts; and Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) that enables flexible compressed embeddings via attention-head pruning. Evaluations show language-adapted models set new records on CLUB (Catalan) and EvalES (Spanish), domain-adapted models achieve top scores on biomedical and legal MTEB tasks, and attention-based MRL delivers up to 2.4× speedup at 25% capacity with minimal accuracy loss, especially for domain-adapted variants.

## Method Summary

MrBERT builds on ModernBERT, initially pretrained on a 6.1T‑token multilingual corpus across 35 languages. The adaptation framework comprises: (1) **Vocabulary specialization**: a new tokenizer (~50k vocab) is trained on each target language (Catalan, Spanish), the embedding matrix is re‑initialized with shared token reuse, and bilingual continued pre‑training (EN‑ES/CA) aligns the new space, reducing parameters to 150M. (2) **Domain adaptation**: the full 300M multilingual base undergoes continued pre‑training on curated domain corpora (biomedical: 24B tokens, legal: 9B tokens), preserving broad multilingual coverage while internalizing jargon. (3) **Matryoshka Representation Learning**: integrated during a final annealing or adaptation phase, with two variants—MLP hidden‑slicing (MAT) and attention‑head pruning (AttMAT)—to produce nested representations; AttMAT is preferred for its superior throughput gains. Models are evaluated on multilingual (XTREME), language‑specific (CLUB, EvalES), domain (MTEB) benchmarks, and throughput.

## Key Results
- Language‑adapted (150M) models achieve state‑of‑the‑art on Catalan (85.49 CLUB) and Spanish (89.83 EvalES), outperforming larger multilingual variants.
- Domain‑adapted (300M) models attain the highest average scores on biomedical (48.56) and legal (58.15) MTEB tasks.
- Attention‑head pruning (AttMAT) enables up to 2.4× inference speedup at 25% capacity with minimal performance degradation, particularly robust for domain‑adapted models; vocabulary‑adapted models suffer larger drops under aggressive pruning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Vocabulary Specialization

- **Claim:** Reducing the embedding matrix via a language‑specific tokenizer yields smaller models without sacrificing (and often improving) language‑specific performance.
- **Mechanism:** Train a new tokenizer (~50k vocab) on target‑language data, reuse embeddings for overlapping tokens, randomly initialize new tokens, and continue pre‑training on bilingual (EN‑target) corpora to align the embedding space.
- **Core assumption:** A smaller, tailored vocabulary reduces noise and parameter overhead, allowing the fixed model capacity to focus on target linguistic structures.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] vocabulary specialization yields 150M models with SOTA on CLUB/EvalES; [section 3.3] details token overlap (64.24% ES, 32.15% CA‑ES) and initialization from Spanish‑adapted weights for Catalan.
- **Break condition:** Very low token overlap (<20%) or insufficient bilingual data leads to convergence difficulties and degraded performance (observed for Catalan under aggressive MRL).

### Mechanism 2: Continued Pre‑training for Domains

- **Claim:** Continued pre‑training on domain‑specific corpora adapts a multilingual encoder to high‑stakes domains while preserving cross‑lingual capabilities.
- **Mechanism:** Take the full multilingual base model and perform additional MLM training on curated domain texts (legal: 9B tokens, 79.5% EN/20.5% ES; biomedical: 24B tokens, mostly EN/ES). The weights adjust to capture domain terminology and discourse patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Domain knowledge is encoded in lexical/syntactic patterns learnable via MLM on in‑domain text, and this can be acquired without destroying previously learned multilingual representations.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] domain‑adapted models achieve highest MTEB averages; [section 3.3] describes data mixtures, epoch selection via validation loss, and the decision to apply CPT on the multilingual base; [section 5.3] Tables 4–5 show gains over baselines.
- **Break condition:** Overfitting (excessive epochs or narrow/noisy data) or overly language‑specific data may cause loss of general multilingual utility; mitigated by validation‑guided epoch selection and multilingual mixtures.

### Mechanism 3: Matryoshka Representation Learning via Attention‑Head Pruning

- **Claim:** AttMAT enables flexible embedding compression with minimal performance loss and up to 2.4× inference speedup by pruning entire attention heads.
- **Mechanism:** During a combined annealing+matryoshka phase, the model learns to produce semantically consistent representations across nested sub‑spaces; at inference, a subset of attention heads is removed, reducing quadratic memory/latency costs.
- **Core assumption:** Attention heads exhibit functional redundancy; pruning a subset is tolerable if the model learns to distribute critical information across remaining heads.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] AttMAT provides 2.4× speedup at 25% capacity; [section 4] explains variants (MLP vs. AttMAT) and training curriculum; [section 5.4] Figures 2–4 and Tables 10–14 show superior throughput and robustness for domain‑adapted models.
- **Break condition:** Aggressive pruning (<50% heads) severely harms vocabulary‑adapted models (e.g., Catalan drops 3.06 points at 25% compression), likely due to insufficient capacity to handle both new token embeddings and compressed attention; domain‑adapted models are more resilient.

- **Continued Pre‑training vs. vocabulary adaptation**  
  Why needed: The paper’s core innovation is the matched choice: CPT for domains (preserves vocab) and vocab adaptation for languages (reduces params). Knowing CPT (Gururangan et al., 2020) and vocab adaptation (Lakew et al., 2018) explains the divergent model families.  
  Quick check: Why might CPT on a domain corpus hurt performance if applied to a language‑adapted model with a restricted vocabulary?

  Why needed: MRL solves deployment efficiency; distinguishing MLP‑based vs. attention‑head‑based MRL is critical for hardware‑aware trade‑offs.  
  Quick check: What is the theoretical motivation for pruning attention heads instead of MLP dimensions in MRL for encoder models?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  1. **ModernBERT base:** Pretrained on 35 languages + code (6.1T tokens), 308M params, 8k context.  
  2. **Tokenizer modules:** Salamandra multilingual tokenizer, plus dedicated Spanish/Catalan tokenizers (~50k vocab) with embedding re‑initialization.  
  3. **Adaptation pipelines:**  
     - Language adaptation: Bilingual CPT (EN‑ES/CA) on smaller corpora (615B/47.4B tokens), reducing model to 150M via embedding matrix shrinkage.  
     - Domain adaptation: CPT on biomedical/legal corpora (24B/9B tokens) on the full 300M model.  
  4. **MRL modules:** Integrated during final annealing/adaptation, training for nested representations via either MLP projection (MAT) or attention‑head grouping/pruning (AttMAT).  
  5. **Evaluation harness:** XTREME (multilingual), CLUB/EvalES (monolingual), MTEB + novel Spanish datasets (domain), plus throughput benchmarking.

- **Critical path:**  
  Pretrain ModernBERT base → (a) Tokenizer training & embedding adaptation → Bilingual CPT → (b) Domain CPT on base → MRL integration → Fine‑tune on downstream tasks → Benchmark.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Vocabulary size vs. specialization: Smaller vocab reduces parameters and noise but risks losing cross‑lingual capacity; overlap analysis guides initialization.  
  - CPT scale vs. retention: Domain CPT uses more data (24B) and preserves full 300M to capture complex jargon; language CPT uses less data (47B) and reduces params for efficiency.  
  - MRL variant: AttMAT gives 2.4× speedup but is less robust for vocabulary‑adapted models; MAT preserves accuracy better but offers smaller speed gains.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High validation perplexity after vocabulary adaptation → token overlap too low or insufficient bilingual data.  
  - Domain CPT overfitting (validation loss rises) → too many epochs or corpus not truly in‑domain.  
  - MRL compression collapse (steep performance drop at 25% heads) → applied to vocabulary‑adapted models; should prioritize domain‑adapted models for aggressive pruning.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Vocabulary overlap sanity check:** Compute token overlap between Salamandra and new language tokenizer; if <40%, expect slower convergence and consider more bilingual data or gradual vocabulary expansion.  
  2. **CPT epoch sweep:** Monitor validation cross‑entropy on a held‑out domain slice; stop at minimum to avoid overfitting (as done for legal: 10 epochs; biomedical: 2 epochs).  
  3. **MRL variant benchmark:** On target hardware (H100), measure throughput (samples/sec) at 25%/50%/75% head pruning for both AttMAT and MAT on a validation set; choose AttMAT if speed is priority and model is domain‑adapted, else MAT.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: Why do vocabulary‑adapted models suffer greater performance loss under MRL compression than domain‑adapted models?

- **Question:** Why do vocabulary‑adapted models suffer greater performance loss under Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) compression than domain‑adapted models?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Figure 4: MrBERT‑ca degrades by 3.06 points on CLUB at 25% head pruning, while domain‑adapted variants retain most performance. Hypothesis: vocabulary adaptation consumes representational capacity needed to learn new tokens, leaving less room for compression.
- **Why unresolved:** The comparison confounds model size (150M vs. 300M) and data quantity; no controlled experiment isolates adaptation type as the sole variable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation where model size, training data scale, and compression ratios are held constant, varying only the adaptation objective (vocabulary vs. domain). Include probing of attention patterns and hidden representations to understand the degradation mechanism.

### Open Question 2: What is the optimal training order and combination of adaptation strategies?

- **Question:** What is the optimal training order and combination of adaptation strategies (vocabulary specialization, domain continued pre‑training, MRL integration) for balancing performance and efficiency?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit: "To maintain broad language coverage... we perform domain adaptation directly on the multilingual base model rather than the language‑adapted versions." This ordering is chosen a priori without comparison to alternatives.
- **Why unresolved:** No experiments evaluate sequences such as base → language → domain or simultaneous multi‑stage training with MRL. Interactions between these stages remain unknown, and the claimed benefit of preserving multilingualism may come at a cost to domain specialization speed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic factorial study covering all plausible orders (including joint training), with and without MRL at each stage, using consistent evaluation on both monolingual/domain tasks and multilingual benchmarks. Analysis of feature distributions (e.g., language vs. domain signals in hidden layers) would provide explanatory insights.

### Open Question 3: How robust are the Catalan and Spanish adapted models to dialectal variation and informal language?

- **Question:** How robust are the Catalan and Spanish adapted models to dialectal variation and informal language use, given their reliance on curated, formal corpora?
- **Basis in paper:** The Impact Statement acknowledges under‑representation of dialectal variation (e.g., Latin American Spanish) as a limitation. Appendix A shows training data sources are primarily formal (news, legal, parliamentary, scientific).
- **Why unresolved:** Evaluation is restricted to formal benchmarks (CLUB, EvalES). No tests are conducted on dialectal or informal registers, leaving the models' generalization to real‑world variation unmeasured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluation on dialectal datasets (e.g., Latin American Spanish variants, Balearic Catalan) and informal domains (social media, conversation transcripts). Complementary experiments could fine‑tune on dialectal data to assess adaptability and identify specific linguistic phenomena that cause failures.

## Limitations

- **Data composition uncertainty:** Total token counts are provided, but exact dataset contributions, sampling distributions, and curation pipeline details (CURATE thresholds, sorting) are unspecified, blocking faithful reconstruction of training data.
- **Architecture/hyperparameter opacity:** ModernBERT’s exact configuration and optimizer settings are not stated; MRL implementation details (head grouping, loss weighting) are underspecified.
- **Conditional robustness:** MRL’s effectiveness depends strongly on model type and compression ratio; vocabulary‑adapted models degrade sharply under aggressive pruning, and the boundary conditions are not fully mapped.

## Confidence

- **Vocabulary specialization (150M models):** Medium confidence. Strong benchmark results support efficacy, but dependence on token overlap (32–64%) and initialization strategy is not systematically studied.
- **Domain CPT (300M models):** High confidence. Clear MTEB improvements, validation‑guided epoch selection, and intentional multilingual mixture align with established CPT mechanisms.
- **MRL via attention‑head pruning:** Medium confidence. Reported speedup and robustness for domain models are compelling, but implementation details are sparse and vulnerability of vocabulary‑adapted models suggests limited generalizability.
