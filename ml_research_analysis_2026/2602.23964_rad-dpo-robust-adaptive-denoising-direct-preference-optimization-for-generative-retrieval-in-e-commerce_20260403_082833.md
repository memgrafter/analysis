# RAD-DPO: Robust Adaptive Denoising Direct Preference Optimization for Generative Retrieval in E-commerce

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23964
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23964
- Reference count: 36
- Primary result: +0.34% lift in User Conversion Rate (UCVR) in online A/B testing; offline gains on 700M logs: Recall@10 ↑ from 0.2731 to 0.3048, MRR ↑ from 0.2286 to 0.2481.

## Executive Summary
Generative retrieval for e-commerce often uses structured Semantic IDs (SIDs) that encode a product’s hierarchical category. Standard Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) applied to such sequences suffers from three failure modes: it suppresses shared prefix tokens (which are semantically meaningful), is vulnerable to noisy pseudo‑negatives from implicit feedback, and exacerbates probability squeezing in multi‑label queries where many items are relevant. RAD‑DPO introduces three complementary innovations—Token‑Level Gradient Detachment, Similarity‑based Dynamic Reward Weighting, and Multi‑Label Global Contrastive objective with global SFT—to address each issue. Trained on 700M logs from JD.com, RAD‑DPO achieves Recall@10 of 0.3048 versus DPO’s 0.2731 and MRR of 0.2481 versus 0.2286, while online A/B testing with millions of users shows a significant +0.34% lift in User Conversion Rate. The improvements hold across model scales from 0.6B to 8B parameters, demonstrating robustness.

## Method Summary
RAD‑DPO follows a two‑stage training process: (1) Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) on successful retrieval sequences (query + positive SIDs) to maximize log‑likelihood across all positives; (2) RAD‑DPO fine‑tuning that combines global SFT with a weighted contrastive loss. Given a query with positives \(Y_{\text{pos}}\) and a pool of negatives \(Y_{\text{neg}}\), the method selects a top‑tier positive \(y_w^*\) (e.g., highest score) and computes a pairwise reward \(\hat{r}(y) = \log \pi(y|x) / |y|\), where \(\pi\) is the language model. The core loss is \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}\), with \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}\) averaging \(\log \pi(y|x)\) over \(Y_{\text{pos}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}} = -\log \sigma(\beta \log \sum_{\text{neg}} w(\text{sim}) \exp(\hat{r}(y_w^*)-\hat{r}(\text{neg})))\). Three modules tailor this to hierarchical SIDs:  
- **Token‑Level Gradient Detachment (TLGD)** computes the longest common prefix length \(k\) between \(y_w^*\) and each negative; it applies stop‑gradient to the negative’s prefix tokens during backpropagation, preserving shared semantics.  
- **Similarity‑based Dynamic Reward Weighting (RDRW)** computes the cosine similarity between the final hidden states of \(y_w^*\) and each negative, maintains quartile anchors (\(Q25, Q50, Q75\)) from a cached distribution, and assigns weight \(w(\text{sim})\) via a smooth piecewise function (1 if \(\text{sim}<Q25\), 0.5 if \(\text{sim}>Q75\), otherwise smoothly transition) to down‑weight ambiguous negatives.  
- **Multi‑Label Global Contrast (MLGC)** uses block‑diagonal masking to efficiently aggregate the contrastive loss over the whole negative pool in a single batch, avoiding repeated encoding.  
Training uses a low learning rate (\(10^{-6}\)) and batch size 64; hyperparameters such as the DPO temperature \(\beta\) and quartile update frequency are not fully specified but are critical for replication.

## Key Results
- Offline: Recall@10 ↑ from 0.2731 to 0.3048; MRR ↑ from 0.2286 to 0.2481 on 700M industrial logs.
- Online: +0.34% UCVR lift in A/B test with millions of users.
- Robustness: Gains hold across model scales (0.6B to 8B parameters).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Prevents destructive gradient conflicts on shared hierarchical SID prefixes by truncating negative sample gradients only at divergence points.
- **Mechanism:** Computes longest common prefix length \(k\) between positive and negative SIDs. Applies stop‑gradient (SG) to the log‑probability of prefix tokens (\(t \le k\)) in the negative sample during loss backpropagation, preserving correct prefix generation while still penalizing divergent suffixes.
- **Core assumption:** Shared prefix tokens represent semantically valid hierarchical categories (e.g., product class) and should not be suppressed even when the full negative sequence is incorrect.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions Token‑Level Gradient Detachment; Section 2.2 provides Equation (4) with indicator \(1_{\text{diff}}(t)\) and SG operation; neighboring papers (FORGE, PRISM) address SID structure but not gradient‑level prefix handling.
- **Break condition:** Prefix detection fails (e.g., tokenization inconsistency) or pre‑trained prefix representations already corrupted; then detachment may prevent necessary correction.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Reduces optimization on noisy pseudo‑negative samples by adaptively softening their penalty weight based on semantic similarity to the positive.
- **Mechanism:** Computes cosine similarity between final hidden states of positive and negative sequences. Uses quartile‑based anchors (\(Q25, Q50, Q75\)) from a cached distribution to assign weight \(w(\text{sim})\) via a smooth piecewise function, lowering weight for high‑similarity (ambiguous) pairs. Scales DPO loss by this weight.
- **Core assumption:** High embedding similarity indicates the negative may be a false negative (relevant but unclicked) rather than a true preference violation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes similarity‑based dynamic reward weighting; Section 2.3 details similarity computation, quartile anchoring, and weight function; neighboring literature on implicit feedback bias confirms noise but not this specific weighting.
- **Break condition:** Similarity metric poorly correlates with true relevance (representation collapse) or quartile anchors fail to track distribution shift during training; then weighting may misclassify true negatives as noisy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Counters probability “squeezing” in multi‑label e‑commerce queries by replacing pairwise contrast with a global contrast over all negatives, while using global SFT to maintain positive coverage.
- **Mechanism:** For a query with multiple positives \(Y_{\text{pos}}\), selects top‑tier positive \(y_w^*\). Computes DPO‑style loss using log‑sum‑exp over the entire negative pool \(Y_{\text{neg}}\) (avoiding repeated pair encoding). Combined with global SFT loss that averages log‑likelihood over all positives.
- **Core assumption:** Multi‑label queries require joint optimization across all valid items to prevent the model from disproportionately lowering probability for some correct candidates when contrasted only against a single positive.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions multi‑label global contrastive objective; Section 2.1 presents Equations (1) and (2) and block‑diagonal masking for efficiency; related works (SimPO, ORPO) propose reference‑free variants but do not address multi‑label global contrast.
- **Break condition:** Negative pool too large/diverse, log‑sum‑exp dominated by a few hard negatives; or poor selection of \(y_w^*\) (not truly representative) misaligns contrast.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**  
  *Why needed here:* Baseline method that RAD‑DPO modifies; understanding its pairwise objective and reference model reliance is essential to see why it fails on structured SIDs.  
  *Quick check question:* How does DPO’s use of a uniform penalty across the entire negative sequence lead to “suppression” of shared prefixes in hierarchical outputs?

- **Concept: Hierarchical Semantic IDs (SIDs)**  
  *Why needed here:* The structured output format (e.g., category → subcategory → item) creates shared prefixes; this is the root cause of gradient conflicts and squeezing.  
  *Quick check question:* Why do items from the same broad category share initial SID tokens, and how does this property break standard pairwise contrast?

- **Concept: Implicit Feedback Noise**  
  *Why needed here:* Industrial logs (clicks, orders) contain position bias and random clicks, creating pseudo‑negatives (relevant items not clicked). This noise must be mitigated for robust learning.  
  *Quick check question:* What are two sources of noise in e‑commerce implicit feedback that could cause a relevant item to be treated as a negative in DPO training?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Backbone LLM (e.g., Qwen-1.7B) with SID decoder head → TLGD Module (longest common prefix mask, stop‑gradient on negative prefixes) → RDRW Module (hidden state caching, cosine similarity, quartile anchoring, weight calculation) → MLGC+Global SFT (batch construction with block‑diagonal mask, global SFT over all positives, global contrast over \(y_w^*\) vs. full negative pool) → Combined loss (\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}\)) → Parameter update.

**Critical path:**  
1. Input batch (query, context, positives, negatives) → backbone forward pass generates all sequences and extracts EOS hidden states.  
2. RDRW computes cosine similarities between \(y_w^*\) and each negative, updates quartiles, and calculates weights \(w(\text{sim})\).  
3. TLGD identifies longest common prefix lengths and applies stop‑gradient to negative prefix log‑probs.  
4. MLGC assembles \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}\) (average over positives) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}\) (weighted log‑sum‑exp over negatives) using block‑diagonal masking for efficiency.  
5. Backward pass updates model parameters; only non‑detached gradients flow.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- TLGD adds prefix‑computation overhead but stabilizes hierarchical generation by preventing gradient conflict on shared prefixes.  
- RDRW requires caching and quartile tracking (memory/compute) but effectively down‑weights noisy pseudo‑negatives.  
- MLGC increases batch size requirements (to include large negative pools) but improves recall on multi‑intent queries by avoiding probability squeezing.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High hallucination rate (SID structure breakdown).  
- Oscillating early‑layer gradients (indicative of prefix conflict).  
- Low Recall@K on multi‑intent queries (failure to avoid squeezing).  
- Online UCVR drop despite offline gains (misalignment between offline metrics and user satisfaction).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablation study:** Remove TLGD, RDRW, or Multi‑label SFT individually to isolate each component’s contribution.  
2. **Scaling experiment:** Evaluate RAD‑DPO vs. DPO across model sizes (0.6B, 1.7B, 8B) to test robustness to capacity.  
3. **Online A/B test:** Deploy RAD‑DPO branch in a live system with millions of users and measure UCVR lift against the baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can we replace the heuristic piecewise weight function with a learnable model (e.g., a small neural network) that dynamically assigns soft penalties based on features of the positive‑negative pair, thereby improving robustness to noisy implicit feedback?
- Instead of selecting a single “top‑tier” positive for the contrastive loss, can we design a multi‑positive contrast that jointly considers all relevant items, and does this improve recall on multi‑label queries?
- Would a soft, similarity‑based modulation of gradients on prefix tokens (instead of the hard stop‑gradient used in TLGD) better preserve useful shared semantics while preventing harmful gradient conflicts?
- Is the RAD‑DPO framework effective on other e‑commerce platforms or search domains where the item taxonomy, implicit feedback quality, and SID construction differ significantly?

## Limitations
- Missing β schedule and quartile update frequency for RDRW → difficult to replicate precisely.
- Underspecified \(y_w^*\) selection rule and negative pool configuration → global contrast sensitive to these choices.
- Evaluation solely on hierarchical SIDs; generalizability to flat ID spaces remains unproven.

## Confidence
- TLGD: High (ablation confirms; mechanism aligns with theory).
- RDRW: Medium (ablation shows benefit but critical hyperparameters underspecified).
- MLGC+Global SFT: High (consistent multi‑label Recall gains).
- Overall lift (Recall@10, UCVR): High (industrial A/B test with millions of users).

## Next Checks
1. **Prefix robustness test:** Intentionally corrupt prefix alignment (e.g., mis‑tokenize negatives) and measure TLGD’s impact on Recall.
2. **Quartile update ablation:** Compare fixed quartiles vs. EMA updates vs. per‑batch recomputation to identify a stable weighting strategy.
3. **Top‑positive selection study:** Vary \(y_w^*\) choice (random, highest model score, longest) and evaluate multi‑label recall to understand sensitivity.
