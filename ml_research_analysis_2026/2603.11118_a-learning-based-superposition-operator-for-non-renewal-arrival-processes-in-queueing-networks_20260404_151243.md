# A Learning-Based Superposition Operator for Non-Renewal Arrival Processes in Queueing Networks
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11118
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11118
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Neural operator predicts merged stream moments and autocorrelation with <2.7% second-moment error and reduces queue-length error by up to 69% in a decomposition framework.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a neural superposition operator to enable tractable analysis of queueing networks with merging non-renewal arrival flows. The operator learns a mapping from low-order moments (up to fifth order) and short-range autocorrelation descriptors of input streams to those of their merged process. Trained on exact Markovian arrival process (MAP) superpositions, it reconstructs the aggregate’s first five moments and short-lag autocorrelations with high fidelity. Across heterogeneous test regimes, second-moment errors are below 2.7%, while classical renewal approximations often exceed 100% error. When integrated into a decomposition framework for feed-forward networks, the operator reduces mean queue-length error by up to 69% relative to prior work and operates in milliseconds.

## Method Summary
The core of the method is a feed‑forward neural network (NN1) that takes as input the log‑transformed first five inter‑arrival moments and the autocorrelation coefficients (lags ≤ 2, polynomial orders ≤ 2) of two arrival streams (total 34 features). It outputs the corresponding descriptors (5 log‑moments + 8 autocorrelation values) of the superposed stream. Training data consists of 500 k pairs of MAP processes generated by three algorithms covering wide variability and dependence ranges; exact superpositions are computed via Kronecker sums. NN1 has five hidden layers (50, 50, 70, 70, 50) with ReLU activations and uses a weighted MSE loss on moments and autocorrelation. Two additional networks (NN2 for departure processes and NN3 for steady‑state distributions) extend the pipeline to full feed‑forward queueing networks. By focusing on compact statistical descriptors, the approach scales to network analysis while preserving higher‑order effects neglected by classical renewal approximations.

## Key Results
- Second‑moment prediction MAPE < 2.7% across 40 heterogeneous test regimes, whereas classical renewal approximations often exceed 100% error.
- Autocorrelation MAE < 0.032 for lags ≤ 2.
- In feed‑forward network benchmarks, mean queue‑length error reduced by up to 69% relative to Whitt and You (2022) with inference times in milliseconds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
The superposition operator learns a mapping from low‑order moments and short‑range autocorrelation to the superposed stream’s descriptors, circumventing the need for explicit process models. A neural network is trained on exact MAP superpositions (via Kronecker sums) to predict the first five moments and eight short‑lag autocorrelation coefficients of the merged process, using only the corresponding descriptors of the input streams. The core assumption is that these statistical descriptors are sufficient to reconstruct the key properties of the superposed stream that affect downstream queueing performance. Evidence: the abstract states the mapping; Section 3 formally defines the descriptor sets; Section 6.1 shows prediction accuracy saturates at \(n=5\) input moments, \(n_1=3\) lags, and \(n_2=2\) polynomial order. Break condition: if the queueing analysis requires higher‑order moments (e.g., >5th) or long‑range dependence beyond lag 2, the operator may fail to capture necessary structure.

### Mechanism 2
The learned operator substantially outperforms classical renewal‑based approximations (Whitt, Albin) in predicting the second moment of the superposed stream across heterogeneous variability and correlation regimes. The neural model directly learns complex nonlinear interactions between input streams’ variability and dependence from data, whereas classical methods reduce streams to renewal surrogates using only mean and SCV, discarding higher‑order and correlation information. The training data (MAP superpositions) spans a broad domain of realizable non‑renewal processes, enabling generalization to unseen arrival streams beyond MAPs. Evidence: abstract reports <2.7% second‑moment errors vs. >100% for renewal approximations; Section 6.2 tables show neural MAPE consistently < 2.7% while Whitt/Albin errors range from ~10% to >3900%; Section 4.1 describes the MAP generator covering wide SCV, skewness, kurtosis, and autocorrelation ranges. Break condition: deployment on arrival processes whose descriptors lie far outside the training distribution (e.g., extreme SCV > 15, very strong long‑range dependence) could lead to large prediction errors.

### Mechanism 3
The operator enables scalable decomposition‑based analysis of feed‑forward queueing networks with merging flows by preserving higher‑order variability and dependence information through the network. By providing accurate moment/autocorrelation descriptors of merged streams, the operator feeds downstream neural modules (for departure processes and steady‑state distributions) with richer statistical information than renewal surrogates, improving end‑to‑end queue‑length prediction. The compact descriptor set (5 moments, 8 autocorrelation terms) is assumed sufficient for the downstream modules (NN2, NN3) to approximate departure processes and steady‑state distributions accurately, even after multiple superposition/departure steps. Evidence: abstract reports up to 69% REM improvement over Whitt and You (2022); Section 7 shows System 1 (~69% improvement) and System 2 (~61% improvement); Section 8.2 notes controlled error accumulation in tested topologies. Break condition: in deep networks with many merging/departure stages, error propagation could amplify, leading to significant degradation in steady‑state distribution accuracy at downstream nodes.

## Foundational Learning
### Log-transformed moments
**Why needed here:** Moments span wide numerical ranges (e.g., 5th moment can be orders of magnitude larger than mean), causing training instability. Log‑transformation stabilizes gradients and reduces dynamic range.  
**Quick check:** If you omitted log‑transform, what training symptom would likely appear? Gradient explosions or slow convergence due to large output values.

### Autocorrelation descriptors (polynomial transforms)
**Why needed here:** Superposition induces nonlinear interactions between streams’ dependence structures. Linear combinations of first‑lag autocorrelations are insufficient; polynomial terms up to order 2 capture essential pairwise interactions.  
**Quick check:** Why include terms like \(\rho(k, a_1=2, a_2=1)\)? To model how, e.g., squared inter‑arrival times from one stream correlate with linear inter‑arrivals from another after superposition.

### MAPs as data generator (not deployed model)
**Why needed here:** MAPs provide an analytically exact superposition mechanism (Kronecker sum) and are dense in non‑negative point processes, allowing generation of a wide variety of ground‑truth superpositions for training.  
**Quick check:** At inference time, does the system require MAP parameters? No; only moments and autocorrelations of the input streams.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  NN1 (Superposition): Input = {log(moments up to 5), autocorr (lags ≤ 2, poly ≤ 2)} for each of two streams; Output = same descriptors for superposed stream.  
  NN2 (Departure): Input = NN1 output + service‑time moments (up to 5); Output = departure process descriptors (moments + autocorr).  
  NN3 (Steady‑state): Input = NN2 output (for each incoming stream to a node) + service‑time moments; Output = steady‑state probabilities \(p_i\) (i < 500).
- **Critical path:** For a three‑station network (System 2): External streams A/B → NN1 at station a → NN2 at a → NN1 at c (merging departures a/b) → NN3 at c. Each NN must be applied sequentially; outputs become inputs downstream.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • Descriptor compactness vs. completeness: Using only 5 moments and short‑lag autocorr enables scalability but discards tail dependence and higher‑lag correlations.  
  • Universal vs. specialized models: A single NN trained across wide SCV/correlation ranges offers generality but may have higher errors in extreme sub‑regimes compared to a domain‑specialized model.  
  • Training cost vs. inference speed: Exact MAP superposition for labeling is computationally heavy (~8 min/sample) but enables a fast (<1 ms) inference operator.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  • High SCV + strong positive correlation in both inputs → largest moment errors (up to ~2.7% in paper’s tests, but could be higher out‑of‑distribution).  
  • High utilization (ρ > 0.7) → increased REM in queue‑length prediction.  
  • Deep networks → potential error accumulation; monitor increasing SAE across stations.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Sensitivity to input descriptor dimension: For fixed test set, vary \(n\) (2–10), \(n_1\), \(n_2\) (1–5) during training; select \((n=5, n_1=3, n_2=2)\) that minimizes moment and autocorr errors on validation set (Section 5.1).  
  2. Robustness across regimes: Partition test data by SCV (<3/>3) and first‑lag autocorrelation (4 bins) for each stream; compute average PARE/MAE per regime (Tables 2‑3). Verify errors stay low and stable.  
  3. Queueing network validation: Implement System 1 (single merge) and System 2 (three‑station with two merges) using NN1+NN2+NN3 pipeline. Compare REM and SAE against simulation ground truth and Whitt’s method (Section 7). Expect ~60‑70% REM improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extending to splitting and probabilistic routing** – The current framework handles merging only; can an additional neural module accurately approximate flow splitting and enable analysis of general feed‑forward networks with both merging and routing?  
2. **Error propagation in deep networks** – How does approximation error accumulate across multiple successive superposition operations in deep topologies, and what theoretical bounds exist on error growth? The paper only evaluates up to three stations.  
3. **Generalization to real‑world non‑MAP processes** – To what extent does the operator generalize to empirical arrival streams (e.g., network traffic, hospital arrivals) whose dependence structures may lie outside the MAP family used for training?  
4. ** Incorporating uncertainty quantification** – Can the superposition operator output predictive distributions (e.g., via Bayesian neural networks or ensembles) to provide confidence intervals and stabilize recursive evaluations in deeper networks?

## Limitations
- **Descriptor sufficiency:** The set of five moments and short‑lag autocorrelation may fail to capture tail dependence or higher‑order correlations needed for extreme regimes.
- **Training data domain:** Generalization to processes with descriptors far outside the MAP training distribution (e.g., SCV > 15, very strong long‑range dependence) could yield large errors.
- **Error propagation:** In deep networks with many merges, accumulated prediction error may become significant; validation beyond three stations is lacking.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation on loss weighting α:** Train models with α = {0.1, 0.5, 0.9} to measure sensitivity of moment vs. autocorrelation accuracy; report changes in test MAPE/MAE.
2. **Autocorrelation bound enforcement:** Modify output layer with \(\tanh\) or add penalty for predictions outside \([-1,1]\); compare MAE and validation stability against original linear output.
3. **Out‑of‑distribution stress test:** Generate test pairs with SCV ∈ [15, 30] and/or strong long‑range dependence (e.g., FARIMA with H > 0.7); evaluate whether moment errors remain <5% or degrade.
