# Riemannian Langevin Dynamics: Strong Convergence of Geometric Euler-Maruyama Scheme

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.03626  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03626  
- **Reference count**: 17  
- **Primary result**: The Geometric Euler–Maruyama (GEM) method for SDEs on Riemannian manifolds achieves strong order 1/2 under uniform curvature and tubular neighborhood assumptions; for any compact manifold these hold (via Nash embedding).

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of strong convergence theory for intrinsic numerical schemes on Riemannian manifolds. While the classical Euler–Maruyama (EM) method in Euclidean space attains order 1/2, its geometric analogue—GEM, which uses the manifold’s exponential map—has resisted similar analysis. The authors resolve this by constructing an extrinsic extension of the intrinsic SDE to a well‑posed Euclidean SDE on ℝⁿ. This extension uses a tubular neighborhood and a smooth bump function to obtain globally Lipschitz coefficients. Applying the standard EM scheme to the extended SDE, they bound the pathwise discrepancy between the resulting process and the intrinsic GEM process via a Taylor expansion of the exponential map and uniform geometric bounds on the second fundamental form and reach. Under Assumption I (uniform extrinsic curvature bound) and Assumption II (uniform tubular neighborhood), together with a C¹‑bounded drift, they prove that GEM converges with strong order 1/2 for any p ≥ 1. Via the Nash embedding theorem, these conditions automatically hold on any compact Riemannian manifold. As an application, they derive a Wasserstein convergence bound for Riemannian Langevin dynamics under the Bakry–Émery curvature condition: \(W_p(\mu_\infty, \hat\mu_n) \le O(e^{-T} + h^{1/2})\), where \(h\) is the step size.

## Method Summary
The core technique is to embed the manifold into Euclidean space and to modify the drift and diffusion outside a tubular neighborhood so that the resulting SDE on ℝⁿ has smooth, globally Lipschitz coefficients. Within the tubular neighborhood the extended SDE coincides with the original intrinsic SDE (via the pushforward of the embedding). A smooth bump function ensures the extension is valid globally without altering dynamics on the manifold itself. The standard Euler–Maruyama scheme is then applied to the extended SDE, yielding a sequence in ℝⁿ. The key step is to compare these Euclidean EM iterates with the intrinsic GEM iterates (which use the Riemannian exponential map). By expanding the exponential map to first order and using uniform bounds on the second fundamental form and the reach of the embedding, the authors show that the difference between the two processes after one step is \(O(h)\). Combining this with the known strong order 1/2 of the Euclidean EM gives the same order for GEM. The drift’s \(C^1\) boundedness controls the Taylor remainder, while the uniform geometric assumptions guarantee the tubular neighborhood contains the iterates with high probability.

## Key Results
- GEM is **p‑strongly convergent** with order 1/2 for any \(1 \le p < \infty\) under Assumptions I and II and a \(C^1\)-bounded drift.
- For any **compact Riemannian manifold**, the two geometric assumptions are satisfied (via the Nash embedding), so GEM attains order 1/2 without further conditions.
- Applied to **Riemannian Langevin dynamics** under the Bakry–Émery curvature condition, the algorithm yields the Wasserstein bound \(W_p(\mu_\infty, \hat\mu_n) \le O(e^{-T} + h^{1/2})\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The extrinsic extension turns the intrinsically defined SDE into a Euclidean SDE with **globally Lipschitz coefficients**. This permits the direct application of the classical EM scheme and its well‑understood strong convergence analysis (order 1/2). The GEM method can be viewed as the restriction of this Euclidean EM process to the manifold when the initial point lies on the embedded submanifold. The **Taylor expansion** of the Riemannian exponential map—together with **uniform bounds on curvature** (second fundamental form) and the **reach** of the embedding—ensures that the geometric error introduced by replacing the exponential map with a Euclidean increment is of order \(O(h)\). Since \(O(h)\) is of higher order than \(O(\sqrt{h})\) for small \(h\), the dominant error remains the stochastic EM error, preserving the overall strong order 1/2. The uniform tubular neighborhood guarantees that EM iterates stay within the region where the extension faithfully represents the intrinsic dynamics with high probability. Thus the combination of extrinsic regularity and local geometric approximation yields the desired convergence result.

## Foundational Learning
- **Riemannian manifold**  
  *Why needed*: Provides the geometric setting (metric, Levi‑Civita connection, exponential map) for defining SDEs and GEM.  
  *Quick check*: Verify that the sphere \(S^n\) with the round metric satisfies Riemannian properties (positive‑definite metric, smoothness).

- **Stochastic differential equation on manifolds**  
  *Why needed*: The object being approximated; the intrinsic SDE uses the Laplace–Beltrami operator or Brownian motion on the manifold.  
  *Quick check*: Write the Langevin SDE on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and check that it is equivariant under coordinate changes respecting the metric; then express it intrinsically as \(dX_t = \frac{1}{2}\nabla \log \pi(X_t) dt + dW_t^M\).

- **Euler–Maruyama scheme**  
  *Why needed*: The basic time‑stepping method; its strong order 1/2 in Euclidean space is the gold standard.  
  *Quick check*: Apply EM to \(dX = \mu dt + \sigma dW\) and verify the mean‑square error scales like \(h\) over a fixed interval.

- **Geometric integrator**  
  *Why needed*: Numerical methods that preserve the manifold’s geometry (e.g., stay on the manifold). GEM is a geometric integrator using the exponential map.  
  *Quick check*: Show that one step of GEM on a point \(x \in M\) yields another point on \(M\) because \(\exp_x\) maps to \(M\).

- **Nash embedding theorem**  
  *Why needed*: Guarantees that any smooth (compact) Riemannian manifold can be isometrically embedded into some Euclidean space, satisfying Assumptions I–II automatically for compact cases.  
  *Quick check*: Confirm that the 2‑torus \(T^2\) with a flat metric admits an isometric embedding into \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (e.g., the standard donut shape).

- **Bakry–Émery curvature condition**  
  *Why needed*: Guarantees exponential convergence of the Langevin diffusion to its invariant measure; combined with discretization error gives the Wasserstein bound.  
  *Quick check*: Verify that on a compact Riemannian manifold with \(\text{Ric} \ge \kappa > 0\), the Bakry–Émery condition holds for the reference measure \(\pi \propto e^{-V}\).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Intrinsic SDE on \(M\) --(embedding + bump)--> Extended Euclidean SDE on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) --(Euler–Maruyama)--> Discrete Euclidean path --(restriction to \(M\))--> GEM iterates on \(M\)

**Critical Path**  
1. Construct an isometric embedding of \(M\) into \(\mathbb{R}^n\).  
2. Define a tubular neighborhood and a smooth bump function that is 1 on a smaller neighborhood.  
3. Build the extended drift \(\tilde{f}\) and diffusion \(\tilde{\sigma}\) that agree with the intrinsic ones when projected, and are globally Lipschitz.  
4. Apply the standard EM scheme to the extended SDE.  
5. Show that the EM iterates, when projected onto \(M\), differ from the GEM iterates by \(O(h)\) per step using Taylor expansion of \(\exp\) and curvature bounds.  
6. Combine with the Euclidean EM strong convergence (\(O(\sqrt{h})\)) to obtain GEM order 1/2.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Extrinsic vs intrinsic*: The extrinsic approach leverages the mature Euclidean theory and yields global Lipschitz coefficients, but obscures geometric insight and introduces dependence on the embedding. A purely intrinsic analysis would be more elegant but is technically challenging because of lacking global Lipschitz properties.
- *Bump function*: The choice of cutoff affects the smoothness of the extended coefficients; a smoother bump yields higher regularity (needed for \(C^1\) bounds) but may complicate the Lipschitz constants. The construction must balance these factors.
- *Assumption reliance*: The result holds under uniform extrinsic curvature and tubular neighborhood assumptions; these are automatically satisfied for compact manifolds but may be restrictive for non‑compact or singular spaces.

**Failure Signatures**  
- If Assumption I (uniform bound on second fundamental form) fails, the discrepancy between EM and GEM may grow larger than \(O(h)\), degrading strong convergence.
- If Assumption II (uniform tubular neighborhood) fails, the EM iterates could leave the region where the extension matches the intrinsic dynamics, leading to errors that accumulate faster than \(O(\sqrt{h})\).
- Numerically, one would observe that the strong error no longer scales like \(h^{1/2}\) when the step size is reduced, e.g., plotting log(error) vs. log(\(h\)) yields a slope < 0.5.

**First Experiments**  
1. Validate order 1/2 on a simple compact manifold (2‑sphere) by comparing GEM to high‑precision reference paths and measuring strong error across multiple step sizes.
2. Test the necessity of the geometric assumptions on a non‑compact manifold with unbounded curvature (hyperbolic plane) and check whether the empirical strong order drops below 1/2.
3. Compare the extrinsic‑extension implementation of GEM with a purely intrinsic scheme (using Riemannian exponential and parallel transport directly) on a torus to confirm that both achieve the same convergence rate, indicating the extrinsic detour does not affect the order.

## Limitations
- The proof relies on an extrinsic embedding to achieve global Lipschitz coefficients; it is unclear whether a purely intrinsic analysis can avoid these assumptions or yield the same order under weaker conditions.
- Assumption I (uniform extrinsic curvature) and Assumption II (uniform tubular neighborhood) may not hold for many non‑compact or singular Riemannian manifolds; their sharpness and necessity for order 1/2 remain open.
- The Wasserstein bound \(W_p \le O(e^{-T} + h^{1/2})\) for Riemannian Langevin dynamics is likely non‑optimal in \(h\); the interplay between the exponential decay of bias and the \(h^{1/2}\) discretization error could be tightened, and the dependence on the Bakry–Émery constant could be made explicit.

## Confidence
- **High**: Order 1/2 strong convergence of GEM on any compact Riemannian manifold (thanks to Nash embedding ensuring Assumptions I–II).
- **Medium**: The main theorem under Assumptions I–II with a \(C^1\)-bounded drift; the assumptions are plausible but restrict generality.
- **Low**: The specific \(W_p \le O(e^{-T} + h^{1/2})\) bound is likely non‑optimal in the step‑size exponent; the constant and regime dependence of the exponential term require further verification.

## Next Checks
1. **Assumption necessity**: Build a non‑compact Riemannian manifold violating Assumption I or II (e.g., hyperbolic space) and run numerical experiments to see if GEM’s strong order deteriorates below 1/2.
2. **Extrinsic vs intrinsic**: Implement a purely intrinsic geometric integrator (using only the manifold’s exponential map) on a sphere/torus and compare strong errors against the GEM scheme to isolate any effect of the extrinsic extension.
3. **Sharpness under BE condition**: Simulate Riemannian Langevin dynamics on a compact manifold with positive Ricci curvature, vary step size \(h\), and empirically estimate the convergence rate of \(W_p(\mu_\infty, \hat\mu_n)\); check whether \(h^{1/2}\) dominates and whether the exponential decay matches the theoretical constant.
