# PreScience: A Benchmark for Forecasting Scientific Contributions
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20459
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20459
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** PreScience introduces a holistic benchmark for forecasting scientific contributions, revealing substantial headroom: frontier LLMs achieve only moderate similarity (GPT-5 averages 5.6/10 on LACERScore) and end-to-end simulation yields synthetic corpora less diverse and novel than human-authored research.

## Executive Summary
PreScience presents a comprehensive benchmark for forecasting scientific contributions, comprising four interdependent tasks—collaborator prediction, prior work selection, contribution generation, and impact prediction—applied to a dataset of 98K AI papers. It introduces LACERScore, an LLM-based metric that better aligns with human judgments than traditional text similarity measures. Experiments with state-of-the-art LLMs show only moderate performance, and a full 12-month simulation demonstrates that synthetic research trajectories become systematically less diverse and novel over time. The benchmark highlights key challenges in modeling scientific discovery and provides a foundation for future work.

## Method Summary
The benchmark operationalizes scientific contribution forecasting as a pipeline of four generative tasks. The dataset spans 98K arXiv AI papers (Oct 2023–Oct 2025) with disambiguated authors and citation links to 502K total papers. Each target paper is described by its authors (C), key references (R), title/abstract (A), and 12-month citations (I). The historical corpus H<t contains all papers published before the target date. Tasks are evaluated with metrics: nDCG@1000 and R-precision for ranking (collaborators, references); LACERScore (1–10), ROUGE-L, BERTScore for generation; MAE and correlations for impact regression. Baselines include frequency counts, embedding fusion (GTR, Specter2, GRIT), clustering, and linear projection; LLMs (8B fine-tuned and frontier models) for generation; XGBoost for impact. LACERScore calibrates GPT-5 by generating interpolations between a key reference (score 1) and a paraphrase (score 10), then judges new pairs with few-shot examples. End-to-end simulation (Algorithm 1) chains uncontaminated models month-by-month to generate a synthetic corpus, measuring diversity via mean LACERScore to nearest neighbors within and across months.

## Key Results
- Frontier LLMs (GPT-5) average only 5.6/10 on LACERScore, indicating substantial headroom in generating human-like contributions.
- The end-to-end simulation produces a synthetic corpus that is systematically less diverse and less novel than contemporaneously human-authored research, as measured by nearest-neighbor LACERScore.
- LACERScore, the novel LLM-based metric, outperforms existing automatic metrics (ROUGE-L, BERTScore) and approximates human agreement for evaluating generated contributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark succeeds by decomposing the complex phenomenon of scientific forecasting into four tractable, interdependent subproblems that collectively capture the essential components of a research contribution. The large-scale dataset with strict temporal splitting prevents data leakage and mirrors real-world prediction constraints. LACERScore's calibration process anchors the evaluation scale to human-interpretable anchors (key reference = 1, paraphrase = 10), ensuring consistent and human-aligned judgments. The end-to-end simulation exposes how errors propagate and accumulate across the pipeline, leading to a measurable drop in diversity and novelty—this feedback loop is critical for understanding the limitations of current models. The design choices prioritize reproducibility and incremental progress while revealing the substantial gap between synthetic and human research patterns.

## Foundational Learning
**Decomposition into four tasks**  
*Why needed:* Scientific contribution is multifaceted; splitting into collaborator, prior work, contribution, and impact makes the problem computationally tractable and allows targeted model development.  
*Quick check:* Do the four tasks together reconstruct a plausible paper given historical context?

**LACERScore as evaluation metric**  
*Why needed:* Traditional text similarity (ROUGE, BERTScore) fails to capture scientific novelty and correctness; an LLM-based judge with human-like calibration can better assess contribution quality.  
*Quick check:* Does LACERScore correlate with human evaluations on a held‑in set better than automatic metrics?

**Temporal data alignment**  
*Why needed:* To simulate realistic forecasting, all inputs must be known at the time of prediction; leakage of future information invalidates results.  
*Quick check:* Are all companion papers (author histories, references) published strictly before the target paper's date?

**End-to-end simulation**  
*Why needed:* Isolating task performance does not reveal how errors compound in a full research trajectory; simulation shows emergent properties like diversity collapse.  
*Quick check:* Does the synthetic corpus over time show a decreasing trend in nearest-neighbor LACERScore relative to real papers?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
H<t → Collaborator Predictor → C_t → Prior Work Selector → R_t → Contribution Generator → A_t → Impact Predictor → I_t → H<t+1  

**Critical path:** The simulation must execute tasks in strict temporal order: first predict collaborators (C_t) from H<t, then select prior work (R_t) using H<t and possibly C_t, generate content (A_t) conditioned on C_t and R_t, predict impact (I_t) from A_t and context, then add the synthetic paper to H<t for the next month.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Unidirectional flow:* Assumes no feedback between tasks (e.g., generated content influencing prior work selection) to maintain tractability, but may oversimplify real scientific dynamics.  
- *Text-only input:* Excludes figures, tables, and equations to simplify data processing, but loses critical information often central to contributions.  
- *Citation-based impact:* Uses 12-month citation count as a proxy for influence, which is noisy and field-dependent; longer-term impact may differ.  
- *Disambiguated authors without institutions:* Omits social/economic factors that heavily influence collaboration and research direction.

**Failure signatures:**  
- LACERScore calibration drift: gold paraphrase scores deviate significantly from 10 or key reference scores from 1–2.  
- Rapid entropy drop in collaborator/prior work predictions across simulation months, indicating model collapse to a narrow set of authors/papers.  
- Impact prediction performance degrades sharply on papers published after a model's knowledge cutoff, hinting at contamination.  
- Diversity metrics (mean LACERScore to within-month neighbors) decline faster than the real corpus's monthly trend.

**Three first experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Implement frequency and embedding-fusion baselines for collaborator and prior work tasks; verify that nDCG@1000 and R-precision on the test set are within 5% of Table 2 values.  
2. **LACERScore sanity check:** For 50 random test papers, generate calibration sequences and compute scores; ensure paraphrase average ≥9.5 and key reference average ≤2.0. Then compare LACERScore against human judgments (if available) to confirm superiority over ROUGE-L/BERTScore.  
3. **Short simulation run:** Execute the end-to-end pipeline for the first 3 months (Oct–Dec 2024) using the recommended uncontaminated models; plot monthly mean LACERScore to nearest neighbors and verify it starts below the real corpus baseline and trends downward, matching Figure 4's pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can the four-stage decomposition (collaborator, prior work, contribution, impact) be extended to model the co-evolution and feedback loops between these components, rather than treating them as a fixed causal chain?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors acknowledge the simplifying assumption of temporal unidirectional ordering and note that components may co-evolve (Section 2), calling the decomposition an operational choice rather than a complete causal theory (Section 6).  
*Why unresolved:* The current factorization is necessary for tractable modeling but likely misrepresents true dynamics, where generating a contribution can reshape which prior work is seen as key, and team formation can influence problem framing.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Develop and evaluate dynamic or bidirectional graphical models allowing reciprocal dependencies; success would show improved prediction accuracy in end-to-end simulation or ability to capture observed phenomena like research theme convergence.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent do the forecasting challenges and model performance in PreScience generalize beyond the specific, fast-moving domain of recent AI research on arXiv?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors explicitly state the domain scope limitation: findings may not generalize to slower-moving fields, different authorship norms, or non-preprint venues (Section 6).  
*Why unresolved:* The dataset's patterns (rapid preprinting, numerous authors, industry involvement, skewed citations) are highly specific; success on AI papers does not guarantee performance in mathematics, organic chemistry, or humanities.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the benchmark framework in other domains (biomedical, physics, social sciences) with domain-specific criteria; comparative analysis would reveal if modeling approaches maintain relative performance and if headroom/synthetic degradation persist.

**Open Question 3:** Are the current proxies for scientific influence (Semantic Scholar's "highly influential citations") and impact (12-month citation count) sufficient to capture the true value and trajectory of a scientific contribution, or do they bias the benchmark toward certain types of research?  
*Basis in paper:* Authors admit these choices favor influence through formal citation and short horizons; negative results or conceptual advances may be disadvantaged (Section 6).  
*Why unresolved:* Citations are noisy, discipline-specific, and temporally biased; a 12-month window is extremely short for many fields, likely undervaluing contributions with slower recognition.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies comparing model predictions against alternative impact metrics (patent citations, awards, textbook mentions) years later; or incorporate human expert assessments of novelty/importance and correlate with short-term citations.

**Open Question 4:** How critical is multimodal understanding (e.g., reading and reasoning about figures, tables, and mathematical notation) to accurate scientific contribution forecasting, and can current LLM-based models effectively leverage such information?  
*Basis in paper:* The conclusion lists multimodal information as a potential dataset enrichment (Section 7); current model uses only title/abstract (Section 4.3).  
*Why unresolved:* Core advances are often encoded uniquely in figures, equations, tables; missing these may cause fundamental misunderstanding.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Extend contribution generation to include extracted representations of figures/tables; measure improvement in LACERScore/human evaluation beyond text-only context gains.

**Open Question 5:** How do institutional, social, and economic factors (like lab affiliation, funding sources, and geographical proximity), when incorporated into the forecasting model, change the accuracy of predictions for collaboration, novelty, and impact?  
*Basis in paper:* Authors repeatedly note missing factors: institutions, conferences, funding are abstracted away (Section 2); limitations include institutional incentives, funding, venue selection, social dynamics (Section 6); impact statement speculates low collaborator prediction accuracy may reflect lack of institutional proximity signals.  
*Why unresolved:* Real team formation and research direction are heavily influenced by non-content factors (colocation, grants, conferences); omitting these is a major gap.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Enrich dataset with institutional metadata, funding acknowledgments, geography; retrain baselines to quantify predictive power; significant performance gains, especially for collaborator prediction and novelty, would resolve.

## Limitations
- Exact model versions, preprocessing, and generation parameters are unspecified, hindering faithful replication of baselines.
- LACERScore relies on a specific GPT-5 API version; score scale may drift with model updates, affecting comparability.
- End-to-end simulation assumes fixed candidate pool sizes and sampling strategies; deviations can artificially cause diversity collapse.
