# Beyond NNGP: Large Deviations and Feature Learning in Bayesian Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22925
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22925
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Large-deviation theory provides variational rate functions on predictors for wide Bayesian neural networks, enabling data-dependent kernel selection beyond the fixed-kernel NNGP limit.

## Executive Summary
Wide Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) with Gaussian weights and Lipschitz activations satisfy a large-deviation principle (LDP) in the infinite-width limit, where the log-density of network outputs scales with the width *n*. This LDP assigns a variational rate function *I_prior(h)* to any predictor *h* defined on a finite input set. Conditioning on data via a tempered likelihood adds the empirical loss to this rate, so the posterior concentrates around functions minimizing *I_prior(h) + loss(h)*. Critically, *I_prior(h)* is computed by jointly optimizing over *h* and internal layer kernels *κ*, allowing the posterior to select kernels adapted to the data—unlike the NNGP limit where kernels are fixed a priori. The authors derive explicit recursive formulas for the kernel rates via Legendre transforms of conditional log-moments, and show that nonlinear activations (e.g. ReLU) induce non-quadratic prior rates, leading to heavy tails and non-Gaussian posterior behavior. Numerical experiments on shallow networks confirm: (i) non-quadratic growth of prior rates, (ii) posterior deformation and non-zero kernel gaps, (iii) LDP-MAP predictions deviating from NNGP posterior means, and (iv) empirical tail decay rates converging to the LDP predictions as width increases. The work thus reveals a variational pathway to feature learning in BNNs beyond the NNGP regime.

## Method Summary
The method applies large-deviation theory to wide BNNs by treating the network output vector *h* on a finite set of inputs as a random variable satisfying an LDP with speed *n*. The prior rate *I_prior(h)* is expressed as a nested minimization over internal kernels: *I_prior(h) = inf_κ [½||h||_κ² + I_{L-1}(κ)]*, where *I_{L-1}(κ)* is recursively defined via layerwise costs *J_σ(κ'|κ)* (Legendre-Fenchel transforms of conditional log-MGFs of kernels). Bayesian conditioning adds the loss term, yielding the posterior rate *I_post(h) = I_prior(h) + ½∑_D ||h(x_i)-y_i||²* (up to constants). The maximum a posteriori (MAP) predictor is obtained by minimizing *I_post* over *h*. In practice, exchanging the min over *h* and *κ* produces a kernel-only objective (Equation 4.9) that reveals a data-dependent kernel selection penalty. For shallow networks, the authors compute *J_σ* either analytically or via Monte Carlo, then optimize the resulting variational problems to obtain rate functions and LDP-MAP predictions. The approach is validated by comparing with finite-width Monte Carlo simulations under an *n*-tempered likelihood, which should concentrate according to the LDP.

## Key Results
- Prior and posterior output rate functions display non-quadratic growth for ReLU networks (approximately linear tails), and distort visibly when conditioning on data (Figures 1–2).
- LDP-MAP predictors differ systematically from NNGP posterior means, with a non-zero gap between the posterior-optimal kernel and the NNGP kernel, confirming adaptive feature learning (Figures 3, 6).
- Empirical tail probabilities of network outputs converge to the theoretical LDP rates as width grows; posterior samples under *n*-tempered scaling concentrate around LDP-optimal values, while NNGP scaling yields a broader Gaussian (Figures 7–8).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: LDP turns Bayesian inference into variational optimization.** In the large-width limit, the distribution of network outputs satisfies an LDP with speed *n*. Conditioning on data corresponds to adding the empirical loss to the prior rate (a change of measure). Prediction (integrating over test outputs) reduces to minimization over the test output because of the contraction principle. Thus the LDP-MAP predictor is simply the minimizer of the posterior rate. This relies on the network having Gaussian weights and Lipschitz activations so that the Gärtner–Ellis theorem applies.

**Mechanism 2: Joint optimization over predictors and kernels.** The prior rate *I_prior(h)* couples *h* to the internal kernels via a nested infimum. After conditioning, the posterior rate becomes *inf_κ [ loss(h) + ½||h||_κ² + I_{L-1}(κ) ]*. Exchanging the order of minimization yields a kernel selection penalty that includes a term *½ y_Dᵀ (κ_DD + I)^{-1} y_D*, encouraging kernels that balance rarity (via *I_{L-1}*) and data fit. Consequently the posterior kernel can differ from the NNGP kernel, enabling the network to learn features from data.

**Mechanism 3: Non-quadratic prior rates from deep nonlinear architectures.** The layerwise cost *J_σ(κ'|κ)* is the Legendre transform of the conditional log-MGF of the next-layer kernel given the previous. For nonlinear activations (ReLU, tanh) this cost is non-quadratic, and repeated application through layers yields a prior output rate that is not quadratic. For shallow ReLU nets the prior rate grows linearly for large outputs; for linear activations with depth *L*>1 it scales as |*y*|^{2/(L+1)}. Such heavy tails persist in the posterior and explain the observed deviations from Gaussian (quadratic) approximations.

  *Why needed here:* The prior rate function *I_prior(h)* acts as a data-independent penalty on predictor functions, arising solely from the random architecture’s law. This links the Bayesian prior to generalization without explicit constraints and quantifies the “complexity” of a function in terms of atypical kernel configurations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Network specification (depth *L*, dimensions, width *n*, activation σ, Gaussian weights with LeCun scaling) → Finite input set *X* (training + test) → Layerwise kernels κ^(ℓ) ∈ S_{|X|}^+ defined by empirical second moments → Prior LDP: compute layerwise rate functions I^(ℓ)(κ) via recursion I^(ℓ)(κ') = inf_κ [J_σ(κ'|κ) + I^(ℓ-1)(κ)] → Output rate I_prior(h) = inf_κ [½||h||_κ² + I_{L-1}(κ)] → Posterior rate I_post(h) = I_prior(h) + ½∑_D ||h(x_i)-y_i||² → MAP predictor: argmin_h I_post(h) (or exchange min to get kernel-only objective).

**Critical path:**
1. Choose a finite set *X* and compute the deterministic NNGP kernels κ₀^(ℓ) for each layer.
2. For a grid of kernel matrices κ, evaluate the conditional cost J_σ(κ'|κ) using analytical formulas or Monte Carlo estimation of the conditional log-MGF.
3. Solve the recursive variational problem to obtain the prior kernel rate I_{L-1}(κ) as a function on kernel space.
4. For a candidate output function *h* (or just its values on *X*), compute I_prior(h) by minimizing over κ; alternatively, exchange the minimizations to derive a kernel-only objective (Equation 4.9).
5. Incorporate the loss term, re-optimize to obtain the posterior kernel κ* and the LDP-MAP predictor.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Activation choice:** ReLU yields non-convex kernel costs J_σ, potentially making I_{L-1}(κ) non-convex for *L*>1; tanh is smoother but still non-quadratic.
- **Kernel representation:** Parameterize κ via Cholesky factors to maintain positive semidefiniteness; avoid near-singular matrices (det≈0) that cause numerical instability.
- **Width scaling:** The LDP is asymptotic in *n*; for finite *n* the rate functions approximate log-densities up to constants. The *n*-tempered likelihood must match the theoretical scaling to observe concentration.
- **Dimensionality:** Complexity grows with |*X*| (number of input points), not with input dimension *d_in*. Large datasets therefore require approximations or batched computation.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Kernel gap ≈ 0** everywhere: posterior selects the NNGP kernel, indicating no feature learning. Possible causes: linear activation, depth=1, or zero training targets.
- **Non-convergent optimization:** Gradient norms remain high, Cholesky diagonal entries approach zero (ill-conditioned), or optimizer oscillates. May require more samples for J_σ, jitter, or switching optimizer (Adam → L‑BFGS).
- **Monte Carlo mismatch:** Empirical tail decay rates do not approach theoretical I_prior(y) as width increases; could be due to insufficient MC samples, non-Gaussian weights, or violation of LDP assumptions.
- **Posterior samples too broad:** Under *n*-tempered scaling the posterior should be sharply concentrated. If standard deviation remains large (like NNGP scaling), the tempering is incorrect or width is insufficient for LDP regime.

**First three experiments:**
1. **Prior/posterior rate computation:** Compute I_prior(y) and I_post(y) on a 1D grid for ReLU/tanh activations; verify non-quadratic shapes and posterior deformation (reproduces Figures 1–2).
2. **LDP vs NNGP comparison:** Optimize the kernel-level objective to obtain the LDP-MAP predictor across a test grid; compare with NNGP posterior mean and compute kernel gap ||κ* – κ₀||/||κ₀|| (reproduces Figures 3/6).
3. **Monte Carlo validation:** For widths n∈{32,64,128,256}, simulate finite-width networks, estimate tail probabilities P(√n h(x_test) > y), and compare to I_prior(y); also sample *n*-tempered posterior (e.g., with MALA) and compare histogram to exp(–n I_post(y)) (reproduces Figures 7–8).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scalability to deep networks and large data:** Can the LDP rate functions be computed efficiently for deep architectures and large datasets, and do they remain accurate? The current experiments are limited to shallow (one hidden layer) networks and small synthetic data; extending to deeper nets and realistic datasets is an open challenge.
- **Comparison with standard BNN inference:** How do LDP-based predictions (both MAP and sampled) compare empirically to NNGP regression, variational inference, and MCMC in terms of predictive performance, uncertainty calibration, and computational cost? Systematic benchmarking is needed.
- **Extension to structured architectures:** Does the LDP framework extend to convolutional neural networks or transformers? The analysis currently assumes fully connected networks with i.i.d. Gaussian weights; weight sharing and other structures require new theoretical developments.
- **PAC-Bayes generalization bounds:** Can the LDP rate function be used to derive non-vacuous, data-dependent PAC-Bayes bounds for wide BNNs? The rate function resembles a KL divergence on predictor functions, but a formal translation into tight generalization guarantees remains to be established.

## Limitations
- **Theoretical assumptions:** LDP derivation requires Gaussian weights and Lipschitz activations; it does not cover non-Gaussian priors or discrete activations like ReLU (though ReLU is Lipschitz, the analysis may have subtleties). The extension to broader settings is unknown.
- **Non-convex optimization:** The joint minimization over *h* and *κ* is non-convex in general; while shallow networks appear tractable, global optimality for deeper architectures is not proven, and the optimization may get stuck in local minima.
- **Empirical scope:** Experimental validation is restricted to one-hidden-layer MLPs with widths up to 256 and small synthetic datasets. Scalability to deeper networks, larger widths, and real-world data remains unexplored. Additionally, the MALA sampling and optimizer hyperparameters are not fully reported, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- LDP theory for Gaussian, finite-input BNNs: **High** confidence (rigorous proof).
- Global optimality of kernel–predictor minimization for depth >1: **Low** confidence (non-convex, no proof).
- Empirical validation of LDP predictions on shallow nets: **Medium** confidence (results align with theory but limited scale and some implementation details missing).
- Scalability to deep architectures: **Low** confidence (no evidence).
- Exact reproducibility given documentation: **Low** confidence (missing hyperparameters, bias handling specifics).

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness of rate computation:** Recompute *J_σ* and the kernel optimization using alternative estimators (analytical formulas for ReLU if available, varied MC sample sizes) to confirm the stability of rate-function shapes and tail exponents.
2. **Depth scaling:** For depths *L*=2 and *L*=3, compute posterior rate functions and kernel gaps; check whether feature learning (measured by non-zero kernel gap) intensifies with depth as predicted by the theory.
3. **Larger-scale sampling:** Run simulations with widths *n*=512 and 1024 using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo under the *n*-tempered likelihood; compare tail decay rates and posterior concentration to LDP predictions, and verify that the LDP-MAP remains a good approximation of the posterior mode.
