# LAD: Learning Advantage Distribution for Reasoning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20132  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20132  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: LAD improves Avg@32 to 40.08 and CodeForces percentile to 82.50% while matching GRPO’s training cost.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning (RL) for LLM reasoning often collapses to a single high-reward trajectory because the objective maximizes expected advantage, overfitting to dominant signals and suppressing valid alternatives. LAD addresses this by replacing advantage maximization with a distribution-matching objective: it minimizes an f-divergence between the policy-induced distribution over responses and an advantage-induced target distribution derived from the advantages. This yields a gradient that increases likelihood for high-advantage responses while adaptively limiting overconfident probability growth, preventing mode collapse without needing auxiliary entropy regularization. Experiments on a 50-arm bandit, six math, and three code benchmarks with Qwen2.5-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B show LAD outperforms GRPO (Avg@32 40.08 vs 37.28) and sets a new state-of-the-art CodeForces percentile (82.50% vs 79.20%). Training cost remains comparable.

## Method Summary
LAD operates on a set of sampled completions for a given prompt. Using a reward model, advantages are computed (reward minus baseline) for each completion. The advantages define a target probability distribution via a softmax (with temperature). Instead of maximizing the expected advantage, the policy distribution is trained to minimize an f-divergence (e.g., KL, χ²) to this target. The resulting gradient has an adaptive form: it increases the probability of high-advantage, low-probability completions, while for completions already favored by the policy the gradient is dampened because the target-to-policy ratio approaches one. This automatically regulates the growth of dominant modes, preserving diversity without an explicit entropy term. The method integrates seamlessly into existing RL pipelines like GRPO, replacing the advantage-weighted likelihood update with the divergence minimization.

## Key Results
- In a 50-arm bandit with a bimodal advantage distribution, LAD recovers both modes while GRPO collapses to a single arm.
- Across six math and three code benchmarks, LAD with Qwen2.5-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B achieves Avg@32 = 40.08 versus GRPO’s 37.28.
- LAD attains a CodeForces percentile of 82.50% (best baseline 79.20%), with higher lexical diversity (distinct-3: 0.3498 vs 0.3315) and logical diversity judged by GPT-4 (2.58 vs 2.10).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The f-divergence gradient can be expressed as an expectation of a weighted score function, where the weight is the ratio of target to policy probability for each completion. When a completion has high advantage but low policy probability, the ratio is large, giving a strong push to increase its likelihood. Conversely, for completions already probable, the ratio is near one, so the update is neutral or weakly negative, preventing runaway dominance. This adaptive reweighting acts as a trust region effect: the policy is encouraged to match the shape of the advantage-induced distribution but can deviate without penalty as long as the divergence stays low. As a result, multiple high-advantage modes are retained, alleviating mode collapse inherently, unlike standard advantage maximization that amplifies the highest-advantage signal regardless of other good options.

- **Policy Gradient Methods**  
  *Why needed*: Provide the basic framework for adjusting the LLM policy based on reward signals.  
  *Quick check*: Is the gradient estimate derived from sampled returns or advantages? Yes.

- **Advantage Estimation**  
  *Why needed*: Reduces variance of policy gradients by subtracting a baseline (e.g., value function).  
  *Quick check*: Does the algorithm compute an advantage as reward minus baseline? Yes.

- **Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)**  
  *Why needed*: Ensures stable policy updates by limiting divergence between successive policies.  
  *Quick check*: Is there a constraint or penalty on the KL divergence between old and new policies? Often yes.

- **f‑Divergence**  
  *Why needed*: A family of divergences (e.g., KL, χ², JS) used to measure distributional discrepancy in a flexible manner.  
  *Quick check*: Is the objective expressed as an expectation of a convex function of the likelihood ratio? Yes.

- **Mode Collapse in RL**  
  *Why needed*: Describes the phenomenon where the policy concentrates on a single high-reward trajectory, ignoring other viable strategies.  
  *Quick check*: Does the policy output show low diversity despite multiple rewarding options? Yes.

- **Entropy Regularization**  
  *Why needed*: Explicitly maintains exploration by penalizing low entropy in the policy distribution.  
  *Quick check*: Is an entropy term added to the loss to encourage randomness? Often.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Policy Model (LLM) → Sample Completions → Advantage Estimator → Target Distribution Builder → f‑Divergence Loss → Policy Update → Policy Model  
(Additionally, a baseline estimator/vvalue model may be used within Advantage Estimator.)

### Critical Path
1. For a batch of prompts, the policy generates multiple completions per prompt.  
2. A reward model (or environment) assigns a scalar reward to each completion; a baseline (e.g., value function) is subtracted to obtain advantages.  
3. Advantages are transformed into a normalized probability distribution (softmax).  
4. The policy computes log‑probabilities of the sampled completions.  
5. The f‑divergence loss between the policy distribution and target distribution is computed (often via a dual form).  
6. Gradients flow back to the policy parameters, which are updated (e.g., via Adam).  
7. Optionally, the target distribution is recomputed each iteration based on fresh advantages.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Choice of f‑divergence**: KL yields strong matching but can be brittle; χ² or JS offer different penalty shapes affecting diversity vs. performance.  
- **Computational cost**: Exact f‑divergence may require full support alignment; approximations (e.g., using sampled completions) reduce cost but introduce bias.  
- **Discrete outputs**: Token‑level distributions lead to sequence‑level dependencies; LAD operates on whole‑sequence advantages, which simplifies but ignores intra‑sequence token contributions.  
- **Adaptive suppression**: While preventing over‑dominance, may slow convergence if high‑advantage completions are initially very low probability.

### Failure Signatures
- **Persistent mode collapse**: Target distribution entropy is too low or f‑divergence is insufficiently weighted, leading to one dominant mode.  
- **Gradient explosion**: Large likelihood ratios for rare completions cause unstable updates; clipping or trust region may be needed.  
- **Mismatched scales**: Advantages not normalized can produce target distribution with extreme values, causing numerical issues.  
- **Policy degradation**: If the target distribution is estimated from a noisy or biased reward model, LAD may inherit those flaws without correction.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to Qwen2.5-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B on math/code tasks; generalization to other model families or domains remains unverified.  
- The method assumes accurate, low‑bias advantage estimates; robustness to noisy or reward‑hacked advantages is not studied.  
- Diversity metrics rely on automated counts and GPT‑4 judgments, which may introduce systematic biases.
