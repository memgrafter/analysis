# Crashing Waves vs. Rising Tides: Preliminary Findings on AI Automation from Thousands of Worker Evaluations of Labor Market Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01363  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01363  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The success-duration curve is flat, indicating rising-tide automation, not crashing waves.

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes over 17,000 human evaluations of 3,000+ text-based labor tasks to test two patterns of AI automation: sudden, narrow “crashing waves” versus broad, gradual “rising tides.” Using logistic regression, the authors find a flat relationship between AI success rates and human task duration, supporting the rising‑tide pattern. Frontier models improved rapidly, doubling the feasible task duration every 3.8 months, and extrapolations suggest most text tasks could reach 80–95% success by 2029. Notably, larger models boost short‑task performance disproportionately, while newer models improve evenly across task lengths. These findings contrast with some prior benchmarks and highlight the need to consider gradual capability expansion when assessing AI’s labor market impact.

## Method Summary
The study uses a dataset of expert evaluations for text tasks derived from O*NET. Each task instance (≤150 words) was completed by 40+ LLMs, and domain experts rated the output on a 1–9 scale. The primary binary outcome is “minimally sufficient” acceptance (score ≥7). Logistic regressions model acceptance probability as a function of log₁₀(task duration in hours) and model release date, with standard errors clustered by participant. Frontier models are identified per quarter to estimate time trends, and separate regressions explore heterogeneity by job family, model size, and vintage. Projections of future success rates rely on a constant slope assumption and linear improvement in release-date coefficients.

## Key Results
- The success‑duration logistic curve is flat (β ≈ -0.3), meaning longer tasks do not become disproportionately harder for AI—consistent with broad, gradual “rising tide” capability gains.
- Frontier models show exponential progress: feasible task duration doubles every 3.8 months; extrapolating to 2029 yields 80–95% success on most text tasks at minimally sufficient quality.
- Larger models (>100B parameters) improve short‑task success more than long tasks, causing an outward rotation of the success‑duration curve; newer model releases shift the curve upward in parallel across all durations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** AI capability improvements are broad and gradual (rising tide) rather than concentrated on specific task durations (crashing waves).
- **Mechanism:** Newer model releases improve performance across both short and long tasks in an approximately parallel shift of the logistic success‑duration curve, indicating general capability gains not tied to solving a specific complexity class.
- **Core assumption:** Task duration (log) is a meaningful proxy for the serial depth of critical steps required (see Section 4.2). A flat slope means longer tasks do not become disproportionately harder.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...the success‑duration curve is flat, indicating rising‑tide automation, not crashing waves."
  - [section 2.2, Figure 5b]: "comparing newer versus older models again produces an approximately parallel shift."
  - [section 4.2]: Micro‑foundation linking logistic slope to serial coupling parameter γₔ/σ.
- **Break condition:** If future data shows a statistically significant time‑varying slope (β changes over time) or steepening slopes for newer models, the rising‑tide pattern would weaken.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Larger models provide disproportionate benefits on shorter‑duration tasks compared to longer ones.
- **Mechanism:** Scaling model parameters improves performance on tasks with lower serial depth (shorter duration) more than on deeply coupled long tasks, causing an outward rotation of the success‑duration curve for larger models.
- **Core assumption:** Serial complexity is the primary bottleneck for long tasks; scaling helps overcome local decision noise but not the fundamental difficulty of managing many coupled steps.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2, Figure 5a]: "large models’ performance advantage is greatest for short tasks and attenuates as task duration increases."
  - [Table A.1–A.3]: Negative interaction coefficients for log(duration)×Large.
- **Break condition:** If larger models show parallel shifts instead of rotation (no significant interaction), scaling would be broadly beneficial regardless of task length.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Exponential capability growth (doubling feasible task duration every ~3.8 months) has been sustained over the observation period.
- **Mechanism:** Linear improvements in model release date (Rₘ) in log‑odds space translate to multiplicative growth in the sustainable serial horizon (10^ρ) and thus to exponential expansion of addressable task duration at fixed success rates.
- **Core assumption:** The logistic slope (β) remains constant over time; progress is a pure intercept shift in log‑odds (Eq. 2).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2, Figure 6b]: Implied doubling time of 3.8 months from pooled frontier model trend.
  - [Figure 7]: Projections based on linear trend in release date.
- **Break condition:** If the release‑date coefficient δ diminishes, or if slope β changes over time (Table A.4 shows no significant interaction, but future data could), exponential growth would slow or change form.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Logistic regression for bounded probabilities.**  
  Why needed: Success rates (0–100%) are bounded; logistic link maps linear predictor to probability. The slope β captures how log‑odds decay with log task duration.  
  Quick check: Why can't we just regress success rate on log(duration) with OLS? What property of the outcome makes logistic appropriate?

- **Concept: Task duration as a complexity proxy (serial coupling).**  
  Why needed: The model posits that longer human duration implies more sequentially dependent steps (N ~ T^γ). The slope β = -γ/σ links duration to failure probability.  
  Quick check: Under the micro‑foundation, what would a steeper (more negative) β imply about tasks in that job family?

- **Concept: Distinguishing task automation from occupational impact.**  
  Why needed: Partial task automation does not directly map to job loss; Autor & Thompson (2025) show task embedding in occupations matters for wage/employment effects.  
  Quick check: Give one reason why a task being automatable (high AI success) might not reduce demand for the human occupation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Task corpus -> Instance generation -> LLM response collection -> Human evaluation -> Regression engine -> Projection module

- **Critical path:**  
  Task selection (O*NET filtering) -> Instance generation (GPT-5, 6 vignettes/task) -> Model response generation (40+ LLMs) -> Expert evaluation (1–9 rating) -> Data cleaning (attention/experience checks) -> Regression estimation (pooled, by family/size/vintage) -> Frontier trend analysis -> Future projection.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Binary outcome (≥7) vs. multi‑level: Binary simplifies interpretation but discards quality gradations.  
  - Fixed task duration vs. evaluator‑reported: Reported duration captures real effort but introduces noise; O*NET times are less accurate.  
  - Pooling across domains vs. separate: Pooling increases power but masks family heterogeneity.  
  - Pilot vs. main data timing: Pre‑ vs. post‑evaluation time reporting differs; paper uses composite measure.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Non‑flat baseline slope → evidence for crashing waves.  
  - Significant time×duration interaction → slope changing over time.  
  - High exclusion rate (>34%) → bias toward easier‑to‑survey occupations.  
  - Position bias in non‑randomized LLM display → distorted acceptance rates.  
  - Task instances failing realism checks (>2/task) → poor instance generation.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Figure 4: Fit logistic regression of acceptance≥7 on log₁₀(duration); verify flat slope (~-0.3).  
  2. Test job‑family heterogeneity: Regress acceptance on log(duration)×family dummies; identify families with statistically different slopes.  
  3. Decompose improvement channels: Replicate Figure 5 using model size (>100B?) and vintage indicators; test interaction significance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **OQ1:** How will the complete dataset covering all 900+ occupations, especially under‑represented blue‑collar jobs, alter the estimated success‑duration relationship and job‑family slopes?  
  *Basis:* Current sample is preliminary and under‑represents blue‑collar work; full collection is ongoing.

- **OQ2:** What are the quantitative “last‑mile” implementation costs and other task‑specific constraints that prevent high LLM success rates from translating into real‑world automation?  
  *Basis:* Success rates ≠ automation shares due to adoption barriers (implementation costs, regulations); future work will examine these.

- **OQ3:** Why do the success‑duration curves appear flat (rising tide) in this study, whereas METR (2025) finds steeper slopes (crashing waves)? Is the discrepancy due to task environment differences (real‑world non‑deterministic vs. deterministic benchmarks), model pooling, or benchmark coverage?  
  *Basis:* Authors contrast with METR/Kwa et al., propose explanations but note it remains unresolved.

- **OQ4:** What is the relative contribution of model size increases versus model vintage improvements to overall AI capability growth, and why do larger models disproportionately improve short‑duration tasks?  
  *Basis:* Paper shows larger models have steeper slopes (outward rotation) while newer models show parallel shifts; contributions are not yet decomposed.

## Limitations
- Data not publicly available; replication depends on author sharing.  
- Sample bias: tasks pre‑filtered for LLM time‑saving potential; expert raters may overrepresent some occupations.  
- Extrapolations rely on constant slope and parallel shifts unverified beyond 2025.
