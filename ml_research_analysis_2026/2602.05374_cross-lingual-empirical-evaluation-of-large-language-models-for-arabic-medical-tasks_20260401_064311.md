# Cross-Lingual Empirical Evaluation of Large Language Models for Arabic Medical Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05374
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05374
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: Except for DeepSeek-V3.2, all evaluated LLMs show a significant accuracy gap between Arabic and English medical QA, which widens with question length and difficulty, and is exacerbated by higher token fragmentation and free-form generation requirements.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why large language models (LLMs) underperform on Arabic medical question answering compared to English. Using the parallel MedAraBench dataset (Arabic and machine-translated English), the authors evaluate several open-source models. They find a consistent Arabic-English performance gap across most models, which grows for longer and more difficult questions. Tokenization analysis reveals Arabic text suffers from greater subword fragmentation, increasing computational load. Free-form text generation widens the gap further, and model confidence scores are negatively correlated with correctness in both languages. The study highlights the need for language-aware design in medical LLMs and provides a diagnostic framework for cross-lingual evaluation.

## Method Summary
The study employs a cross-lingual evaluation framework using MedAraBench, an Arabic medical multiple-choice question (MCQ) dataset (19,894 train, 4,989 test samples across 19 specialties) and its automatically generated English translation. A diverse set of open-source LLMs—both general-purpose (LLaMA, Mistral, DeepSeek) and medically adapted (Meditron, Med42, MedGemma)—are evaluated in zero- and few-shot settings. Four prompt templates are used: (1) letter-select MCQ, (2) free-form text generation, (3) confidence reporting, and (4) explanation generation. Primary metrics are accuracy (option label match) and token-level sequence similarity for text generation. Secondary analyses quantify tokenization fragmentation (tokens/word, single-character tokens) and correlate confidence scores with correctness. All Arabic inputs are paired with English system prompts.

## Key Results
- Most models show a significant Arabic-English accuracy gap in MCQ (e.g., Med42-70B: 33.6% vs 53.2%, Δ19.6 pp); only DeepSeek-V3.2 performs equally.
- Arabic text undergoes higher token fragmentation (2.4 tokens/word vs. 1.6 for English) and more single-character tokens.
- Free-form text generation increases the Arabic-English gap (Δ10.9 pp for token-level similarity).
- Model confidence is negatively correlated with correctness (Arabic: r = -0.56, English: r = -0.50), and explanation prompting often reduces accuracy or creates reasoning-label misalignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Tokenization Fragmentation as a Structural Constraint
- **Claim**: Arabic medical text experiences higher subword fragmentation under common LLM tokenizers compared to English, leading to longer effective input sequences and reduced robustness as question complexity increases.
- **Mechanism**: Tokenizers like BPE, trained predominantly on English or mixed-corpora, produce more tokens per Arabic word (2.4 vs. 1.6) and more single-character tokens. This increases the computational load on the model’s context window and may degrade its ability to maintain coherent representations across longer, more complex medical queries.
- **Core assumption**: Tokenization quality directly impacts the model’s capacity to process and reason over input sequences, especially when those sequences are linguistically complex or domain-specific.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Tokenization analysis exposes structural fragmentation in Arabic medical text."
  - [section 4.4] Provides quantitative fragmentation statistics across multiple models, showing consistent higher `tokens/word` and `single-character token` rates for Arabic.
- **Break condition**: If using an Arabic-optimized tokenizer (like CAMeLBERT) on the same models eliminates or significantly reduces the Arabic-English performance gap, especially on longer questions, then fragmentation is a primary causal factor. If the gap persists, other mechanisms dominate.

### Mechanism 2: Task Complexity Amplification of Linguistic Representation Gaps
- **Claim**: The performance disparity between Arabic and English is not static but widens systematically with increasing question length, educational difficulty, and specialty nuance, indicating that Arabic representations are less robust under cognitive and linguistic load.
- **Mechanism**: Simpler or shorter questions can be answered with surface-level pattern matching or memorized facts that survive tokenization noise. As questions become longer (more text to process) or harder (requiring finer-grained reasoning), the accumulated representation instability from fragmented tokens and reduced effective context disproportionately harms Arabic performance.
- **Core assumption**: Model capacity is finite, and linguistic inefficiencies (like fragmentation) consume a larger share of that capacity for Arabic inputs, leaving less for complex reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "This gap widens with question length and difficulty."
  - [section 4.2] Figures 1 & 2 show accuracy degradation trends as length and difficulty increase, with the Arabic-English gap growing larger for longer/harder questions for both DeepSeek-V3.2 (stable) and Med42-70B (large gap).
- **Break condition**: If performance gaps are uniform across all question lengths and difficulty levels (no interaction effect), then complexity amplification is not a key driver. If gaps are only present on long/hard questions, the mechanism holds.

### Mechanism 3: Output Format Sensitivity and Alignment Constraints
- **Claim**: The measured performance gap is partially an artifact of evaluation format. Free-form text generation (vs. constrained letter selection) reveals larger discrepancies, suggesting Arabic outputs are less stable in lexical and morphological realization under open-ended generation.
- **Mechanism**: Multiple-choice (letter selection) evaluates the model’s ability to map a representation to a discrete label, which can be resilient to some input noise. Free-form text generation requires precise morphological conjugation, lexical choice, and syntactic correctness in Arabic, exposing weaknesses in the model’s generative alignment for the language.
- **Core assumption**: Evaluation metrics conflate reasoning correctness with linguistic fluency. A model may have the correct medical knowledge but fail to express it in a way that matches the gold Arabic text string exactly.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Free-form text generation increases gaps (Δ10.9 pp)."
  - [section 4.3] Shows token-level sequence match accuracy for free-form text has a larger Arabic-English gap (Δ10.92 pp) than the letter-based MCQ gap for the same model (Med42-70B: Δ19.62 pp in Table 1, but note different metrics). The text-match gap is substantial and larger than for some models in the MCQ task.
- **Break condition**: If the Arabic-English gap is identical under both letter-based and exact text-match evaluation (after controlling for length), then output format is not a major contributor. If free-form generation eliminates the gap, it would contradict the claim.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Subword Tokenization and Morphological Complexity**
  - **Why needed here**: The core mechanism (1) hinges on how tokenizers split Arabic words. Understanding BPE/WordPiece algorithms and how they handle agglutinative/morphologically rich languages is essential to interpret the fragmentation metrics.
  - **Quick check question**: Why might a tokenizer produce a high rate of single-character tokens for Arabic medical text, and how would that differ from English?
- **Concept: Cross-Lingual Diagnostic Evaluation Design**
  - **Why needed here**: The paper’s methodology (RQ1) relies on a parallel Arabic-English dataset to isolate language effects from content. This requires understanding the pitfalls of translation, the importance of item-level pairing, and the difference between aggregate score comparison and interaction analysis.
  - **Quick check question**: What are the critical assumptions and potential confounders when using an automatically translated test set for cross-lingual comparison?
- **Concept: Calibration and Confidence Estimation in LLMs**
  - **Why needed here**: Mechanism 5 (confidence/explanation unreliability) is a key finding. Interpreting the negative correlation between confidence and accuracy requires knowledge of calibration (e.g., Expected Calibration Error) and the difference between model certainty and factual correctness.
  - **Quick check question**: What does a negative correlation between a model’s self-reported confidence and its accuracy imply about the model’s internal "certainty" signal?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Data Input (MedAraBench Arabic + English) -> Model Zoo (LLaMA, DeepSeek, Mistral, Meditron, Med42, MedGemma) -> Prompting Engine (letter-select, text-gen, confidence, explanation templates) -> Metrics Engine (accuracy, sequence match, tokenization stats, confidence-accuracy correlation) -> Analysis Modules (regression by length/difficulty/specialty, correlation analysis)
- **Critical path**: 1) Validate parallel dataset alignment → 2) Run baseline accuracy experiments (Table 1) → 3) Conduct tokenization analysis on a fixed sample (Table 2) → 4) Run free-form generation and text-match scoring → 5) Collect and correlate confidence scores → 6) Analyze performance by question properties (length, difficulty, specialty).
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Quantization**: 4-bit NF4 for 70B models enables evaluation but may subtly affect performance or tokenization sensitivity; full precision used for smaller models creates a confound if comparing across sizes.
  - **Prompt Language**: Using English system prompts for Arabic inputs (for stability) creates a mixed-language input scenario, which differs from a native-language prompt setup and may interact with the mechanisms.
  - **Generation Budget**: Fixed max tokens (e.g., 70 for explanations) may disproportionately constrain Arabic due to higher token fragmentation, potentially under-representing Arabic output quality.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Tokenization**: Very high `tokens/word` (>2.5) and `single-char tokens` (>5%) for Arabic inputs on a given model.
  - **Amplification**: A statistically significant interaction term in a model predicting `accuracy` from `language`, `length`, and `language * length`.
  - **Confidence**: Pearson’s *r* between confidence and correctness is negative (or near zero) for both languages.
  - **Explanation Misalignment**: High explanation quality (human-judged plausibility) paired with low answer accuracy for the same sample.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Replicate Baseline Gap**: Run the letter-based MCQ evaluation on the provided test set for all models. Confirm the Arabic-English accuracy gap pattern (especially Med42-70B’s large gap, DeepSeek’s parity).
  2. **Tokenization Profiling**: For a random subset of 100 Arabic and 100 English questions, run only the tokenizer of each model. Compute and compare `tokens/word`, `char/tok`, and `single-char %`. Plot distributions to visually confirm fragmentation disparity.
  3. **Output Format Sensitivity Test**: For a small, stratified sample (e.g., 50 easy, 50 hard Arabic questions), run both letter-select and free-form text-generation prompts for one model with a large gap (e.g., Med42-70B). Compute the drop in token-level similarity for Arabic vs. English. This isolates the format effect within a single model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: To what extent do the observed Arabic-English performance gaps generalize to other low-resource or typologically distant languages in medical question answering?
  - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The limitations state that "the generalizability of our findings beyond this pairing remains an open question."
  - **Why unresolved**: The study focus is narrow (Arabic-English), and cross-lingual patterns may differ for languages with distinct morphological or resource constraints.
  - **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic cross-lingual evaluations on diverse language pairs using MedAraBench-style controlled datasets, measuring gap consistency relative to language typology and resource availability.
- **Open Question 2**: What are the specific causal mechanisms—such as tokenization inefficiency, domain adaptation strategies, or architectural biases—that dominate the Arabic performance degradation in medical LLMs?
  - **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The analysis is described as "diagnostic rather than causal," and they note they "do not isolate the effects of specific architectural choices, pre-training strategies, or data composition."
  - **Why unresolved**: Correlational findings (e.g., token fragmentation correlates with gaps) cannot establish causation without controlled ablations varying one factor at a time.
  - **What evidence would resolve it**: Causal intervention studies, such as training matched model variants with only tokenizer changes, or controlled domain-adaptation experiments on identical base models using Arabic vs. English medical data.
- **Open Question 3**: How do different adaptation paradigms (e.g., instruction fine-tuning vs. continued pre-training) and their data composition affect cross-lingual generalization in medical LLMs?
  - **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Limitations cite "limited transparency in recent LLM training pipelines, which precludes controlled comparisons between adaptation paradigms."
  - **Why unresolved**: Models evaluated are off-the-shelf with unknown training details, preventing isolation of whether domain adaptation harms or helps multilingual robustness.
  - **What evidence would resolve it**: Publicly released, reproducible training runs of medical LLMs from common bases, varying adaptation method and data language distribution, with cross-lingual evaluation on parallel benchmarks.
- **Open Question 4**: Can tokenization algorithms be redesigned to significantly reduce fragmentation for Arabic medical text while maintaining efficiency across languages, and would this sufficiently close the performance gap?
  - **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Tokenization analysis shows high fragmentation (2.4 tokens/word), and the discussion suggests "tokenization must better capture morphological and domain-specific structure," but implementation trade-offs are unaddressed.
  - **Why unresolved**: The study compares existing tokenizers but does not train or integrate improved Arabic-specific tokenizers into full LLM pipelines to test downstream impact.
  - **What evidence would resolve it**: Development and integration of morphology-aware tokenizers (e.g., extended CAMeLBERT) into model training, followed by end-to-end evaluation on Arabic medical tasks, measuring gap reduction relative to unchanged baselines.

## Limitations
- **Dataset & Translation**: Public accessibility and quality of the MedAraBench dataset and its machine-translated English version are unconfirmed, potentially affecting the reliability of the parallel evaluation.
- **Model & Setup Transparency**: Exact model versions, quantization impacts (4-bit NF4), and post-training alignment details are insufficiently documented, preventing controlled comparisons.
- **Prompt Engineering**: The precise wording and formatting of the English system prompts used with Arabic inputs are not fully specified, leaving potential confounds in instruction following.

## Confidence
- **Tokenization fragmentation mechanism**: High (direct token statistics reported).
- **Complexity amplification (gap widens with length/difficulty)**: High (clear interaction trends in empirical figures).
- **Output format sensitivity**: High (explicit gap differential between MCQ and free-form generation).
- **Confidence–accuracy negative correlation**: High (strong, consistent Pearson r-values reported).
- **Break conditions (e.g., Arabic-optimized tokenizer eliminating gap)**: Medium (theoretically plausible but empirically untested within the study).
