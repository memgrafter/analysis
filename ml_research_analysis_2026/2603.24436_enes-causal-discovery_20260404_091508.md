# Enes Causal Discovery
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24436
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24436
- **Reference Count:** 16
- **Primary Result:** Enes achieves higher accuracy (0.8818) and precision (0.7214) with a lower Structural Hamming Distance (SHD=14.30) than linear Pearson and PC algorithm baselines on the Sachs protein-signaling dataset.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Enes, a Mixture of Experts (MoE) neural architecture for causal discovery from observational data. It addresses the challenge of the Sachs protein-signaling network, where simple linear methods are strong and neural networks typically fail due to a lack of formal training data. Enes uses a dual-expert system (linear and nonlinear) with a gating mechanism and incorporates physics-informed constraints (Pearson correlation, cosine similarity, adjacency penalties) to classify edge-node-edge triplets and infer causal graphs. Training is stabilized via Langevin dynamics. On Sachs and larger synthetic Michaelis-Menten graphs, Enes demonstrates superior precision and lower SHD compared to linear and PC baselines, though with reduced recall on larger graphs.

## Method Summary
Enes is a neural causal discovery model that classifies causal motifs (e.g., chains, forks, colliders) from triplets of node data. Its core is a Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture with two parallel experts—one linear and one nonlinear—whose outputs are weighted by a gating network. The model is trained on synthetic data generated from random Structural Equation Models (SEMs) and Michaelis-Menten dynamics, deliberately kept separate from test data to avoid overfitting. A multi-component loss function combines a classification objective with three "physics-informed" regularizers: a Pearson correlation penalty, a cosine similarity consistency loss between expert outputs, and an adjacency matrix/DAG penalty. Training uses Langevin dynamics (stochastic gradient MCMC) for enhanced stability in this complex loss landscape. The final causal graph is reconstructed from the predicted triplet classes.

## Key Results
- Enes outperforms the Linear Pearson baseline and the constrained PC algorithm on the Sachs dataset in accuracy (0.8818 vs. 0.8694) and precision (0.7214 vs. 0.6223), with a lower SHD (14.30 vs. 15.80 and 36.00).
- On larger synthetic Michaelis-Menten graphs (up to 50 nodes), Enes maintains the best SHD and superior precision compared to baselines, indicating better scalability and robustness in identifying correct causal edges.
- Enes exhibits a precision-recall trade-off at scale, with recall dropping on larger 50-node graphs while precision remains relatively high.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The MoE architecture flexibly captures both linear and nonlinear causal relationships within triplets, improving performance over pure linear or nonlinear models on datasets like Sachs where simple linear baselines are strong.
**Mechanism:** Two expert networks (linear and nonlinear) process the same input triplet. A gating network dynamically weights each expert's output (`Score = w·A + (1-w)·B`). The final classification is derived from this weighted combination, allowing the model to select the appropriate expert per triplet.
**Break condition:** If the gating mechanism fails to learn specialization (weights collapse) or if the synthetic training data distribution does not overlap with the true Sachs generating process, the hybrid advantage vanishes.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Incorporating physics-informed constraints (Pearson correlation penalty, cosine similarity, adjacency penalties) into the loss function guides the MoE to learn representations that respect causal graph properties (e.g., acyclicity, sparsity), stabilizing learning without ground-truth graphs.
**Mechanism:** During training, predictions are penalized based on: (1) Pearson correlation between predicted adjacency and input features, (2) cosine similarity between expert outputs for consistency, and (3) an explicit DAG enforcement penalty. These differentiable regularizers shape the model toward valid graph structures.
**Break condition:** If the constraints are misaligned with true causal semantics (e.g., Pearson is high for confounded pairs), they will mislead the model. The paper states penalties produce stable results but does not prove causal sufficiency.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Using Langevin dynamics for optimization instead of standard gradient descent improves training stability and helps escape poor local minima in the complex, multi-objective loss landscape.
**Mechanism:** Langevin dynamics adds controlled noise to gradient updates, allowing broader exploration early in training to find better trade-offs between classification loss and constraint penalties, before converging.
**Break condition:** If the noise scale is not properly annealed, the model may fail to converge precisely or converge too slowly. The claimed stability benefit is not independently verified.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Triplet -> Shared Feature Extractor -> [Linear Expert & Nonlinear Expert in parallel] -> Gating Network (weights experts) -> Combined Expert Output -> Constraint Modules (Pearson, Cosine, Adjacency penalties) -> Classifier Head -> Causal Motif Prediction
- **Critical path:** Input Triplet -> Shared Features -> Parallel Experts -> Gated Score -> Constraint Penalties Calculated -> Combined Loss (Classification + λ₁*Pearson + λ₂*Cosine + λ₃*Adjacency) -> Parameter Update via Langevin Dynamics.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - MoE Complexity vs. Expressivity: More experts capture more forms but increase optimization difficulty; two experts is a minimal, interpretable split.
  - Constraint Strength vs. Flexibility: Strong penalties force unrealistic graphs; weak penalties may not guide learning. Balancing λ coefficients is crucial.
  - Langevin Noise vs. Convergence: Higher noise aids exploration but slows/precises convergence; requires careful annealing.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor Precision/High SHD: Too many false positives. Likely cause: constraints too weak or gating not differentiating experts.
  - Very Low Recall: Overly conservative. Likely cause: constraints too strong, especially adjacency penalty; or synthetic training data lacks diversity.
  - Gating Collapse: Gating weights `w` converge to ~0 or ~1 for all inputs, making MoE redundant.
  - Unstable Training Loss: Oscillation or divergence. Likely cause: incorrect Langevin parameters (noise scale, decay).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Baseline Replication:** Rigorously re-implement Linear Pearson and PC algorithms on Sachs dataset. Verify reported metrics (Acc, Prec, SHD) to establish a firm baseline.
  2.  **Ablation Study:** Systematically disable key components: (a) only Linear Expert, (b) only Nonlinear Expert, (c) remove one penalty (Pearson, Cosine, or Adjacency), (d) replace Langevin with SGD. Measure impact on SHD/Precision.
  3.  **Scalability Stress Test:** Train and evaluate Enes on larger synthetic MM graphs (25, 50, 100 nodes). Monitor training time, memory, and degradation in SHD/Precision. Compare scaling trend to baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does Enes compare to other neural causal discovery methods (e.g., NOTEARS, DAG-GNN) on the Sachs and Michaelis-Menten datasets?
- Does training on randomly generated SEM data introduce a bias that limits generalization to real-world causal structures like the Sachs network?
- Why does Enes show lower recall on larger synthetic graphs (50 nodes), and can the architecture be adapted to improve recall without compromising precision?
- What is the theoretical impact of Langevin dynamics on convergence and stability, and could alternative optimization methods yield better performance?

## Limitations
- **Architectural & Training Specs:** Exact layer dimensions, activation functions, gating structure, DAG penalty formulation, and all hyperparameters (loss weights, LR, Langevin schedule) are unspecified.
- **Training Data Distribution:** The process for generating synthetic SEM/MM data is vague; the degree of distributional mismatch with real Sachs data is unknown and likely critical.
- **Constraint Justification:** The choice and theoretical grounding of the "physics-informed" constraints are heuristic; their alignment with true causal principles is not established.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (MoE for Lin/Non-lin Mix):** **Medium** – Plausible design but core assumption about Sachs data's mixture not validated; gating specialization not empirically proven.
- **Mechanism 2 (Physics Constraints):** **Low** – Constraints are applied but no evidence they are causally sufficient or better than alternatives; contribution not disentangled.
- **Mechanism 3 (Langevin Dynamics):** **Low** – Claim is self-asserted with no ablation comparing SGD vs. Langevin on this specific loss.
- **Scalability & Robustness Claims:** **Medium** – Results on synthetic MM graphs support the claim within that regime, but generalizability is uncertain.

## Next Checks

2.  **Constraint Sensitivity & Misalignment Test:** Deliberately mis-specify the DAG constraint (e.g., use a weak L2 penalty) or invert the Pearson penalty. If performance degrades catastrophically, it confirms heavy reliance on specific, possibly fragile, constraint choices.
3.  **Synthetic-to-Real Distribution Shift Analysis:** Train Enes on synthetic data from three distinct processes: (a) the paper's unreported process, (b) linear Gaussian SEMs, (c) Sachs-matched parameters.
