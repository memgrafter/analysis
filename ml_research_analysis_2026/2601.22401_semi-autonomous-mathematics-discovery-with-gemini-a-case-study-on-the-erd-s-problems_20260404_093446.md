# Semi-Autonomous Mathematics Discovery with Gemini: A Case Study on the Erdős Problems
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.22401
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Hybrid AI-human evaluation resolved 13 out of 700 'Open' Erdős conjectures, with 4 novel autonomous solutions, 2 partial AI solutions, 4 independent rediscoveries, and 5 identified existing literature solutions, while most mathematically correct AI outputs (63) did not align with intended problem statements.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents a semi-autonomous framework combining Gemini's natural language capabilities with expert human review to tackle the vast collection of unresolved Erdős conjectures. Out of 700 "Open" problems, the pipeline generated 200 candidate solutions, of which 63 were mathematically correct but only 13 truly addressed the conjectures' intent. The study highlights that many problems remained open due to obscurity rather than inherent difficulty, and raises concerns about "subconscious plagiarism" where AI may reproduce existing knowledge without citation.

The findings demonstrate both the potential and pitfalls of using large language models for mathematical discovery: AI can produce novel solutions and rediscover known results, but often misinterprets subtle problem statements or generates overly complex proofs. The labor-intensive literature verification step emerged as a critical bottleneck, suggesting the need for improved retrieval and attribution methods.

## Method Summary
The authors applied a hybrid AI-human pipeline to a curated set of 700 Erdős conjectures marked "Open" in Bloom's database. Gemini was prompted to generate proof attempts or disproofs in natural language. Generated solutions underwent automated consistency checks and were filtered for mathematical plausibility. The resulting 200 candidates were then subjected to rigorous expert review to evaluate correctness, novelty, and relevance to the original problem statement. Additionally, a comprehensive literature search was conducted to determine whether the solutions (or similar ideas) already existed in the mathematical literature, addressing both rediscovery and potential unconscious borrowing from training data.

## Key Results
- 13 conjectures were definitively resolved: 4 via novel autonomous AI solutions, 2 via partial AI contributions, 4 via independent human rediscovery, and 5 by identifying pre-existing solutions in the literature.
- From 200 candidate solutions, 63 (31.5%) were mathematically correct, but only 13 (6.5%) correctly addressed the intended problem statement; the remaining 50 were vacuous due to misinterpretation.
- Literature identification proved the most labor-intensive step, often requiring extensive manual search, and raised concerns about AI "subconscious plagiarism" where models reproduce known results without attribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The hybrid approach leverages Gemini’s ability to generate mathematically plausible arguments at scale, while human experts provide the contextual understanding necessary to judge correctness, originality, and alignment with problem intent. AI narrows the vast search space, flagging promising candidates and performing initial verification; experts then perform final validation, ensuring rigor and proper attribution. This division of labor allows systematic exploration of many open problems that would otherwise remain neglected due to obscurity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Hybrid Human-AI Collaboration**: Needed to combine AI’s scalability with human judgment for nuanced evaluation. Quick check: Track the proportion of AI-generated candidates that survive expert review.
- **Natural Language Verification**: Enables handling of informal, historical problem statements without requiring formalization. Quick check: Evaluate if AI can correctly verify solutions expressed in natural language for a set of known problems.
- **Literature Search Automation**: Essential to identify existing solutions and assess novelty, preventing reinvention and detecting possible plagiarism. Quick check: Measure recall of known solved Erdős problems when AI is queried for literature evidence.
- **Problem Misinterpretation Detection**: Critical to avoid spending effort on vacuous solutions that misinterpret the conjecture. Quick check: Introduce common ambiguities and monitor whether AI requests clarification or produces trivial solutions.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Problem Ingestion -> Normalization -> Gemini Model (Solution Generation) -> Automated Consistency Check -> Candidate Filtering -> Expert Review -> Resolution & Documentation.

Critical path: The longest dependency chain runs from ingestion through normalization, solution generation, consistency check, and expert review before a resolution is reached.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Speed vs. verification depth: Aggressive filtering reduces expert workload but risks false negatives.  
- Natural language vs. formal representation: Natural language offers flexibility but increases misinterpretation risk; formal methods improve precision but require problem formalization.  
- Automation vs. human oversight: Higher automation scales better but necessitates robust checks to maintain quality.

Failure signatures:  
- High false positive rate (68.5% of candidates flawed).  
- Significant verbosity and overcomplication in AI proofs.  
- Misaligned solutions that are mathematically correct but irrelevant to the conjecture.  
- Manual literature search becoming a bottleneck.

Three first experiments:  
1. Pilot on 50 known Erdős problems: calibrate verification thresholds and measure false positive/negative rates.  
2. Ambiguity stress test: inject common misstatements to evaluate AI’s ability to flag or clarify.  
3. Literature retrieval benchmark: assess AI’s capability to locate existing solutions in a curated corpus.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can AI-generated mathematical solutions be reliably checked for subconscious plagiarism, i.e., derivation from training data without attribution?
- Basis: [explicit] The authors identify "subconscious plagiarism" as a key risk where AI reproduces knowledge from training without citing sources (Section 1.2, 4). They note that even when solutions are correct, determining if they are independent rediscoveries is difficult because indirect leakage from pretraining is undetectable via logs.
- Why unresolved: Current methods rely on manual literature review and log scanning, which cannot trace indirect influences. The scale of mathematical literature makes exhaustive comparison impractical.
- What evidence: A study comparing AI outputs against a comprehensive, timestamped corpus of mathematical literature using advanced source attribution techniques, perhaps with watermarking of training data or improved retrieval-augmented generation to track influences.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What causes systematic misinterpretations of Erdős problem statements by AI, and how can these be prevented to ensure solutions address the intended problem?
- Basis: [explicit] The paper reveals that 50 of 63 technically correct AI solutions were vacuous due to misinterpreted problem statements (Section 1.1, 1.4). Examples include Erdős-75 where the model solved a non-intended formulation, and ambiguities in notation/conventions led to trivial solutions.
- Why unresolved: AI lacks the contextual understanding to detect subtle errors in problem transcription or intended meaning, and formal verification doesn't resolve semantic intent. The authors note that even experts struggled with ill-posed statements.
- What evidence: Developing a benchmark of Erdős problems with known misstatements and measuring AI's ability to flag inconsistencies or request clarifications; incorporating metadata from sources like Bloom's database into AI prompts.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent are 'Open' problems in curated databases like Bloom's Erdős Problems actually already solved but unrecorded due to obscurity, and how can database curation be improved?
- Basis: [explicit] The authors conclude that many 'Open' statuses were due to obscurity not difficulty, with 5 solutions found via literature identification (Section 1.2, 1.4). They cite Erdős-333 as solved by Erdős himself before the problem was posed.
- Why unresolved: No systematic audit exists for such databases; reliance on community submissions means many solutions exist in obscure literature or as folklore. The paper's own effort required extensive manual literature search.
- What evidence: A comprehensive literature review project for all 'Open' Erdős problems, cross-referencing with historical journals, preprints, and expert consultations to estimate the fraction of misclassified problems.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Why do AI-generated mathematical proofs tend to be unnecessarily complicated or verbose, and how can this be mitigated to improve interpretability and trust?
- Basis: [inferred] The authors note that AI outputs are "far more verbose" than journal papers (Section 1.3) and give overkill arguments (e.g., double-exponential in Erdős-75 solution). This suggests training objectives prioritize correctness over elegance.
- Why unresolved: Current evaluation metrics for AI math focus on correctness, not proof quality. Human experts value simplicity and insight, which are hard to quantify.
- What evidence: Comparative analysis of AI vs. human proofs on a set of problems, using metrics like proof length, number of lemmas, and expert ratings. Training AI with rewards for conciseness alongside correctness.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How can the hybrid AI-human evaluation pipeline be scaled to handle large volumes of conjectures without proportional increases in expert mathematician time?
- Basis: [inferred] The paper used AI to narrow 700 problems to 200 candidates, but only 13 were meaningful after expert review (Section 1.1). The bottleneck is human evaluation of correctness and novelty, especially literature search.
- Why unresolved: Expert time is scarce; AI verifiers reduce volume but still produce many false positives (68.5% flawed). Literature identification remains manual-intensive.
- What evidence: Developing AI tools specifically for literature search and attribution, perhaps with enhanced retrieval from mathematical corpora; training non-experts to use AI assistants for initial vetting under expert supervision.

## Limitations
- The conclusion that many problems remained open due to obscurity relies on Gemini’s literature search success; this may conflate AI search limitations with actual problem accessibility.
- The concern about subconscious plagiarism is noted but not systematically tested; no overlap analysis between AI outputs and identified sources is reported.
- Sample selection bias: Evaluation focused on 200 candidate solutions from 700 conjectures; selection criteria are unclear, possibly overweighting problems with accessible solutions or phrasing amenable to the model.

## Confidence
- High: Raw counts (13 resolved, 63 mathematically correct) are well-defined but depend on the human-AI verification protocol.
- Medium: The breakdown of resolution types (autonomous, partial, rediscovery, literature) and the 6.5% alignment rate are plausible but lack independent replication.
- Low: The broader inference that AI can transform mathematical discovery through semi-autonomous workflows—extrapolating from one case study on a specialized corpus.

## Next Checks
1. Source accessibility audit: For a random sample of unresolved conjectures, manually verify original publication availability, indexing, and language barriers to quantify "obscurity" independently of AI search.
2. Plagiarism detection test: Compute textual similarity (e.g., using embedding-based retrieval or n-gram overlap) between AI-generated "novel" solutions and their identified source literature to measure paraphrasing extent.
3. Expert re-alignment assessment: Ask domain experts to judge whether the 63 mathematically correct but "misaligned" solutions could be reinterpreted as valid (partial) solutions to the intended problems, reducing the misalignment rate.
