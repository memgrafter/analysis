# Powering Up Zeroth-Order Training via Subspace Gradient Orthogonalization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17155
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17155
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** ZO-Muon requires only 24.7% of MeZO's queries to reach the same SST-2 accuracy for LLM fine-tuning and improves accuracy by 25.1% on ViT-B fine-tuning on CIFAR-100, achieving win-win gains in convergence speed and final accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization enables memory-efficient fine-tuning of large models by estimating gradients without backpropagation, but suffers from high variance that hurts accuracy and query efficiency. This paper introduces ZO-Muon, which combines two innovations: (1) Subspace Randomized Gradient Estimator (Subspace RGE) that projects the optimization into a low-dimensional subspace to reduce variance by exploiting the low-rank structure of model updates, and (2) Gradient Orthogonalization (GO) from the Muon optimizer, applied within the subspace to extract spectral information from noisy ZO estimates. The synergy between these components yields simultaneous improvements in accuracy and query/runtime efficiency across LLMs and Vision Transformers, outperforming state-of-the-art ZO baselines like MeZO.

## Method Summary
ZO-Muon operates by first projecting the model parameters (as matrices) onto a random column-orthonormal subspace of rank \(r\) (typically 64–128). Gradient estimation is then performed within this subspace using the Subspace RGE with \(N_q > 1\) forward queries, reducing variance from \(O(mn/N_q)\) to \(O(rn/N_q)\). The resulting noisy subspace gradient estimate is passed through the `msign` operator (gradient orthogonalization), which approximates the matrix sign function via Newton–Schulz iterations to produce a spectrally isotropic update direction. This orthogonalized gradient is lifted back to the full parameter space and used for the update. The projection matrix is lazily resampled every \(v\) steps (default 100) to maintain efficiency. Key hyperparameters include \(N_q=4\) for LLMs and \(N_q=16\) for ViTs, learning rate \(1\mathrm{e}{-2}\), and scaling factor \(\mu=1\mathrm{e}{-3}\).

## Key Results
- On SST-2 (LLM fine-tuning), ZO-Muon achieves the same test accuracy as MeZO using only **24.7% of the queries**.
- On CIFAR-100 with ViT-B, ZO-Muon improves accuracy by **25.1%** over the previous SOTA ZO method.
- Across diverse tasks (SuperGLUE, CIFAR-10/100) and models (LlaMA3, OPT, ViT), ZO-Muon demonstrates **faster convergence** and **higher final accuracy** while maintaining lower runtime for equivalent query budgets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Subspace RGE Reduces Variance via Low-Rank Projection
**Claim:** Operating ZO gradient estimation in a low-dimensional subspace reduces variance because the true gradient has low effective rank during training.
**Mechanism:** Standard RGE variance scales as \(O(mn/N_q)\). By approximating \(X \approx PZ\) with \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}\), \(r \ll \min(m,n)\), the Subspace RGE estimates \(\nabla_Z f(PZ)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{r \times n}\) and lifts via \(P\), lowering variance scaling to \(O(rn/N_q)\). The projection \(PP^\top\nabla_X f(X)\) captures the principal subspace.
**Evidence:** Abstract; Fig. 3(a) shows low-rank gradient structure in FO training; Section 3 derives Eq. 5–6.
**Break condition:** If the true gradient is high-rank or \(P\) fails to capture the principal subspace, variance reduction diminishes and bias may harm convergence.

### Mechanism 2: Subspace GO Extracts Spectral Information from Noisy Estimates
**Claim:** Applying gradient orthogonalization (`msign`) to the *subspace* RGE estimate (not full-space) stabilizes and accelerates convergence by enhancing spectral signal.
**Mechanism:** `msign(G)=G(G^\top G)^{-1/2}` whitens \(G\), creating a spectrally isotropic update. In ZO-Muon, it is applied to the low-rank subspace gradient \(\hat{\nabla}_Z\), making `msign` computationally cheaper (scales with \(r\)) and more stable because noise is filtered by focusing on principal subspaces.
**Evidence:** Abstract; Section 4 derives Eq. 10 and Proposition 1; Fig. 5 shows full-space GO (ZO-Muon-V0) fails while subspace GO succeeds.
**Break condition:** If the subspace estimate is too noisy (e.g., \(N_q=1\)), `msign` amplifies spurious directions; hence \(N_q>1\) is necessary.

### Mechanism 3: Synergy Achieves Win-Win in Accuracy and Efficiency
**Claim:** Combining Subspace RGE and Subspace GO yields simultaneous gains: fewer total queries (due to variance reduction) and faster convergence per step (due to higher-quality updates), with minimal overhead because `msign` scales with \(r\).
**Mechanism:** 1) Subspace RGE reduces per-query variance, meaning fewer iterations are needed. 2) Subspace GO provides a more stable, spectrally normalized update from the lower-variance estimate. 3) Overhead from sampling \(P\) and low-rank `msign` is amortized by the dramatic reduction in required steps.
**Evidence:** Abstract; Fig. 1 (faster convergence), Fig. 2 (fewer queries), Tables 1–2 (accuracy/runtime); Fig. A2 shows \(N_q>1\) overcomes multi-query paradox for ZO-Muon but hurts Subspace-MeZO.
**Break condition:** Requires balanced hyperparameters: \(r\) too small discards signal; \(r\) too large reintroduces noise; \(N_q=1\) breaks `msign`; overly frequent \(P\) resampling causes instability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Random Projection Sampler (P) → Subspace RGE Engine → Gradient Orthogonalization Module (msign) → Lift & Update

**Critical path:**  
1. Sample \(P\) (lazily, every \(v\) steps).  
2. For iteration, draw \(N_q\) random perturbations \(P\Psi_i\) (\(\Psi_i \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}\)).  
3. Perform forward queries, compute finite-difference average to obtain \(\hat{\nabla}_Z\).  
4. Apply `msign(\(\hat{\nabla}_Z\))`.  
5. Multiply by \(P\) to lift to full space: \(O = P\,\text{msign}(\hat{\nabla}_Z)\).  
6. Update parameters: \(X \leftarrow X - \eta O\).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Subspace rank \(r\): Low \(r\) (e.g., 16) discards signal; high \(r\) (e.g., 256) reintroduces noise/instability. Moderate \(r\) (e.g., 64) balances.
- Query count \(N_q\): \(N_q=1\) cheap per step but makes `msign` input too noisy; \(N_q>1\) (4–16) stabilizes at cost of higher per-iteration cost, offset by fewer total steps.
- `msign` implementation: SVD is accurate but ~3× slower than Newton–Schulz (NS); NS is default.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Full-space GO (ZO-Muon-V0/JAGUAR-Muon): accuracy plummets vs. MeZO. *Fix:* must use Subspace RGE first.  
2. \(N_q=1\): convergence unstable/stalls. *Fix:* use \(N_q \geq 4\).  
3. Extreme \(r\): too small → slow loss decrease; too large → late instability. *Fix:* grid search \(r\) around expected low-rank (e.g., {64,128}).  
4. Frequent \(P\) resampling (\(v=1\)): oscillations. *Fix:* lazy resampling (\(v=100\)).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce baseline gap: Run MeZO vs. Subspace-MeZO (Eq.6, \(N_q=1\)) on OPT-1.3B/SST-2; confirm improved convergence/accuracy (Fig.3).  
2. Validate GO synergy: Run ZO-Muon-V0 (full-space GO) vs. ZO-Muon (subspace GO); verify only the latter excels (Fig.5).  
3. Hyperparameter sweep: For ViT-B/CIFAR-100, sweep \(r \in \{16,64,128,256\}\) and \(N_q \in \{1,4,16\}\); plot accuracy vs. total queries to identify stable regime.

## Limitations
- Implementation details not fully specified: Newton–Schulz iteration count, handling of vector parameters (embeddings/LayerNorm), and ViT batch size semantics.
- Validity of low-rank gradient assumption not extensively validated across architectures, training stages, or tasks.
- No theoretical convergence proof for the combined ZO-Muon algorithm under non-linear `msign`.
