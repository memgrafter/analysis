# OmniSapiens: A Foundation Model for Social Behavior Processing via Heterogeneity-Aware Relative Policy Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10635  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10635  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** OmniSapiens-7B 2.0 with HARPO achieves new SOTA on Human Behavior Atlas, improving multitask accuracy by up to +16.85%, zero-shot held-out tasks by +9.37%, and beating GRPO by up to +42.29% on individual tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the limitation of task-specific AI models in social behavior processing by proposing a unified foundation model trained with Heterogeneity-Aware Relative Policy OptimHARPO). HARPO dynamically balances learning across heterogeneous tasks by modulating advantages based on per-sample and per-task contribution signals, using geometric‑mean references and inertial smoothing to stabilize scaling. Trained on the Human Behavior Atlas (10 tasks), the resulting OmniSapiens-7B 2.0 model demonstrates state‑of‑the‑art performance and more explicit reasoning traces.

## Method Summary
HARPO extends group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO) by scaling policy updates according to contributions of individual rollouts and tasks. For each rollout group, group‑normalized advantages $\hat{A}$ are computed. Contribution signals $p_{(m,q)}$ (sample average of $|\hat{A}|$) and $p_m$ (task average) are updated via exponential moving average (βρ=0.95). Geometric means of these signals per task ($\bar{p}_{ref,m}$) and across tasks ($\bar{p}_{ref,M}$) serve as references. Modulation factors $s_{(m,q)} = \bar{p}_{ref,m} / p_{(m,q)}$ and $s_m = \bar{p}_{ref,M} / p_m$ are then smoothed multiplicatively (βs=0.95). The HARPO advantage is $A_H = s_{(m,q)} \cdot s_m \cdot \hat{A}$, used in a PPO‑clipped surrogate loss with optional KL penalty omitted. The base model is Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B, and training uses a multitasocial behavior dataset (Human Behavior Atlas) with a reward composition of 0.8·r_task + 0.2·r_fmt + 0.75·r_len; classification tasks use exact match, QA tasks use cosine similarity with MiniLM, formatting checks require $\boxed{}$, and a length penalty is applied for responses exceeding 812 tokens. Optimization employs LR=1e‑6, batch=256, 5 rollouts per sample, PPO clip ε=0.2, and early stopping based on average weighted F1.

## Key Results
- Multitask performance improved by up to **+16.85%** over prior models.  
- Zero‑shot held‑out task performance improved by up to **+9.37%**.  
- On specific tasks, HARPO outperforms GRPO by up to **+42.29%**.  
- Reasoning traces become more explicit and robust (validated by ablations).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** HARPO balances learning across heterogeneous behavioral tasks by modulating advantage magnitudes so no task or sample dominates the policy update.  
- **Mechanism:** For each rollout, the method computes a *contribution signal* $p(t)$ as the average absolute group‑normalized advantage. Separate signals are maintained per sample ($p(t)_{(m,q)}$) and per task ($p(t)_m$). These signals are then transformed into *modulation factors* $s(t)$ via the reciprocal of their ratio to a geometric‑mean reference, and the factors are applied multiplicatively to the advantages before the PPO update.  
- **Assumption:** The magnitude of a rollout’s advantage correlates with its contribution to the policy gradient; thus scaling advantages directly scales influence.  
- **Core assumption:** Heterogeneity in advantage distributions across tasks/samples is the primary cause of uneven learning, and uniformizing advantage scales yields balanced gradient contributions.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “HARPO modulates advantages to ensure that no single task or sample carries disproportionate influence during policy optimization.”  
  - [section 3.2] Full derivation of contribution signals, geometric references, and modulation factors, including the multiplicative update for inertial smoothing.  
  - [corpus] The nearby work “Constrained Group Relative Policy Optimization” (FMR 0.62) explores GRPO extensions but does not address heterogeneity; this highlights that HARPO’s specific modulation mechanism is novel in this context.  
- **Break condition:** If contribution signals become extremely noisy (e.g., due to very small rollout groups), the derived modulation factors may not reflect true contribution, leading to ineffective balancing.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Using geometric‑mean references to construct modulation factors preserves the overall scale of the update while enabling balanced scaling across tasks/samples.  
- **Mechanism:** The geometric mean (GM) of contribution signals within a task and across tasks serves as reference. Modulation factors are defined as $s = GM / p$. This ensures the product of all sample‑level factors equals 1 (and similarly for task‑level), so up‑scaling for some is exactly offset by down‑scaling for others.  
- **Core assumption:** The geometric mean provides a stable, outlier‑resistant baseline that keeps multiplicative adjustments comparable across different magnitudes.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.2] “Taking reference from the geometric‑mean ensures that scaling is directly comparable across samples and tasks, and does not change the overall update size.”  
  - [section 3.2] Proof that $\prod s(t)_{(m,q)} = 1$ and $\prod s(t)_m = 1$ (Appendix D.2).  
  - [section 4.5, Fig. 6] Ablation using inverse scaling ($s = 1/p$) yields geometric means > 2.5 and worse performance, demonstrating the importance of centering.  
- **Break condition:** If any contribution signal is zero, the geometric reference becomes zero, making the modulation factor undefined or infinite. The method must tolerate zero advantages (likely via a small ε).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Inertial smoothing of contribution signals and modulation factors prevents over‑reaction to stochastic fluctuations in on‑policy rollouts.  
- **Mechanism:** Contribution signals are smoothed by exponential moving average (EMA) with decay βρ. Modulation factors are updated multiplicatively: $s(t) = (s(t‑1))^{βs} * (s_raw)^{1‑βs}$ instead of additive updates. This keeps the modulation dynamics on a slower time scale than policy updates.  
- **Core assumption:** Raw advantage‑based contribution signals are high‑variance; smoothing isolates persistent trends that should drive long‑term balancing.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.2] Equations (11) for inertial updates of both signals and factors.  
  - [section 4.5, Fig. 5] Without inertial control, modulation factors fluctuate sharply; with smoothing they change gradually.  
  - [section 4.5, Tab. 3] Removing inertial control degrades average rank from 1.90 to 2.70, indicating performance harm.  
- **Break condition:** Inappropriate βρ/βs values can cause either excessive lag (too high) or residual noise (too low), both impairing the balancing effect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Policy Gradient Methods  
  *Why needed here:* HARPO is an on‑policy RL algorithm that directly modifies the policy gradient through advantage scaling. Understanding the gradient theorem $\nabla J = \mathbb{E}[A \nabla \log \pi]$ is essential.  
  *Quick check question:* If you double all advantages, how does the policy gradient change?

- **Concept:** Group‑Normalized Advantages  
  *Why needed here:* HARPO builds on GRPO, which normalizes advantages within each rollout group to make rewards comparable across samples. This is the baseline that HARPO modulates.  
  *Quick check question:* Why compute mean and standard deviation per group rather than globally?

- **Concept:** Geometric Mean and Multiplicative Balancing  
  *Why needed here:* The geometric mean ensures that the product of modulation factors equals 1, preventing unintended global step‑size changes.  
  *Quick check question:* If you have two numbers $a$ and $b$ and you multiply one by $\sqrt{ab}/a$ and the other by $\sqrt{ab}/b$, what happens to the product of the two factors?

- **Concept:** Exponential Moving Average (EMA)  
  *Why needed here:* EMA provides a simple way to maintain a smoothed estimate of a time‑varying quantity (contribution signals, modulation factors) with low memory overhead.  
  *Quick check question:* How does an EMA with β=0.9 weight the new observation vs. the previous estimate?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Base model Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B → multimodal input → rollout generation → reward computation (accuracy, format, length penalty) → GRPO group‑normalized advantage $\hat{A}$ → sample/task contribution signals $p_{(m,q)}$, $p_m$ → geometric‑mean references $\bar{p}_{ref,m}$ (per task) and $\bar{p}_{ref,M}$ (global) → modulation factors $s_{(m,q)}=\bar{p}_{ref,m}/p_{(m,q)}$, $s_m=\bar{p}_{ref,M}/p_m$ (multiplicative EMA) → HARPO advantage $A_H = s_{(m,q)} \cdot s_m \cdot \hat{A}$ → PPO policy update (clipped surrogate, optional KL).

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Sample a batch of queries across tasks.  
  2. Generate rollout groups per query, compute rewards.  
  3. Compute group‑normalized advantages.  
  4. Update inertial estimates of contribution signals ($\bar{p}_{(m,q)}$, $\bar{p}_m$) via EMA.  
  5. Compute geometric references for each task and across tasks using current $\bar{p}$.  
  6. Compute raw modulation factors ($s_{raw}$) and apply multiplicative smoothing to get $s$.  
  7. Scale advantages by $s_{(m,q)}$ and $s_m$ to obtain $A_H$.  
  8. Perform PPO‑style policy update using $A_H$.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Sample‑level vs. task‑only modulation:** Adding sample‑level narrows advantage distributions, helping most tasks but not all (e.g., EMO, INT, SOC show limited gains).  
  - **Inertial smoothing:** Smoother modulation improves stability but may delay response to genuine shifts in task difficulty.  
  - **KL penalty:** Omitting KL (β=0) simplifies and avoids over‑regularization but risks larger policy deviations; the balance should be tuned.  
  - **Group size:** Number of rollouts per query affects the stability of $\hat{A}$ and thus $p$; too small increases variance.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Task performance collapse:** One or more tasks see large drops relative to GRPO (e.g., SAR drops from 53.58% to 27.56% in GRPO vs. 70.64% in HARPO).  
  - **Diverging modulation factors:** $s$ values grow without bound or collapse to zero, indicating reference instability or extreme advantage imbalances.  
  - **No smoothing effect:** Modulation factors jitter wildly step‑to‑step, suggesting βs, βρ too low.  
  - **Geometric mean zero:** If any $p_{(m,q)}$ becomes zero, the reference becomes zero; should be guarded by ε.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Multitask benchmark:** Train HARPO on Human Behavior Atlas (10 tasks) and compare per‑task accuracy against GRPO, RLOO, GPG, RE++ (Table 1).  
  2. **Ablation study:** Remove sample‑level modulation, inertial control, and geometric centering one at a time; measure average task rank (Table 3).  
  3. **Dynamics analysis:** Track relative task advantage and modulation factors for a weak task (SAR) over training; verify that HARPO lifts its advantage while GRPO does not (Fig. 4, 5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What factors determine whether sample‑level modulation in HARPO improves performance, given that it benefits some tasks (e.g., ANX) but not others (e.g., EMO, INT, SOC)?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Ablation results (Table 3, Fig. 5) show that removing sample‑level modulation degrades overall multitask rank and narrows advantage distributions, but the paper does not explain why this effect varies across tasks.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper speculates that sample‑level modulation suppresses extreme advantages, but it does not investigate whether task‑specific properties (e.g., dataset size, reward sparsity, difficulty) drive the differential impact, leaving a gap in understanding the mechanism.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Correlate task‑level metrics (e.g., initial advantage variance, number of training samples, label entropy) with performance changes from adding sample‑level modulation. Controlled experiments on synthetic tasks with controlled variance could isolate causal factors.

2. **How does the geometric‑mean reference in HARPO’s structured modulation compare to alternative centering strategies (e.g., arithmetic mean, median) in terms of optimization stability and multitask balance?**  
   *Basis in paper:* HARPO uses geometric mean to construct modulation factors with a geometric mean of 1 (Sec. 3.2), and an ablation removes structured modulation entirely, showing performance loss. However, no comparison with other reference choices is provided.  
   *Why unresolved:* The geometric mean is theoretically motivated for multiplicative scaling, but its practical necessity is unclear; the ablation only shows that some structure helps, not whether the geometric mean is optimal or if simpler centering would suffice.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement HARPO variants with arithmetic mean, median, or a learned reference; compare modulation factor trajectories, gradient norm stability, and final multitask performance across multiple seeds to determine if geometric mean provides a significant edge.

3. **Does the balanced learning induced by HARPO yield representations that generalize to a broader range of novel behavioral tasks and domains than the two held‑out tests (AUT, SER) currently demonstrate?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Sec. 4.3 reports improved zero‑shot transfer on AUT and SER, attributing gains to more uniform multitask learning. However, evaluation is limited to these two datasets.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper claims that HARPO’s balanced optimization promotes generalizable features, but this remains speculative; it is unknown whether the generalization extends to other tasks (e.g., cross‑cultural cues, different modalities) or if the observed gains are dataset‑specific.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct extensive zero‑shot evaluations on a diverse suite of held‑out behavioral benchmarks, including tasks with different modalities, annotation protocols, and cultural contexts. Additionally, analyze representation similarity (e.g., via CKA or probing) to see if HARPO learns more shared features across tasks.

## Limitations
- Preprocessing details for converting raw audio/video to Qwen2.5‑Omni inputs are not described.  
- Key hyperparameters (PPO clip ε, KL weight β, rollout generation limits) are omitted.  
- Evaluation metrics aggregation (“average weighted F1”) and reliance on a proprietary LLM‑Judge (GPT‑5 nano) reduce reproducibility.

## Confidence

- **SOTA performance:** Medium (gains large, but evaluation inconsistencies)  
- **Improved reasoning traces:** Low (qualitative, judge‑dependent)  
- **Geometric‑mean reference & inertial smoothing:** High (theoretical proof and ablations confirm)
