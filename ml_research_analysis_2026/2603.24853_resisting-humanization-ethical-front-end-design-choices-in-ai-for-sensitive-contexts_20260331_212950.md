# Resisting Humanization: Ethical Front-End Design Choices in AI for Sensitive Contexts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24853
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24853
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Chayn discontinued its anthropomorphizing chatbot and adopted a non-conversational, transparent UI to prioritize user autonomy in sensitive contexts.

## Executive Summary
This paper highlights the ethical oversight in AI front-end design, particularly how humanizing elements in conversational interfaces can mislead users in sensitive contexts like gender-based violence support. Through a case study of Chayn, the authors examine the shift from the Little Window chatbot—discontinued after chatlogs showed users consistently perceived it as human despite warnings—to Survivor AI 2, which employs a non-conversational UI, explicit AI identity markers, and process transparency. This design transition enhanced user agency and trust calibration, demonstrating that principled front-end restraint, by prioritizing autonomy over engagement, can ethically shape human-AI interactions. The work calls attention to front-end design as a form of procedural ethics and advocates for trauma-informed approaches that resist anthropomorphism.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a qualitative single-case study of Chayn, analyzing design documentation and chatlogs from the Little Window chatbot and the subsequent Survivor AI 2 prototype. No reproducibility details or raw data are provided, limiting external verification.

## Key Results
- Little Window chatbot was discontinued after analysis revealed users consistently misperceived it as human, even with explicit warnings.
- Survivor AI 2 replaces conversational interaction with a non-conversational UI, explicit AI identity labeling, and step-by-step process transparency.
- This principled restraint improved user agency and trust calibration, showing that front-end design choices can prioritize autonomy without compromising utility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The ethical efficacy arises from deliberately avoiding anthropomorphic cues (e.g., human-like dialogue, avatars) and instead embedding clear signals of artificial agency. Explicit AI identity markers prevent users from forming false mental models, while process transparency—explaining each step and the AI’s role—empowers users to maintain control and make informed choices. This aligns with trauma-informed design by reducing unpredictability and power imbalances, thereby calibrating trust and safeguarding vulnerable users.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Anthropomorphism**  
   - *Why needed:* In sensitive contexts, attributing human traits to AI can foster misplaced trust, emotional dependency, or unsafe disclosures.  
   - *Quick check:* Do users spontaneously use human pronouns or ascribe consciousness to the system?

2. **Trust Calibration**  
   - *Why needed:* Miscalibrated trust (overtrust or undertrust) can lead to harm; users need accurate expectations of AI capabilities.  
   - *Quick check:* Are users' self-reported trust levels aligned with the system's actual performance and limitations?

3. **User Agency**  
   - *Why needed:* Vulnerable users must retain control to avoid re-traumatization and support autonomous decision-making.  
   - *Quick check:* Can users easily pause, correct, or exit the interaction without penalty?

4. **Process Transparency**  
   - *Why needed:* Opaque AI operations confuse users; transparency demystifies the interaction and reduces power asymmetry.  
   - *Quick check:* Do users understand what data is collected, how it is used, and what the AI does at each stage?

5. **Trauma-Informed Design**  
   - *Why needed:* Engagement-maximizing patterns may trigger trauma; design must prioritize safety, predictability, and empowerment.  
   - *Quick check:* Does the interface avoid surprises, offer clear navigation, and provide emotional support resources?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map:** Identity Badge -> Step Wizard -> Input Forms -> Review Panel -> Support Link
- **Critical Path:** User arrives, immediately sees the AI identity badge, proceeds through sequential steps with clear explanations, provides input via structured forms, reviews AI-generated output with explicit attribution, and accesses support resources. The path emphasizes predictability and user control.
- **Design Tradeoffs:** 
  - Reduced engagement vs. increased safety: Avoiding conversational flair lowers satisfaction but prevents anthropomorphism.
  - Increased cognitive load vs. transparency: Explicit explanations require effort but empower decisions.
  - Limited expressiveness vs. consistency: Non-conversational input restricts natural language but ensures predictable processing.
- **Failure Signatures:** 
  - Users attempting to converse with the system (e.g., using chat language).
  - Expressions of frustration about lack of empathy or feeling "robotic".
  - Skipping or ignoring transparency elements (e.g., step descriptions).
- **First Experiments:**
  1. A/B test comparing Little Window (conversational) vs. Survivor AI 2 (non-conversational) on anthropomorphism, trust calibration, and agency with GBV survivors.
  2. Think-aloud study to observe interpretation of AI identity badges and transparency cues, identifying confusion points.
  3. Longitudinal deployment tracking user outcomes (help-seeking, distress) associated with the restrained design.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can front-end design choices be systematically evaluated for ethical impact in AI systems, particularly regarding user autonomy and trust calibration?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The abstract and introduction highlight that ethical debates have primarily focused on back-end issues, overlooking front-end design's role in shaping user mental models and trust, especially in vulnerable contexts.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper argues front-end design is a form of procedural ethics but provides no concrete evaluation framework or metrics to assess whether design choices (e.g., avoiding humanization) actually uphold autonomy and calibrated trust.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and validation of assessment rubrics or mixed-methods tools that measure user outcomes (e.g., anthropomorphism perception, agency, reliance) across diverse AI interfaces, tested in longitudinal or comparative studies with vulnerable populations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Which specific non-conversational UI elements (e.g., explicit AI identity markers, step transparency, non-emotive language) are most effective in mitigating anthropomorphic misperceptions without compromising usability or accessibility in sensitive domains?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The case study contrasts Little Window (conversational, discontinued due to user misperception) with Survivor AI 2 (non-conversational, designed with restraint), but does not isolate which design interventions drove the improved outcome or their trade-offs.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper describes design principles but lacks empirical breakdown of individual elements' contributions to user understanding and safety, leaving unclear how to optimize such interfaces.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled A/B testing or qualitative analysis comparing variations of specific front-end features (e.g., avatar absence vs. clear labels) with target users, measuring comprehension, trust, and emotional responses.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can trauma-informed design principles be operationalized and adapted into generalizable front-end guidelines for AI systems across diverse sensitive contexts (e.g., mental health, legal aid, domestic violence) with different vulnerability factors?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Chayn's framework is rooted in GBV survivor support; the paper suggests trauma-informed design as a broader approach but does not address how to abstract or transfer these principles to other high-stakes domains with unique ethical risks.
- **Why unresolved:** Principles are context-specific; without a methodology for adaptation, designers in other fields lack guidance on applying similar restraint while addressing domain-specific needs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cross-domain comparative case studies or participatory design research that maps common and divergent trauma-informed requirements, yielding adaptable design pattern libraries validated through user testing in multiple contexts.

## Limitations
- Generalizability limited to gender-based violence context; findings may not apply to other sensitive domains or user populations.
- Causal link between user misperception and chatbot discontinuation is inferred, not experimentally proven; other factors likely contributed.
- Claims about Survivor AI 2’s effectiveness lack empirical validation with target users; impact on agency and trust remains theoretical.

## Confidence
- User misperception of Little Window as human: Medium
- Misperception as primary cause of discontinuation: Low
- Ethical intent of Survivor AI 2 design: Medium
- Demonstrated user impact of Survivor AI 2: Low
- Generalizability beyond GBV context: Low
