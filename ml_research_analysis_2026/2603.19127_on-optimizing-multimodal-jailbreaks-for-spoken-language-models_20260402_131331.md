# On Optimizing Multimodal Jailbreaks for Spoken Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19127
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19127
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: JAMA boosts multimodal jailbreak success by 1.5×–10× over unimodal baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper exposes a critical vulnerability in Spoken Language Models (SLMs): multimodal jailbreaks that jointly optimize adversarial text and audio inputs. The authors introduce Jama (Joint Audio-text Multimodal Attack), which combines GCG for text suffix optimization and PGD for audio perturbation, and a faster sequential alternative (SAMA). Across four SLMs and four audio types, Jama significantly outperforms unimodal attacks, demonstrating that evaluating safety in unimodal settings is insufficient for securing multimodal systems.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates white-box gradient-based attacks on SLMs using the AdvBench dataset. Jama simultaneously perturbs a discrete text suffix (via GCG) and a continuous audio waveform (via PGD) to trigger affirmative responses to harmful queries. GCG performs token-level greedy search over 1000 steps with a candidate width of 32 and top-k 16, while PGD iteratively updates the audio spectrogram with a projected gradient step (ε=0.001). A sequential variant (SAMA) first optimizes the text suffix without audio conditioning and then freezes it to optimize the audio perturbation, achieving similar success rates at 4–6× lower computational cost. Models tested include Audio Flamingo 3, Qwen2 Audio 7B, Gemma 3N 2B, and Qwen2.5 Omni 7B, with four audio types (audiobook, Switchboard, music, non-speech). Success is measured via LLaMA Guard 3 and string matching. The implementation ensures differentiable audio pipelines, adapting models to enable backpropagation through feature extractors.

## Key Results
- Jama increases jailbreak success rates by 1.5× to 10× over unimodal GCG or PGD attacks across all tested models and audio types.
- Sequential SAMA achieves comparable success (within ~10% on average) while reducing compute time by 4–6×.
- Unimodal robustness evaluations are insufficient: Gemma 3N resists unimodal attacks but remains vulnerable to multimodal Jama.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Joint audio-text optimization exploits complementary gradients in the SLM's fused representation space. Three mechanisms enable its effectiveness:

### Gradient complementarity
Optimizing both modalities simultaneously perturbs partially independent decision surfaces, increasing the probability of an affirmative target response. The joint loss averages over a batch of malicious queries, allowing gradients from text (GCG) and audio (PGD) to cooperatively push the model into an adversarial subspace.

### Early GCG dominance
During joint optimization, the discrete text suffix (GCG) drives initial rapid loss reduction due to steeper loss curvature in the token embedding space. PGD updates on the continuous audio spectrogram become more influential in later steps as text tokens saturate locally.

### Sequential approximation efficiency
Because GCG dominates early steps and likely converges to a suffix largely independent of the audio perturbation, a sequential attack (optimize text first, then audio) recovers most of the joint performance with large compute savings. The expensive GCG candidate evaluations are decoupled from audio conditioning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gradient-based adversarial attacks**: GCG for discrete token suffixes uses greedy coordinate descent to find token substitutions that maximize loss; PGD for continuous inputs applies iterative projected gradient steps. These are the building blocks of Jama/SAMA.
  - *Why needed*: Understanding their step logic (candidate sweep vs. continuous projection) is essential to grasp the optimization process.
  - *Quick check*: How does GCG handle non-differentiability of discrete tokens? Why does PGD require projection?
- **Multimodal fusion in SLMs**: Audio encoders process waveforms into representations that are combined with text tokens, e.g., via cross-attention or concatenation. The attack targets the fused latent space.
  - *Why needed*: The joint attack exploits the point where audio and text interact; architecture determines where perturbations exert influence.
  - *Quick check*: If audio features are concatenated with text embeddings before the LLM, where would a joint perturbation have its most direct effect?
- **Safety alignment and jailbreaking**: Models are trained to refuse harmful requests; jailbreaks craft inputs that bypass refusals, often targeting affirmative response strings.
  - *Why needed*: The objective (affirmative response) and evaluation (LLaMAguard) define success.
  - *Quick check*: What typical loss is used to reverse alignment? Why an "affirmative" string?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input (raw audio waveform, text prompt) → Audio feature extractor (log-Mel spectrogram) → SLM backbone (audio encoder + LLM, fused via cross-attention or pooling) → Output token probabilities. Attack perturbs: (1) discrete suffix tokens appended to text, (2) continuous delta added to raw waveform.

**Critical path**: 1. Ensure differentiable audio processing pipeline (model-specific adaptations to avoid gradient shattering). 2. Compute joint loss over a batch of malicious queries with target affirmative responses. 3. Alternate updates: PGD step on audio delta (normalized gradient, clip to ε) followed by GCG step on text suffix (Top-K token sweep conditioned on current audio).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Joint (Jama) vs. sequential (Sama): Joint may capture subtle audio-text interactions but is 4–6× slower due to audio conditioning in every GCG candidate evaluation.
- GCG hyperparameters: Suffix length vs. compute trade-off; longer suffixes increase capacity but exponentially more candidate evaluations (search width × top-k).
- PGD hyperparameters: Audio length vs. perceptibility; longer perturbations allow more capacity but risk audible artifacts (ε controls imperceptibility).

**Failure signatures**:
- High unimodal robustness but low multimodal robustness (e.g., Gemma 3N).
- Unimodal attacks occupy separable embedding subspaces; successful jailbreaks correspond to embeddings far from benign manifold.
- Gradient energy ratio (ρ) remains GCG-dominated throughout; if PGD gradient never catches up, joint attack may not outperform strong GCG-only.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Unimodal baselines replication**: Run GCG-only and PGD-only on one SLM (e.g., Qwen2.5 Omni) with variable suffix/audio lengths; establish baseline success rates and compute profiles matching Table 1.
2. **Jama proof-of-concept**: With GCG length=8, PGD length=4s, run joint optimization; verify success rate exceeds unimodal baselines by a clear margin (>10% absolute) and loss curve shows GCG-like early drop then PGD-like continuation.
3. **Sama speed/accuracy tradeoff**: Execute Sama (GCG then PGD) on same configuration; measure wall-clock time vs. Jama; expect ~5× speedup with a small success rate gap (if GCG length sufficient).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does Sama sometimes outperform Jama?** For Gemma 3N with Switchboard audio, Sama exceeds Jama by ~20% under optimal conditions; the authors suspect gradient interactions but leave the cause open.
2. **Why does embedding drift not correlate with success?** PGD-only audio attacks induce larger representation shifts than GCG-only yet achieve lower jailbreak rates; the relationship remains unclear.
3. **How to defend against multimodal jailbreaks?** The paper highlights that unimodal robustness is insufficient and calls for strong multimodal guardrails but offers no concrete defenses.

## Limitations
- Reproduction is hindered by unspecified details: AdvBench split definition, differentiable audio pipeline modifications, and LLaMA Guard 3 evaluation template.
- Minimal external validation: The paper has zero references and the niche area lacks corroborating studies.
- Some findings (e.g., Gemma 3N anomaly) are unexplained, limiting generalizability.
