# WildSci: Advancing Scientific Reasoning from In-the-Wild Literature

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05567
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05567
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Training Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on the automatically generated 56K-question WildSci dataset yields a 7.26% average improvement on GPQA-Aug, SuperGPQA, and MMLU-Pro (24.52% → 31.78%).

## Executive Summary
WildSci introduces a scalable pipeline to automatically generate a large corpus of multiple-choice scientific reasoning questions directly from peer-reviewed research papers. By leveraging LLMs for question generation, heuristic filtering, model-voting for clarity, and reinforcement learning with simple answer-matching rewards, the approach produces a dataset (WildSci) that significantly improves out-of-domain scientific reasoning performance when used for training. Models trained on this data generalize beyond the training distribution, demonstrating improved reasoning on challenging benchmarks like GPQA and MMLU-Pro.

## Method Summary
The pipeline constructs a dataset from open-access *Nature Communications* papers (pre-April 2024). For each paper, an LLM generates three context-independent multiple-choice questions with rationales. Questions are filtered via heuristics (e.g., removal of figure/table references) and n-gram deduplication against target benchmarks. A refinement step expands options to ten and removes surface cues. An ensemble of models performs "model voting" by answering each question; questions where a majority select an "unanswerable" fallback are discarded. The resulting aligned data subsets (e.g., All Aligned) are used to train instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B-Instruct) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The reward is a binary exact match between the model's final answer choice and the synthetic label, with option shuffling each epoch to prevent position bias. Training occurs via the verl framework for 700 steps, with evaluation on in-domain (WildSci-Val) and out-of-domain (GPQA-Aug, SuperGPQA, MMLU-Pro) benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct trained on WildSci achieves a **+7.26% average gain** on GPQA-Aug, SuperGPQA, and MMLU-Pro (24.52% → 31.78%).
- In-domain validation accuracy jumps **+33.78 points** (46.7% → 80.48%).
- The 3B model variant gains **+4.45% average** on the same out-of-domain benchmarks.
- Generalization continues after in-domain overfitting, with progress concentrated in subdomains having greater WildSci coverage.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Automated synthesis from peer-reviewed papers yields diverse, high-quality scientific questions at scale.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs read paper titles/abstracts/body and generate context-independent multiple-choice questions with rationales. This transforms unstructured research into structured QA pairs without human annotation.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can accurately extract and formulate challenging questions that reflect the paper's core contributions.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states the generation of 56K questions from papers across nine disciplines. Section 2.1 details the prompt instructions and context-independence constraint. Neighboring datasets like SCP-116K use educational materials, making WildSci's focus on research papers unique.
- **Break condition:** If generated questions contain factual inaccuracies or require figures/tables (violating context-independence), the pipeline's quality degrades.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Model voting using an ensemble of LLMs filters out ambiguous or unanswerable questions, improving dataset reliability.
- **Mechanism:** Each model answers each question with a fallback "unanswerable" option; questions where the majority selects unanswerable are discarded. This uses model consensus as a proxy for clarity.
- **Core assumption:** Stronger models can reliably detect unanswerability in questions generated by weaker models.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions "model voting to ensure clarity." Section 2.1 details the process and the fallback option. Similar techniques appear in other LLM data curation pipelines (e.g., SciInstruct), though not directly cited.
- **Break condition:** If ensemble models systematically disagree on answerability due to bias toward certain reasoning styles, valid but unconventional questions may be removed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Reinforcement learning with simple answer-matching rewards transfers effectively to out-of-domain scientific benchmarks because the MCQ format trains reasoning patterns rather than format tricks.
- **Mechanism:** GRPO updates policy based on whether the final answer choice matches the synthetic label. Random shuffling prevents position bias.
- **Core assumption:** The synthetic labels are correct and consistent enough to serve as a reward signal; improvements reflect genuine reasoning gains beyond format adaptation.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract describes "Reinforcement learning with GRPO employs simple answer-matching rewards." Section 2.3 defines the reward function; Section 4.3 shows accuracy improves after format adaptation saturates, indicating reasoning improvement. Neighbors like DeepseekMath and Open-Reasoner-Zero show similar RL success in math; WildSci extends this to science.
- **Break condition:** If performance gains are solely due to overfitting to question distribution or memorizing option patterns, generalization would not occur; the observed post-overfitting generalization contradicts this.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Data ingestion -> QA Generation (LLM) -> Filtering (heuristics + deduplication) -> Refinement (expand options, remove cues) -> Model Voting (ensemble) -> Data selection (agreement-based splits) -> Training (GRPO with answer-match reward) -> Evaluation (in-domain + OOD benchmarks)

- **Critical path:**
  Raw papers -> QA generation -> Filtering -> Refinement -> Model Voting -> Data selection -> Training -> Evaluation.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - MCQ vs. open-ended: MCQs enable verifiable rewards but may limit depth; WildSci uses 10 options and refinement to increase difficulty.
  - Automatic generation vs. human annotation: Scales to 56K questions but risks noise; mitigated by voting and filtering.
  - Broad domain coverage vs. depth: Covers 9 disciplines but some subdomains sparse; leads to uneven generalization.
  - Simple reward vs. complex reward: Answer-matching is cheap but may not capture partial reasoning; RL still shows gains.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low model voting agreement: Many questions classified as unanswerable or divergent; indicates poor clarity.
  - In-domain validation accuracy plateaus early and declines while OOD still improves? Actually post-overfitting generalization is expected; but if OOD also declines, likely overfitting.
  - High similarity scores among questions (>0.9) could indicate redundancy, but analysis shows low redundancy.
  - Disproportionate performance on high-coverage vs. low-coverage domains suggests data imbalance.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Compare training on All Aligned vs. Majority Aligned subsets to assess impact of data difficulty.
  2. Ablate refinement: train with and without the refinement step (4 options vs. 10) to measure its effect on generalization.
  3. Mixed training with MATH: combine WildSci and math data to test transferability across reasoning domains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we effectively evaluate and train open-ended scientific reasoning abilities beyond the constraints of multiple-choice formats?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the Limitations section that evaluating and verifying open-ended research questions such as causal reasoning and analysis remains an open challenge and that the MCQ format inherently risks models exploiting spurious heuristics.
- **Why unresolved:** The field currently relies on simplified, verifiable rewards for training and evaluation, but true scientific discovery often involves generating and justifying novel hypotheses or explanations without predefined options.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of new evaluation frameworks that use automated assessors or expert human evaluation to score free-form scientific reasoning, paired with training methods that can learn from such sparse or comparative feedback.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What causes the phenomenon of "post-saturation generalization," where models continue to improve on out-of-distribution benchmarks after overfitting on the in-domain validation set?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors observe in Section 4.1 that models continue to generalize even after overfitting on the validation set and present WildSci as an ideal testbed for investigating RL reasoning in scientific contexts for this behavior.
- **Why unresolved:** This pattern contradicts typical overfitting curves where test performance degrades after validation saturation. The underlying mechanism—whether related to curriculum effects, internal representation changes, or task-specific dynamics—is not explained.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablations varying data difficulty, training dynamics, and model architecture, combined with probing analyses (e.g., representation similarity, gradient alignment) to trace when and how generalization improvements emerge post-saturation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the source of data (peer-reviewed papers from a single journal like *Nature Communications*) introduce subtle biases that limit the generalizability of WildSci-trained models to all scientific subdomains and research styles?
- **Basis in paper:** While the dataset spans 9 disciplines, the paper notes that some subdomains (e.g., Social Sciences) have fewer papers because they are less prevalent in the source journal. The automatic pipeline may also favor certain rhetorical or structural patterns common in that journal.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates coverage and diversity metrics but does not analyze whether the content's epistemological or methodological assumptions (e.g., experimental vs. theoretical, quantitative vs. qualitative) are balanced. This could create blind spots for models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi-source replication study (e.g., using papers from different publishers, preprint servers) to measure performance variance across subdomains, coupled with a qualitative audit of question types to assess representation of diverse scientific practices.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the automatically generated "refined" questions with 10 options and removed surface cues genuinely increase reasoning depth, or do they primarily alter task difficulty without improving transferable scientific skills?
- **Basis in paper:** The refinement stage explicitly aims to increase both their difficulty and diversity by expanding options and removing obvious clues. The ablation (Table 3) shows improved OOD performance with refinement.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether gains come from forcing models to discriminate among more subtle distractors (improving fine-grained reasoning) or simply from increased task difficulty that may not generalize. The nature of the "reasoning depth" added is not systematically measured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human evaluation of refined vs. unrefined questions on dimensions like required inference steps and conceptual complexity. Additionally, training models on each variant and testing on a held-out set of human-authored, difficulty-matched questions to isolate the effect of refinement on genuine reasoning versus guesswork reduction.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the simple answer-matching reward signal used in GRPO training adequately capture the quality of scientific reasoning, or does it incentivize superficial pattern matching over genuine understanding?
- **Basis in paper:** The reward function is defined solely as matching the prediction and the synthetic label. The authors acknowledge the MCQ format introduces heuristic risks but adopt it for scalability.
- **Why unresolved:** This reward does not account for the reasoning process (chain-of-thought) that leads to an answer. A model could produce correct answers for wrong reasons, limiting true scientific reasoning development. The improvement on benchmarks might reflect format adaptation rather than robust knowledge.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Process-based reward models that score intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., via LLM-as-a-judge or formal verification where possible), and comparing training outcomes using answer-only vs. process-aware rewards on benchmarks that include reasoning trace analysis or perturbation tests.

## Limitations
- Exact heuristic filter rules (e.g., regex patterns) and 13-gram deduplication details are unspecified, risking reproduction variance.
- Missing GRPO hyperparameters (e.g., KL coefficient, batch size) may affect training stability; reward relies on brittle exact string matching.
- Gains are strongest in high-coverage subdomains; low-coverage areas show weaker transfer, indicating data imbalance limits breadth.

## Confidence
- **Scalable data synthesis:** Medium (voting mitigates but doesn’t eliminate noise).
- **RL with answer-matching:** Medium (post-overfitting generalization supports reasoning gain, but reward simplicity may cap potential).
- **Cross-domain improvement:** Medium-High (consistent OOD gains, but uneven by coverage).
