# Distribution-Aware Companding Quantization of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00364
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00364
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Primary result:** DACQ reduces weight reconstruction error compared to uniform PTQ baselines like AWQ, but its best variant (Hybrid) yields higher WikiText-2 perplexity and lower MMLU accuracy on Llama3-8B, indicating a trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and downstream performance.

## Executive Summary
Post-training quantization (PTQ) is essential for deploying large language models (LLMs) on resource-constrained devices, but uniform quantizers often fail to capture the heavy-tailed, layer-specific distributions of transformer weights. This paper introduces Distribution-Aware Companding Quantization (DACQ), a PTQ method that models each layer’s weight distribution as logistic, applies a cumulative distribution function (CDF) transform to map weights to a uniform domain, performs uniform quantization, and then inversely transforms. DACQ also incorporates activation-aware per-channel scaling from AWQ to protect salient channels. Experimental results on 360 submodules (192 Llama + 168 Qwen) show that DACQ consistently achieves lower weight reconstruction error (MSE/MAE) than uniform baselines. However, on end-task metrics, DACQ-Hybrid—the variant that interpolates between logistic and uniform quantization levels—performs worse than AWQ: for Llama3-8B, WikiText-2 perplexity is 8.48 vs. 6.53 and MMLU accuracy is 41.08% vs. 43.44%. The paper demonstrates a key trade-off: improved reconstruction fidelity does not guarantee better downstream performance, as the method may under-represent critical outlier weights.

## Method Summary
DACQ operates in two phases. First, a calibration pass runs a small dataset through the model to cache activations for each layer. Then, for each layer independently:
1. Compute per-channel activation statistics (average absolute activation) to guide scaling.
2. Optimize a per-channel scaling factor via grid search to minimize the activation output error when weights are scaled.
3. Scale the weights by the optimized factors.
4. Fit a logistic distribution to the scaled weights, deriving its scale parameter.
5. Generate two candidate quantization level sets: logistic levels (via inverse CDF quantiles) and uniform levels (spanning min/max of scaled weights).
6. Form a hybrid level set by convexly mixing the two with coefficient γ, chosen via grid search to minimize activation-weighted reconstruction error.
7. Quantize the scaled weights to the nearest hybrid level and then de-scale to obtain the final quantized weights.
The approach requires no retraining and only a small calibration set, while adapting quantization to each layer’s distribution and the importance of channels as indicated by activations.

## Key Results
- DACQ achieves lower weight reconstruction error (MSE/MAE) than uniform PTQ baselines (e.g., AWQ) across all tested submodules.
- On Llama3-8B, DACQ-Hybrid yields higher WikiText-2 perplexity (8.48 vs. 6.53) and lower MMLU accuracy (41.08% vs. 43.44%) compared to AWQ, despite better reconstruction.
- DACQ retains most of the efficiency gains of quantization over full-precision inference, though detailed hardware benchmarks are not provided.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Distribution-Aware Binning
- **Claim:** Distribution-aware companding reduces quantization error by aligning quantization bin density with the empirical weight distribution’s probability mass.
- **Mechanism:** For each layer, weights are transformed via the empirical CDF of a fitted logistic distribution, mapping them to a uniform probability domain. Uniform quantization is applied in this domain, and values are inverted back to the original space. This allocates more quantization levels to dense central regions and fewer to sparse tails.
- **Core assumption:** Transformer layer weights follow a logistic-like, symmetric, heavy-tailed distribution that can be well-approximated by a two-parameter logistic fit.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper shows Q-Q plots and RMSE/MAE comparisons across 192 Llama and 168 Qwen submodules, where logistic distribution achieves the lowest errors. The abstract explicitly mentions modeling each layer’s weight distribution as logistic and applying a CDF transform.
- **Break condition:** If a layer’s weight distribution significantly deviates from the logistic prior (e.g., strong asymmetry or multi-modality), the CDF transform will misallocate bins, increasing distortion beyond uniform quantization.

### Mechanism 2: Activation-Aware Channel Scaling
- **Claim:** Activation-aware channel scaling protects weights with high downstream influence from quantization error, complementing distribution-aware binning.
- **Mechanism:** Using a small calibration set, per-channel activation magnitude (average absolute activation) is computed. Channels with higher statistics are scaled up (raised to a power α) before quantization, reducing relative quantization error on salient paths. The optimal scaling factor per layer is selected by grid search to minimize the activation output error.
- **Core assumption:** A channel’s average activation magnitude on calibration data is a reliable proxy for its importance to model outputs.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that DACQ is combined with activation-aware scaling from AWQ. Section 4.1 describes computing S_ℓ = E[|A_ℓ|] and searching over s_ℓ(α) = S_ℓ^α to minimize L_ℓ(α) = ||(Ŵ(s)A_ℓ - W A_ℓ)||².
- **Break condition:** In architectures with strong inter-layer dependencies (e.g., residual connections, grouped-query attention), minimizing per-layer activation error may not minimize global output error, as quantization perturbations compound nonlinearly through the network.

### Mechanism 3: Hybrid Non-Uniform Quantization
- **Claim:** Hybrid non-uniform quantization interpolates between logistic-based and uniform grids to preserve both dense-region precision and critical outlier weights.
- **Mechanism:** For each layer, two candidate level sets are generated: logistic levels (from inverse CDF quantiles) and uniform levels (spanning min/max). A convex combination Λ(γ) = (1−γ)Λ_logis + γΛ_uni is formed. The mixing coefficient γ is chosen via grid search to minimize activation-weighted reconstruction error on calibration data.
- **Core assumption:** Some quantized outlier weights, though statistically rare, are essential for model performance; uniform levels better preserve them than pure logistic levels.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that DACQ exhibits slightly worse perplexity, stating that its compression disproportionally penalizes outlier weights, some of which encode critical model knowledge. Section 4.4 explains that the logistic component captures the dominant mass while the uniform component ensures sufficient coverage of large-magnitude outliers.
- **Break condition:** If the model’s performance is not sensitive to rare outlier weights (e.g., if knowledge is distributed), the hybrid’s uniform component wastes precision and may increase overall error compared to pure logistic quantization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)**: Quantization is applied after the model is fully trained, without retraining. This makes it efficient but limits adaptation; DACQ relies on calibration data to set quantization parameters. *Why needed*: Understanding PTQ constraints explains why DACQ uses a small calibration set and cannot adjust weight distributions. *Quick check*: What is the difference between PTQ and Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) in terms of when the quantization scheme is applied?
- **Heavy-Tailed Distributions in Neural Weights**: Weight tensors in transformers often exhibit a sharp central peak and long tails (heavy-tailed). Uniform quantization places equal-width bins, which is inefficient for such distributions because many bins fall in low-density regions while the dense center is underrepresented. *Why needed*: DACQ’s motivation is to address this mismatch by using non-uniform bins aligned to the distribution. *Quick check*: Why might a uniform grid of quantization levels perform poorly on a distribution with a sharp central peak and long tails?
- **Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) Transform (Companding)**: Companding applies a nonlinear transform (typically the CDF of a fitted distribution) to data, making it approximately uniformly distributed. After uniform quantization in this transformed space, the inverse CDF maps back, effectively yielding non-uniform quantization bins in the original space. *Why needed*: This is the core technique enabling DACQ to allocate bins according to the weight distribution. *Quick check*: If you have a set of values following a Gaussian distribution, how would using its CDF as a transform before uniform quantization change the effective bin widths in the original space?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map** (data flow):
Calibration Data Loader -> Activation Statistic Computer
Activation Statistic Computer -> Scaling Factor Optimizer
Scaling Factor Optimizer -> Distribution Fitter
Scaling Factor Optimizer -> Quantization Level Generator
Distribution Fitter -> Quantization Level Generator
Quantization Level Generator -> Mix Coefficient Optimizer
Activation Statistic Computer -> Mix Coefficient Optimizer
Scaling Factor Optimizer -> Quantizer Applier
Mix Coefficient Optimizer -> Quantizer Applier

- **Critical path**: The process begins with a calibration pass: run the model on a small dataset to cache activations for all layers. After this, per-layer operations are independent and can be parallelized. For each layer: (1) compute activation statistics S_ℓ from cached activations; (2) optimize per-channel scaling factor s_ℓ via grid search to minimize activation output error; (3) scale the weights by s_ℓ; (4) fit a logistic distribution to the scaled weights; (5) generate logistic and uniform quantization level sets; (6) optimize the mixing coefficient γ to minimize activation-weighted reconstruction error; (7) quantize the scaled weights to the hybrid levels and de-scale. Once all layers are processed, the quantized weights constitute the quantized model.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Reconstruction fidelity vs. downstream performance*: Lower weight error does not ensure better perplexity or accuracy; the method may sacrifice critical outliers.
  - *Non-uniform vs. uniform quantization*: DACQ’s non-uniform bins improve reconstruction but may introduce lookup overhead and lower throughput compared to uniform quantizers like AWQ.
  - *Logistic prior vs. data-driven*: Assuming a logistic distribution is fast and scalable but may mismatch some layers; greedy level optimization would be more accurate but computationally prohibitive.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - High weight reconstruction error (MSE/MAE) relative to AWQ indicates poor distribution fit or suboptimal γ selection.
  - Disproportionate perplexity increase on WikiText-2 (e.g., >2× FP16) suggests critical outliers were misquantized.
  - Throughput drop >15% compared to AWQ may indicate inefficient kernel implementation for non-uniform dequantization.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Validate distribution fit per layer**: For a subset of layers, plot histograms and Q-Q plots against Gaussian, Laplace, and logistic distributions; compute RMSE/MAE to confirm logistic is the best fit.
  2. **Compare reconstruction metrics**: Quantize a single layer with AWQ (uniform) and DACQ variants (logistic, hybrid); measure MSE/MAE against FP16 weights. Expect DACQ to achieve lower error.
  3. **Evaluate downstream trade-off**: Run inference on WikiText-2 and MMLU for fully quantized models using AWQ, DACQ-Logistic, and DACQ-Hybrid. Expect DACQ-Hybrid to have lower perplexity than DACQ-Logistic but possibly higher than AWQ, illustrating the outlier trade-off.

## Limitations
- The logistic distribution assumption may not hold for all layers; asymmetric or multi-modal weight distributions could degrade quantization quality.
- Activation-aware per-channel scaling does not account for cross-layer dependencies (e.g., residual connections), which may limit its effectiveness in preserving global output accuracy.
- The claimed efficiency gains over full-precision inference lack hardware-level validation; non-uniform quantization could introduce overhead in practice.

## Confidence
- Reconstruction fidelity (DACQ reduces MSE/MAE): High
- Downstream performance (DACQ-Hybrid worse than AWQ): High
- Outlier preservation (Hybrid balances dense and outlier precision): Medium
- Efficiency retention (maintains most gains vs. FP16): Medium
