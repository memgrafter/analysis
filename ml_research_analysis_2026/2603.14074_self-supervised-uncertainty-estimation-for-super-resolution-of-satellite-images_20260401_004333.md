# Self-Supervised Uncertainty Estimation For Super-Resolution of Satellite Images

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14074  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14074  
- **Reference count:** 22  
- **Primary result:** A self-supervised loss for uncertainty-aware super-resolution eliminates the need for paired high-resolution ground truth, achieving reconstruction quality (PSNR ~54.05 dB) and uncertainty calibration (V-RMSE ~2.50e-5) comparable to supervised training on synthetic SkySat data.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a self-supervised method to quantify aleatoric uncertainty in satellite image super-resolution without requiring high-resolution ground truth. The approach extends an existing self-supervised super-resolution framework by incorporating a Gaussian negative log-likelihood loss derived from decision-theoretic Bayesian risk minimization, which yields the posterior mean and variance as optimal estimators. A random shift operator ensures all subsampling patterns are observed during training, and the loss corrects for signal-dependent noise. Experiments on a synthetic SkySat dataset show that the method achieves reconstruction accuracy and uncertainty calibration on par with a model trained with supervised Gaussian NLL. Coverage tests confirm that the uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated, demonstrating that meaningful aleatoric uncertainty can be learned in a purely self-supervised manner.

## Method Summary
The method builds upon a self-supervised super-resolution framework that uses multiple low-resolution, sub-pixel shifted frames of the same scene. It augments this framework with a Gaussian negative log-likelihood loss, motivated by Bayesian risk minimization under a Gaussian observation model. The loss compares the downsampled, shifted high-resolution reconstruction to an input low-resolution frame, while explicitly accounting for signal-dependent noise via an affine variance model. A random shift operator is applied to the degradation operator during training to ensure all possible subsampling patterns are experienced, which is crucial for identifying the true posterior statistics. The network outputs both a high-resolution image estimate and a per-pixel variance, trained end-to-end using the proposed loss.

## Key Results
- Achieves reconstruction quality (PSNR ~54.05 dB) and uncertainty calibration (V-RMSE ~2.50e-5) on a synthetic SkySat dataset comparable to a supervised baseline trained with Gaussian NLL.
- Coverage probability tests show the self-supervised uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated (e.g., 95% confidence intervals contain the true value approximately 95% of the time).
- Qualitative analysis reveals checkerboard patterns in uncertainty maps, with lower error for pixels corresponding to the top-left subsampling pattern, attributed to direct information from the reference frame.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core innovation lies in deriving a loss that directly targets the posterior mean and variance under a Gaussian noise assumption via Bayesian risk minimization. For the specific randomized subsampling degradation operator \( A_\tau = D S_\tau \) (where \( D \) is downsampling and \( S_\tau \) a random shift), the stationarity conditions of this loss have a unique solution corresponding to the true posterior statistics when all shift patterns are observed. The random shift operator \( S_\tau \) is essential: it ensures the training data covers all possible ways the high-resolution signal could have been downsampled, thereby preventing the network from learning spurious correlations tied to a fixed subsampling pattern. The noise correction term \( \hat{R}_\tau(v) = \text{diag}(a A_\tau \hat{u}(v) + b) \) accounts for signal-dependent variance, preventing the posterior variance from being inflated by observation noise. This combination allows the network to output meaningful uncertainty estimates without ever seeing the true high-resolution image.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self-supervised learning:** Exploits inherent structure in unlabeled data (multiple sub-pixel shifted low-res views) to define a training signal. *Why needed:* Paired high-resolution satellite imagery is rarely available. *Quick check:* Verify that the method can reconstruct a held-out high-resolution image using only low-resolution inputs.
- **Aleatoric uncertainty:** Quantifies irreducible noise (e.g., sensor noise) via per-pixel variance. *Why needed:* Provides reliability estimates for each pixel in the reconstructed image. *Quick check:* Confirm that predicted variance correlates with reconstruction error on synthetic data with known noise.
- **Bayesian risk minimization:** A decision-theoretic framework that chooses estimators (mean, variance) by minimizing expected loss under a utility function. *Why needed:* Derives the optimal Gaussian NLL loss for estimating posterior statistics. *Quick check:* Compare the empirical risk of the learned mean/variance against alternative estimators.
- **Randomized subsampling:** Applies random sub-pixel shifts to the degradation operator during training. *Why needed:* Guarantees all subsampling patterns are observed, ensuring uniqueness of the posterior statistics solution. *Quick check:* Ablate the random shift and measure increase in reconstruction error or uncertainty miscalibration.
- **Signal-dependent noise correction:** Models noise variance as an affine function of the signal. *Why needed:* Avoids overestimating posterior variance due to observation noise. *Quick check:* Plot estimated variance against signal intensity and verify it matches the known noise model.
- **Gaussian negative log-likelihood:** Assumes observations are Gaussian-distributed around the downsampled reconstruction. *Why needed:* Provides a tractable loss that yields both mean and variance estimates. *Quick check:* Assess normality of residuals (downsampled reconstruction minus input) on validation data.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input low-resolution burst -> Motion estimation network -> Optical flows \( F_t \) -> Random shift operator \( S_\tau \) -> Degradation operator \( A_\tau = D S_\tau \) -> Super-resolution network (decoder) -> Output high-resolution estimate \( \hat{u} \) and variance \( \hat{\sigma}^2 \) -> Gaussian NLL loss with \( \hat{R}_\tau(v) \) -> Parameter updates.

**Critical path:**  
1. **Motion compensation:** Networks estimates flows \( F_t \) to align all frames to a reference.  
2. **Random shift application:** \( S_\tau \) introduces random sub-pixel offsets to \( F_t \), forming \( A_\tau \).  
3. **Reconstruction:** SR network processes \( A_\tau \hat{u} \) to produce \( \hat{u} \) and \( \hat{\sigma}^2 \).  
4. **Loss computation:** Compare \( D S_\tau \hat{u} \) to input \( v_1 \), weighted by \( \hat{R}_\tau(v_1)^{-1} \).  
5. **Backpropagation:** Gradients update motion network, SR network, and noise parameters \( a,b \).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Random shift vs. computational cost:** Shifts improve robustness to unknown subpixel offsets but require multiple forward passes per batch.  
- **Gaussian assumption vs. real noise:** Simplifies derivation and training but may mismatch complex satellite sensor noise (Poisson-Gaussian, correlated).  
- **Multi-frame input vs. motion errors:** Using multiple bursts improves reconstruction but relies on accurate flow estimation, which can fail in low-resolution, noisy inputs.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Checkerboard patterns in uncertainty maps, especially at subpixel grid boundaries.  
- Poor coverage (e.g., 95% CI contains far less/more than 95% of errors) on validation.  
- Performance drop when moving from synthetic to real data, indicating degradation model mismatch.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic validation:** Train on synthetic SkySat bursts with known high-resolution ground truth. Report PSNR, SSIM, V-RMSE, and coverage at 50%, 80%, 95% confidence levels.  
2. **Ablation - random shift:** Remove \( S_\tau \) (use deterministic subsampling) and compare all metrics and uncertainty map visualizations.  
3. **Noise model sensitivity:** Train with AWGN, test on data with Poisson-Gaussian noise; train with affine noise model, test on data with non-linear variance function; measure change in calibration metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the proposed self-supervised Gaussian NLL loss be directly applied to other linear inverse problems beyond super-resolution (e.g., deblurring, MRI reconstruction) where the degradation operator does not yield unique minimizers for the posterior mean and variance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the conclusion: "Future work should explore the application of the proposed loss to other inverse problems. The challenge here is that in the general linear case the posterior mean and variance are not the unique minimizers." They also note in Section 4 that Proposition 4.3 (guaranteeing uniqueness) only holds for the specific randomized subsampling degradation.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical guarantee of recovering true posterior statistics is only proven for the specific subsampling operator \( A_\tau = D S_\tau \). For a general linear degradation \( A_\tau \), Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 provide only necessary conditions, and other unwanted solutions to the stationarity equations may exist.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical analysis deriving conditions under which the posterior mean and variance become unique minimizers for a broader class of degradation operators. Alternatively, empirical validation on multiple inverse problems (e.g., deblurring, compressive sensing) showing that the learned uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated despite the lack of uniqueness guarantees.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the performance and uncertainty calibration of the self-supervised method change when the assumption of signal-dependent Gaussian noise with an affine variance model \( g(s) = as + b \) is violated, such as with real sensor noise patterns (e.g., Poisson-Gaussian, spatially correlated noise)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method's derivation and noise correction (Section 3.2 and 4) explicitly assume an affine variance model to estimate \( \hat{R}_\tau(v) = \text{diag}(a A_\tau \hat{u}(v) + b) \). The experiments use synthetic AWGN with standard deviation between 5 and 18, which implicitly follows this model. The paper does not test robustness to mismatched or more complex noise models.
- **Why unresolved:** The correction term \( \hat{R}_\tau(v) \) is crucial for avoiding overestimation of posterior variance by the observation noise variance. If the true noise deviates significantly from the assumed model, the estimated \( \hat{R}_\tau(v) \) will be inaccurate, potentially leading to poorly calibrated uncertainty estimates (e.g., over- or under-confidence).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments where the synthetic training noise is replaced with a different distribution (e.g., Poisson noise, real sensor noise) or where the noise variance function is non-linear, followed by evaluation of calibration metrics (coverage, V-RMSE) on held-out data with known ground truth.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do the observed checkerboard patterns in the uncertainty maps (Section 5.2) and the varying performance across the four subgrids reflect inherent limitations of the randomized shift operator \( S_\tau \) versus architectural biases in the network (e.g., the decoder's upsampling layers)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper reports a qualitative checkerboard effect in the uncertainty estimates and quantifies different metrics (V-RMSE, ECE, Sharpness, RMSE) for pixels corresponding to the four subsampling patterns. They attribute the lower error for top-left pixels to the reference frame providing direct information for even pixels during inference. However, they do not disentangle whether the pattern is primarily due to the training objective's design (the random shifts) or to the network's inductive biases.
- **Why unresolved:** The random shift operator ensures all subsampling patterns are observed during training, but the loss still computes errors on the downsampled, shifted outputs. The network architecture (e.g., using transposed convolutions for upsampling) might inherently produce periodic artifacts. The paper does not include an ablation study isolating the effect of the shift operator from the network design.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation experiment where the random shift is removed (leading to a deterministic subsampling pattern) and the resulting uncertainty maps and calibration are compared. Alternatively, testing different upsampling methods (e.g., sub-pixel convolution, bilinear interpolation in the decoder) to see if the checkerboard pattern persists.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How well do the self-supervised uncertainty estimates transfer to real, unpaired satellite data where the true high-resolution image is unavailable and the degradation model (e.g., precise motion, noise statistics) may be inaccurate or unknown?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The entire validation is performed on a synthetic SkySat dataset where ground truth is available for evaluation. The paper claims the method makes a "practical framework for uncertainty-aware satellite image reconstruction," but this claim is not tested on real, unlabeled satellite bursts. The degradation model used for synthesis (random sub-pixel shifts, AWGN, exposure variations) is a simplified approximation of real push-frame sensors.
- **Why unresolved:** The core value of self-supervised methods is applicability when ground truth is absent. However, the calibration of uncertainty estimates is evaluated against known ground truth in simulation. In real scenarios, one cannot compute V-RMSE or coverage against the true HR image. The only way to assess reliability would be through downstream task performance (e.g., change detection accuracy) where uncertainty can guide decisions, which is not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Application of the method to real, unpaired satellite burst data (e.g., actual SkySat L1B products). Calibration could be indirectly assessed via techniques like consistency checks across multiple reconstructions of the same scene or by measuring the correlation between predicted variance and errors in a downstream task where a proxy ground truth exists (e.g., higher-resolution data from a different sensor).

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the impact of the motion estimation network's accuracy on the quality and calibration of the final uncertainty estimates? Specifically, how do errors in the estimated optical flows \( F_t \) propagate through the self-supervised Gaussian NLL loss?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method relies on a motion estimation network to predict flows \( F_t \) between each input frame and the reference (Section 3.1). The random shift \( S_\tau \) is added to these flows during training to maintain alignment. The loss is computed between \( D S_\tau \hat{u} \) and \( v_1 \). The paper assumes accurate motion compensation but does not analyze sensitivity to flow errors. In real data, motion estimation can be inaccurate, especially with low-resolution, noisy inputs.
- **Why unresolved:** The derivations in Section 4 assume the degradation model \( z = A_\tau u + n \) holds exactly. If the estimated flows \( \hat{F}_t \) are incorrect, the effective degradation operator applied by the network will deviate from \( A_\tau = D S_\tau \), violating the assumptions of Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 and Proposition 4.3. This could lead to biased mean estimates and miscalibrated variances.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments on the synthetic dataset where Gaussian noise is added to the true flows used as input to the network, simulating estimation errors. The resulting PSNR and V-RMSE (using available ground truth) could be plotted as a function of the flow error magnitude to quantify robustness. Alternatively, using a deliberately inaccurate motion network (e.g., with fewer layers) and comparing uncertainty calibration.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to a synthetic SkySat dataset; real satellite imagery involves complex artifacts (atmospheric distortions, varying sensor noise, motion blur) not captured in simulation.
- Calibration validation details are sparse (e.g., specific confidence intervals tested, diversity of scenes); confidence in real-world uncertainty quantification remains Medium.
- The method assumes an affine variance model for signal-dependent noise; robustness to non-Gaussian or spatially correlated noise is not tested.

## Confidence
- **Reconstruction quality (PSNR ~54 dB) on synthetic data:** High  
- **Uncertainty calibration (V-RMSE ~2.50e-5) matches supervised Gaussian NLL:** Medium  
- **Self-supervised loss yields meaningful aleatoric uncertainty without HR ground truth:** Medium
