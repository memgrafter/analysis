# Performance Evaluation of Open-Source Large Language Models for Assisting Pathology Report Writing in Japanese

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11597
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11597
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** Thinking models achieved 100% accuracy on reasoning tasks (A2), medical-specialized models led in typo correction (B), but explanatory text had low usability (25-33% rated 4-5) and high rater disagreement (ICC ≤0.466).

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated seven open-source large language models for assisting Japanese pathology report writing across three tasks: (A) structured generation/extraction, (B) typo correction, and (C) explanatory text evaluation. The results revealed task-dependent performance: models with explicit reasoning architectures ("thinking" variants) and large gpt-oss models achieved perfect accuracy in pT and grade determination, a task requiring multi-step logical inference. For typo correction, the model Q3N-In attained the highest macro F1 score (0.697), while medical-specialized models like MedGemma were competitive. In contrast, for generating explanatory text, only 25-33% of outputs were rated as usable (score 4-5) by clinicians and pathologists, with low inter-rater agreement (ICC ≤0.466). These findings suggest that open-source LLMs can be effective for structured and typo tasks but face challenges in producing universally acceptable explanatory content, likely due to subjective human preferences. The authors propose that future systems may need personalization to institutional or individual reporting styles.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed three benchmark tasks based on Japanese pathology reporting standards. Task A (structured generation/extraction) involved converting JSON records into institutional or guideline templates and vice versa, with a subtask (A2) requiring pT staging and grade determination from measurements. Task B used 31 real pathology reports (100–400 Japanese characters) into which synthetic typos (deletion, insertion, transposition, kanji conversion) were injected. Task C comprised 23 cases with immunohistochemical workup, prompting the model to generate resident-friendly explanations. Seven open-source LLMs (various sizes and specializations) were evaluated using the `llama.cpp` framework (commit b7640) on a Mac Studio M2 Ultra with 196 GB RAM. Quantizations included Q4_K_XL, Q8_0, and MXFP4. For tasks A and B, inference used a low temperature (0.1) for determinism; task C used model-recommended settings. Automated metrics (character-level F1, Jaccard, exact match, accuracy) were computed for A and B, while task C relied on human ratings from eight clinicians/pathologists on a 1-5 usability scale, with inter-rater reliability measured by ICC(2,1) and ICC(2,k). Code, prompts, and evaluation scripts are publicly available at https://github.com/enigmanx20/PathLMbench_JP.

## Key Results
- **Structured reasoning (A2):** Thinking models (e.g., Q3N-Th) and large gpt-oss models achieved 100% accuracy in pT and grade determination; non-thinking models performed near chance despite high string overlap.
- **Typo correction (B):** Q3N-In achieved the highest macro F1 (0.697). Medical-specialized models (MedGemma-27b, SIP-jmed) were competitive, with MedGemma attaining the highest micro precision (0.827).
- **Explanatory text (C):** Only 25-33% of model outputs received scores of 4-5 (usable with minor/no revision) from human raters. Inter-rater agreement was low, with ICC(2,1) ≤ 0.466 for clinicians and ≤ 0.138 for pathologists, indicating substantial variability in preferences.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed performance differences can be explained by three mechanisms:

1. **Explicit reasoning improves accuracy on complex rule-based tasks.** Thinking models generate intermediate reasoning steps (chain-of-thought) before final output, reducing logical errors in multi-step inference like pT staging. This decomposition makes the model's reasoning more transparent and less reliant on brittle heuristics. The 100% accuracy of thinking models on A2, contrasted with near-chance performance of non-thinking models, supports this mechanism. However, if reasoning steps themselves contain medical inaccuracies or the task does not benefit from decomposition, the advantage may disappear.

2. **Medical specialization enhances domain-specific language handling.** Models trained or fine-tuned on medical corpora develop better internal representations of pathology terminology, abbreviations, and report structure, enabling them to distinguish true typos from valid jargon. This explains the competitive performance of MedGemma and SIP-jmed in typo correction. The benefit may diminish if the specialization does not cover an institution's specific phrasing or if errors are orthographic rather than lexical.

3. **High inter-rater variability reflects subjective stylistic and judgment preferences.** Explanatory text generation is under-constrained, yielding multiple valid outputs. Human evaluators apply personal heuristics regarding clarity, thoroughness, and target audience (e.g., resident vs. clinician), which are not captured by a single rubric. The low ICC values confirm that agreement is poor. If a standardized, consensus-driven rubric with explicit factual checkpoints were enforced, agreement might improve.

## Foundational Learning
Several key concepts underpin the evaluation and deployment of LLMs in this domain:

- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning:** For tasks requiring multi-step logical rules (e.g., pT staging), CoT forces the model to emit intermediate calculations, improving accuracy over standard next-token prediction. Quick check: Can the model output a coherent step-by-step reasoning trace even when the final answer is incorrect? This separates reasoning format errors from knowledge gaps.

- **Domain-Specific Tokenization and Vocabulary:** Japanese pathology reports mix kanji, kana, Latin characters, and specialized abbreviations. A tokenizer that fragments key terms (e.g., "浸潤癌") harms extraction and correction performance. Quick check: Does the model's tokenizer treat common pathology terms as single tokens or multiple fragments? Fragmentation degrades performance.

- **Human-AI Alignment in High-Stakes Domains:** Generating explanatory text that aligns with diverse expert preferences requires more than factual correctness; it must satisfy unwritten conventions about pedagogy, conciseness, and emphasis. Quick check: Is the evaluation metric (1-5 score) measuring factual accuracy, clarity, or adherence to an implicit institutional template? Without disentangling these, improving the model is ill-defined.

## Architecture Onboarding
Deploying LLMs for pathology report assistance involves several interconnected components:

**Component map:** Preprocessor -> Task Dispatcher -> Model (Model Zoo) -> Inference (Hardware Abstraction) -> Postprocessor -> Evaluator

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare dataset per task (synthetic JSON for A, real reports with injected typos for B, case reports for C).
2. Select model and quantization based on memory/speed trade-offs (Table 1).
3. Load model via `llama.cpp` server.
4. Apply task-specific prompt (e.g., "Convert this JSON to hospital template...").
5. Run inference with low temperature (0.1) for deterministic tasks; use model-recommended parameters for C.
6. Score output automatically (A/B) or collect human ratings (C).
7. Iterate: adjust prompt, try different model, or modify quantization.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Precision vs. Throughput:** Thinking models (e.g., Q3N-Th) are 5-10x slower but perfect on reasoning tasks; choose based on task criticality.
- **Specialization vs. Generalization:** Medical-specialized models excel in typo correction but may underperform on general formatting tasks; use task-specific model selection.
- **Quantization:** Higher precision (Q8_0) preserves accuracy for complex reasoning; lower precision (Q4_K_XL) saves memory and speeds inference but may degrade fine-grained performance.

**Failure signatures:**
- **A2:** Non-thinking models show high string F1 but near-chance pT/grade accuracy → model matches surface patterns without true reasoning.
- **B:** High count of large deletions (LD) indicates over-correction or hallucination (e.g., SIP-jmed LD=4).
- **C:** Low ICC (<0.2) even for top models → no consensus exists on what constitutes a good explanation; further data collection may not help without clarifying the rubric.
- **All:** Timeouts or crashes on long contexts (e.g., Q3N-Th in A3) → context window or speed limitations; need truncation or faster variant.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce A2 split: Run Q3N-In vs. Q3N-Th on 10 A2 cases to confirm the reasoning gap and check for intermediate reasoning steps.
2. B typo ablation: Test MedGemma vs. Gemma on a hand-curated set of 5 pathology-specific typos (e.g., "癌"→"症") to verify that medical specialization improves recall on domain errors.
3. C rater calibration: Have all 8 raters score 5 common outputs from the top model (e.g., Q3N-Th). Compute ICC(2,1). If ICC < 0.3, the rating scale is too subjective and needs refinement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper highlights several unresolved questions:
1. **Personalization strategies:** What specific personalization or customization methods are needed to accommodate the high variability in human preferences for explanatory text? (The paper calls for personalization but does not propose concrete techniques.)
2. **Comparison with closed-source models:** How do open-source LLMs stack up against the latest commercial models (e.g., GPT-5.2, Claude 4) on Japanese pathology tasks? (No direct comparison was made.)
3. **Impact of quantization:** What is the effect of different quantization levels on clinical accuracy and reliability, and what is the optimal trade-off for pathology applications? (Quantization was used but not isolated as a variable.)
4. **Root causes of low inter-rater agreement:** What exactly drives the low ICC (≤0.466) in evaluating explanatory text—medical correctness, stylistic preferences, or inconsistent rubrics? (The paper speculates but did not dissect the sources.)

## Limitations
Key uncertainties that may affect reproducibility and interpretation:
- **Prompt/template secrecy:** The exact prompts, few-shot examples, and JSON schemas for tasks are not in the paper and must be extracted from the GitHub repository; minor wording differences could alter performance.
- **Synthetic typo injection methodology:** The algorithm for generating typos (distribution, target selection) is not detailed, making exact recreation of Task B dataset impossible.
- **Subjective evaluation rubric ambiguity:** The 1-5 usability score conflates factual accuracy, clarity, and style; low ICC indicates the metric itself may be unreliable for model comparison.

## Confidence
Assessment of major claim clusters:
- **Thinking models excel in multi-step reasoning tasks (A2):** High (direct evidence: 100% vs. near-chance accuracy).
- **Medical-specialized models are competitive for domain typo correction (B):** Medium (competitive F1 scores observed, but advantage not cleanly isolated from model size).
- **Human preferences for explanatory text (C) are highly variable and subjective:** High (low ICC directly supports variability; claim about stylistic preference as primary cause is plausible but less certain).

## Next Checks
To validate and extend the findings, three concrete steps are recommended:
1. **Prompt & template audit:** Clone the GitHub repository, catalog all task-specific prompts, few-shot examples, and output parsers, and verify they match the paper's methodology.
2. **Synthetic typo injection protocol reconstruction:** From the code, reverse-engineer the exact typo injection algorithm (dictionary, error rates, filters) and test it on a held-out set to confirm the typo distribution.
3. **Independent rater calibration study:** Recruit at least 3 pathologists and 2 clinicians to score 10 random outputs from a top model using the 1-5 scale; compute ICC. If ICC remains <0.3, the rubric requires refinement before meaningful comparisons.
