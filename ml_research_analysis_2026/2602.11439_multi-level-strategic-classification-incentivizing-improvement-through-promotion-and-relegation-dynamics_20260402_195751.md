# Multi-Level Strategic Classification: Incentivizing Improvement through Promotion and Relegation Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11439
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11439
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A multi-level promotion/relegation system with selective classifiers can incentivize agents to honestly improve attributes, achieving arbitrarily high target attributes without external interventions under mild conditions.

## Executive Summary
In strategic classification, agents often resort to cheap manipulations (gaming) rather than genuine improvement to secure favorable predictions. This paper investigates how a principal can design a sequential, multi‑level system—comprising promotion, relegation, and selective classification (abstention)—to steer agents toward honest attribute growth. The model incorporates an agent’s farsightedness (discount factor β), skill retention (γ < 1), and a leg‑up boost δ upon promotion. Faced with a Markov decision process, the agent chooses costly improvement (c+) or cheaper gaming (c–). The principal selects a sequence of thresholds {µ_l} to ensure the agent’s optimal strategy never involves gaming and eventually attains an arbitrarily high target attribute M while reaching the top level.

Theoretically, the paper shows that when (1–βγ)c+ < c–, the set of achievable targets expands beyond what is possible in one‑shot settings. If δ > 0 and the reward rate r is sufficiently large (r ≥ ((1–β)/(1–γ)) c+δ), the linear threshold sequence µ_l = δ(l–1)/(1–γ) is feasible for any M and optimal when r equals the bound. Without leg‑up (δ = 0), an absolute bound applies: M ≤ r / ((1–β)(1–γ)² c+). Simulations on the FICO dataset illustrate key trade‑offs: lower gaming costs force higher rewards and more levels, while retention (γ) expands achievable M more effectively than farsightedness (β).

## Method Summary
The principal designs a dynamic classification system with L hierarchical levels. Each agent possesses a scalar attribute x_t that evolves stochastically. In every period the agent selects an action: costly true improvement (cost c+) or cheaper gaming (cost c–). The classifier selectively abstains: if x ≥ µ_l the agent is promoted to level l+1; if x ≤ µ_{l-1} the agent is relegated to level l-1; otherwise the agent “stays” in the current level. Promotion delivers an immediate leg‑up δ to the attribute. Skill retention γ ∈ (0,1) scales the attribute when moving between levels (including after relegation), modeling loss of skill. The agent’s objective is to maximize the discounted sum of rewards (r upon reaching the top level) minus action costs, forming a Markov decision process (MDP) over states (x, l). The principal chooses the threshold sequence {µ_l} and reward r to guarantee incentive compatibility: the agent’s MDP optimum must avoid gaming and ensure that the top level is reached with attribute at least M. Feasibility conditions are derived by analyzing the Bellman equations and constructing a “no‑gaming” region where improvement strictly dominates gaming.

## Key Results
- Under the mild condition (1–βγ)c+ < c–, the incentivizable region of achievable M is strictly larger than any one‑shot classification setting.
- With a positive leg‑up (δ > 0) and a reward rate satisfying r ≥ ((1–β)/(1–γ)) c+δ, the linear threshold sequence µ_l = δ(l–1)/(1–γ) is feasible for any target M; moreover, this sequence is optimal when r exactly meets the lower bound.
- If δ = 0, an absolute bound on achievable M emerges: M ≤ r / ((1–β)(1–γ)² c+).
- Retention (γ) expands the feasible M more effectively than farsightedness (β) for a wide range of parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism aligns the agent’s incentives with the principal’s goal by creating a ladder of thresholds that can only be climbed via honest improvement. The selective classifier’s “stay” intervals force the agent to increase the attribute to cross the next threshold; gaming would only yield a temporary gain that is likely to decay due to retention (γ < 1) and may trigger relegation. The leg‑up δ provides a discontinuous boost upon promotion, making it easier to reach successive thresholds without needing to game. The condition (1–βγ)c+ < c– ensures that the present value of retained improvement outweighs the immediate cheaper cost of gaming. By spacing thresholds linearly in the level index (µ_l ∝ l), the agent can steadily progress through levels by repeatedly improving in stay regions, receiving leg‑ups, and avoiding relegation. The reward r must be large enough to compensate the cumulative improvement costs but can be minimized at the derived bound. Consequently, under the stated parameter conditions, the agent’s unique optimal policy is to improve honestly and eventually achieve arbitrarily high attributes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map** (plain text)  
Agent → MDP (states: attribute x, level l) → actions: improve (+) or game (–) → transitions: retention γ, promotion/relegation by thresholds µ_l, leg‑up δ on promotion → reward r at top level.  
Classifier module: applies promotion (x ≥ µ_l), relegation (x ≤ µ_{l-1}), or abstain (stay).  
Principal module: designs {µ_l}, r, and monitors feasibility conditions.

**Critical Path**:  
1. Agent starts at a low level with modest attribute.  
2. In “stay” regions, agent chooses improvement (because gaming is deterred).  
3. Attribute crosses a threshold → promotion → leg‑up δ.  
4. Repeat steps 2–3, climbing levels; eventually reaches top level with x ≥ M.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Lower gaming cost c– forces the principal to increase reward r or add more levels to maintain deterrence.  
- Larger leg‑up δ reduces required threshold density but may be costly to implement.  
- Higher retention γ preserves improvement, making it easier to achieve high M; however, excessive γ might slow progression? (Actually γ helps)  
- More levels (larger L) give finer control but increase administrative burden.  
- Reward r must balance agent’s incentive with principal’s expenditure.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Gaming becomes profitable in some region (violates incentive compatibility).  
- No threshold sequence exists (e.g., (1–βγ)c+ ≥ c–).  
- δ = 0 leads to bounded M; target M exceeds bound.  
- Numerical instability when parameters lie near boundary conditions.

**First Experiments**:  
1. **Baseline feasibility**: Simulate the linear threshold policy with paper‑suggested parameters; verify that the agent never games and reaches a high M (e.g., 100).  
2. **Gaming cost sensitivity**: Vary c– and measure the minimal r needed for feasibility; confirm the predicted monotonic relationship.  
3. **Retention vs. farsightedness**: Compare achievable M across varying γ and β to quantify the relative impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can the main feasibility results (e.g., Theorem 4.2) be rigorously extended to multi‑dimensional attribute vectors with arbitrary transition dynamics g(·)?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The Discussion "Scalar vs. Vector Settings" suggests replacing the scalar Lipschitz constant with the maximum singular value of the Jacobian, but no formal proof is given.  
- **Why unresolved**: The scalar analysis critically uses order properties (monotonicity, intervals) that may not translate to vector spaces; extension requires handling more complex geometric and dynamical interactions.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A formal proof of the impossibility region under general vector dynamics, a corresponding condition involving the Jacobian norm, and possibly a counterexample showing when scalar analog fails.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does heterogeneity in agent parameters (costs c+, c−, retention γ, discount β) affect the feasibility of a fixed threshold sequence, and what principal policies can handle such diversity?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The model assumes homogeneous agents; no analysis considers a mixture of types or distributional robustness.  
- **Why unresolved**: Feasibility derived in Section 4 relies on a single set of inequalities; if agents differ, a threshold sequence that deters gaming for one type may induce it for another, violating constraints.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive incentive compatibility constraints for multiple types, characterize the feasible set of universal thresholds, or prove that no universal thresholds exist without additional instruments (e.g., subsidies, randomization). Empirical evaluation on synthetic heterogeneous populations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the optimality gap of the greedy heuristic (Algorithm 2) in solving the principal’s design problem P(M, r)?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Algorithm 2 is presented as a "greedy heuristic" with a correctness guarantee (Theorem 5.1) but no claim of optimality or approximation bound.  
- **Why unresolved**: The problem is non‑convex; greedy construction may not minimize L or maximize M, and the theoretical performance (e.g., approximation ratio) is unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compute exact optimal solutions for small instances via dynamic programming or integer programming; empirically measure the gap across parameter sweeps; attempt to prove a (tight) bound or establish NP‑hardness.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is the ability to abstain (i.e., the ternary classifier) necessary for the mechanism to achieve arbitrarily high targets, or could a binary promotion/relegation scheme suffice?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The model relies on selective classification (abstention) to define "stay" regions, but the authors do not examine whether this component is essential.  
- **Why unresolved**: The feasibility proofs (e.g., Theorem 4.2) use the stay region to create intervals where the agent must improve to reach the next threshold; without abstention, the agent might be forced into binary decisions that could either over‑promote or over‑relegate, breaking the incentive structure.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Formulate a binary variant (e.g., promotion if feature > threshold, relegation otherwise); replicate the analytical feasibility analysis; check via simulations whether arbitrarily high attributes can still be achieved under similar parameter conditions.

## Limitations
- **Parameter realizability**: Conditions such as (1–βγ)c+ < c– and r ≥ ((1–β)/(1–γ)) c+δ require precise alignment of agent costs, discount, retention, and leg‑up, which may be difficult to guarantee in practice.
- **Generalizability of simulations**: Empirical validation is limited to the FICO dataset; whether the findings hold in other domains (e.g., hiring, education) remains unknown.
- **Model simplifications**: Assumes constant linear retention γ, instant leg‑up δ, and homogeneous agents; real‑world progression may be non‑linear and agents may exhibit bounded rationality.
