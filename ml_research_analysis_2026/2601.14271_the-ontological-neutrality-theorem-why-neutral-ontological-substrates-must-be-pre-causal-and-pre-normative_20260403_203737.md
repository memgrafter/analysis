# The Ontological Neutrality Theorem: Why Neutral Ontological Substrates Must Be Pre-Causal and Pre-Normative

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2601.14271
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14271
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Neutral substrates cannot embed causal or normative primitives; they must be limited to entities and identity conditions, with interpretation, evaluation, and explanation externalized to higher layers.

## Executive Summary

Designing ontologies for data systems that remain neutral across persistent legal, political, and analytic disagreement is challenging. The authors address this by formalizing neutrality via two constraints—interpretive non‑commitment (no framework‑variant propositions) and extension stability (substrate remains consistent when any admissible framework is added)—using Floridi’s Levels of Abstraction. They prove the Ontological Neutrality Theorem, which shows that any ontology including causal or normative primitives violates neutrality. Consequently, neutral substrates must be strictly pre‑causal and pre‑normative, restricting foundational assertions to entities and identity conditions while externalizing contested interpretation, evaluation, and explanation to higher layers.

## Method Summary

The paper develops a formal framework based on classical first‑order logic. An ontology *S* is a set of foundational axioms. A set of admissible interpretive frameworks *F* (each a consistent theory) may be added to *S*. Neutrality requires: (1) interpretive non‑commitment: *S* contains no proposition whose truth value differs across frameworks; (2) extension stability: for every framework *F*, the union *S* ∪ *F* is consistent. The central theorem proves that if *S* includes any causal or normative primitive (e.g., `Caused(e1,e2)`, `Obligated(a,act)`), then by the diversity of frameworks there exists some *F* that contradicts that primitive, breaking extension stability. Conversely, if *S* asserts only entity existence and identity conditions—assumed invariant across frameworks—both conditions hold. The proof relies on the meta‑assumption that frameworks are sufficiently diverse and that entity/identity predicates are framework‑independent. The method is primarily theoretical, with an illustrative example of layering but no empirical evaluation.

## Key Results

- Neutrality is characterized by interpretive non‑commitment and extension stability.
- **Ontological Neutrality Theorem:** Any ontology embedding causal or normative primitives cannot satisfy neutrality.
- Therefore, neutral substrates must be pre‑causal and pre‑normative, limited to entities and identity conditions, with interpretation, evaluation, and explanation externalized to higher layers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Restricting the Foundational Level of Abstraction

The paper shows that neutrality is achieved by selecting a Level of Abstraction (LoA) that only includes invariant observables—entity existence and identity—and excludes framework‑variant predicates such as causal or normative relations. This restriction ensures that every substrate assertion holds in all admissible interpretive frameworks, which directly yields interpretive non‑commitment (no framework‑variant propositions are asserted) and extension stability (adding any framework maintains consistency). The core assumption is that entities and their identity conditions are stable across frameworks whereas disagreements arise only at the level of causation, norms, or evaluations. If a domain exhibits irreducible contestation over entity identity (e.g., whether two records refer to the same real‑world actor), this assumption fails and a neutral substrate cannot be constructed without favoring one view.

### Mechanism 2: Equivalence of Extension Stability and Exclusion of Causal/Normative Primitives

Extension stability—the requirement that *S* ∪ *F* be consistent for each framework *F*—is logically equivalent to omitting causal and normative primitives. If *S* contained a causal or deontic atomic formula *p*, the framework‑dependence of such predicates guarantees the existence of two admissible frameworks *F*₁ ⊢ *p* and *F*₂ ⊢ ¬*p*. Combining *S* with *F*₂ would then produce a contradiction, violating extension stability. Conversely, if *S* contains only entity/identity axioms, no framework can contradict these (by the invariance assumption), so *S* ∪ *F* stays consistent for any *F*. Thus, the necessity and sufficiency of pre‑causal/pre‑normative content for extension stability follow.

### Mechanism 3: Layered Architecture Externalizing Interpretation

The proposed solution organizes the system into distinct Levels of Organization (LoO). The foundational LoO‑0 (the neutral substrate) records only what exists and how entities are identified. Higher LoOs (LoO‑1+) host interpretive frameworks that attach causal graphs, deontic rules, or evaluations to the substrate’s entities via additional predicates. Because the substrate is silent on contested matters, multiple conflicting frameworks can be added monotonically without substrate revision; disagreement is represented as different views on the same base rather than as contradictory substrate facts. The paper notes that reification of claims (turning a statement into an entity) can help maintain neutrality by allowing frameworks to ascribe truth values without embedding the claim as an axiom. This layering is described in Section 5.3, with reification discussed in Section 5.5. Success depends on disciplined separation: frameworks must never assert statements that directly negate substrate facts.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**

1. **Neutral Substrate (LoO‑0):** Stores entities, identity criteria (persistent IDs), and persistence conditions; excludes causal/normative predicates; may include reified Assertion entities.
2. **Interpretive Frameworks (LoO‑1+):** Modules that link to substrate entities via relations (e.g., caused, obligated, permitted); each framework corresponds to an admissible interpretation (e.g., a jurisdiction’s law); frameworks may conflict with each other but must not contradict the substrate.
3. **Boundary Interface:** API enabling queries against the substrate alone or combined with specific frameworks; ensures substrate queries remain neutral and merges results from selected interpretive layers.
4. **Registry of Admissible Frameworks:** Metadata catalogue describing each framework’s scope, assumptions, and version; used to validate extension stability during onboarding.

**Critical path:**

1. Identify the application domain and determine the minimal set of entity types and identity conditions required for accountability.
2. Define the substrate schema (classes, identification attributes, disjointness constraints, persistence rules) while rigorously excluding any causal or normative properties.
3. Implement the substrate as an immutable database or graph store.
4. For each required interpretive paradigm, develop a separate framework module that maps its concepts onto substrate entities.
5. Provide query composition tools allowing users to select which framework(s) to apply for interpreting neutral facts.
6. Test extension stability by adding competing frameworks and checking that the substrate remains consistent and unchanged.

**Design tradeoffs:**

- **Expressiveness vs. Neutrality:** Adding richer relational structures (e.g., temporal chains) is safe if they remain within invariant observables; any relation that implies causation (e.g., “triggered”) must be avoided or reified to keep the substrate neutral.
- **Performance:** Cross‑layer queries can become complex; the substrate should be kept lean to ensure fast baseline access.
- **Interoperability:** The substrate must adopt domain‑neutral identifiers (e.g., UUIDs) to avoid privileging any framework’s naming conventions.
- **Evolutionary flexibility:** New frameworks can be added without modifying the substrate, but if the substrate’s identity model is later found insufficient, extending it may violate invariance; therefore identity design must be thorough from the start.

**Failure signatures:**

- Contradiction arises when combining the substrate with a newly added framework, detected by a reasoner as unsatisfiable constraints.
- Frequent modifications to the substrate schema in response to new frameworks, indicating the substrate was not truly neutral.
- Query results differing across frameworks solely due to evaluative predicates in the substrate (which should belong to interpretive layers).
- Logs showing direct insertion of causal or normative triples into the substrate store.

**First 3 experiments:**

1. **Baseline neutrality test:** Populate the substrate with a simple example (e.g., incident reports). Implement two conflicting legal frameworks (negligence and strict liability) as separate layers. Demonstrate that both frameworks can query the same substrate data without modification and produce internally consistent but possibly contradictory outcomes.
2. **Extension stability stress test:** Add a third framework that contradicts the first two. Verify that the substrate remains unchanged and consistent with each framework individually; ensure that no inconsistency propagates back to the substrate layer.
3. **Reification effectiveness:** Model a reified Assertion entity representing the causal claim “Agent A caused Outcome B”. Show that multiple frameworks can assign different truth values to the same assertion without altering the substrate’s core facts. Compare with a design where causal claims are embedded directly, which would immediately break consistency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Operationalizing neutrality in concrete domains.** The paper states that demonstrating the constraints in specific areas (e.g., legal, biomedical, environmental) is future work. Without such case studies, the practical feasibility of building neutral substrates remains untested.
2. **Adapting existing upper ontologies.** It is unclear how ontologies like BFO or DOLCE—which often embed causal or normative assumptions—can be profiled or extended to satisfy neutrality without losing their established utility. Empirical analysis and modularization strategies are needed.
3. **Ensuring consistent reification.** Reification is proposed as a way to keep causal/normative claims out of the substrate, but the paper offers no mechanism to guarantee that reified claims are not inadvertently asserted as facts. Tool‑supported design patterns or validation rules are required to prevent such errors in practice.
4. **Handling domains with contested identity.** The theorem’s boundary condition admits that neutrality is impossible where entity identity itself is irreducibly disputed (e.g., whether two records refer to the same real‑world actor). A typology of identity contestation and alternative architectural approaches (e.g., multi‑substrate or context‑aware referencing) are necessary to address such cases.

## Limitations

- The impossibility proof assumes that for every causal or normative primitive, the set of admissible frameworks is sufficiently diverse to include both a framework asserting it and one denying it. This may not hold in practice.
- The theorem relies on the strong assumption that entity existence and identity conditions are invariant across all frameworks; many domains exhibit irreducible disagreement over identity.
- The formal proof is sketched in natural language and has not been fully formalized or machine‑checked, leaving potential gaps. Additionally, no empirical evaluation demonstrates the approach in a real‑world data system.

## Confidence

- **Impossibility theorem (neutrality incompatible with causal/normative primitives):** High
- **Characterization via interpretive non‑commitment and extension stability:** Medium
- **Practical principle (neutral substrates must be pre‑causal/pre‑normative, externalizing interpretation):** Medium

2. **Empirical invariance study:** Select a candidate domain (e.g., legal incidents), gather at least three distinct interpretive frameworks, and verify whether basic entity‑existence and identity predicates are indeed invariant across them.
3. **Prototype stress test:** Build a minimal neutral substrate (e.g., a property graph with UUID‑identified entities) and implement two conflicting interpretive layers. Test whether adding a third layer forces substrate revision or introduces inconsistencies, and evaluate the effectiveness of reification in maintaining neutrality.
