# Multilevel Training for Kolmogorov Arnold Networks

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.04827
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04827
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Multilevel training for spline KANs improves accuracy and efficiency by 2–3 orders of magnitude compared to conventional KAN training and MLPs.

## Executive Summary
Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) with spline activations admit efficient multilevel training. This work establishes an equivalence between spline KANs and multichannel MLPs with power ReLU activations via a linear change of basis; the transformation matrix acts as a preconditioner, causing gradient descent in the ReLU basis to prioritize smooth functions while the spline basis supports localized, oscillatory updates. Based on this insight, the authors develop a multilevel training algorithm using uniform spline knot refinement and geometric interpolation operators, defining a properly nested hierarchy that preserves coarse-model progress and ensures complementary optimization across levels. Numerical experiments on function regression and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) demonstrate substantial improvements: for 1D regression, multilevel spline KAN achieved an MSE of 3.67×10⁻⁵ versus 1.65×10⁻³ for coarse spline KAN and 2.35×10⁰ for fine ReLU KAN; for the 2D Poisson PINN, multilevel spline KAN reached lower error faster than an MLP and significantly outperformed multilevel ReLU KAN.

## Method Summary
The core of the method is the observed linear equivalence between a KAN with spline activations and a multichannel MLP with power ReLU activations. This equivalence is realized by a change-of-basis matrix that reweights the contributions of the basis functions. This matrix effectively preconditionens gradient descent: in the ReLU basis, updates smooth out the function; in the spline basis, updates are localized and can capture oscillations. To exploit this, the authors construct a multilevel hierarchy by uniformly refining the spline knot grid. At each level, a coarse KAN is trained; then the knot grid is refined and a geometric interpolation operator transfers the coarse spline coefficients to the finer grid, initializing the next-level KAN. This interpolation ensures that the nested hierarchy preserves the coarse model’s solution, and training at finer levels complements rather than overwrites previous progress. The overall algorithm iterates through levels, yielding faster convergence and higher accuracy than training a single fine model from scratch or using ReLU-based MLPs.

## Key Results
- Multilevel spline KAN achieved an MSE of 3.67×10⁻⁵ on 1D regression, improving over coarse spline KAN (1.65×10⁻³) and fine ReLU KAN (2.35×10⁰) by 2–3 orders of magnitude.
- For the 2D Poisson PINN, multilevel spline KAN converged to lower error faster than an MLP and significantly outperformed multilevel ReLU KAN.
- Across tasks, the proposed approach demonstrates 2–3 orders of magnitude improvement in accuracy and training efficiency compared to conventional training or comparable MLPs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the linear change of basis between spline KANs and multichannel MLPs with power ReLU activations. The transformation matrix acts as a preconditioner: when optimizing in the ReLU basis, gradients naturally suppress high-frequency components, gradually building smooth functions; conversely, in the spline basis, the same gradients become localized, allowing oscillatory adjustments that refine details. The multilevel training algorithm harnesses this by starting with a coarse spline grid that captures global smooth structure. Uniform knot refinement consistently increases resolution, while geometric interpolation (e.g., linear or higher-order) transfers coarse coefficients to the fine grid exactly, ensuring the coarse solution is embedded in the fine model’s initial state. Subsequent fine-level training then focuses on adding high-frequency details without losing the coarse-level accuracy. This complementary optimization across scales yields dramatic efficiency gains. In contrast, standard MLPs lack such a structured basis and multilevel methods fail to preserve coarse progress, leading to slower convergence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs)**: KANs replace fixed activation functions on edges with learnable univariate functions, often parameterized as splines, enabling adaptive representation. *Why needed*: Provides a flexible, interpretable alternative to MLPs with potentially better sample efficiency. *Quick check*: Implement a single KAN layer with spline activations and verify it can fit a simple nonlinear function with far fewer parameters than an MLP.
- **Spline parameterization**: Represent activation functions via spline coefficients on a fixed knot grid; the function is a piecewise polynomial. *Why needed*: Allows smooth, local modifications and efficient derivative computation. *Quick check*: Construct a cubic spline from coefficients and knots; ensure continuity and evaluate at arbitrary points.
- **Change of basis / Preconditioning**: The linear transformation that maps spline coefficients to equivalent ReLU‑weighted channels reveals that the same function can be expressed in two bases. *Why needed*: Understanding this equivalence explains why optimization behaves differently in each basis and suggests preconditioning strategies. *Quick check*: For a small network, compute both representations and verify they produce identical outputs.
- **Multilevel (multiscale) methods**: Coarse‑grid correction and interpolation are classic techniques in numerical PDEs; here they are adapted to neural network training. *Why needed*: To build a hierarchy of models where coarse solutions inform finer ones, reducing computational cost. *Quick check*: Apply a two‑level method to a simple linear system and observe faster convergence versus single‑level.
- **Geometric interpolation operators**: Interpolation that maps coarse spline coefficients to a finer knot grid while preserving the underlying function. *Why needed*: Guarantees that the coarse solution is a valid initial guess for the fine model, preventing loss of progress. *Quick check*: Interpolate a cubic spline from coarse knots to fine knots and confirm the interpolated spline matches the original at coarse knot locations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Input data → Coarse spline KAN (level 0) → Train on coarse level → Uniform knot refinement → Geometric interpolation (coarse→fine) → Finer spline KAN (level 1) → Train on fine level → (Repeat refinement/interpolation/training for deeper levels) → Final trained fine KAN.

**Critical Path**:  
1. Initialize a spline KAN with a uniform knot grid (e.g., G coarse knots per dimension).  
2. Train using gradient descent until approximate convergence.  
3. Refine the knot grid uniformly (e.g., double the number of knots).  
4. Construct a finer KAN with the new knot grid.  
5. Interpolate the coarse spline coefficients to the fine grid using a geometric interpolation operator (e.g., linear or spline‑based).  
6. Set the fine KAN’s parameters to these interpolated values.  
7. Train the fine KAN, optionally using a smaller learning rate.  
8. If further refinement is desired, return to step 3; else output the model.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Uniform vs. adaptive refinement*: Uniform refinement simplifies hierarchy construction and ensures nested grids, but may place knots unnecessarily in smooth regions, increasing parameter count. Adaptive refinement could concentrate knots where needed but breaks the simple nested structure.  
- *Interpolation operator choice*: Simple linear interpolation preserves coarse‑level values exactly but may not maintain spline smoothness; higher‑order interpolation (e.g., spline) can preserve continuity but may introduce small oscillations.  
- *Memory vs. speed*: Storing coarse models for later interpolation adds memory overhead but avoids retraining from scratch; the multilevel approach trades extra memory for faster convergence.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Loss of coarse‑level accuracy after interpolation (e.g., fine model initial loss much higher than coarse final loss) indicates a flawed interpolation or non‑nested hierarchy.  
- Divergence or oscillation during fine‑level training suggests inappropriate learning rates or that the coarse model has already captured relevant features, leaving little room for improvement.  
- No significant improvement over single‑level training may indicate that the function lacks multiscale structure or that the knot refinement strategy is mismatched to the problem.

**First Experiments**:  
1. Validate the linear equivalence by constructing a tiny spline KAN (e.g., 1 input, 1 output, 2 spline knots) and deriving the corresponding multichannel MLP with power ReLU; confirm identical outputs on a test set.  
2. Reproduce the 1D regression experiment: fit a known smooth function (e.g., \(f(x) = \sin(5x)\)) using multilevel spline KAN, single‑level coarse/fine spline KAN, and a ReLU MLP; compare MSE and training iterations.  
3. Extend to a 2D Poisson PINN (as in the paper): solve \(-\Delta u = f\) on a unit square with Dirichlet boundary conditions using multilevel spline KAN, a standard MLP, and multilevel ReLU KAN; track error decay over epochs.

## Limitations
- The theoretical equivalence derivation assumes specific spline knot placement and activation regularity; its applicability to a broader range of functions, non‑uniform knots, or higher‑dimensional splines remains to be fully investigated.
- The multilevel algorithm is evaluated only on a 1D regression task and a 2D Poisson PINN; scalability to higher‑dimensional PDEs, complex geometries, or noisy data is unknown.
- Baseline comparisons use MLPs that may not be optimally tuned; without comprehensive hyperparameter searches and open‑source code, the reported advantages might be overstated.

## Confidence
- Equivalence theory: Medium
- Multilevel algorithm efficacy: Low
- Generalization claims: Low
