# Evaluating LLM-persona Generated Distributions for Decision-making
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06357
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06357
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLM‑generated distributions are useful for operational decisions, especially with limited data, and should be evaluated via decision‑aware competitive ratios rather than statistical distances.

## Executive Summary
Operational decisions often rely on accurate demand distributions. Large language models (LLMs) can generate synthetic distributions, but evaluating their quality for decision‑making is non‑trivial. Conventional metrics like Wasserstein distance measure statistical closeness but do not guarantee good downstream decisions. This paper introduces LLM‑SAA, a framework where an LLM produces a distribution that is used in sample‑average approximation. To assess distribution quality, the authors propose decision‑aware competitive ratios: the ratio of the reward achieved by a decision optimized under the estimated distribution to the optimum using the true distribution, considering both worst‑case and average‑case parameter settings. They develop efficient algorithms to compute these ratios for three canonical problems—assortment, pricing, and newsvendor—and conduct extensive experiments. Results show that LLM‑generated distributions often match or exceed the performance of small empirical samples and consistently beat random baselines, particularly when historical data are scarce. Moreover, standard statistical distances are found to mislead, while persona‑based prompting significantly boosts decision quality despite poor individual‑level imitation, highlighting the importance of aggregate diversity.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses three real‑world datasets. For assortment optimization, the SUSHI dataset provides 600 rankings of 10 sushi items. For pricing, the Ballesteros et al. data gives willingness‑to‑pay for 6 cocoa products from 100 respondents. For the newsvendor, H&M fashion sales data for Trouser items (300 items, 30 weeks each) is used. The authors prompt several LLMs (GPT‑4o, GPT‑5‑mini, Gemini 3 Flash, Mistral Large 3) via four generation strategies: (i) Sampling directly from the model; (ii) Persona‑sampling, conditioning on demographic personas; (iii) Batch‑generation, producing a full set in one call; and (iv) Description, asking the model to output parametric forms (e.g., Normal mean/variance) with or without few‑shot examples.

Given a ground‑truth distribution \(F\), each LLM‑generated method yields an empirical distribution \(\hat{F}\). The decision‑making pipeline follows sample‑average approximation: for a problem‑specific parameter \(\theta\) (e.g., costs, budget, critical fractile) the optimizer chooses an action \(a\) that maximizes the expected reward according to \(\hat{F}\). The quality of \(\hat{F}\) is measured by competitive ratios: WorstCR = \(\inf_\theta \frac{R_\theta(\hat{a})}{R_\theta(a^*)}\) and AvgCR = \(\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\frac{R_\theta(\hat{a})}{R_\theta(a^*)}\big]\), where \(a^*\) is optimal under \(F\). To compute these efficiently, the authors exploit structural properties of each problem (e.g., monotonicity of optimal price, the MNL model’s substitution pattern, the newsvendor’s critical fractile) and derive algorithms that reduce the continuous search over \(\theta\) to a finite set of breakpoints. They also compute decision‑agnostic distances (Wasserstein, Kolmogorov) for comparison. Baselines include a Random uniform distribution, ‘\(d\)‑real‑data’ (\(d\) samples drawn from \(F\)), and for assortment the Popularity‑score heuristic.

## Key Results
- LLM‑generated distributions typically match or surpass the performance of small empirical samples (e.g., 20 real observations) and always outperform uniform random baselines, with the gap most pronounced when real data are scarce.
- Statistical distances such as Wasserstein often misrepresent distribution quality: the Random baseline attains lower Wasserstein than some LLM methods yet yields far worse competitive ratios, underscoring that decision‑aware metrics are essential.
- Persona‑based prompting achieves the highest competitive ratios across problems despite its PersonaMAE (individual imitation error) being nearly identical to a Shuffled baseline; this indicates that the benefit arises from aggregate diversity rather than accurate individual modeling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Decision‑aware competitive ratios
- **Claim:** Competitive ratios (comparing the reward of a decision based on \(\hat{F}\) to the optimum under \(F\)) provide a more valid evaluation for decision‑making than statistical distances.
- **Mechanism:** The metric directly measures downstream impact on expected reward, sweeping over problem parameters. A distribution can be “good enough” if it captures decision‑relevant features (e.g., the point where demand drops) even if it deviates statistically in other aspects.
- **Core assumption:** The decision‑maker cares only about maximizing expected reward, not about matching every aspect of \(F\).
- **Evidence:** The paper defines \(C_\theta(F,\hat{F})\) in Section 3.2 and shows in Section 4.1 that Wasserstein rankings conflict with CR rankings (e.g., Random has best Wasserstein but worst CR).
- **Break condition:** If the decision is extremely sensitive to infinitesimal differences (e.g., a narrow demand spike), a distribution with large statistical distance could still yield near‑optimal decisions; the CR might then over‑look subtle but critical errors.

### Mechanism 2: Persona‑based prompting induces useful aggregate diversity
- **Claim:** Conditioning generation on diverse personas improves downstream decision quality even though individual‑level imitation is poor.
- **Mechanism:** Personas encourage the LLM to explore a broader range of the outcome space, producing a synthetic distribution whose aggregate shape (support, moments) better matches the population heterogeneity rather than each individual’s response.
- **Core assumption:** The decision problem (e.g., assortment, pricing) is sensitive to overall dispersion or multi‑modality, not just the mode.
- **Evidence:** Section 4.3 reports that PersonaMAE ≈ ShuffledMAE (no individual improvement) but Persona‑sampling attains the highest CR metrics. Section 4.2 shows it outperforms the Popularity baseline in “Hard” regimes where simple marginals fail.
- **Break condition:** If decisions target specific subpopulations or if personas are poorly aligned with true population segments, the induced diversity may become irrelevant noise and degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3: Efficient worst‑case ratio computation via structural properties
- **Claim:** Worst‑case competitive ratios over continuous parameter spaces can be computed exactly (or with small discrete sets) for the studied problems.
- **Mechanism:** Each problem’s reward function has monotonicity/unimodality in the optimal action relative to the parameter. The minimax ratio \(\inf_\theta \frac{R_\theta(\hat{a})}{R_\theta(a^*)}\) reduces to checking a finite set of breakpoints (e.g., costs where optimal price changes) and verifying the existence of parameters that make \(\hat{a}\) the unique maximizer under \(\hat{F}\) while \(a^*\) is unique under \(F\).
- **Core assumption:** The parameter space \(\Theta\) (e.g., costs, sizes, critical fractiles) fully captures uncertainty; reward functions are known and smooth; ties are broken adversarially.
- **Evidence:** The paper provides Theorem 1 for assortment, Lemmas 2‑3 for pricing, Lemmas 4‑5 for newsvendor, and Algorithms 1‑3 that implement the search over a small candidate set.
- **Break condition:** If reward functions become non‑smooth or have many local optima, the finite breakpoint approach may miss true worst cases. Computational cost grows exponentially with the size of the decision space (e.g., \(2^n\) assortments for \(n\) items).

## Foundational Learning
- **Sample Average Approximation (SAA)**
  - Why needed: LLM‑SAA directly applies SAA by replacing the unknown true expectation with an empirical average based on the LLM‑generated samples. This is the standard technique for stochastic programming with limited data.
  - Quick check: If \(\hat{F}\) is uniform over generated samples \(\{\xi_i\}_{i=1}^N\), the SAA objective \(\max_a \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_\theta(a,\xi_i)\) is exactly the empirical estimate of \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}}[r_\theta(a,\xi)]\), leading to the decision \(\hat{a} = \arg\max_a \hat{R}_\theta(a)\).

- **Competitive Ratio in Robust/Online Optimization**
  - Why needed: The paper’s evaluation metric is borrowed from competitive analysis; it measures the robustness of a decision based on an estimated distribution against the optimum using the true distribution, under worst‑case parameter perturbations.
  - Quick check: In pricing, if \(F\) and \(\hat{F}\) have survival functions that drop at different prices, the optimal price under each distribution will be at one of those drop points. As cost \(c\) varies, the ratio \(R_c(\hat{a})/R_c(a^*)\) will be piecewise and may achieve its minimum when \(c\) lies between the two drop points.

- **Decision‑Making Under Unknown Demand (Newsvendor, Assortment, Pricing)**
  - Why needed: Each problem has a well‑studied formulation (e.g., newsvendor’s critical fractile, MNL model for assortment, linear demand for pricing) whose structural properties are essential for deriving efficient worst‑case ratio algorithms.
  - Quick check: For newsvendor with underage cost \(q\) and overage cost \(1-q\), the optimal order quantity \(a^*\) satisfies \(F(a^*) = 1-q\) (the critical fractile). If \(q\) increases, \(a^*\) increases because the critical fractile becomes larger.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
LLM Generation Module → Distribution Constructor → Optimization Solver → Competitive Ratio Evaluator
Competitive Ratio Evaluator (AvgCR) → Parameter Sweep Engine
Baseline Comparator → Distribution Constructor

### Critical path
1. Load ground‑truth \(F\) from dataset.
2. For each generation method/model: generate multiple \(\hat{F}_k\).
3. For each \(\hat{F}_k\): compute WorstCR(\(F,\hat{F}_k\)) via worst‑case algorithm; compute AvgCR(\(F,\hat{F}_k\)) by sampling \(\theta \sim \Pi\) and solving for \(\hat{a}\) each time.
4. Also compute statistical distances (Wasserstein, Kolmogorov) between \(F\) and \(\hat{F}_k\).
5. Aggregate metrics over \(k\) and across problem instances/products.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Precision vs. scalability:** WorstCR algorithms are exact but exponential in \(n\) (assortment). For larger \(n\), need approximations (e.g., heuristic search over candidate decisions).
- **LLM cost vs. diversity:** Batch‑generation reduces API calls but may produce less diverse samples than repeated sampling. Persona‑sampling increases diversity but requires persona design.
- **Few‑shot info leakage:** Providing real examples risks overfitting \(\hat{F}\) to particular \(F\); must ensure examples are from outside \(F\)’s support.
- **Continuous vs. discrete \(\hat{F}\):** Descriptive prompts yield parametric forms (e.g., Normal), which may smooth out multimodality present in sampling methods.

### Failure signatures
- **Disconnect between Wasserstein and CR:** If a method has low Wasserstein but low CR, it matches \(F\) statistically but misses decision‑critical features (e.g., tail behavior for pricing). This signals statistical distances are misleading.
- **WorstCR << AvgCR:** The \(\hat{F}\) is fragile; small parameter changes cause large CR drops. Indicates overfitting to specific \(\theta\).
- **PersonaMAE ≈ ShuffledMAE but high CR:** LLM isn’t imitating individuals but producing useful aggregate diversity—this is expected for persona methods, not a failure.
- **LLM methods underperform Random in WorstCR:** \(\hat{F}\) is actively harmful; maybe the LLM is over‑confident in a wrong mode.

### First 3 experiments
- **Unit test worst‑case ratio algorithm:** For a small problem (e.g., \(n=5\) items, known \(F,\hat{F}\) with finite supports), manually verify the computed WorstCR matches brute‑force search over \(\theta\).
- **Baseline sanity check:** Confirm Random baseline has uniform distribution; verify \(d\)-real‑data CR approaches 1 as \(d \to |F|\).
- **Persona diversity test:** For persona‑sampling, compute pairwise distances between generated rankings; ensure diversity is higher than non‑persona sampling. Also check PersonaMAE vs. ShuffledMAE difference is small (as expected).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Automatic prompt optimization:** How can we automatically optimize LLM prompts to maximize downstream decision quality, as measured by competitive ratios? The paper used fixed prompts; it remains unknown which prompt characteristics matter most.
2. **Understanding and designing decision‑aware metrics:** Why do standard decision‑agnostic distribution distances often misalign with decision‑aware performance, and can we design new metrics that reliably correlate with downstream decision quality? A theoretical characterization of the misalignment is lacking.
3. **Automatic persona discovery:** Does the benefit of persona‑based generation stem from increased aggregate diversity, and can we automatically discover a set of personas that optimizes decision quality? The paper’s personas are manually defined; optimal persona granularity and features are unknown.
4. **Generalization to complex decisions:** To what extent do the encouraging results for LLM‑SAA in low‑data regimes generalize to more complex, multi‑stage or combinatorial decision problems? The study focuses on three canonical single‑stage problems.

## Limitations
- **Limited scope:** Only three OR problems (assortment, pricing, newsvendor) and a few datasets are studied; no comparisons to advanced generative models (e.g., GANs, normalizing flows) or multi‑stage decisions.
- **Persona design sensitivity:** The advantage of persona‑prompting relies on a fixed set of manually crafted personas; its robustness to different persona definitions and its performance for subpopulation‑specific decisions remain unexplored.
- **Scalability:** Exact worst‑case competitive ratio algorithms scale exponentially with problem size, potentially limiting practicality for large‑scale instances (e.g., >20 items).

## Confidence
- Decision‑aware competitive ratios are more valid than statistical distances: **High**
- Persona‑based prompting improves decisions through aggregate diversity: **Medium**
- Efficient worst‑case competitive ratio computation for canonical OR problems: **High**

## Next Checks
