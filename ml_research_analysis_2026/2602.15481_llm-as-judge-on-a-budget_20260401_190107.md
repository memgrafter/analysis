# LLM-as-Judge on a Budget

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.15481
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15481
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Variance-adaptive multi-armed bandit algorithms (ROBIN and ROBIN-HOOD) minimize worst-case error in LLM-as-judge evaluation, achieving ~50% improvement over uniform allocation.

## Executive Summary
LLM-as-judge evaluation is widely used to score model outputs, but repeated queries to an LLM judge yield stochastic scores with varying uncertainties across prompt-response pairs. Given a fixed query budget, the goal is to allocate those queries to minimize the maximum absolute estimation error across all pairs. This paper recasts the problem as a multi-armed bandit (MAB) where each arm corresponds to a prompt-response pair and the reward variance differs across arms. The authors propose two variance-adaptive allocation algorithms: ROBIN (when true variances are known) greedily selects the arm with the highest variance-per-pull, while ROBIN-HOOD (when variances are unknown) first explores uniformly to estimate variances and then uses upper confidence bounds to guide further pulls. Both achieve a worst-case error of order √(∑σ_i²/B) with high probability, which is near-optimal. Empirical evaluation on the HelpSteer2 dataset with Llama 3.1 8B and GPT-4.1 nano judges across four attributes shows that ROBIN-HOOD reduces worst-case error by approximately 50% compared to uniform allocation at the same budget, and it improves correlation with human ratings as the budget increases.

## Method Summary
The task: given K prompt-response pairs and a total query budget B, allocate a number of LLM judge queries to each pair to estimate its true mean score, minimizing the maximum absolute error max_i |s_i - ˆs_i|. The key challenge is that the variance of the judge’s scores differs across pairs. The solution frames this as a fixed-budget multi-armed bandit problem with a max-error objective. When variances σ_i² are known, the optimal allocation is proportional to σ_i. ROBIN implements this online via a greedy rule: always pull the arm i that maximizes σ_i² / n_i(current pulls). When variances are unknown, ROBIN-HOOD first performs an initial uniform exploration phase (t₀ pulls per arm) to obtain variance estimates, then uses an upper confidence bound ¯V_i(t) = ˆσ_i(t)² / (1 - √(4 log(4KB/δ)/n_i(t))) as a surrogate for σ_i² and continues with the same greedy selection rule using ¯V_i. The algorithms guarantee that the worst-case error scales as O(√(∑σ_i²/B)) up to logarithmic factors.

## Key Results
- ROBIN-HOOD reduces worst-case error by ≈50% compared to uniform allocation at budgets of 50k–100k queries on HelpSteer2.
- Equivalently, ROBIN-HOOD achieves the same error as uniform using 100k queries with only 50k queries, halving the required budget.
- As the budget grows, the correlation between the estimated scores and human ratings (Pearson, Spearman, Kendall) improves monotonically for ROBIN-HOOD, while uniform allocation’s correlation plateaus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The fixed-budget LLM evaluation problem is equivalent to a multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem where arms correspond to prompt‑response pairs and the objective is to minimize the maximum absolute estimation error across all arms.
- **Mechanism:** Each query to the LLM judge yields a noisy score; allocating the budget becomes deciding how many times to pull each arm. Reformulating as MAB makes variance‑adaptive allocation natural: high‑variance arms require more pulls to bound error.
- **Core assumption:** Scores from repeated queries are independent with zero mean; variances differ across pairs but are constant over time.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We frame this as a multi‑armed bandit problem… minimize the maximum absolute estimation error across all pairs."
  - [section 2] "We formalize LLM judge evaluation as a variance‑adaptive resource allocation problem through a multi‑armed bandit (MAB) lens."
  - [corpus] Neighbors discuss LLM‑as‑judge and preference alignment, but do not address MAB framing directly. Evidence is tangential.
- **Break condition:** If score noise exhibits strong autocorrelation or systematic bias, the independent‑arm model breaks. If variances are nearly homogeneous, the problem collapses to uniform allocation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** With known variances, the greedy algorithm **ROBIN** (pull the arm maximizing σ_i² / n_i(t)) converges to the variance‑proportional allocation n_i ∝ σ_i and achieves worst‑case error **O**(√(∑σ_i²/B)).
- **Mechanism:** The rule balances arms with high uncertainty (large σ_i²) against those under‑sampled (small n_i). Over B rounds the online greedy process yields the same allocation as the offline solution that minimizes the max error.
- **Core assumption:** The true variances σ_i² are known to the algorithm; scores are sub‑Gaussian (or bounded) to apply concentration inequalities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "ROBIN (known variances) greedily selects arms with highest variance‑per‑pull."
  - [page 5, Alg.1] "Selects the arm i_t that maximizes the ratio σ_i² / n_i(t‑1)."
  - [page 6, Lemma 2] "ROBIN pulls Arm‑i at least ⌊λ_i B⌋ and at most ⌈λ_i B⌉ times."
  - [corpus] No specific evidence on ROBIN; corpus focuses on broader LLM‑judge work.
- **Break condition:** The analysis assumes no ties in the greedy selection (non‑integer λ_i). With very small B relative to K, rounding may cause deviation from the proportional allocation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** With **unknown** variances, **ROBIN‑HOOD** (exploration + UCB‑guided greedy pulls) attains the same error rate **O**(√(∑σ_i²/B)) up to a logarithmic factor, adaptively learning which arms are noisy.
- **Mechanism:** First pull each arm t₀ times (uniform exploration) to obtain initial variance estimates. Afterwards, use upper confidence bounds ¯V_i(t) = ˆσ_i(t)² / (1 – √(4 log(4KB/δ)/n_i(t))) as a proxy for σ_i² and continue the greedy pull rule from Mechanism 2. The UCB ensures with high probability that pulls remain proportional to the true variances.
- **Core assumption:** The score noise is Gaussian (or sub‑Gaussian) to guarantee sharp variance concentration; the exploration budget t₀ must be logarithmic in K to estimate variances accurately.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "ROBIN‑HOOD (unknown variances) first explores uniformly to estimate variances, then uses upper confidence bounds to guide allocation."
  - [page 8, Alg.2] "Pulls arm maximizing ¯V_i(t‑1)/n_i(t‑1) after initial exploration."
  - [page 9, Theorem 5] "ROBIN‑HOOD achieves WCE ≤ O(√(∑σ_i²/B · log(4KB/δ))."
  - [page 10, Cor.9] "With probability ≥1–δ/2, for all t,i: σ_i² ≤ ¯V_i(t) ≤ 3σ_i²."
  - [corpus] No direct neighborhood evidence; UCB approach is novel for this LLM‑as‑judge budget problem.
- **Break condition:** The guarantee relies on the UCB concentrating; heavy‑tailed noise or non‑stationary variances could invalidate the bound. If t₀ is too small, the variance estimates become unreliable and high‑variance arms may be starved.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** LLM Judge -> Arm Manager -> Allocator (ROBIN/ROBIN-HOOD); Exploration Controller (only for unknown variance)

**Critical path:**
1. Initialize n_i = 0 for all i.
2. If variances unknown, perform t₀ pulls per arm (uniform exploration).
3. For each remaining round until budget B:
   a. Compute score = (σ_i²/n_i) for known variances or (¯V_i/n_i) for unknown.
   b. Choose arm i_t = argmax score.
   c. Query judge, observe X.
   d. Update n_i, ˆs_i, ˆσ_i², and recompute ¯V_i (if needed).
4. Return final ˆs_i = average of observed scores for each arm.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Exploration length t₀: larger t₀ improves variance estimates but reduces exploitable budget; smaller risks mis‑allocation.
- Confidence δ: smaller δ forces longer exploration (t₀ ∝ log(1/δ)) and looser UCBs; too large δ causes optimistic bias.
- Complexity: Each round O(K) to find argmax; feasible for moderate K, but may need indexing structures for very large K.

**Failure signatures:**
- Under‑estimated variance for some arms → those arms receive too few pulls → high worst‑case error.
- t₀ too small: initial variance estimates are noisy, causing persistent mis‑allocation.
- δ mis‑specified: either wasted exploration (δ too small) or insufficient coverage (δ too large).
- Non‑stationary σ_i: if true variance drifts, UCBs become outdated and allocation lags.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline comparison: Run ROBIN-HOOD, ROBIN, and Uniform on HelpSteer2 with Llama 3.1 8B and GPT-4.1 nano across four attributes; measure worst-case error at 50k and 100k queries (Tab. 2, Fig. 3‑5).
2. Cost‑savings analysis: For each model‑attribute pair, compare the budget at which ROBIN-HOOD reaches the same error as Uniform at 100k (≈ 50k, 50% reduction).
3. Correlation validation: Using ROBIN-HOOD estimates, compute Pearson, Spearman, Kendall correlation with human ratings as budget increases; expect monotonic improvement (Fig. 6‑7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can contextual features (e.g., query complexity, answer length, semantic embeddings) be integrated into the variance-adaptive allocation algorithm to predict per-pair variances and further improve query efficiency in LLM-as-judge systems?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future Directions" section explicitly suggests "Contextual prediction: Incorporating features... could enable predictive models that estimate variances with personalization."
- **Why unresolved:** The current ROBIN-HOOD algorithm treats each prompt-response pair as an independent arm without leveraging side information. Theoretically and empirically, the benefits of contextual variance prediction remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a contextual bandit variant of ROBIN-HOOD that uses features to model variance; evaluate it on diverse datasets with annotated features; compare worst-case error and budget savings against the non-contextual baseline across varying feature richness.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the variance-adaptive framework be extended to jointly allocate queries across multiple, correlated evaluation attributes (e.g., helpfulness, correctness, coherence) to exploit inter-attribute correlations and achieve super-additive cost savings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future Directions" section proposes "Multi-attribute evaluation: Extending our framework to joint allocation across correlated evaluation dimensions... presents opportunities to exploit correlation structure for further efficiency gains."
- **Why unresolved:** The present method allocates budgets independently per attribute, ignoring potential statistical dependencies between attribute scores for the same or different pairs. The impact of such correlations on worst-case error minimization is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formulate a multi-attribute allocation problem where arm rewards are vector-valued with known or estimated covariance; design an allocation policy that considers covariance (e.g., via Mahalanobis distance); test on multi-attribute datasets (like HelpSteer2) by comparing total budget needed to achieve a target joint error bound.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** In large-scale evaluation with thousands of prompt-response pairs, how does strategically selecting *which* pairs to evaluate (active pair selection) compare to allocating queries across a fixed set of pairs in terms of worst-case error per unit cost?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future Directions" section asks: "Active pair selection: Beyond allocating queries across fixed pairs, strategic selection of *which* pairs to evaluate... could also yield order-of-magnitude cost reductions."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper assumes the set of K pairs is fixed. In practice, one may have a large pool of candidate pairs and wish to identify a subset that yields the most accurate overall evaluation (e.g., for model comparison). The trade-off between exploring new pairs vs. refining estimates of known pairs is unstudied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Combine ROBIN-HOOD with best-arm identification or pure exploration objectives; design a two-stage or unified algorithm that alternates between exploring new pairs and exploiting high-variance known pairs; simulate on large pools (e.g., 10k+ pairs) with varying variance structures and measure error vs. budget.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is the practical performance of ROBIN-HOOD to the choice of the confidence parameter δ (which governs the exploration warm-up period t0), and can δ be tuned automatically or adaptively based on observed variance heterogeneity rather than heuristically?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper states "choosing an appropriate value of δ is crucial for the algorithm’s success" and shows that a poorly chosen δ (e.g., δ=0.07 in Fig. 5) leads to plateaued error, matching uniform allocation. However, no theoretical or empirical guidance is provided for selecting δ given a budget B and unknown variances.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis requires t0 = Θ(log K) for theoretical guarantees, but the constant factors (hidden in O(·) and the specific log term) depend on δ and affect practical performance. The optimal trade‑off between exploration cost and bound tightness is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a systematic sensitivity analysis on multiple datasets with varying variance spreads; derive adaptive rules (e.g., based on early variance estimates) to set t0 on the fly; compare adaptive vs. fixed δ in terms of WCE at common budget points; possibly establish a data-dependent regret bound that suggests an optimal δ.

## Limitations
- LLM judge scores may violate the i.i.d. assumption due to autocorrelation, systematic bias, or non‑stationarity across repeated queries.
- Theoretical guarantees rely on sub‑Gaussian noise and accurate variance estimates; heavy‑tailed or drifting variances could invalidate the error bounds.
- Evaluation is limited to HelpSteer2 with two judges (Llama 3.1 8B, GPT‑4.1 nano); generalizability to other models and datasets remains untested.
- Exact judge prompt templates are missing; replication depends on inferred wording from the appendix.
- The confidence parameter δ was tuned ad hoc; its robustness across different variance structures is not established.

## Confidence
- Theoretical guarantees: Medium (rigorous proofs but strong assumptions).
- Empirical performance: Medium (significant improvement on one dataset; external validation needed).
- Algorithmic design: High (well‑grounded in MAB theory, matches offline optimum intuitively).
