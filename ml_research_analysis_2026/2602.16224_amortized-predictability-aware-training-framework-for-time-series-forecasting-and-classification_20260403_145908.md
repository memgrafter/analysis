# Amortized Predictability-aware Training Framework for Time Series Forecasting and Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16224
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16224
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** APTF reduces prediction error by 2%–9.79% on long-term and 3%–15.36% on short-term forecasting, and improves classification accuracy by ~1%, while yielding flatter loss landscapes.

## Executive Summary
Time series models often struggle with low-predictability, high-noise samples that destabilize training. The proposed Amortized Predictability-aware Training Framework (APTF) addresses this by introducing a Hierarchical Predictability-aware Loss (HPL) that dynamically identifies and progressively penalizes such samples, and an amortization model to correct estimation bias. Evaluated across 11 forecasting and 128 classification datasets, APTF consistently improves performance and generalization, evidenced by flatter loss landscapes and reduced error metrics.

## Method Summary
APTF augments any base time series model with two components: (1) the Hierarchical Predictability-aware Loss (HPL), which partitions each minibatch into `K` buckets ordered by ascending loss (a proxy for predictability). Samples in lower-loss buckets receive higher weights, and as training progresses through `S` stages, bucketing configurations are hierarchically accumulated, allowing gradual inclusion of more samples without discarding earlier reliability assessments. (2) An amortization model—a delayed copy of the base model—that absorbs bias in the source model’s predictability estimates. The source model uses the amortization model’s previous-stage bucket assignments for its loss, while the amortization model uses the source’s current assignments. This cross-model feedback mitigates systematic overconfidence. Training is orchestrated by a controller that manages stage transitions (`ε` epochs), bucket recomputation, and synchronized updates.

## Key Results
- Reduces MSE/MAE by 2%–9.79% on long-term forecasting and 3%–15.36% on short-term forecasting across 8 public and 3 fund datasets.
- Improves average classification accuracy by ~1% over 128 UCR datasets.
- Produces visibly flatter loss landscapes, correlating with better generalization and robustness to noise.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The Hierarchical Predictability-aware Loss (HPL) stabilizes training by dynamically down-weighting gradients from low-predictability samples while preserving signals from high-predictability ones.  
**Mechanism:** HPL sorts minibatch losses into `K` ascending buckets per stage. Lower-index buckets (lower loss, higher predictability) get larger weights; higher-index buckets get progressively smaller weights. Crucially, losses are averaged across all prior stage bucket partitions (hierarchical accumulation), gradually incorporating more samples without abrupt shifts.  
**Core assumption:** Loss magnitude is a valid, though imperfect, early-training proxy for sample predictability.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "dynamically identifies and progressively penalizes these samples"; [section 3.1.1–3.1.4] details bucket grouping, weights, and hierarchical accumulation; ablation studies (Table 4) show HPL’s necessity.  
**Break condition:** If initial loss-based bucketing misseparates high/low-predictability samples (e.g., due to poor initialization or extreme label noise), the hierarchical scheme may mispenalize from the start.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The amortization model reduces predictability estimation bias from the source model’s overconfidence, preventing misidentification of high-predictability samples.  
**Mechanism:** A secondary model is updated one step behind the primary "source" model. The source’s current bucket assignments weight the amortization model’s loss; conversely, the amortization’s *previous*-stage assignments weight the source’s current loss. This cross-loop absorbs the source’s bias before it propagates back.  
**Core assumption:** The source model’s bias is systematic (e.g., overfitting leads to underestimating loss of noisy samples) and can be corrected by an out-of-sync partner.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "amortization model that mitigates predictability estimation errors"; [section 3.2] describes the one-step delayed mutual estimation; Table 8 shows amortization’s benefit particularly on noisy data.  
**Break condition:** If amortization model converges to the same biased representation as the source (e.g., identical architectures/data streams), the correction collapses.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** HPL induces flatter loss landscapes, associated with improved generalization and noise robustness.  
**Mechanism:** Hierarchical averaging across stage-weightings discourages sharp minima that fit noise in high-loss samples. The progressively expanding penalty range forces parameters to perform reasonably across sample difficulties, smoothing loss curvature.  
**Core assumption:** Flatter minima generalize better, especially under noise.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "yielding flatter loss landscapes"; [section 4.3.3] visualizations (Figure 3) show flatter minima with APTF; [section 4.3.1] ablation links HPL removal to worse performance.  
**Break condition:** Overly aggressive penalty expansion may force an overly flat but high-loss region, sacrificing fit on predictable patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Loss Landscape Geometry:** Understanding how gradient modifications via sample weighting influence optimization topology is key. Flat minima often correlate with overfitting to noise; down-weighting high-loss samples can steer the optimizer away from sharp, brittle solutions.  
  *Quick check:* How might progressively down-weighting high-loss samples prevent descent into a sharp minimum that fits noise?
- **Predictability as a Dynamic Property:** A sample’s predictability (inferred via loss) evolves as the model learns; a fixed bucketing strategy fails because early high-loss samples may become predictable later, or vice versa. HPL’s hierarchical accumulation accounts for this evolution.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a sample’s training loss decrease not because it becomes more predictable, but because the model is overfitting to its noise?
- **Amortization in Training Loops:** The amortization model uses a delayed auxiliary model to correct systematic bias in a primary model’s internal estimates (here, sample predictability). This requires grasping how out-of-sync feedback can stabilize estimation.  
  *Quick check:* In the amortization setup, why is a one-step delay used, and what would happen if both models were updated synchronously?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Base Model (`f(X, Θ)`) -> HPL Module (bucketing/weighting) -> Loss Aggregation -> [Amortization Model (optional, with delayed bucket sharing)] -> Training Loop Controller (stage/orchestration) -> Parameter Updates.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Forward pass with source model on minibatch.  
  2. Compute per-sample loss.  
  3. HPL sorts losses, assigns current-stage buckets `{B₁..Bₖ}`, retrieves accumulated bucket groups from all prior stages.  
  4. Aggregate weighted loss hierarchically across all stage groups.  
  5. If amortization enabled: source model’s current losses weighted by amortization’s *previous*-stage buckets; amortization model’s losses weighted by source’s *current* buckets.  
  6. Backpropagate aggregated losses for source and amortization models separately.  
  7. Update amortization model parameters *after* source model update (one-step delay).  
  8. On `ε`-epoch boundary, increment stage, create new bucket group, reset buckets for next stage.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Bucket count (`K`) vs. granularity:** More buckets enable finer discrimination but increase overhead and risk empty buckets.  
  - **Amortization vs. overhead:** Adds ~2× memory/time; benefit is largest when source bias is severe; HPL alone may suffice on cleaner data.  
  - **Stage interval (`ε`) vs. responsiveness:** Smaller `ε` adapts faster to predictability evolution but may cause instability from frequent gradient distribution shifts.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Training divergence/oscillation:** Likely due to conflicting gradients from hierarchical averaging; reduce `K` or increase `ε`.  
  - **No improvement/degradation:** Loss may be a poor predictability proxy; validate bucket separation (avg loss per bucket). Try disabling amortization.  
  - **Over-penalization of easy samples:** High-predictability samples placed in low-weight buckets early; ensure initial weights are sufficiently large (e.g., first bucket weight=1).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablation - HPL core:** Use APTF but disable hierarchical accumulation (only current-stage bucketing). Expect worse performance than full HPL (Table 4).  
  2. **Ablation - Amortization:** Train with HPL but `amortization=False`. Compare to full APTF; expect gap on noisy datasets (Table 4).  
  3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity - Bucket count (`K`):** Grid search `K` ∈ {3,5,7,9,11,15} on validation. Expect improvement then plateau (Figure 4 left); avoid overly sparse buckets.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can APTF’s hyperparameters be tuned automatically to avoid dataset-specific manual tuning?
- **Open Question 2:** What theoretical guarantees (e.g., convergence, stability) does HPL provide, and how does it relate to observed loss landscape flattening?
- **Open Question 3:** Is the amortization model necessary, or can comparable gains be achieved with more efficient bias-reduction techniques?
- **Open Question 4:** Beyond forecasting and classification, can APTF improve other time series tasks (e.g., anomaly detection, imputation)?

## Limitations
- Core assumption that loss is a valid predictability proxy may fail under extreme label noise or poor initialization.
- Implementation details for baseline hyperparameters and dataset splits (e.g., fund sales) are unclear, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Amortization’s benefit is shown only on the paper’s chosen architectures; generalization to arbitrary models is unverified.
- Corpus evidence for novelty of hierarchical bucketing is limited (low citation neighbors), lacking external validation.

## Confidence
- **Error reduction claims (2–9.79% TSF, ~1% TSC):** High – supported by 11+128 dataset results and ablations.
- **Mechanism 1 (HPL stabilizes via dynamic weighting):** Medium – internal ablation evidence; no independent verification of loss-predictability correlation.
- **Mechanism 2 (Amortization corrects bias):** Medium – plausible logic but fragile dependency on delayed pairing; break condition not stress-tested.
- **Mechanism 3 (Flatter landscapes via HPL):** Medium – visual evidence (Figure 3) is correlational; causal link not isolated.
- **Generalization to all noisy TSF/TSC tasks:** Low – datasets are standard but not exhaustive; extreme noise regimes untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Proxy sanity check:** On a high-noise dataset (e.g., random-label UCR subset), verify early-training per-sample loss correlates with true predictability (e.g., label consistency under augmentation). Weak correlation implies HPL may misbucket.
2. **Amortization isolation test:** Train source and amortization models with identical architectures but different initializations. Measure bucket agreement over epochs; high agreement indicates collapse, undermining bias correction.
3. **Noise-stress benchmark:** Introduce controlled synthetic noise (e.g., Gaussian input/output scaling) to a clean dataset (e.g., ETTh1). Compare APTF’s performance decay against baselines as noise variance increases; expect more graceful degradation if APTF handles low-predictability samples effectively.
