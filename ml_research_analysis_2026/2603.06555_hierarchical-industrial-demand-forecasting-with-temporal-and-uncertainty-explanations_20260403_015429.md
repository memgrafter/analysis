# Hierarchical Industrial Demand Forecasting with Temporal and Uncertainty Explanations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06555
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06555
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Subtree approximation and non‑parameterized quantile translation improve explanation accuracy by 62.0% (deterministic) and 26.0% (probabilistic) while drastically reducing computation time.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the interpretability gap in hierarchical probabilistic time-series forecasting—a key need for industrial demand planning. It introduces two novel adaptations: **subtree approximation**, which breaks down cross‑hierarchy variable importance into adjacent‑level scores to preserve coherence and cut noise, and **non‑parameterized translation**, which leverages output quantiles to explain arbitrary probabilistic forecasts without altering the model. Through extensive semi‑synthetic evaluations on multiple real‑world datasets (including one from a large chemical company) and real‑world case studies, the method yields substantial gains in explanation accuracy (up to 62% for deterministic, 26% for probabilistic forecasts) and scales efficiently, enabling stakeholders to uncover drivers, temporal patterns, and uncertainty sources.

## Method Summary
The authors extend post‑hoc explainability techniques (LIME, Integrated Gradients, Feature Occlusion, SmoothGrad) to large‑scale hierarchical probabilistic forecasting. Their approach consists of two core components:  
1. **Subtree approximation (Algorithm 3)** – Instead of evaluating importance across the entire hierarchy at once, it traverses the hierarchy tree breadth‑first and aggregates local scores, enforcing parent‑child coherence and reducing computational overhead.  
2. **Non‑parameterized quantile translation** – For probabilistic models that output quantiles, the method treats each quantile as a deterministic surrogate and applies the same explanation pipeline, thus providing explanations for any forecast distribution without retraining.

Models (HAILS, HierE2E, SHARQ, DeepAR) are trained with standard losses (MSE for deterministic, quantile loss for probabilistic) on 60% of the data, forecasting 12 steps ahead. Explanations are generated on test forecasts and evaluated against semi‑synthetic ground truth using Importance Accuracy Score (IAS) and External Variable Detection Accuracy (EVDA). Scalability is assessed by varying hierarchy depth and measuring runtime.

## Key Results
- For cross‑variable deterministic forecasts, subtree‑augmented explanations achieve an average **62.0% improvement** in IAS over naive all‑variable baselines.  
- For probabilistic forecasts across all hierarchies, the method shows an average **26.0% IAS improvement**.  
- Computation time drops dramatically (e.g., from ~6000s to ~100s on the Dow hierarchy), confirming near‑linear scaling with subtree approximation.  
- Real‑world case studies demonstrate practical utility in identifying key drivers, temporal patterns, and shifts in uncertainty.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Subtree approximation** works because it respects the hierarchical aggregation structure: importance scores computed at the level of immediate parent‑child interactions are coherent by construction, avoiding contradictory contributions that arise when unrelated leaf nodes influence distant ancestors. This decomposition also isolates the explanation scope, drastically reducing the number of model evaluations required. **Non‑parameterized translation** works because quantile forecasts already provide a deterministic summary of the predictive distribution; applying local XAI methods directly to these summaries yields faithful explanations for each quantile without needing to model the full distribution’s derivatives. Together, these adaptations maintain the fidelity of the original forecasting models while making their outputs interpretable and computationally feasible even for very large hierarchies.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw hierarchical time series (with optional externals) -> Preprocessing (hierarchy encoding, synthetic anomaly injection) -> HTSF models (HAILS, HierE2E, SHARQ, DeepAR) -> Trained model -> 12‑step forecasts (deterministic or quantile) -> Explanation engine (applies LIME/IG/FO/SG) -> Subtree approximation (BFS traversal) + Non‑parameterized translation (quantile mapping) -> Importance scores -> IAS/EVDA evaluation -> Runtime metrics -> Results.

**Critical path:** Data ingest → Model training → Forecast generation → Explanation computation → Metric calculation. The bottleneck typically lies in the explanation step, which subtree approximation alleviates.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Subtree approximation* trades potential cross‑level interactions for coherence and speed; it may miss subtle long‑range influences but is empirically effective.  
- *Quantile translation* discards full distributional shape for simplicity; explanations reflect chosen quantiles but not the entire predictive density.  
- Default hyperparameters ease replication but may not be optimal for all datasets.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Out‑of‑memory errors on large hierarchies when using naive all‑variable baselines.  
- Low IAS (< 0) indicating mis‑specified hierarchy or metric implementation errors.  
- Discrepancy between expected and observed Spearman correlation among parent‑child importance, suggesting broken subtree logic.

**First experiments (3):**  
1. End‑to‑end replication on M5 dataset with default hyperparameters; verify IAS improvement of ~62% (deterministic) and ~26% (probabilistic).  
2. Coherence check: on a synthetic 3‑level balanced tree, compute Spearman’s ρ between parent and child importance with and without subtree approximation; expect significantly higher ρ with subtree.  
3. Scaling benchmark: measure runtime of naive vs. subtree explanation on artificial hierarchies of 100, 1 k, 10 k nodes; confirm near‑linear growth for subtree vs. super‑linear for naive.

## Limitations
- The proprietary Dow dataset is unavailable, preventing full replication on that industrial case.  
- Model hyperparameters and anomaly generation details are underspecified, requiring researcher choices that may affect performance.  
- Explanation metrics are highly sensitive to exact implementation; small deviations from the paper’s equations could invalidate comparisons.

## Confidence
- Improved IAS (deterministic): **Medium** – evidence from semi‑synthetic benchmarks but dependent on unspecified parameters.  
- Improved IAS (probabilistic): **Medium** – similar to deterministic case; quantile translation seems sound but needs broader validation.  
- Scalability gains: **High** – runtime reduction is mathematically justified and empirically demonstrated.
