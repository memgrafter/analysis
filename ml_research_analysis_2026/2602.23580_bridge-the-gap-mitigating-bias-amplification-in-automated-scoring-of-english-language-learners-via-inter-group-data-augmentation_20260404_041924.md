# BRIDGE the Gap: Mitigating Bias Amplification in Automated Scoring of English Language Learners via Inter-group Data Augmentation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23580
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23580
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: BRIDGE reduces fairness gaps (MSG Gap by 93.28%) without sacrificing scoring accuracy (<0.24% change).

## Executive Summary
Bias amplification in automated scoring disproportionately harms English Language Learners (ELLs) by under-predicting high scores due to sparse high-scoring ELL examples. The BRIDGE framework addresses this by synthesizing high-scoring ELL responses: it extracts construct-relevant content from abundant high-scoring non-ELL responses and reformulates it using authentic ELL linguistic patterns via an LLM, followed by a discriminator for quality filtering. On CAST datasets, BRIDGE dramatically reduces fairness metrics (e.g., MSG Gap reduction of 93.28%) while maintaining overall scoring accuracy, demonstrating that inter-group data augmentation can effectively mitigate bias amplification.

## Method Summary
BRIDGE is a two-stage data augmentation method designed to mitigate bias amplification in automated scoring for ELLs. In Stage 1, it uses a large language model (GPT-4o) to generate synthetic high-scoring ELL responses by combining content from selected high-scoring non-ELL responses with linguistic style from authentic ELL responses. Stage 2 employs a lightweight discriminator model to filter low-quality or inauthentic synthetics, retaining only those that resemble genuine ELL outputs (authenticity score > γ=0.6). The filtered synthetics are added to the low-resource training set (1% subsample) to train a BERT-based scoring model.

Key specifications: The base scorer is fine-tuned BERT-base with cross-entropy loss, AdamW optimizer (lr=2e-5, weight decay=0.01), batch size 16, and early stopping. Training data uses a 70/15/15 split, with the majority (99%) of training data held out as donor pools. Evaluation uses fairness metrics (MSG Gap, BiasAmp) and accuracy metrics (QWK, MAE, Macro-F1). The method’s success hinges on the disentanglement of construct-relevant content and linguistic style, which enables the transfer of high-score content to ELL patterns without requiring real high-scoring ELL data.

## Key Results
- MSG Gap decreased by 93.28% (0.0134 → 0.0009) on a dataset, and BiasAmp moved toward negative values, indicating reduced bias against ELLs.
- Overall scoring accuracy changed by less than 0.24% on average, showing no significant trade-off.
- The fairness improvement is similar to that achieved by adding real high-scoring ELL data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) on data with representation bias amplifies prediction disparities for sparse high-scoring ELLs.  
**Mechanism**: Under standard ERM, gradient updates are proportional to group sample fractions. The extremely sparse high-scoring ELL subgroup contributes negligibly to the total gradient, so the decision boundary is shaped predominantly by abundant non-ELL examples, causing under-prediction of high scores for ELLs and over-prediction for non-ELLs.  
**Core assumption**: The model conflates construct-relevant content with majority (non-ELL) linguistic patterns as a proxy for proficiency.  
**Evidence**: The paper’s gradient decomposition (Section 2.2) and discussion of representation bias.  
**Break condition**: Fails if training data contains sufficient high-scoring ELL examples or if the learning algorithm reweights losses by group.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Synthesizing high-scoring ELL responses by combining content from high-scoring non-ELLs with linguistic patterns from ELLs mitigates representation bias.  
**Mechanism**: BRIDGE extracts construct-relevant content (C) from high-scoring non-ELL responses and reformulates it using linguistic style (S) from ELL responses via an LLM: x_syn = G(C(x_NE), S(x_EL)). This creates synthetic examples in the sparse high-scoring ELL region.  
**Core assumption**: Construct validity holds—scores should reflect content/proficiency, not ELL-specific linguistic patterns, making content transferable.  
**Evidence**: Paper’s formalization (Section 3.1) and prior evidence that responses at equal scores remain discriminable by latent style.  
**Break condition**: Fails if the assessment’s construct incorporates linguistic proficiency (e.g., writing assessments), making content-style disentanglement invalid.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: A discriminator model filters synthetic responses to ensure authenticity, preventing low-quality samples from degrading training.  
**Mechanism**: A discriminator D classifies responses as authentic or synthetic; only samples with D(x_syn) > γ (e.g., 0.6) are retained. This removes artifacts and non-typical ELL text.  
**Core assumption**: The discriminator can reliably distinguish authentic ELL responses from LLM-generated ones based on context-relevant features.  
**Evidence**: Ablation in Section 4.4 shows Stage 2 provides robustness, especially on noisier datasets.  
**Break condition**: Fails if discriminator is poorly calibrated or threshold γ is set incorrectly.

## Foundational Learning
### Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
- **Why needed**: Underlies the paper’s analysis of how standard ERM’s uniform loss weighting interacts with data sparsity to under-weight underrepresented subgroups.
- **Quick check**: If a subgroup is 1% of training data, what fraction of total gradient signal comes from that subgroup under standard ERM? (Answer: ~1% if loss weighting is uniform.)

### Representation Bias
- **Why needed**: This is the root cause—the skew in subgroup representation (few high-scoring ELLs) that predisposes the model to learn majority patterns.
- **Quick check**: How does representation bias differ from label noise or measurement bias in this context? (Answer: It’s about unequal sample sizes across groups, not errors in labels or feature collection.)

### Construct Validity
- **Why needed**: Justifies the content-style disentanglement central to BRIDGE; assumes that scoring rubrics target content/proficiency, making linguistic style construct-irrelevant.
- **Quick check**: In a science short-answer assessment, why might linguistic style be considered construct-irrelevant for a native English speaker but not for an ELL? (Answer: The construct (science knowledge) should be separable from language form; however, style may inadvertently influence scores if the model conflates language with content.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Donor Pools (high-scoring non-ELLs and all ELLs) → LLM Generator (Stage 1, GPT-4o) → Discriminator (Stage 2) → Filtered Synthetic Samples → Augmented Training Data → Scoring Model (fine-tuned BERT)

### Critical Path
1. Prepare stratified train/val/test splits; subsample 1% of training data as low-resource set.
2. For each synthetic sample: select a high-scoring non-ELL (content donor) and an ELL (style donor); generate x_syn via LLM.
3. Apply discriminator; retain x_syn if D(x_syn) > γ.
4. Add retained synthetics to the 1% training set.
5. Fine-tune BERT-base on this augmented set.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Quality vs. Diversity**: Higher LLM temperature or diverse style donors increases variation but risks content distortion or inauthentic text.
- **Authenticity Threshold (γ)**: Lower γ retains more samples but may include noise; higher γ ensures quality but reduces quantity. Paper uses γ=0.6 without extensive tuning.
- **Donor Selection**: Content donors are fixed as high-scoring non-ELLs; using all may introduce irrelevant content; style donor diversity impacts variability.

### Failure Signatures
- Synthetic samples lack content fidelity (missing rubric criteria) or stylistic authenticity (not typical ELL errors).
- Discriminator overfits to LLM artifacts, filtering valid samples or letting in low-quality ones.
- No fairness improvement after augmentation (MSG Gap/BiasAmp unchanged or worsened).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Bias Check**: Train scorer on unaugmented 1% data; confirm significant positive BiasAmp (p<0.01) to establish bias.
2. **Ablation - Stage 1 vs Full BRIDGE**: Compare augmentation without discriminator (Stage 1 only) vs full BRIDGE; expect Stage 2 to improve robustness.
3. **Parameter Sensitivity - Discriminator Threshold γ**: Test γ in {0.4, 0.6, 0.8} to find optimal fairness/accuracy trade-off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does content-style disentanglement hold for assessments where linguistic competence is integral?**  
   The framework assumes construct-relevant content and linguistic style are separable, but if language proficiency is part of the construct (e.g., writing rubrics), synthesizing by recombining may compromise validity. Empirical validation on such assessments is needed.

2. **How does BRIDGE generalize to longer responses and non-science domains?**  
   Current experiments are limited to short science answers. Longer essays have complex discourse, and different subjects may have different content-style relationships. Testing on datasets like TOEFL essays or history responses is required.

3. **How sensitive are fairness gains to design choices (LLM, discriminator, threshold)?**  
   The paper uses fixed choices (GPT-4o, specific discriminator, γ=0.6) without exploring alternatives. Systematic ablations varying these factors are needed to understand sensitivity and optimize performance.

4. **Can BRIDGE mitigate bias for other underrepresented subgroups (e.g., students with disabilities, low-SES)?**  
   The method targets ELL linguistic patterns. Other subgroups may face different biases not captured by style alone, requiring alternative mappings. Adaptation and testing on such subgroups remain open.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction blockers**: Missing exact LLM prompt and discriminator training details (architecture, data mix, epochs) hinder exact replication.
- **Construct validity assumption**: Relies on linguistic style being construct-irrelevant; may not hold for language-focused assessments, risking invalid synthetic samples.
- **Generalization scope**: Evaluated only on short science responses; transfer to other constructs, languages, or longer texts is unproven.

## Confidence
- Bias amplification via ERM/gradient under-weighting: High
- Inter-group synthesis efficacy: Medium
- Discriminator necessity and calibration: Low
