# Towards Understanding Adam Convergence on Highly Degenerate Polynomials

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09581
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09581
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Adam achieves automatic linear convergence on highly degenerate polynomials \(L(x) = \frac{1}{k} x^k\) (\(k \ge 4\) even) without learning rate decay, while Gradient Descent and Momentum suffer sublinear power‑law rates due to vanishing curvature.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the convergence behavior of the Adam optimizer on a class of highly degenerate polynomial objectives \(L(x) = \frac{1}{k} x^k\) with \(k \ge 4\) even. Such functions have zero curvature at the minimum, leading to sublinear convergence for standard first‑order methods like Gradient Descent (GD) and Heavy‑Ball Momentum. In contrast, the authors prove that Adam attains linear convergence without any learning‑rate decay. The key mechanism is a decoupling of Adam’s second moment estimate \(v_t\) from the instantaneous squared gradients \(g_t^2\); once the gradient vanishes quickly enough, \(v_t\) decays geometrically as \(\beta_2^t\), which implicitly generates an exponential learning‑rate schedule. Under the condition \(\beta_1 < \beta_2^{k/(2(k-2))}\) (and a mild lower bound), the dynamics possess a stable fixed point that yields the asymptotic rate \(\lim_{t\to\infty} \|x_{t+1}\|/\|x_t\| = \beta_1^{1/(2(k-2))}\). Moreover, three dynamical regimes are identified: (I) stable exponential convergence, (II) transient convergence followed by spikes, and (III) SignGD‑like oscillations, depending on the relationship between \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\). Theoretical analysis and experiments align precisely, revealing a clear function class where Adam enjoys an inherent advantage.

## Method Summary
The authors perform a theoretical analysis of Adam’s update equations applied to the deterministic 1‑dimensional monomial loss \(L(x)=\frac{1}{k}x^k\). They introduce normalized variables—\(\omega_t = m_t/g_t\) and \(\lambda_t = x_t^{k-2}/\sqrt{v_t}\)—to rewrite the recurrence in a simpler form. Fixed points of this 3‑dimensional dynamical system are derived, and local stability is determined via Jacobian linearization, leading to explicit conditions on \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\). The analysis shows that when \(\beta_1\) lies below a threshold, the second moment \(v_t\) enters a decoupled regime where it decays geometrically, effectively scaling the step size exponentially and enabling linear convergence. The authors also prove that GD and Momentum, with constant step sizes, only achieve sublinear power‑law rates on the same problem. Experimental validation on the degenerate polynomial objective confirms the three predicted regimes, the decoupling behavior of \(v_t\), and the predicted convergence rates.

## Key Results
- Adam achieves automatic linear convergence on highly degenerate polynomials \(L(x) = \frac{1}{k} x^k\) (\(k \ge 4\) even) without learning rate decay, while GD and Momentum suffer sublinear power‑law rates.
- The asymptotic convergence rate is \(\lim_{t\to\infty} \|x_{t+1}\|/\|x_t\| = \beta_1^{1/(2(k-2))}\).
- Three distinct dynamical regimes exist, determined by the relationship between \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\): (I) stable exponential convergence, (II) transient convergence then spikes, (III) SignGD‑like oscillations, all stemming from a decoupling effect where \(v_t\) decays geometrically independent of \(g_t^2\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Exponential Decay via Decoupling of *v*<sub>*t*</sub> and *g*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>2</sup>
- **Claim**: On highly degenerate polynomials, Adam’s second moment *v*<sub>*t*</sub> decouples from the instantaneous squared gradient *g*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>2</sup> and approaches a geometric decay *v*<sub>*t*</sub> ∝ *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>*t*</sup>. This acts as an implicit exponential learning‑rate schedule, yielding linear convergence.
- **Mechanism**: As *x*<sub>*t*</sub> decreases, the gradient |*g*<sub>*t*</sub>| = |*x*<sub>*t*</sub>|<sup>*k*−1</sup> vanishes faster than the memory decay *β*<sub>2</sub>. Once the gradient decay rate ρ<sub>*t*</sub> = *g*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>2</sup>/*g*<sub>*t*−1</sub><sup>2</sup> drops below *β*<sub>2</sub>, the update *v*<sub>*t*</sub> = *β*<sub>2</sub>*v*<sub>*t*−1</sub> + (1−*β*<sub>2</sub>)*g*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>2</sup> becomes dominated by the previous value, i.e., *v*<sub>*t*</sub> ≈ *β*<sub>2</sub>*v*<sub>*t*−1</sub>. Consequently 1/√*v*<sub>*t*</sub> ∝ *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>−*t*/2</sup>, exponentially amplifying the effective step size and counteracting the vanishing curvature.
- **Core assumption**: The loss is a monomial 1/*k* *x*<sup>*k*</sup> with *k* ≥ 4 even; bias‑correction terms become negligible for large *t* (or are omitted in analysis); noise is absent (deterministic gradients); initialization *v*<sub>0</sub> = *g*<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup>.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Section 5.2] Lemma 5.4 proves that if iterates converge then *v*<sub>*t*</sub>/*v*<sub>*t*−1</sub> → *β*<sub>2</sub> (geometric decay). Lemma 5.5 shows that such an exponential schedule yields exponential convergence for the degenerate polynomial.
  - [Section 4] introduces the normalized variables ω<sub>*t*</sub> = *m*<sub>*t*</sub>/*g*<sub>*t*</sub> and λ<sub>*t*</sub> = *x*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>*k*−2</sup>/√*v*<sub>*t*</sub>, from which the decoupling appears as the term √*β*<sub>2</sub> in the λ‑update.
  - [Figure 6(d,e,f)] experimental log‑plots of *v*<sub>*t*</sub> show a linear tail with slope ln(*β*<sub>2</sub>), confirming autonomous decay, while *g*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>2</sup> decays much faster.
- **Break condition**: Decoupling fails if the gradient decay is not sufficiently fast (*ρ*<sub>*t*</sub> ≥ *β*<sub>2</sub>), which occurs when the learning rate is too large initially or the function is not degenerate enough (*k*=2). If the momentum parameter *β*<sub>1</sub> is too large, the first moment may not track the gradient, leading to instability before decoupling can take effect.

### Mechanism 2: Local Stability of the Non‑Trivial Fixed Point & Three Regimes
- **Claim**: Adam’s normalized dynamics (ω<sub>*t*</sub>, λ<sub>*t*</sub>, *x*<sub>*t*</sub>) admit a non‑trivial fixed point representing linear convergence. Its asymptotic stability is determined by the pair (*β*<sub>1</sub>, *β*<sub>2</sub>). This produces three distinct regimes: (I) stable exponential convergence, (II) unstable fixed point causing transient convergence then spikes, and (III) no non‑trivial fixed point (SignGD‑like oscillations).
- **Mechanism**: The Jacobian of the 3‑D recurrence (5) evaluated at the fixed point (7) has a block upper‑triangular form. The eigenvalue associated with *x* is *β*<sub>1</sub><sup>1/(2(*k*−2))</sup>. Stability of the (ω,λ) block imposes two inequalities: *β*<sub>1</sub> < *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>*k*/(2(*k*−2))</sup> (upper bound) and a lower bound that becomes negligible for *β*<sub>2</sub> > ((*k*−2)/*k*)<sup>2(*k*−2)</sup>. The regime boundaries are *β*<sub>1</sub> = *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>(*k*−1)/(2(*k*−2))</sup> (existence) and *β*<sub>1</sub> = *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>*k*/(2(*k*−2))</sup> (stability).
- **Core assumption**: Local analysis near the fixed point; the Lyapunov stability notion for discrete systems; the fixed point exists only for *β*<sub>1</sub> < *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>(*k*−1)/(2(*k*−2))</sup>.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Section 4] Theorem 4.1 states the existence condition (i) and the stability condition in (ii) with the two inequalities. It also gives the convergence rate in (iii).
  - [Section 6] Theorem 6.1 directly maps the three regimes based on *β*<sub>1</sub> relative to the two thresholds.
  - [Figure 3] theoretical phase diagram for *k*=4 matches the condition *β*<sub>1</sub> < *β*<sub>2</sub> for stability.
  - [Figure 6] empirical trajectories for the three regimes show the characteristic behaviors: (a,d) stable decay, (b,e) spike after initial progress, (c,f) oscillations.
- **Break condition**: When *β*<sub>1</sub> exceeds the stability upper bound the fixed point is unstable; if *β*<sub>1</sub> also exceeds the existence bound (*β*<sub>1</sub> > *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>(*k*−1)/(2(*k*−2))</sup>) the non‑trivial fixed point does not exist, so the system cannot approach a linear‑convergence regime.

### Mechanism 3: Acceleration Over GD/Momentum via Implicit Exponential Schedule
- **Claim**: On degenerate polynomials, Adam achieves global linear convergence (under conditions) while Gradient Descent and Heavy‑Ball Momentum only attain sublinear power‑law convergence (*x*(*t*) ∝ *t*<sup>−1/(*k*−2)</sup>). This results in an exponential separation in iteration complexity: *T*<sub>ε</sub> ∼ ln(1/ε) for Adam vs. *T*<sub>ε</sub> ∼ ε<sup>−(*k*−2)</sup> for GD.
- **Mechanism**: GD/Momentum use a constant learning rate; their update step size shrinks as |*g*| ≈ |*x*|<sup>*k*−1</sup>, causing the parameter to evolve as a gradient flow with vanishing drift. Adam’s decoupling produces an exponential decay 1/√*v*<sub>*t*</sub> ∝ *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>−*t*/2</sup>, which compensates exactly for the power‑law decay and yields linear convergence. The discrete stability condition requires *β*<sub>2</sub> > ((*k*−2)/*k*)<sup>2(*k*−2)</sup> (Proposition 5.6), a mild condition easily satisfied in practice.
- **Core assumption**: The comparison uses constant step size for all algorithms; Adam’s *η* is fixed and not decayed; analysis for RMSProp (*β*<sub>1</sub>=0) is extended to Adam with *β*<sub>1</sub> < *β*<sub>2</sub><sup>*k*/(2(*k*−2))</sup> and appropriate initial condition.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Section 5.1] Theorem 5.1 (GD) and Theorem 5.3 (Momentum) prove the power‑law rate *x*(*t*) ∼ *t*<sup>−1/(*k*−2)</sup>.
  - [Section 5.2] Lemma 5.4–5.5 establish that the geometric decay of *v*<sub>*t*</sub> (decoupling) leads to exponential convergence when the system remains bounded.
  - [Theorem 5.7] proves global linear convergence for RMSProp under the stability condition *β*<sub>2</sub> > ((*k*−2)/*k*)<sup>2(*k*−2)</sup> and a mild initial condition.
  - [Figure 2(a)] shows Adam linear decay vs. GD plateauing; Figure 2(b) shows λ<sub>*t*</sub> stabilizes for *k*≥4 but diverges for *k*=2.
- **Break condition**: The acceleration hinges on the decoupling occurring early enough and the parameter staying within the basin of attraction of the stable fixed point. If *β*<sub>1</sub> is too large (Regime III) the coupling between *m*<sub>*t*</sub> and *g*<sub>*t*</sub> prevents exponential schedule; if the initial *η*·*x*<sub>0</sub><sup>*k*−2</sup> is too large, the system may diverge before decoupling sets in.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept 1: Degenerate Polynomial Objectives
*Why needed*: The paper’s analysis focuses on \(L(x)= \frac{1}{k} x^k\) with \(k\ge4\) even. These functions are “highly degenerate” because the Hessian (second derivative) vanishes at the minimum, making the local curvature zero. Standard theory predicts sublinear convergence for first‑order methods, which the paper overturns for Adam.  
*Quick check*: *How does the solution of the gradient flow \(\dot{x} = -\eta x^{k-1}\) behave as \(t \to \infty\)?*  
Answer: \(x(t) \sim \bigl[(k-2)\eta t\bigr]^{-1/(k-2)}\), i.e., polynomial decay.

### Concept 2: Adaptive Moment Estimation Dynamics
*Why needed*: Adam couples two exponential moving averages: first moment \(m_t\) (with decay \(\beta_1\)) and second moment \(v_t\) (with decay \(\beta_2\)). Understanding their interaction with the gradient decay is crucial to see why \(v_t\) can decouple.  
*Quick check*: *Define the normalized variable \(\lambda_t = x_t^{k-2}/\sqrt{v_t}\). What does \(\lambda_t\) represent physically?*  
Answer: It captures the ratio of the intrinsic curvature (\(x^{k-2}\)) to the adaptive step size \(1/\sqrt{v_t}\).

### Concept 3: Fixed‑Point Stability in Discrete Dynamical Systems
*Why needed*: The proof of convergence relies on linearizing the 3‑D recurrence (5) around the non‑trivial fixed point. The Jacobian’s spectral radius determines local asymptotic stability. This is a standard tool in nonlinear dynamics.  
*Quick check*: *What is the eigenvalue associated with the \(x\)-coordinate at the fixed point, and why does it give the convergence rate?*  
Answer: \(\beta_1^{1/(2(k-2))}\). Because \(x_{t+1}/x_t = 1 - \eta \omega_t \lambda_t\) and at the fixed point this product equals \(1 - \beta_1^{1/(2(k-2))}\).

### Concept 4: Exponential vs. Polynomial Convergence Classes
*Why needed*: The paper’s main result is a complexity separation: Adam achieves linear (exponential) convergence on a class where GD/Momentum suffer sublinear (polynomial) rates. Understanding these classes and their iteration complexities is key to appreciating Adam’s advantage.  
*Quick check*: *For GD on \(L(x) = \frac{1}{6} x^6\), what is the iteration complexity to reach precision \(\varepsilon\)?*  
Answer: \(T_\varepsilon = \Theta(\varepsilon^{-4})\) because \(k-2 = 4\).

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
- **Core optimizer**: Adam (bias‑correction optional in asymptotic analysis; \(\varepsilon\) negligible). State: scalar \(x_t\), first moment \(m_t\), second moment \(v_t\).
- **Building blocks**: Standard Adam updates (2). For analysis also compute normalized variables: \(\omega_t = m_t / g_t\) (with \(g_t = x_t^{k-1}\)) and \(\lambda_t = x_t^{k-2}/\sqrt{v_t}\). Diagnostic ratios: \(R_t^{(v)} = v_t / g_t^2\) (coupling of \(v\) to \(g^2\)) and effective sharpness \(u_t = \eta x_t^{k-2}\).
- **Hyperparameters**: constant learning rate \(\eta\), \(\beta_1 \in [0,1)\), \(\beta_2 \in [0,1)\).
- **Stability check**: Compute thresholds \(\beta_{\text{crit}}^{(k)} = \beta_2^{k/(2(k-2))}\) and \(\beta_{\text{crit}}^{(k-1)} = \beta_2^{(k-1)/(2(k-2))}\).

### Critical Path
1. **Initialize** \(x_0\) (small), \(m_0 = 0\), \(v_0 = g_0^2\) (or small constant).
2. **Run Adam updates** without learning‑rate decay.
3. **After sufficient iterations** compute \(\omega_t\), \(\lambda_t\), \(R_t^{(v)}\).
4. **Identify regime**:
   - \(\beta_1 < \beta_{\text{crit}}^{(k)}\) → Regime I (stable).
   - \(\beta_{\text{crit}}^{(k-1)} < \beta_1 < \beta_{\text{crit}}^{(k)}\) → Regime II (unstable fixed point, expect spike).
   - \(\beta_1 > \beta_{\text{crit}}^{(k-1)}\) → Regime III (no fixed point, SignGD‑like).
5. **Monitor convergence**: loss should decay linearly on a log‑scale in Regime I; in Regime II loss drops then rises; in Regime III loss plateaus near \(L(\eta/2)\) with oscillations.
6. **Validate decoupling**: In Regimes I/II, \(R_t^{(v)}\) should grow large (\(v_t \gg g_t^2\)); in Regime III it stays ≈1.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Larger \(\beta_2\) makes decoupling easier (gradients vanish faster relative to memory) but increases memory; too small \(\beta_2\) forces tight coupling (Regime III).
- Larger \(\beta_1\) helps the initial descent but narrows the stability region; if \(\beta_1\) approaches the stability boundary, convergence becomes fragile and prone to spikes.
- The learning rate \(\eta\) must be small enough to keep effective sharpness \(u_t = \eta x_t^{k-2} < 2\) for all \(t\) to avoid instability. The stable fixed point requires \(\eta \omega_t \lambda_t < 2\), with steady state \(\omega^*\lambda^* = (1 - \beta_1^{1/(2(k-2))})/\eta\).
- Trade‑off: Regime I guarantees stability but may be conservative; Regime II can achieve very low loss before the spike, though the final loss may be high.

### Failure Signatures
- **No exponential tail** in log‑loss plot (flat plateau → sublinear behavior).
- **Loss spike** after initial exponential decay: indicates violation of \(\omega\lambda < 2/\eta\) due to unstable fixed point.
- **Persistent oscillations** around a constant loss: \(R_t^{(v)} \approx 1\), \(v_t\) tracks \(g_t^2\) tightly, mimicking SignGD.
- **Early divergence**: if initial \(\eta x_0^{k-2} > 2\), the first step is already unstable.
- **Computational check**: spectral radius of Jacobian >1 (computable from \(\beta_1,\beta_2,k\)) predicts instability.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Phase diagram sweep**: For a fixed degenerate function (e.g., \(L(x)=\frac{1}{4}x^4\)), run Adam over a grid of (\(\beta_1,\beta_2\)) with constant \(\eta\). Record final loss after many iterations and plot a heatmap overlaid with theoretical stability boundaries (expected to match Figures 3/7).
2. **Decoupling measurement**: Pick one point in each regime. Plot \(v_t\) and \(g_t^2\) on a log scale and compute \(R_t^{(v)}\). Expected: Regime I – \(R_t\) grows roughly as \(\beta_2^t\); Regime II similar until near the spike; Regime III stays near 1.
3. **Convergence‑rate validation**: In Regime I, fit a line to log‑loss vs. iteration; the slope should equal \(\frac{k\ln\beta_2}{2(k-2)}\) (Theorem 4.1(iii)). Also measure \(\lim x_{t+1}/x_t\) and compare to \(\beta_1^{1/(2(k-2))}\). For \(k=4\), test with \(\beta_1=0.81, \beta_2=0.9\) gives rate \(\sqrt{0.81}=0.9\); increasing \(\beta_2\) should make the observed rate approach this value.

## Limitations
- The analysis is restricted to 1‑dimensional deterministic monomials \(L(x)=\frac{1}{k}x^k\) (\(k\ge4\) even); real‑world losses often involve noise, high dimensionality, and non‑polynomial curvature.
- The convergence guarantees are local (around a non‑trivial fixed point) and assume bounded iterates; global convergence is not proven.
- The role of bias‑correction and the extension to higher‑dimensional separable sums are not addressed, leaving questions about practical Adam usage.

## Confidence
- **Decoupling mechanism**: Medium‑High
- **Three‑regime phase diagram**: Medium
- **Adam’s automatic linear convergence advantage**: Medium

## Next Checks

3. Implement full Adam with bias correction and compare regime thresholds, convergence rates, and early‑iteration behavior against the bias‑free predictions.
