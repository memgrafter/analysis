# A Theory of Random Graph Shift in Truncated-Spectrum vRKHS

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23880
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23880
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Derives a generalization bound for graph domain adaptation using a truncated-spectrum vector-valued RKHS, factorizing the transfer penalty into latent Wasserstein distance, spectral geometry, and amplitude.

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a theoretical framework for graph classification under domain shift, leveraging random graph models (RGMs) to formalize shifts as changes in latent distributions or edge kernels. The main contribution is a generalization bound expressed in a truncated-spectrum vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space (vRKHS). The bound cleanly separates the transfer error into three interpretable factors: (i) domain discrepancy quantified by a latent Wasserstein distance between source and target RGM measures, (ii) a spectral‑geometry term governed by the r‑th eigenvalue (λr) of the integral operator, reflecting the effective dimensionality of the loss mapping, and (iii) an amplitude term aggregating convergence (graph size), optimization/deformation (RGM perturbation and network stability), approximation, and label noise. Empirical experiments on benchmark datasets confirm key predictions: the latent Wasserstein correlates strongly with test loss, the truncated-spectrum assumption holds with effective rank far smaller than the total, target loss decreases with graph size, and layerwise regularization can improve stability by dampening perturbation amplification. The result yields explicit, testable levers for understanding and mitigating graph domain shift.

## Method Summary
The authors model each class by an RGM Γ = (W, P, f) where latent positions x ∼ P induce edge probabilities via kernel W and node features via f. Domain shift between source S and target T arises from differences in P or W (with f fixed). To bound the adaptation error, they consider the loss mapping ℓh,gD and place it in a vRKHS HKℓ associated with an operator‑valued kernel Kℓ. Under the truncated‑spectrum assumption (Assumption 3.4) that the integral operator TKℓ has rapidly decaying eigenvalues, the norm ∥ℓh,gD∥HKℓ is upper‑bounded by (C/λr)∥ℓh,gD∥∞, where λr is the r‑th eigenvalue and r is a truncation rank capturing most energy. The ∞‑norm term is decomposed by comparing the discrete MPNN predictor ¯hG to its continuous RGM‑lifted counterpart ¯hW,P(f). Concentration arguments give a convergence error ΔN that shrinks with graph size N; a construction‑change operator T captures perturbations in the RGM (τ) and network weights (ΔΘ), leading to an optimization/deformation error ΔΓ,Θ that scales with a product of layerwise Lipschitz constants; additional approximation (ε3) and label noise (ε4) complete the amplitude. The final bound (Theorem H.1) sums these terms:  
transfer penalty ≤ O(ΔD + C/λr·(ΔN + ΔΓ,Θ + ε3 + ε4)).  
This factorization reveals how domain discrepancy, spectral truncation, graph size, and network architecture jointly govern domain‑adaptation performance.

## Key Results
- The generalization bound factorizes the transfer error into (i) latent Wasserstein distance (ΔD), (ii) spectral geometry (λr), and (iii) amplitude (ΔN + ΔΓ,Θ + ε3 + ε4).
- Empirical validation: latent Wasserstein correlates with test loss (PCC≈0.726) on PTC; the truncated‑spectrum assumption holds with effective rank ≪ total dimensions and exponential eigenvalue decay; target loss decreases as training graph size increases.
- The amplitude decomposition is confirmed: convergence error (ΔN) drops with larger N, and layerwise regularization (e.g., front‑heavy) reduces the amplification effect captured by ΔΓ,Θ, improving transfer accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Domain Discrepancy via Latent Wasserstein
The theory models domain shift as changes in the random graph model components—either the latent distribution P or the edge kernel W—while keeping the feature mapping f fixed. By viewing observed graph signal distributions µS, µT as push‑forwards of the latent measures, the authors prove (Proposition 3.3) that the 1‑Wasserstein distance between µS and µT can be upper‑bounded by a quantity ∆D that depends on the class‑wise latent Wasserstein distances between Pj S and Pj T. Thus the domain discrepancy term in the bound directly captures the separation between source and target latent distributions. Core assumptions: intra‑class graphs share the same RGM; the latent space has bounded diameter and covering number; f is Hölder continuous. Evidence anchors: Proposition 3.3; Exp 1 shows a strong correlation (PCC~0.726) between estimated latent WD and test loss on PTC. Corpus evidence is weak because the RGM‑based formalism is novel. The mechanism breaks if the shift cannot be expressed via P/W changes (e.g., label distribution shift) or if RGM fitting is poor/unidentifiable.

### Spectral Factorization via Truncated Spectrum
The vRKHS norm of the loss mapping is controlled by the spectrum of the integral operator TKℓ associated with the kernel Kℓ. Assumption 3.4 posits that TKℓ has rapidly decaying eigenvalues so that the majority of the energy of ℓh,gD lies in the top‑r eigenspace. Under this assumption, Proposition 3.5 yields ∥ℓh,gD∥HKℓ ≤ (C/λr)∥ℓh,gD∥∞. Here λr (the r‑th eigenvalue) acts as an inverse complexity measure: a smaller λr inflates the bound. The amplitude ∥ℓh,gD∥∞ is further bounded using Lipschitz constants and hypothesis‑labeler disagreement. Empirical support: Exp 2 computes Gram matrices for 1‑WL, GIN, and PPGN on multiple datasets and finds effective rank rε far smaller than the total dimensions, with eigenvalues decaying nearly exponentially—validating the truncated‑spectrum assumption. Corpus evidence is moderate; spectral methods are common in graph neural networks but the specific vRKHS truncation for domain adaptation is original. The mechanism fails if the spectrum of the loss mapping is heavy‑tailed, i.e., does not concentrate on a few eigenvectors, forcing a large r and weakening the bound.

### Amplitude Decomposition via Convergence and Stability
The ∞‑norm disagreement between the discrete MPNN predictor ¯hG and the ideal continuous labeling rule gD is decomposed into interpretable components. Theorem 3.7 shows that as the number of nodes N grows, ¯hG converges to its continuous counterpart ¯hW,P(f) at a rate ΔN that depends on the RGM’s covering number and the network’s Lipschitz constants. Theorem 3.9 addresses the optimization/deformation error ΔΓ,Θ arising from perturbations in the RGM (τ) and network weights (ΔΘ). The bound reveals a layerwise product structure: downstream Lipschitz constants amplify earlier‑layer perturbations, making deep networks sensitive to weight updates. The amplitude term thus aggregates ΔN (convergence), ΔΓ,Θ (construction‑change), approximation error ε3, and label noise ε4. Evidence: Exp 3 demonstrates that (a) larger N reduces target loss (supporting ΔN), and (b) non‑uniform layerwise regularization (front‑heavy or back‑heavy) alters the layerwise norm product and can improve average transfer accuracy, consistent with the ΔΓ,Θ analysis. Corpus evidence is moderate; prior work covers convergence of MPNNs but the combined decomposition for domain adaptation is new. The mechanism breaks down if graphs are too small (ΔN large), RGM deformations or weight updates are aggressive (ΔΓ,Θ large), or network components violate Lipschitz assumptions.

## Foundational Learning

### Vector‑valued Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (vRKHS)
- *Why needed:* The bound’s complexity term ∥ℓh,gD∥HKℓ lives in a vRKHS. This space accommodates vector‑valued losses and connects the smoothness of the hypothesis to the reproducing kernel Kℓ. Understanding vRKHS is essential to interpret the spectral factorization.
- *Quick check:* Given an operator‑valued kernel Kℓ with eigenpairs (λi, ψi), how does truncating to the top‑r eigenvectors bound the norm of f∈HKℓ?

### Random Graph Model (RGM)
- *Why needed:* RGMs provide a generative lens to define domain shift via changes in W, P, f. They enable lifting discrete MPNNs to continuous objects and analyzing distributional shifts rigorously.
- *Quick check:* How does changing the latent distribution P while keeping W and f fixed induce a shift in the observed graph distribution µ?

### Wasserstein Distance between Graph Distributions
- *Why needed:* The domain discrepancy term depends on W1(µS, µT). The paper pulls this back to a latent Wasserstein distance between RGM measures, making it tangible for empirical estimation.
- *Quick check:* If two class‑wise RGMs differ only in their latent multiclass Gaussian means by a shift vector s, how does the latent W2 scale with s?

## Architecture Onboarding

#### Component Map
Discrete MPNN (¯hG) with mean aggregation produces graph‑level representations → MLP classifier (hcls) yields predictions. The continuous MPNN (¯hW,P(f)) acts as the ideal feature extractor for infinite graphs. The construction‑change operator T perturbs the RGM (τ) and MPNN weights (ΔΘ) to model optimization/deformation effects. Ground‑truth labeling rule gD is decomposed analogously to the predictor.

#### Critical Path
1. Start from a domain‑adaptation error bound (Proposition 2.2).  
2. Pull back domain divergence to a latent Wasserstein distance (Proposition 3.3).  
3. Introduce the truncated‑spectrum vRKHS (Assumption 3.4) and factorize the RKHS norm (Proposition 3.5).  
4. Decompose hypothesis–labeler ∞‑norm disagreement into convergence (Theorem 3.7) and optimization (Theorem 3.9) plus approximation and noise.  
5. Combine all pieces (Theorem H.1) to obtain the final bound (Equation 218).

#### Design Tradeoffs
- **Truncation rank r:** Larger r enriches the hypothesis space but may reduce λr (smaller eigenvalue) and increase the penalty. An effective rank much smaller than the total is desirable.
- **Layerwise Lipschitz vs. stability:** Deeper MPNNs amplify perturbations via a product of Lipschitz constants. Layerwise regularization (e.g., front‑heavy ℓ2) can reduce this product and improve stability.
- **Graph size N:** Training on larger graphs lowers the convergence error ΔN but may affect computational feasibility.
- **RGM deformation strength (∥∇τ∥∞, NPτ):** Strong deformations inflate ΔΓ,Θ. Controlling these (e.g., via data augmentation that respects RGM smoothness) helps transfer.

#### Failure Signatures
- Latent Wasserstein estimate does not correlate with target loss → poor RGM fit or misspecified shift model.
- Spectrum not concentrated (effective rank comparable to total) → truncated‑spectrum assumption violated.
- Target loss does not decrease with larger training graph size N → convergence error ΔN not the dominant factor or other effects mask it.
- Layerwise regularization (non‑uniform) fails to improve or even degrades transfer accuracy → the product‑structure amplification may be absent or other factors dominate.

#### First Three Experiments
1. **Domain discrepancy correlation (Exp 1):** Train a 3‑layer GIN on each subgroup of the PTC dataset; compute class‑wise latent Wasserstein distances using latent space matching (LSM); correlate with cross‑subgroup test losses. Expected positive correlation validates ∆D as a transfer penalty proxy.
2. **Truncated spectrum validation (Exp 2):** Compute Gram matrices for 1‑WL, GIN, and PPGN on IMDB‑MULTI, NCI1, and PROTEINS; report the truncation rank rε needed to capture a fixed energy proportion (e.g., 0.99). Expected: rε ≪ min(n,d) and eigenvalues decay nearly exponentially.
3. **Amplitude terms (Exp 3):**  
   (a) Vary the training graph size N in a synthetic domain‑adaptation setting; observe target loss decrease as N grows.  
   (b) On MUTAGENICITY M0–M3 splits, compare front‑heavy, back‑heavy, and uniform ℓ2 regularization; measure a proxy for the layerwise norm product and its correlation with transfer accuracy. Expected: larger N reduces loss; non‑uniform regularizers affect the product and can improve average transfer accuracy.

## Limitations
- Theory relies on accurate RGM estimation; mis‑specification or unidentifiable shifts (e.g., simultaneous changes in P and W) can invalidate the bound.
- Truncated‑spectrum assumption may break for highly expressive GNN architectures (e.g., 3‑WL GNNs) that exhibit heavy‑tailed spectra.
- Experimental evaluation is limited to a handful of medium‑sized molecular and social datasets, leaving scalability to larger graphs and broader domain shifts untested.
- Reproduction details are sparse, hindering independent verification of the RGM fitting and spectral computation procedures.
