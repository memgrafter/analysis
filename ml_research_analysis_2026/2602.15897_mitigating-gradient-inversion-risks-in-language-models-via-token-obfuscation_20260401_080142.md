# Mitigating Gradient Inversion Risks in Language Models via Token Obfuscation

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2602.15897
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15897
* **Reference Count:** 40
* **Key Result:** GHOST reduces token recovery by gradient inversion attacks to ≤1% while preserving model utility (F1 up to 0.92, perplexity as low as 5.45) and remains robust to adaptive attacks.

## Executive Summary
Federated learning of language models exposes private training data to gradient inversion attacks (GIAs) that reconstruct inputs from shared gradients. Existing defenses operating at the gradient level (e.g., noise, pruning) fail because semantic similarity persists across gradients, embeddings, and tokens. To address this, we introduce GHOST, a token-level obfuscation strategy that replaces each original token with a semantically distinct yet embedding‑proximate “shadow token” drawn from the vocabulary. GHOST first identifies candidate shadow tokens by filtering neighbors based on indirect similarity, mutual similarity, and lemma equivalence. It then selects a sequence of shadow tokens that minimizes the divergence of the model’s hidden states across all layers, preserving features critical for training while breaking the semantic link needed for gradient inversion. Across 21 language models (from BERT to Llama) and diverse tasks, GHOST achieves near‑perfect defense against five state‑of‑the‑art GIAs, lowering token recovery rates to as little as 1% (compared to >90% without defense) and outperforming prior defenses by up to 98% in defended token proportions. Utility remains high: classification F1 scores reach 0.92 and generation perplexity drops to 5.45, matching models trained on original data. GHOST also withstands adaptive attacks where the adversary has full knowledge of the defense. This work demonstrates a practical and effective approach to safeguarding privacy in federated NLP.

## Method Summary
GHOST operates in two phases before the fine‑tuning of a language model. **Searching**: For each token in the model’s vocabulary, its top‑k (k=70) nearest neighbors in embedding space are retrieved. A shadow token candidate set is built by excluding neighbors that share excessive overlapping neighbors (|N(t)∩N(t')|/k > τ_o), are mutual neighbors, or have the same lemma (after lemmatization). **Selection**: Given an input sentence, GHOST obtains the hidden states from all layers of the original tokens. It then initializes an obfuscated token sequence by randomly picking one shadow candidate per position. An iterative process refines the sequence: for each position i, every candidate in the shadow set is tried, and the token that yields the smallest mean‑squared error (MSE) between the original hidden states and those produced by the candidate sequence (aggregated across all layers) is chosen. Iteration stops when the improvement in MSE falls below a threshold τ_d. The resulting obfuscated token IDs are detokenized into a natural‑language sentence that retains the original model‑internal representations (training utility) while being semantically unrelated to the true input (privacy). This obfuscated data is then used for fine‑tuning the model in a standard federated learning setup.

## Key Results
- GHOST reduces token recovery rates under GIAs to ≤1% across 21 models, a dramatic improvement over >90% for undefended models and up to 98% more defended tokens than gradient‑level defenses (noise, pruning).
- Training utility is largely preserved: classification F1 scores reach 0.92 and generation perplexity as low as 5.45, comparable to models trained on clean data.
- The defense remains effective even against adaptive attacks with full adversarial knowledge of GHOST’s parameters, demonstrating robust privacy guarantees.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Gradient inversion attacks rely on the assumption that gradients reflect the semantic content of the input tokens; by back‑propagating gradients, an attacker can find tokens that produce similar loss landscapes. GHOST severs this connection by exploiting the high dimensionality of the token embedding space: many tokens are close in embedding distance yet belong to unrelated semantic categories. Substituting original tokens with such shadow tokens keeps the hidden‑state activations (and thus the training signal) nearly unchanged because the model processes the embeddings, while the gradient significantly diverges from what would be produced by the true tokens due to the nonlinearities of the network. The two‑step selection guarantees that the obfuscated input yields hidden states nearly identical to those of the original input (preserving utility) but is semantically distant, making it infeasible for an attacker to recover the original tokens from the gradient alone. The iterative MSE minimization ensures consistency across all layers, amplifying the privacy protection without sacrificing the optimization trajectory of fine‑tuning.

- **Gradient Inversion Attack (GIA)**: Attacks that reconstruct training data from shared gradients. *Why needed*: It is the primary threat GHOST defends against. *Quick check*: Can you describe the basic principle of a GIA (e.g., DLG)?
- **Token Embeddings**: Vector representations of token IDs in a model’s embedding layer. *Why needed*: GHOST searches for replacement tokens in the embedding space. *Quick check*: How does a token ID map to an embedding vector?
- **Cosine Similarity / Nearest Neighbors**: Measures of vector similarity used to find embedding‑proximate tokens. *Why needed*: The searching step retrieves top‑k similar tokens. *Quick check*: What does a cosine similarity of 1.0 indicate?
- **Hidden States (Transformer Layers)**: The intermediate outputs of each transformer layer. *Why needed*: GHOST’s selection minimises divergence of hidden states to preserve training dynamics. *Quick check*: What information do hidden states capture that is useful for downstream tasks?
- **Lemmatization**: Reducing a word to its base or dictionary form. *Why needed*: GHOST excludes tokens with the same lemma to ensure semantic distinction. *Quick check*: What is the lemma of “running” (as a verb)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Precomputed Neighbor Index → Shadow Candidate Sets → Original Hidden States → Iterative Token Selection → Obfuscated Tokens

**Critical Path:**
1. Build neighbor index (once per model).
2. For each training sentence: obtain original hidden states.
3. Perform iterative token selection to produce obfuscated sequence.
4. Save obfuscated text for fine‑tuning.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- *k* (neighbor count) and τ_o (overlap threshold) control the size and diversity of shadow sets; larger *k* yields more candidates but may include semantically similar tokens; smaller τ_o increases diversity but risks embedding distance.
- Iteration limit (or τ_d) trades off computational cost for obfuscation quality.
- Beam width >1 (if used) improves selection but increases forward passes linearly.
- Privacy vs. utility: stricter filters (lower τ_o, exclude more) enhance privacy but may harm model performance if embeddings diverge too much.

**Failure Signatures:**
- High token recovery rate (>30%) under GIA indicates insufficient semantic obfuscation.
- Significant drop in utility metrics (e.g., F1 decrease >0.1, perplexity increase >2) compared to clean‑data training.
- Excessive memory usage or runtime suggests candidate pools are too large or the iteration does not converge.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Neighbor Index Validation**: For BERT‑base, compute top‑k (k=70) neighbors for a few tokens. Verify that after filtering (τ_o=0.1, mutual, lemma), shadow tokens are semantically unrelated (e.g., for “good”, shadow candidates should not be synonyms).
2. **Selection Convergence**: Run the iterative selection on a short sentence (e.g., “I love this movie.”). Plot MSE vs. iteration; ensure it decreases and stabilizes quickly. Check that final obfuscated tokens have low embedding distance to originals.
3. **End‑to‑End Defense Test**: Fine‑tune BERT‑base on obfuscated SST‑2 (small subset). Evaluate test F1. Then run DAGER attack on gradients from the fine‑tuned model to recover tokens; compute ROUGE‑1. Confirm F1 ≈ clean baseline and ROUGE‑1 ≤0.1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does GHOST's approach of finding semantically distinct yet embedding‑proximate tokens generalize to all tokenization schemes and languages, especially where the token vocabulary is limited or morphological variations cause semantic distinctions to be encoded differently?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from the experimental setup (Section 5.1) which uses only English datasets and standard tokenizers (BERT WordPiece, GPT‑2 BPE, Llama SentencePiece). The core insight – “due to the large scale of the token space, there exist semantically distinct yet embedding‑proximate tokens” – relies on vocabulary size and embedding geometry and is not evaluated on other tokenization methods or languages.
- **Why unresolved:** If the token space is small or embeddings are not sufficiently rich, GHOST’s searching step may fail to find suitable shadow tokens, leading to poor obfuscation or unacceptable utility loss. The paper does not address this scenario.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate GHOST on a diverse set of languages with different tokenization (e.g., character‑level for Chinese, morpheme‑based for agglutinative languages) and on artificially constrained vocabularies. Measure whether adequate shadow tokens exist and whether defense strength (e.g., token recovery rates) remains high.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the computational overhead of GHOST’s two‑step process (searching and selection) when applied to large‑scale training scenarios with millions of sentences and very large models (e.g., LLMs with billions of parameters), and how can it be optimized without sacrificing the privacy‑utility trade‑off?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from the methodology (Algorithms 1 and 2) which involve vocabulary‑wide neighbor searches and iterative hidden‑state optimization per token sequence. The paper mentions a hyperparameter study balancing “utility, privacy, and computational efficiency” (Appendix A.1) but provides no scalability metrics (e.g., runtime, memory) for varying data sizes or model scales.
- **Why unresolved:** In realistic federated learning, training data can be massive. The pre‑processing overhead of GHOST (e.g., forward passes through the model for candidate selection) may become prohibitive, yet its feasibility at scale is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure wall‑clock time, memory usage, and scaling behavior on datasets of increasing size (e.g., 1 k, 10 k, 100 k sentences) and across model families (from BERT‑base to Llama‑13B). Explore optimizations like caching hidden states, using smaller proxy models, or parallelization.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can future gradient inversion attacks that exploit richer information (e.g., multi‑layer gradient statistics, attention patterns, or intermediate activations) potentially circumvent GHOST’s obfuscation, even when assuming full adversarial knowledge of the defense?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from the attack evaluation (Section 5.5) which tests five existing GIAs (DLG, TAG, LAMP, GRAB, DAGER) and five adaptive strategies with full knowledge of GHOST’s parameters. The authors note that GIAs are a rapidly advancing field; they do not claim provable security against all conceivable attacks.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical analysis (Section 4) assumes local smoothness and small parameter updates. Novel attacks might violate these assumptions (e.g., by using second‑order gradients or model‑specific priors) and could find ways to map gradients back to original tokens despite large gradient deviations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design and test additional attack variants that incorporate richer side information (e.g., full Jacobian matrices, multi‑batch statistics, or meta‑learning‑based priors). If such attacks still fail, explore formalizing security guarantees; if they succeed, identify the weaknesses and strengthen GHOST accordingly.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does GHOST’s token obfuscation inadvertently affect other model qualities beyond standard utility metrics (e.g., fairness, robustness to adversarial perturbations, calibration), particularly for tasks that rely on fine‑grained semantics?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from the utility evaluation (Section 5.4) which reports accuracy/F1 for classification and loss/perplexity for generation. No analysis is provided on fairness, adversarial robustness, calibration, or performance on nuanced tasks like named‑entity recognition or question answering.
- **Why unresolved:** Replacing tokens with shadow substitutes may shift the training data distribution in subtle ways that standard test splits do not capture. For instance, sensitive attributes could be unintentionally altered, or models might become more vulnerable to adversarial examples. The broader impact on model behavior remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct comprehensive evaluations on additional benchmarks that measure fairness (e.g., bias audits), robustness (e.g., success rate of adversarial attacks), and calibration (e.g., expected calibration error). Compare models trained on original vs. GHOST‑obfuscated data across these dimensions to detect any adverse effects.

## Limitations
- **Parameter ambiguities:** τ_o threshold (fractional vs absolute), exact hidden‑state aggregation (sum vs mean), and token selection strategy (greedy vs beam) are not precisely defined.
- **Subword lemmatization:** Handling of subword tokens (e.g., “##ing”) across different tokenizers is unspecified, potentially affecting shadow token diversity.
- **Scalability:** Iterative per‑token forward passes are computationally intensive; feasibility on models >1B parameters and long sequences is unproven.

## Confidence
- **Defense efficacy (≤1% token recovery):** Medium
- **Utility preservation (F1 ≈0.92, PPL ≈5.45):** High
- **Robustness to adaptive attacks:** Medium
