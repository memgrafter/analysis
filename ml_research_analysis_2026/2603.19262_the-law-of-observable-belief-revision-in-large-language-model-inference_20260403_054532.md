# The α-Law of Observable Belief Revision in Large Language Model Inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19262
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19262
- Reference count: 18
- Primary result: The α-law (log posterior = α (log prior + log evidence) + constant) quantifies LLM belief revision; α < 1 guarantees asymptotic stability. Empirically, single-step α≈1.163 (R²=0.76), while multi-step α decays from 0.84 to 0.54 (geometric mean 0.75), ensuring contractive long-run dynamics.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the lack of stability guarantees for probability updates in large language models (LLMs) during iterative reasoning processes such as chain-of-thought. It introduces the α-law, a multiplicative scaling law on the log scale that relates a model’s posterior belief to its prior and newly presented evidence. The exponent α captures the balance between prior and evidence; theoretically, α < 1 is necessary and sufficient for asymptotic stability under repeated revision. Across 4,975 problems and multiple state-of-the-art model families (GPT‑5.2, Claude Sonnet 4), the authors measure single‑step α values near 1.16, slightly above the Bayesian optimum (α=1) but still within a range that, when combined with a monotonic decay observed in multi‑step settings, yields long‑run contractive behavior. The α-law provides a principled diagnostic for monitoring the stability and quality of LLM reasoning.

## Method Summary
The study employs a controlled four‑step protocol on multiple‑choice reasoning tasks. First, the model generates a set of candidate answers (M=8). From these, a prior probability distribution *q₀* over answer options is elicited, either through structured verbal probabilities or token log‑probs. Second, a deterministic verifier compares each candidate to the ground truth to construct an evidence distribution *b*—often a peaked distribution at the correct answer with configurable strength *s*. Third, the model is prompted again (with the same problem and the evidence) to produce a posterior distribution *q₁*. Finally, the scaling law log *q₁* = α (log *q₀* + log *b*) + *c* is fitted via ordinary least squares across many problems, yielding the belief revision exponent α. To probe stability under iteration, the process is repeated for multiple steps: the posterior from step *t* becomes the prior for step *t*+1, and a new evidence distribution is generated (either by the same verifier or a simulated one). Per‑step αₜ is estimated similarly, and the geometric mean α_geo is used to evaluate asymptotic stability (α_geo < 1 ensures contraction).

## Key Results
- Identified the α-law: log posterior = α (log prior + log evidence) + constant; α is the belief revision exponent.
- Theoretically proved that α < 1 is necessary and sufficient for asymptotic stability under repeated revision.
- Empirically, single‑step revisions yield α≈1.163 ± 0.084 with mean R²=0.76; multi‑step revisions show a monotonic decay of α from 0.84 to 0.54 over 7 steps, giving a geometric mean α=0.75 and guaranteeing long‑run contractive dynamics.
- Model‑specific trust ratios: GPT‑5.2 relatively balanced between prior and evidence; Claude places greater weight on evidence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLM probability revisions follow a multiplicative scaling law (`log q₁ = α[log q₀ + log b] + c`) where `α` is a belief revision exponent.
- **Mechanism:** The update is the stationary point of a KL‑regularized variational objective `J[q] = α D_KL(q||q₀) - E_q[log b]`. This yields a tempered posterior where evidence and prior are combined on the log‑scale with a tunable weight `α`.
- **Core assumption:** Model‑elicited probability distributions (`q₀`, `q₁`) and constructed evidence (`b`) are meaningful representations of the model's "beliefs" under the verification protocol.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We identify a consistent multiplicative scaling law... logq₁(i) = α[logq₀(i)+logb(i)]+c"
  - [section 3.2] "This update arises as the stationary condition of a KL‑regularized variational objective"
  - [corpus] Weak support. Neighbors focus on logical postulates for belief *change* (AGM/KM), not empirical scaling laws in LLMs. No direct corpus anchor for the specific tempered posterior form in LLM inference.
- **Break condition:** If the model cannot produce valid probability distributions (high fallback contamination), or if the evidence encoding `b` is too weak (near‑uniform), the log‑linear fit degrades (`R²` drops).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Asymptotic stability under repeated revision is guaranteed if and only if `α < 1` (Theorem 1).
- **Mechanism:** In the Hilbert projective metric, the linear map on log‑deviations contracts by factor `α`. For `α<1`, this ensures geometric convergence of the distribution to a fixed point. For `α≥1`, perturbations amplify.
- **Core assumption:** The update rule (2) with fixed `α` and fixed evidence `b` applies at each step. The fixed point `q*` exists and is unique for the given `b`.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] "Theorem 1 (α‑stability). Let q* be a fixed point... If α < 1, then q* is asymptotically stable... If α ≥ 1, the fixed point is unstable."
  - [section 6.1] Multi‑step experiments show `α` decays from ~0.84 to ~0.54, making the *geometric mean* `α_geo = 0.75 < 1`, satisfying the time‑varying stability condition `∏ α_t² → 0`.
- **Break condition:** The theorem assumes fixed `α` and `b`. If evidence `b_t` varies wildly or `α_t` does not decay (e.g., remains >1 across steps), divergence occurs. Also fails if the model's posterior elicitation is inconsistent (e.g., non‑normalizable outputs).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Single‑step `α` values measured across models are near‑Bayesian (`α ≈ 1.16`), but multi‑step revision drives `α` down, yielding contractive long‑run dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** The single‑step measurement reflects the initial "aggressiveness" of evidence integration. Under iteration, implicit regularization (from the model's generation process or the prompting protocol) causes the effective `α` to decay, self‑stabilizing the process.
- **Core assumption:** The decay trajectory (`α_t` decreasing with `t`) is a stable property of the model's iterative revision mechanism and is not solely an artifact of the specific simulation protocol (e.g., GPT‑4 vs. primary models).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "single‑step revisions show α ≈ 1.163 ± 0.084... Multi‑step experiments reveal α decays monotonically from 0.84 to 0.54 over 7 steps"
  - [section 6.1] "α decreases monotonically from 0.838 to 0.543 over 7 steps... statistically significant linear decay (slope = ‑0.040, p=0.014)"
- **Break condition:** The decay is observed in a multi‑step protocol with *simulated* verifier feedback on GPT‑4. It may not hold for the primary models (GPT‑5.2, Claude) under their native revision mechanisms (e.g., CoT, self‑reflection). If `α` does not decay in native settings, long‑run stability is not guaranteed.

## Foundational Learning
**Variational Inference & KL Regularization**  
- **Why needed here:** The α‑law is derived as the stationary condition of a KL‑regularized objective. Understanding this connection interprets `α` as an inverse regularization strength (`α = 1/(1+λ)`).
- **Quick check question:** How does increasing the regularization strength `λ` affect the balance between the prior `q₀` and the evidence `b` in the tempered posterior?

**Projective Metrics & Contraction Mappings**  
- **Why needed here:** Theorem 1’s stability proof uses the Hilbert projective metric, which contracts under the linear update when `α<1`. This is a non‑standard metric for probability distributions.
- **Quick check question:** Why does the constant normalization term `c_t` cancel when computing the Hilbert diameter between `q_t` and the fixed point `q*`?

**Bayesian Updating as a Special Case**  
- **Why needed here:** The α‑law generalizes Bayes (`α=1`). Understanding the baseline is crucial to interpret "near‑Bayesian" (`α≈1.16`) as slight overconfidence and the effect of evidence encoding on absolute `α`.
- **Quick check question:** What does an `α > 1` imply about the model's responsiveness to new evidence compared to a Bayesian updater?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Elicitation Module -> Verification Module -> Regression Module -> Stability Analyzer

### Critical path
1. Run single‑step protocol on a clean dataset →  
2. Fit α‑law, check R² > 0.5 →  
3. If α > 1, run multi‑step validation to measure decay trajectory →  
4. Compute α_geo. If α_geo ≥ 1, system is at risk of divergence under iteration.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Evidence encoding (`s`):** Strong evidence (`s=0.9`) yields cleaner fits but α is encoding‑dependent. Weaker evidence (`s=0.51`) tests robustness but may increase noise. Must report encoding alongside α.
- **Elicitation method:** Logprobs are less prone to parsing artifacts but require model access. Self‑reported probabilities are more generally applicable but can have malformed JSON (30% in paper). Use median α to robustify.
- **Single‑α vs. two‑parameter:** Unified model is empirically sufficient (R² improvement <0.001) but the trust ratio `τ=α_b/α_q0` is poorly identifiable when priors are near‑uniform. Report `τ` only with informative priors.

### Failure signatures
- **High fallback contamination (>20%):** Model fails to produce valid probability distributions; α estimates are artifacts.
- **Low R² (<0.3):** The log‑linear form does not hold; could indicate noisy evidence, inconsistent elicitation, or a model regime where the α‑law does not apply (e.g., base models).
- **α does not decay in multi‑step:** If αₜ remains >1 across steps or increases, iterated revision is expansive and likely to diverge on harder problems.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Single‑step baseline:** Run the 4‑step protocol (generate→prior→verify→posterior) on 100‑200 clean problems from GPQA Diamond with GPT‑5.2/Claude. Fit α‑law, report α [95% CI] and R². Expect α ≈ 1.1‑1.3.
2. **Evidence encoding sensitivity:** For the same problem set, vary `s` in {0.51, 0.60, 0.70, 0.80, 0.90, 0.99}. Recompute α(`s`) each time. Confirm monotonic decrease and that R² remains >0.55 across all `s`.
3. **Multi‑step decay (proof‑of‑concept):** Use a model with accessible API (GPT‑4) on 50 GPQA problems. Implement 3‑5 revision steps using the same verification (or a simulated one). Track αₜ per step. Test for linear decay (αₜ = β₀ + β₁ t) with β₁ < 0.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** Does the near‑Bayesian α ≈ 1.16 observed on high‑accuracy benchmarks generalize to tasks where state‑of‑the‑art LLMs achieve only 40‑70% accuracy?  
   - **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly list as future work: "validation on benchmarks where models achieve 40–70% accuracy, which would test whether the near‑Bayesian finding holds when models genuinely struggle" (Conclusion, page 17).  
   - **Why unresolved:** All experiments in the paper used benchmarks with >99% model accuracy (e.g., GPQA Diamond, TheoremQA). On such easy tasks, the model's beliefs are almost always correct, so the α‑law might reflect overconfidence on trivial problems rather than genuine belief revision dynamics under uncertainty. Testing on harder benchmarks where errors are common is essential to validate the law's applicability to real‑world reasoning.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the same α‑law measurement protocol (four‑step: generate candidates, elicit prior, verify, elicit posterior) to a suite of challenging benchmarks where current LLMs achieve 40‑70% accuracy (e.g., recent competition‑level math problems, adversarial reasoning sets). Compare the resulting α distributions, stability properties, and trust ratios to those from the high‑accuracy benchmarks. If α remains near 1.16 and the law fits well, it confirms robustness; if α shifts or the law breaks, it suggests the finding is task‑dependent.

2. **Question:** Is the observed slight overconfidence (α > 1) causally linked to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) during model alignment?  
   - **Basis in paper:** The authors hypothesize: "Our hypothesis that α > 1 reflects RLHF training is plausible but unverified. Controlled pre‑/post‑RLHF comparisons are needed" (Discussion, page 13). They also list as future work: "systematic pre‑/post‑RLHF comparison to test the alignment training hypothesis" (Conclusion, page 17).  
   - **Why unresolved:** All models tested are instruction‑tuned, meaning they have undergone some form of alignment (RLHF, DPO, etc.). Without comparing the same model before and after alignment, we cannot determine whether RLHF shifts α from the Bayesian optimum (α=1) toward overconfidence. It is possible that base models have α ≈ 1 and alignment increases it, or that base models already show α > 1 and alignment has no effect.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Secure access to a model family at different training stages: a base model, after supervised fine‑tuning (SFT), and after RLHF. For each stage, measure α using the standard protocol on a held‑out set. If α increases monotonically with alignment steps, it supports the hypothesis. Alternatively, compare models known to use different alignment techniques (e.g., GPT‑5.2 vs. Claude) and correlate α with alignment details, though this is observational.

3. **Question:** Does the α decay trajectory observed in multi‑step revisions (from ~0.84 to ~0.54 over 7 steps) hold for GPT‑5.2 and Claude Sonnet 4, the primary models in the study?  
   - **Basis in paper:** The limitation states: "The multi‑step validation used GPT‑4 rather than GPT‑5.2 or Claude Sonnet 4, so we cannot disentangle model and protocol effects on baseline α" (Limitations, page 16). Future work: "multi‑step validation using the primary models (GPT‑5.2, Claude) to disentangle model and protocol effects on baseline α" (Conclusion, page 17).  
   - **Why unresolved:** The multi‑step experiment was performed on GPT‑4 due to API constraints. Since α varies across model families (e.g., single‑step α: GPT‑5.2 ~1.2, Claude ~1.1), the decay pattern could differ for the newer models. The stability guarantee (Theorem 1) requires that the geometric mean of α over steps be <1; if primary models have higher baseline α, the decay might be slower or not dip below 1 within the same number of steps.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the multi‑step revision protocol (7 steps with simulated verifier feedback) on the same 198 GPQA Diamond problems using GPT‑5.2 and Claude Sonnet 4. Estimate per‑step α and check for monotonic decay, final α, and geometric mean. If decay occurs and geometric mean <1, the stability result generalizes; if not, the law may need refinement.

4. **Question:** Does the α‑law hold for non‑dense architectures such as mixture‑of‑experts (MoE), sparse models, and multimodal models?  
   - **Basis in paper:** Future work includes: "extension to mixture‑of‑experts, sparse, and multimodal architectures" (Conclusion, page 17).  
   - **Why unresolved:** The study only evaluated dense, text‑only instruction‑tuned models (GPT‑5.2, Claude, GPT‑4, Llama). Different architectures have distinct internal mechanisms: MoE routes tokens to experts, sparse models have activated subsets, multimodal models integrate vision/audio. These could alter how beliefs are revised, e.g., through different attention patterns or cross‑modal interactions. The α‑law might be specific to dense transformers.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Select representative models: an MoE model (e.g., Mixtral 8x22B), a sparse model (e.g., Grok‑1), and a multimodal model (e.g., GPT‑4o). Apply the α‑law protocol on a common text‑based reasoning benchmark (to control for modality). Test whether the log‑linear relationship (log q1 = α(log q0 + log b) + c) holds with similar R². Compare α values and trust ratios across architectures. If the law fails or α differs systematically, it suggests architecture dependence.

## Limitations
- Evidence distribution *b* is constructed via deterministic, noise‑free verification; real‑world evidence may be ambiguous or contradictory, potentially degrading α‑law fits.
- Multi‑step α‑decay is demonstrated only with GPT‑4 simulating verifier feedback; native revision mechanisms in GPT‑5.2/Claude may not exhibit the same decay trajectory.
- Elicitation of probability distributions relies on structured outputs or logprobs; high fallback contamination (>20%) or malformed responses can bias α estimates.

## Confidence
- α‑law as empirical fit (log‑linear scaling): Medium
- Stability theorem (α < 1 necessary and sufficient): High
- Single‑step α ≈ 1.16 (near‑Bayesian): Medium‑High
- Multi‑step α decay and long‑run stability: Medium
