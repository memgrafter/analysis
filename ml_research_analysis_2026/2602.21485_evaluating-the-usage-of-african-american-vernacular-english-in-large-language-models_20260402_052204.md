# Evaluating the Usage of African-American Vernacular English in Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21485
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21485
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMs significantly underuse most AAVE grammatical features (habitual "be" at 14–48% of human rates), misapply them in different contexts, and replicate stereotypical themes despite generating more positive sentiment.

## Executive Summary
This study examines racial linguistic bias in large language models (LLMs) by evaluating how accurately three models—Llama-3-8B, GPT-4o-mini, and Gemma-2-27B—replicate the frequency and contextual usage of seven African American Vernacular English (AAVE) grammatical features. Human baselines are drawn from the CORAAL interview corpus and the TwitterAAE dataset. Using rule-based detectors to measure feature densities, the authors find that LLMs underproduce these features, often by a large margin, and apply them in inappropriate contexts. Moreover, while model-generated AAVE sentences exhibit higher sentiment positivity, they also reproduce stereotypical themes, highlighting systematic misrepresentation.

The methodology involves generating domain-specific text (interviews and tweets) via role‑based prompts, preprocessing identically to the human corpora, and applying validated spaCy‑based parsers to detect the seven features. Statistical comparisons reveal significant gaps between model and human outputs across all features. The findings underscore the need for improved linguistic fidelity in LLM training and deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors perform a quantitative evaluation by comparing human and model‑generated texts on AAVE feature usage. Human baselines consist of CORAAL (109,234 sentences) and a randomly subsampled TwitterAAE (110,000 tweets after filtering). For each of the three target LLMs, the researchers prompt the models to produce 1,000 interview‑style responses mimicking CORAAL and 750 sets of 250 tweets mimicking TwitterAAE, yielding approximately 187,500 model‑generated tweets total. All texts undergo identical preprocessing: non‑alphanumeric characters are excluded, and sentence segmentation is aligned.

Seven AAVE grammatical features are detected using rule‑based spaCy parsers: habitual *be*, negative concord, *ain’t*, double comparative, perfective *done*, multiple modals, and null copula. The detectors are validated on hand‑annotated CORAAL samples, achieving ≥90% F1 as reported. For each dataset, the primary metric is feature density (count per 10,000 sentences). Secondary analyses include the frequency of features by extracted subject and the sentiment of feature‑containing sentences using VADER. Statistical significance of differences between each model and the human baseline is tested with two‑proportion or Fisher’s exact test.

## Key Results
- LLMs underuse most AAVE grammatical features; for habitual *be*, density ranges from 14% to 48% of human levels.
- Models misapply AAVE features in contextually distinct ways, diverging from human patterns of subject association.
- Despite generating more positive sentiment in sentences with AAVE features, LLMs replicate stereotypical thematic content.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The evaluation relies on linguistically grounded, rule‑based detectors that precisely capture the syntactic properties of each AAVE feature. Validation on human‑annotated data ensures measurement reliability. By computing standardized densities and applying rigorous statistical tests, the study isolates systematic discrepancies between model outputs and human baselines. The controlled generation using role‑based prompts further isolates the models’ ability to emulate AAVE patterns, making the observed underuse and misapplication robust evidence of bias.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Human data (CORAAL/TwitterAAE) -> Preprocessing -> Feature detection -> Density calculation -> Baseline
Prompts -> LLM generation -> Preprocessing -> Feature detection -> Density calculation -> Model outputs
Feature sentences -> Sentiment analysis (VADER)
Dependency parses -> Subject extraction -> Top subjects analysis
Densities -> Statistical comparison -> Significance assessment

Critical path: Define prompts -> Generate model texts -> Preprocess -> Detect features -> Compute densities -> Perform statistical tests. These steps form the longest dependency chain; any error or delay propagates to the final results.

Design tradeoffs:
- Rule‑based detectors vs. ML classifiers: Rules offer transparency and linguistic fidelity but may miss edge cases, favoring precision over recall.
- Using two distinct human corpora enriches generalizability but introduces register variation, complicating direct comparisons.
- Generating fixed volumes (1k interviews, 750 tweet sets) balances computational cost against sample size; more generations could reduce variance but were not pursued.

Failure signatures:
- Detector alignment failure: Reimplemented detectors’ precision/recall fall short of the reported ≥90%, compromising all density comparisons.
- Prompt sensitivity: Minor wording changes or API updates shift model output distributions, leading to inconsistent feature frequencies.
- Sampling instability: The TwitterAAE subset is a small random sample; a different seed could yield baseline densities that differ meaningfully from the paper’s.

First three experiments:
1. **Validate detectors**: On a held‑out CORAAL sample (500 sentences), measure precision, recall, and F1; adjust patterns until ≥90% F1 is reached.
2. **Generate model outputs**: For each model and domain, run the exact prompt templates to produce the required number of texts; apply identical preprocessing as the human data.
3. **Compute and compare**: Calculate feature densities for all datasets, run the specified statistical tests, and record effect sizes and p‑values for each model‑feature combination.

## Limitations
- The exact spaCy rules for the AAVE feature detectors are not provided; reengineering may introduce measurement error.
- The TwitterAAE subsampling method lacks reproducibility details (random seed, stratification), so the human baseline might not be stable.
- Model output variance across seeds and API versions is unaddressed; the study’s findings could be sensitive to these factors.

## Confidence
- Underuse of AAVE features: Medium
- Contextual misalignment: Low
- Sentiment shift (higher positivity): Medium
- Stereotypical themes: Low
