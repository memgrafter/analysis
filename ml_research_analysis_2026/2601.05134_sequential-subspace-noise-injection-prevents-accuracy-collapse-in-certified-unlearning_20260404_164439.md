# Sequential Subspace Noise Injection Prevents Accuracy Collapse in Certified Unlearning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.05134
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05134
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Partitioning model parameters into orthogonal subspaces and sequentially injecting noise during unlearning prevents the severe accuracy collapse seen in standard noisy fine-tuning, while preserving the same (ε, δ) differential privacy budget.

## Executive Summary
Certified unlearning via differential privacy guarantees data removal but typically incurs drastic accuracy loss due to destructive noise injection. This paper introduces sequential subspace noise injection (block-wise noisy fine-tuning), which divides model parameters into orthogonal subspaces and applies noisy unlearning sequentially to one block at a time. By distributing the same total noise variance across both time and subspaces, per-step perturbation is reduced, mitigating harm to model utility while maintaining the original privacy certificate. Empirical results on MNIST and CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 and ViT-Tiny show that while standard noisy fine-tuning accuracy falls below 20% on CIFAR-10 and fails to recover, the proposed method remains near retraining-from-scratch performance. In class-wise deletion, it achieves perfect unlearning accuracy and robustness against membership inference attacks, with lower computational cost than other certified approaches.

## Method Summary
The method extends noisy fine-tuning (NFT) by decomposing the model parameter vector θ ∈ ℝᵈ into k mutually orthogonal subspaces with bases A₁,…,Aₖ. For each block i, the parameters are projected onto that subspace (Bᵢ = Aᵢᵀθ), and noisy fine-tuning is performed for T steps: gradients on the retain set are computed, optionally clipped, Gaussian noise with variance σ² is added, and an update is applied while all other blocks are frozen. Crucially, the initial clipping radius C₀ used in standard NFT is replaced by a high-probability proximity bound Δ(ρ), which captures the typical distance ∥ˆx − ˆx′∥ between the fully trained model and a retrained model on the retain set. This tighter sensitivity bound allows smaller noise σ² for the same (ε, δ) budget. After all k blocks are processed, a brief standard fine-tuning phase refines the full model. The privacy analysis generalizes the shifted Rényi divergence composition to block-wise updates, proving the total (ε, δ) cost equals that of isotropic NFT.

## Key Results
- On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, standard NFT accuracy drops below 20% and does not recover, whereas sequential subspace injection maintains accuracy close to retraining-from-scratch.
- In class-wise deletion tasks, the method achieves perfect unlearning accuracy and strong robustness against membership inference attacks.
- It attains these results with lower computational cost than other certified unlearning methods that rely on full retraining or multiple model copies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Partitioning the parameter space into orthogonal subspaces and injecting noise sequentially reduces per-step distortion compared to isotropic noise injection.
- **Mechanism:** The total noise variance σ² is unchanged from standard NFT, but at each step only a 1/k fraction of parameters (one block) receives noise, rather than all d parameters simultaneously. This distributes the same noise budget across time and subspaces, making each perturbation less destructive to any single weight group.
- **Core assumption:** The blocks are mutually orthogonal and span ℝᵈ, allowing isotropic Gaussian noise to be decomposed into independent per-block Gaussians with the same variance.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "partitioning model parameters into orthogonal subspaces and applying noisy unlearning sequentially across them, distributing noise injection over time and subspaces"; [Section 4.1] "At each stage, we project the parameters onto one of the orthogonal subspaces... noise is injected only into a single block... Hence, while the noise variance σ² is unchanged, the per-step perturbation is smaller." Neighboring work (e.g., Koloskova et al.) focuses on isotropic injection.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Replacing worst-case initial clipping radius C₀ with a high-probability proximity bound Δ(ρ) tightens the privacy analysis and allows smaller noise.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of assuming the initial model can be anywhere in a C₀-ball, the analysis conditions on the event that ∥ˆx − ˆx′∥ ≤ Δ(ρ) with probability ≥ 1−ρ. This eliminates the need for an initial clipping step (x₀ = Π_{C₀}(ˆx)) and reduces the effective shift bound in the privacy proof.
- **Core assumption:** The fully trained model ˆx and the retrained model ˆx′ (trained on Dᵣ with the same random seed) are close in parameter space with high probability; this proximity is architecture- and training-dependent.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 3.2] "the distance between the fully trained model ˆx and the retrained model ˆx′ is much smaller than the clipping diameter 2C₀... we replace the initial clipping with the clipping radius C₀ by a high-probability bound Δ(ρ)/2"; [Definition 3] formalizes Δ(ρ). Neighboring papers generally use worst-case sensitivity, not data-dependent proximity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Sequential subspace noise injection preserves the same (ε, δ) privacy budget as standard NFT when analyzed with the decomposed shifted Rényi divergence framework.
- **Mechanism:** The privacy analysis is extended to handle block-wise updates. The total Rényi divergence εᵣₑₙᵧᵢ sums additively across blocks (Equation 6), and conversion to (ε, δ) yields the same overall budget. The Shift Reduction Lemma is adapted to the decomposed setting.
- **Core assumption:** The block decomposition is fixed and known, per-block noise is independent Gaussian with variance σ², and updates are sequential on disjoint subspaces.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 4.2] "We extend the analysis of noisy fine-tuning to our block-wise schedule and show that it preserves the same (ε, δ) budget."; [Theorem 2] states ε = Σᵢ εᵣₑₙᵧᵢ,ᵢ + log(1/δ)/(q−1). The theory is novel and not directly mirrored in neighbor abstracts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Differential Privacy (DP) and (ε, δ)-unlearning**: Needed because the method aims to satisfy certified unlearning defined via DP-like indistinguishability between unlearned and retrained models. Understanding DP composition and the role of δ is essential for privacy budget accounting. Quick check: Why does adding Gaussian noise to gradient updates in DP-SGD provide privacy, and how does the noise scale relate to the privacy budget (ε, δ)?
- **Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP)**: Used as an intermediate privacy measure; the paper uses RDP (order q) and converts to (ε, δ). Quick check: How does RDP differ from standard DP, and why is it often used for analysis of iterative algorithms like noisy SGD?
- **Argument/Parameter Stability**: The proximity parameter Δ(ρ) is a stability measure between fully trained and retrained models. Stability theory (e.g., Hardt et al.) provides bounds on ∥θ − θ′∥ under data subsampling, underpinning Δ(ρ) calibration. Quick check: What is the difference between uniform stability and argument stability, and how does a small Δ(ρ) relate to generalization?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Block Decomposer -> Sequential Unlearner -> Fine-tuner -> Privacy Calibrator -> Δ(ρ) Estimator
- **Critical path**: Precompute orthogonal matrices Aᵢ (once per architecture) -> Train initial model A(D) -> Estimate Δ(ρ) (optional but recommended) -> For given (ε,δ), compute σ², T from Theorem 1 using C₀’ = Δ(ρ)/2 -> For i=1..k: sequentially run noisy fine-tuning on block Bᵢ -> Run several fine-tuning steps on full model -> Output unlearned model.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Memory: Random orthonormal A requires O(d²) per layer; permutation matrix reduces to O(d); layer-wise grouping has zero extra memory. Privacy-utility: Larger k reduces per-step distortion but increases total steps kT; Δ(ρ) must be conservative enough to avoid privacy violations but tight enough to allow useful noise reduction. Block structure: Random blocks distribute gradient/noise evenly; layer-wise grouping may match heterogeneous dynamics (e.g., head vs. backbone).
- **Failure signatures**: Accuracy collapse during unlearning phase (sharp drop >30% and no recovery) → indicates σ² too large relative to block size or T too small. High MIA score (>50% efficacy) on forget set → indicates Δ(ρ) too small or unlearning incomplete. Theoretical guarantee mismatch: if empirical ∥ˆx−ˆx′∥ exceeds Δ(ρ) with probability > ρ, the (ε,δ) certificate is invalid.

Verify smoother curves and better final accuracy vs. NFT. 2) *Δ(ρ) sensitivity sweep*: For fixed (ε,δ), vary Δ(ρ) (e.g., 0.005,0.01,0.05) and measure MIA efficacy and test accuracy. Aim to find smallest Δ(ρ) that yields MIA efficacy ~100% (perfect unlearning) without sacrificing too much accuracy. 3) *Block design comparison*: On CIFAR-10 ResNet-18, compare random orthonormal blocks vs. permutation matrix vs. layer-wise cyclic grouping (k=4) at same (ε,δ). Evaluate final test accuracy and unlearning stability (max drop during unlearning).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Are current certified unlearning definitions misaligned with practical utility goals, and can they be revised to jointly enforce forgetting and high accuracy? The authors state: "Our findings suggest that current definitions of certified unlearning may be too strict: they enforce indistinguishability in ways that are not always aligned with practical goals, while still not guaranteeing model utility." Existing definitions require worst-case indistinguishability, forcing destructive noise or full retraining.
2. How can the proximity parameter Δ(ρ) be tightly bounded for arbitrary deep neural networks under realistic training conditions? The authors note: "Establishing such bounds for arbitrary deep networks remains an open problem." Current stability analyses cover only specific settings (convex, smooth, shallow networks); modern deep learning involves non-convex, overparametrized models trained with SGD with momentum and augmentation.
3. What is the optimal strategy for partitioning model parameters into orthogonal subspaces for sequential noise injection, and does an adaptive scheme outperform fixed random or layer-wise groupings? The paper proposes several block designs but provides no theoretical criterion for selecting or learning the partition; adaptive decompositions are mentioned as future work.
4. Why does distributing noise across orthogonal subspaces (sequential injection) mitigate accuracy collapse more effectively than injecting isotropic noise at every step, and is the benefit primarily due to reduced per-coordinate distortion or something more structural? The paper lacks analysis of the interaction between noise distribution and the model's loss landscape geometry (e.g., Hessian eigenvalues, curvature).

## Limitations
- The privacy guarantee depends on accurate estimation of the proximity bound Δ(ρ); underestimation invalidates the (ε, δ) certificate. Empirical distribution of ∥ˆx−ˆx′∥ is architecture-specific and may vary with random seeds.
- Orthogonal subspace decomposition assumes exact orthogonality and spanning; practical implementations (e.g., random projections, layer-wise grouping) might introduce approximations that break the noise equivalence.
- Experiments are limited to MNIST, CIFAR-10, ResNet-18, and ViT-Tiny. Scalability to larger models (e.g., ResNet-50, GPT) and more diverse datasets remains uncertain.
- The analysis presumes independent per-block Gaussian noise and linear updates; non-linear components (e.g., batch norm) could violate assumptions.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1** (Same (ε, δ) budget with higher accuracy): Medium. Theory appears sound, but hinges on Δ(ρ) estimation and block orthogonality; empirical support is limited to small models.
- **Claim 2** (Prevents accuracy collapse on CIFAR-10): Medium-High. Reported gains are substantial and consistent, yet need replication across more architectures and random seeds.
- **Claim 3** (Perfect MIA robustness with lower computational cost): Medium. MIA results are strong but risk overfitting to data used for Δ(ρ); computational comparisons require broader baselines.

## Next Checks
1. **Δ(ρ) Stability Test**: For a fixed (ε, δ), estimate Δ(ρ) from multiple independent training runs and verify the high-probability bound holds; also assess sensitivity to the chosen ρ.
2. **Orthogonality Sensitivity**: Compare random orthonormal blocks against layer-wise cyclic grouping on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 under identical privacy budgets; measure any degradation in privacy (via MIA) and accuracy.
3. **Scale-up Experiment**: Apply the method to a larger architecture (e.g., ResNet-50) on a subset of ImageNet; check whether accuracy retention remains high and computational savings over full retraining persist.
