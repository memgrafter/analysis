# Scheduling LLM Inference with Uncertainty-Aware Output Length Predictions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00499
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00499
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The TIE scheduler reduces average per‑token latency by 2.31× over the best prior method (LTR) and improves offline throughput by 1.42×.

## Executive Summary
LLM inference servers suffer from head‑of‑line blocking when long outputs unexpectedly occupy GPU batch slots. Existing schedulers rely on point estimates of output length, ignoring the stochastic, heavy‑tailed nature of actual generation lengths. This paper introduces TIE (Tail Inflated Expectation), a scheduler that models each request’s output length with a log‑t distribution, predicts its parameters (μ,σ) from the prompt using a DeBERTa‑based encoder, and computes a risk‑adjusted score: `Score = E[˜X] + β·CVaRα[˜X]` where ˜X censors at the model’s `max_tokens`. adaptive β (from queue pressure) and waiting‑time decay prevent starvation. Asynchronous prediction with dynamic batching keeps overhead low. In online serving (100 RPS on LMSYS‑Chat‑1M with Llama‑3‑8B), TIE cuts average per‑token latency by 2.31× and improves P90 similarly; in offline data generation it boosts throughput by 1.42×. Gains hold across multiple models and datasets.

## Method Summary
The approach comprises three tightly integrated components. First, **distribution modeling**: for a given prompt, multiple generated outputs are collected and their lengths fitted to a log‑t(μ,σ,ν) distribution (ν fixed at 3.5), which empirically captures heavy tails. Second, **prediction**: a DeBERTa‑v3‑base encoder with multi‑pooling (CLS, mean, max) feeds two separate MLP heads that predict normalized μ and log(1+σ). Third, **scheduling**: each request’s censored distribution (˜X = min(X, x_max)) yields an expectation E[˜X] and a Conditional Value‑at‑Risk (CVaRα, α=0.9). The TIE score combines these: `Score = E[˜X] + β·CVaRα[˜X]`, where β scales with queue pressure (`β = min(0.5, max(0.1, 0.1·Lq/B))`). A periodic decay (`Score′ = Score·γ^(t_w/τ)`, γ=0.9, τ=30s) prevents starvation. The system runs asynchronously: new requests are enqueued with a high default score, a background predictor batches them (max 32, timeout 3ms) and updates scores, while the main scheduler pops the minimum score to fill GPU batch slots. This decoupling ensures scheduling overhead remains negligible.

## Key Results
- 2.31× reduction in average per‑token latency (and similar P90 improvement) versus the best prior scheduler (LTR) on LMSYS‑Chat‑1M at 100 RPS with Llama‑3‑8B.
- 1.42× higher throughput in offline data generation on Alpaca.
- Gains generalize across multiple base models (including Llama‑3‑70B) and datasets (ShareGPT, etc.).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Stochastic Output Length Modeling
Output lengths from LLM decoding are inherently stochastic and exhibit heavy tails (e.g., high skewness, P99/P50 ≈ 10.8). Representing them with a point estimate fails to capture tail risk. The authors instead fit each prompt’s output lengths to a log‑t(μ,σ,ν) distribution with ν fixed at 3.5, which matches empirical data (KS test pass rate 93.1%). The parameters (μ,σ) are predicted from the prompt’s semantic embedding via DeBERTa and multi‑pooling. This probabilistic model allows explicit quantification of tail properties rather than just expected length.

### Tail Inflated Expectation (TIE) Scoring
Under SJF (Shortest Job First), a single extremely long output can cause massive head‑of‑line blocking. TIE directly penalizes such tail risk by adding CVaRα (conditional expectation of the longest α‑fraction, α=0.9) to the censored expectation E[˜X]. The coefficient β adapts to queue pressure—rising when the queue is long—making the scheduler more conservative under load. A waiting‑time decay (γ^(t_w/τ)) gradually lowers the score of long‑waiting requests, ensuring they eventually get serviced and preventing starvation. This combination approximates SJF while hedging against heavy‑tail uncertainty and dynamic system conditions.

### Asynchronous Prediction & Dynamic Batching
Predicting distributions for every request could itself become a bottleneck. The system decouples prediction from scheduling via a two‑thread design. New requests are immediately placed in a min‑heap with a placeholder score (max_tokens) and pushed to a prediction batch queue. A dedicated predictor thread batches requests (up to 32, 3 ms timeout), runs the DeBERTa model, computes the TIE score, and updates the heap. The main scheduler pops the current minimum when a batch slot frees and dispatches to the executor. This keeps average scheduling latency tiny (4.26 ms) compared to TTFT (~48 s) at 100 RPS.

  *Quick check:* Why is CVaR (expected worst α‑fraction) a more risk‑sensitive metric for scheduling than the α‑quantile (e.g., P90) alone?

- **Shortest Job First (SJF) Scheduling and Head‑of‑Line Blocking**  
  *Why needed:* SJF is the optimal deterministic policy for minimizing average completion time if job lengths are known; the paper aims to approximate SJF under length uncertainty.  
  *Quick check:* In a deterministic FCFS queue, how does a single long job placed before many short jobs increase the short jobs’ average completion time compared to an SJF order?

- **Censored Distributions & Monte Carlo Estimation**  
  *Why needed:* LLM inference imposes a hard `max_tokens` limit, so the true output length distribution is censored; the TIE formulas (Eqs. 9–10) are derived for this censored log‑t and evaluated via Monte Carlo.  
  *Quick check:* If the original log‑t has an infinite mean, why does the censored distribution ˜X = min(X, x_max) have a finite expectation? How does the term `x_max·[1−T_ν(ymax)]` in Eq. 9 capture this?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Request Ingest → Wait Queue (min‑heap, default score max_tokens) → Async Predictor (batch → DeBERTa + multi‑pooling + MLP → predict μ,σ) → Score Calculator (apply TIE formula, adaptive β, waiting decay) → Update heap entry → Scheduler (pop min current score) → Executor (GPU batch) → Completion.

### Critical Path
1. New request arrives, enqueued in heap with placeholder score and submitted to prediction batch queue.
2. Async predictor batches requests, runs DeBERTa, outputs (μ,σ).
3. Score calculator computes E[˜X] and CVaR0.9[˜X] via Monte Carlo, applies β (from Lq/B) and decay based on enqueue time, updates heap.
4. Scheduler monitors batch vacancy; when available, extracts heap minimum and assigns to executor.
5. Request generates tokens; upon finishing, it is removed from the batch.
6. Periodically (e.g., every 30 s), a decay update multiplies all waiting scores by γ^(t_w/τ) to prevent starvation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Prediction accuracy vs. overhead:** More complex predictors (e.g., dynamic ν, larger encoder) may fit distributions better but increase latency and memory; fixed ν=3.5 and DeBERTa‑base is a practical compromise.
- **Risk sensitivity (β) vs. starvation:** Higher β reduces average latency by heavily penalizing tail risk but can starve long requests; adaptive β and decay mitigate this, but tuning γ and τ is crucial.
- **Batching timeout (3 ms):** Too short → small predictor batches → low throughput; too long → delayed score updates → stale scheduling decisions. Tied to predictor latency and hardware.
- **Initial score (max_tokens):** Ensures unpredicted requests don’t jump ahead, but if predictions are fast this may unnecessarily delay them; could be made dynamic based on predicted queue pressure.

### Failure Signatures
- **High heap update latency:** Many requests stuck with default max_tokens score → effective FCFS. Indicates predictor backlog (arrival rate too high or slow predictor).
- **Low score diversity:** Scores cluster closely, causing near‑random dispatch → SJF benefits lost. Suggests poor predictor calibration or β set too low.
- **Starvation of long requests:** Long‑output requests experience excessive waits; check if decay factor γ is close to 1 or interval τ too long, or β too high.
- **Predictor memory growth:** Batches not completing due to GPU contention; monitor prediction queue length.
- **Poor distribution fit:** KS pass rate below 90% for prompts; implies log‑t mismatch; consider alternative distributions.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Validate Distribution Fitting:** For 100 random prompts, generate 20 outputs each, fit log‑t(ν=3.5) via MLE, perform KS test. Success: ≥90% of prompts have p‑value >0.05.
2. **Measure Prediction Throughput & Latency:** Under 50 RPS, record average batch size, batch wait time (target ~3 ms), and per‑request prediction latency. Should be orders of magnitude smaller than TTFT (paper: 4.26 ms vs 48.23 s).
3. **Compare Scheduling Policies in Isolation:** Simulate synthetic workloads drawn from fitted log‑t distributions. Compare FCFS, SSJF (using true μ as point estimate), and TIE (using true μ,σ). Vary load; measure average and P90 completion time. Expect TIE to outperform SSJF at high load with heavy tails.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Single‑pass online training:** How to train the predictor without expensive multiple generations per prompt, perhaps by leveraging partial outputs or latent representations from real traffic?
2. **Alternative distribution families:** Would more flexible distributions (mixtures, non‑parametric) fit output lengths better across diverse models and request types, potentially improving scheduling?
3. **Dynamic updates during decoding:** Can the output length distribution be refined as tokens are generated to adapt to early signals (e.g., a long response becoming evident) and adjust priority mid‑generation?
4. **Hybrid with preemption:** Could combining TIE’s uncertainty awareness with lightweight, risk‑triggered preemption (e.g., only for high‑CVaR requests) further reduce HOL blocking without the high overhead of full preemptive schedulers?

## Limitations
- **Heavy‑tail premise may fail:** Domains with low variance or bounded output lengths will have poor log‑t fit, limiting TIE’s advantage.
- **Predictor accuracy dependency:** TIE’s score relies on accurate μ and σ predictions; out‑of‑distribution prompts can degrade calibration and misguide the scheduler.
- **Missing implementation details:** Training data generation parameters (temperature, top_p), MLE initial bounds, prompt truncation for DeBERTa, and decay frequency are unspecified, creating reproduction uncertainty.

## Confidence
- **Log‑t modeling:** Medium (empirical fit strong but may not generalize to low‑variance workloads).
- **TIE scoring:** High (consistent gains across online/offline experiments and multiple models).
- **Async prediction:** Medium (overhead shown negligible at 100 RPS; scalability to higher rates untested).
