# Learning-based Multi-agent Race Strategies in Formula 1

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23056  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23056  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** Agents with a learnable interaction module trained via self-play achieve a top Elo score of 1970 and consistently adapt pit-stop timing, tire selection, and energy management to specific opponents, outperforming other agents (Elo 914–970).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles dynamic Formula 1 race strategy optimization under competitive multi‑agent conditions. The authors extend a pre‑trained single‑agent reinforcement learning policy by adding a trainable interaction module that processes opponent observations. A self‑play training scheme pits a learning agent against a pool of historically strong opponents, enabling the development of strategies that react to competitor actions. The resulting agents, ranked by Elo, demonstrate robust adaptation across diverse opponents, validating the framework for generating interaction‑aware race strategies using only real‑time data.

## Method Summary
The method builds upon a frozen single‑agent RL policy backbone (pre‑trained to minimize race time) and introduces a separate interaction module that consumes both ego and opponent observations to output a correction term $\Delta a$. The final action is the sum of the nominal action from the backbone and this correction. Training proceeds via self‑play: a single learning agent competes against a fixed opponent sampled from a pool of previously trained agents (initially the single‑agent policy). Only the interaction module is updated using SAC, while the backbone remains frozen. Elo ratings are maintained to select opponents and evaluate final agent strength.

## Key Results
- The top‑performing agent achieves an Elo rating of 1970, well above all others (914–970).
- Agents adapt pit‑stop timing, tire compound choices, and energy management in response to specific opponents.
- The best agent typically uses a two‑stop soft‑tire strategy but varies pit laps to counter competitors, maintaining consistent low race times across diverse opponents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The interaction module allows the agent to adapt its pre‑trained single‑agent policy in response to opponent observations, enabling opponent‑aware strategy adjustments without discarding robust baseline behavior.
- **Mechanism:** A frozen single‑agent policy backbone generates a nominal action based solely on ego‑state observations. A separately trained interaction module receives both ego and opponent observations and outputs a correction term ($\Delta a$) that is added to the nominal action. This modular fine‑tuning shifts the policy’s focus from optimal single‑agent performance to competitive multi‑agent performance.
- **Core assumption:** The nominal policy encodes a robust, near‑optimal baseline for the single‑agent problem (minimizing race time), and the correction needed for multi‑agent competitiveness is a relatively small, learnable perturbation on top of this baseline.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “...extends a pre‑trained single‑agent reinforcement learning policy with a learnable interaction module that processes competitor observations...”  
  - Section II.D: “The pre‑trained single‑agent policy... is used as a backbone, whose neural network’s layers are kept frozen during training... The extended agent only requires fine‑tuning to account for interaction effects...”  
  - Related works [11139, 11131] use RL for F1 strategy but lack this frozen backbone + interaction module design, indicating a deliberate choice for multi‑agent transfer.
- **Break condition:** If the optimal multi‑agent strategy deviates fundamentally from the single‑agent optimum (e.g., requires entirely different pit‑stop logic or energy management), the correction module may be unable to generate a sufficiently large or structurally different adjustment from the frozen baseline, leading to convergence to a suboptimal policy.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The self‑play training scheme, where an agent learns against a pool of progressively stronger historical opponents, generates policies that are robust and adaptive, preventing overfitting to any single opponent strategy and driving performance toward a Nash equilibrium‑like capability.
- **Mechanism:** During training, a single learning agent competes against a fixed opponent selected from a pool of previously trained agents. The opponent is periodically updated (e.g., after the learning agent converges or via random selection from the pool). This creates a curriculum where the learning agent must continuously adapt to increasingly sophisticated competitor behaviors, mimicking an arms race.
- **Core assumption:** Competition against a diverse and improving set of opponents is a more effective signal for learning generalizable, robust strategies than repeated play against a static or randomly initialized opponent. The Elo‑based opponent selection biases training toward facing stronger, more informative opponents.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section III.A: “Self‑play provides an efficient and effective framework... The policy is progressively improved by competing always against an opponent with similar expertise... Instead of waiting until convergence, the opponent... is intermittently selected at random from a pool of previously trained agents.”  
  - Section IV, Table II: The final “battle arena” shows a clear performance ranking (Elo 1970 vs. ~914–970), indicating the self‑play process created a hierarchy of agent strength.  
  - Neighbors like [57383] discuss “evolving multi‑agent systems” and “collective learning,” aligning with self‑play for robust policies.
- **Break condition:** If the opponent pool lacks diversity (e.g., all agents converge to a single dominant strategy early in training), the learning agent will overfit to that narrow strategy and fail to generalize to novel opponent behaviors encountered in the final arena or real‑world scenarios.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Elo rating system, used to rank agents and select training opponents, provides a reliable, continuous measure of relative policy strength that correlates with robust race performance (consistent low race times) more effectively than raw cumulative reward.
- **Mechanism:** After each simulated race, Elo ratings are updated based on win/loss outcomes against specific opponents. This creates a dynamic leaderboard where an agent’s score reflects its expected win probability against the population. The training process uses these scores to sample stronger opponents, and final rankings are derived from Elo convergence. The reward function’s secondary “winner reward” ensures the ultimate goal is relative ranking, not just absolute speed.
- **Core assumption:** Head‑to‑head win/loss outcomes against a diverse set of opponents are a more valid and robust measure of strategy quality for a competitive, adversarial task than a scalar reward that mixes absolute performance (race time) and a binary win signal. Elo effectively disentangles these factors.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section III.B: “Agents with similar rewards may have different performance... We adopt the Elo rating system... After each match, ratings are updated... This mechanism is more representative of the relative performance than the winning probability...”  
  - Section IV, Figure 6 & Table II: Higher Elo agents (e.g., A at 1970) consistently achieve lower race time differences ($\Delta T_{\text{race}}$) against a common baseline across different opponents, showing correlation between Elo and consistent performance.  
  - The Elo system is standard in competitive game AI (cf. references [18], [19]), providing a well‑established method for ranking in self‑play contexts.
- **Break condition:** If the reward function’s weighting ($c_{\text{win}}$) between step reward (lap time) and final reward (win) is poorly chosen, agents may learn to manipulate the system for wins (e.g., risky last‑lap overtakes) without achieving consistently low race times, causing Elo to misrepresent true strategic robustness.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation for sequential strategy.**  
  *Why needed:* Race strategy is a sequential decision‑making problem over laps. The MDP formalism (state $s$, observation $o$, action $a$, reward $r$) defines the RL problem: learn a policy mapping observations to actions to maximize cumulative reward.  
  *Quick check:* Why must the state vector include auxiliary variables like $b_{\text{cpd}}$ and $b_{\text{outlap}}$? (Answer: To maintain the Markov property by encoding necessary historical information—e.g., whether two compounds have been used—that is not present in the current lap’s raw states.)

- **Concept: Transfer learning via frozen backbone and trainable adaptation module.**  
  *Why needed:* Training a high‑performing multi‑agent policy from scratch is computationally expensive and unstable. Leveraging a pre‑trained single‑agent policy as a frozen feature extractor provides a strong, stable prior. Only the adaptation layer for interaction is trained, accelerating convergence and preserving single‑agent competence.  
  *Quick check:* What is a key risk of freezing the backbone of the single‑agent policy? (Answer: The frozen weights may encode biases or strategies that are suboptimal or even detrimental in the multi‑agent competitive setting, and the interaction module might be unable to compensate sufficiently.)

- **Concept: Self‑play as a curriculum for competitive multi‑agent RL.**  
  *Why needed:* In competitive games, a fixed opponent leads to cycling or overfitting. Self‑play continuously generates a moving target (new opponent strategies) that the learning agent must counter, driving open‑ended improvement and robustness against a distribution of strategies.  
  *Quick check:* How does the custom self‑play scheme (randomly selecting from a pool of past agents) differ from “play against the latest version” and why? (Answer: It prevents the opponent distribution from collapsing to a single narrow strategy and provides a wider curriculum of experiences, encouraging diversity and preventing oversight.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Race Car Model (Environment) → produces Ego observation $o$ and Opponent observation $\tilde{o}$ → Frozen Backbone (outputs $a_{\text{nom}}$) and Interaction Module (outputs $\Delta a$) → Policy Combiner ($a = a_{\text{nom}} + \Delta a$) → Race Car Model. The Elo Manager maintains the opponent pool, updates ratings after each match, and selects opponents for self‑play.

- **Critical path (data flow):**  
  Environment state $s$ → Ego observation $o$ & Opponent observation $\tilde{o}$ → Frozen policy outputs $a_{\text{nom}}$ & Interaction module uses $[o, \tilde{o}]$ to output $\Delta a$ → Combined action $a$ executed in Environment → New state $s'$ & Reward $r$ (based on $T_{\text{lap}}$ & final gap) → SAC update on *only* the Interaction Module parameters using $(o, \tilde{o}, a, r, s')$.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Frozen Backbone vs. Full Finetuning:* Freezing ensures stability and inherits single‑agent competence but limits adaptation if multi‑agent optimum diverges significantly. Full finetuning risks catastrophic forgetting of robust single‑agent behavior.  
  - *Interaction Module Input:* Using $\tilde{o}$ (gap, tire age, pit status) instead of full opponent state $s_i$ is a trade‑off for realism (pit wall data availability) but limits the agent’s ability to infer hidden opponent states (like battery energy).  
  - *Self‑Play Opponent Selection:* Random from pool vs. “best” Elo. Random promotes exploration of diverse strategies; “best” focuses learning on the current strongest known counter‑strategy. The paper uses a mix.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. Interaction module output is near‑zero for all inputs: Indicates the module failed to learn any meaningful correction; agent behaves identically to the single‑agent policy regardless of opponent.  
  2. Elo ratings diverge wildly and inconsistently across re‑runs: Suggests self‑play instability, possibly due to hyperparameter issues (e.g., $c_{\text{win}}$ weighting) or a non‑stationary environment where new strategies rapidly obsolete old ones.  
  3. Top agent’s strategy drastically changes when facing a novel opponent type (e.g., 3‑stop strategy) not in its training pool: Indicates overfitting to the distribution of opponents seen during self‑play and lack of true generalizable strategic reasoning.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Unit test the interaction module:* Freeze the backbone. With a fixed opponent policy (e.g., the single‑agent policy), verify that varying $\tilde{o}$ (simulate different opponent gap/pit times) causes a measurable, sensible change in the final action $a$ (e.g., earlier pit when opponent pits early). Check for nonsensical outputs (e.g., negative energy allocation).  
  2. *Validate self‑play progression:* Run 5 self‑play iterations. After each, pitstop‑race all new agents against the initial single‑agent policy. Measure the win rate. Expect a non‑decreasing trend. Also monitor the Elo spread within the pool; a healthy spread indicates diversity.  
  3. *Test Elo reliability & reward function:* With a fixed, diverse pool of agents, run a round‑robin tournament (each pair races multiple times with random initial gaps). Compute two ranking metrics: (a) Win rate against all others, and (b) Average $\Delta T_{\text{race}}$ (race time difference vs. a common baseline). Correlate these with Elo. A strong positive correlation between Elo and lower $\Delta T_{\text{race}}$ validates Elo as a proxy for robust performance.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed details of the pre‑trained single‑agent policy (network architecture, hyperparameters, tire degradation model) and the aerodynamic interaction coefficients hinder exact replication.
- Self‑play opponent diversity lacks quantitative analysis, risking pool collapse and overfitting to a narrow set of strategies.
- The Elo rating’s dependence on the reward weighting ($c_{\text{win}}$) was not systematically studied; improper weighting could incentivize wins over consistent low race times.

## Confidence
- Interaction module adaptation: Medium
- Self‑play curriculum effectiveness: Medium
- Elo as performance proxy: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Baseline reconstruction:** Implement the single‑car race environment from [10] with approximate parameters, train a competitive policy, and compare its race times to the frozen backbone used. Quantify the performance gap that the interaction module must address.
2. **Opponent diversity audit:** Log strategy fingerprints (pit laps, tire selections) from the opponent pool during self‑play. Compute entropy or run clustering to confirm a diverse curriculum. If diversity is low, adjust sampling (e.g., increase random agent insertion) and monitor Elo convergence.
3. **Out‑of‑distribution opponent test:** Construct hand‑coded opponents with strategies absent from the pool (e.g., 3‑stop, always‑soft, extreme early pit). Run a full tournament and measure the top agent’s win rate and $\Delta T_{\text{race}}$ degradation. Significant performance drops would indicate overfitting and motivate curriculum enhancements.
