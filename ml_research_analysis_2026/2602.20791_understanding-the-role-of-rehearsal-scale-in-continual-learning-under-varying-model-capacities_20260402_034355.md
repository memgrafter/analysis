# Understanding the Role of Rehearsal Scale in Continual Learning under Varying Model Capacities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20791
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20791
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Rehearsal scale has a double‑edged effect: it can harm adaptability when the model is overparameterized and yields diminishing returns on memory retention for similar, low‑noise tasks; the optimal scale depends on model capacity relative to data.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the size of the rehearsal buffer influences continual learning performance through a theoretical lens. By formulating rehearsal as a minimum‑norm optimization problem under a linear Gaussian model, the authors derive closed‑form expressions for three error components: adaptation (fit to the current task), memory (forgetting of past tasks), and generalization (transfer across tasks). The analysis reveals two counterintuitive findings: (1) increasing rehearsal can actually increase adaptation error when the model is slightly overparameterized, and (2) memory error exhibits a U‑shaped relationship with rehearsal scale under low noise and high task similarity, meaning that beyond a certain point, more rehearsal harms retention. Both effects hinge on a key parameter λ = (p−n−s)/p that demarcates overparameterized (λ>0) and underparameterized (λ<0) regimes. Empirical validation on deep neural networks across MNIST, CIFAR, and Tiny‑ImageNet confirms these trends, demonstrating that the optimal buffer size is not monotonic and must be chosen with respect to model capacity. The work provides both theoretical guarantees and practical guidelines for buffer management in rehearsal‑based continual learning.

## Method Summary
The study considers a sequential continual learning scenario where each task generates data from a linear model with Gaussian features and additive noise. Rehearsal maintains a buffer of `s` samples from every previously seen task. At each step, the learner minimizes the least‑squares loss on the combined set of current data and all buffers. When the total number of parameters `p` exceeds the total number of samples `n+s` (overparameterized), the solution is taken as the minimum ℓ2‑norm estimator; otherwise (underparameterized) it is the unique least‑squares solution. The error metrics are defined as expected squared distance between the estimated weight vector and the true task‑specific optimum. Using properties of random orthogonal projections in high dimensions, the authors compute closed‑form expectations for adaptation, memory, and generalization errors. Two regimes are distinguished by the sign of λ. Theoretical predictions are then tested on deep networks by measuring analogous performance indicators (accuracy, forgetting) on standard benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Rehearsal can impair model adaptability to new tasks when the model is overparameterized, contrary to the common belief that larger buffers always help.
- Memory error exhibits a U‑shaped relationship with rehearsal scale in the overparameterized regime under low noise and similar tasks, indicating diminishing returns; beyond a critical buffer size, memory performance worsens.
- The optimal rehearsal scale depends critically on the relationship between model capacity `p` and the total data size `n+s`; the transition at `s = p−n` marks a qualitative change in all error behaviors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Rehearsal can impair adaptability under overparameterization.**  
- **Claim:** Increasing the buffer size `s` can increase the adaptation error when the model is slightly overparameterized (p ≈ n+s) and tasks are similar.  
- **Mechanism:** In the overparameterized case, the solution lies in the row space of the data matrix, and the noise‑induced error term scales with λ = (p−n−s)/p. As `s` grows, λ becomes positive and the term `a_noise` increases, creating an inflection point where larger buffers hurt adaptation.  
- **Core assumption:** Slight overparameterization (p close to n+s), low noise, high inter‑task similarity.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract statement; Theorem IV.1 and Remark IV.2 provide the error expression and the condition (λ≈0) for the inflection.  
- **Break condition:** Underparameterization (n+s > p) makes adaptation error decrease monotonically with `s`.

**Mechanism 2: Memory error does not always improve with more rehearsal.**  
- **Claim:** Under overparameterization, low noise, and similar tasks, memory error shows a U‑shaped curve with `s`, implying a diminishing lower bound; additional rehearsal eventually harms retention.  
- **Mechanism:** For T=2, the memory error contains a term M1 that dominates in the overparameterized regime. M1 depends on λ and the squared dissimilarity between true task weights; together these produce a convex function of `s`. In the underparameterized regime, memory error becomes independent of `s` and depends only on task similarity.  
- **Core assumption:** Overparameterized (λ>0), small noise σ, and small ‖w*_i−w*_j‖.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Theorem IV.3 gives Equations (9) (U‑shape) and (10) (invariance).  
- **Break condition:** High task dissimilarity or high noise flattens or reverses the U‑shape, potentially yielding monotonic improvement.

**Mechanism 3: Optimal rehearsal is capacity‑dependent.**  
- **Claim:** The effect of rehearsal scale on all error components is governed by λ, so the boundary p = n+s dictates a qualitative shift; thus the optimal buffer size must be chosen relative to model capacity.  
- **Mechanism:** All closed‑form error expressions contain λ multiplicatively. The sign of λ flips when `s` crosses `p−n`, turning decreasing trends into increasing ones (or vice versa). Consequently, the trade‑off between adaptation and memory moves with `p`.  
- **Core assumption:** The linear Gaussian analysis holds; empirical results suggest the pattern extends to deep networks.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorems IV.1, IV.3, IV.4; Figures 2–4 show empirical turning points near `s ≈ p−n`.  
- **Break condition:** None within the theory; in practice, optimization heuristics may blur the exact transition.

## Foundational Learning
- **Overparameterization (`λ = (p−n−s)/p`)**  
  - *Why needed:* Determines regime (over‑ vs under‑parameterized) and the functional form of error terms.  
  - *Quick check:* If p=5000, n=1000, s=3000 → λ=0.2 (overparameterized). Increasing `s` to 3500 gives λ=0.05, still positive; adaptation error would likely still increase with `s`, though the rate may change.

- **Multi‑dimensional error metrics (A, M, G)**  
  - *Why needed:* Rehearsal affects different facets of continual learning; isolating adaptation (current task fit), memory (forgetting), and generalization (overall transfer) enables nuanced trade‑off analysis.  
  - *Quick check:* A high `G(ŵ_t)` after learning task `t` indicates poor average performance across all seen tasks, reflecting either bad adaptation or severe forgetting.

- **Closed‑form analysis of iterative optimization**  
  - *Why needed:* Deriving explicit formulas linking `s` to expected error avoids reliance on empirical sweeps and reveals precise conditions (e.g., inflection points).  
  - *Quick check:* In the underparameterized adaptation error (6), the term `σ²/(p−n−s)` decreases as `s` increases because the denominator grows; `s` directly reduces the variance component by effectively increasing data size.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Theoretical Core (linear regression with Gaussian features/noise) → Rehearsal Mechanism (buffer storing `s` samples per past task) → Error Decomposition (definitions of A, M, G) → Analytical Engine (derivation of closed‑form expectations via orthogonal projections) → Empirical Validation Layer (DNN experiments on MNIST, CIFAR, Tiny‑ImageNet).

**Critical path:**  
1. Model rehearsal as a constrained minimum ℓ2‑norm problem.  
2. Derive expected error formulas for overparameterized (p > n+s) and underparameterized (n+s > p) cases.  
3. Introduce λ = (p−n−s)/p and analyze its partial derivatives to identify monotonicity and inflection points.  
4. Establish theoretical thresholds (e.g., s ≈ p−n) where error behaviors change.  
5. Validate trends on deep networks by measuring analogous metrics.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Theoretical purity vs. realism:* The linear Gaussian model sacrifices neural network complexity for analytic tractability; empirical results should be viewed as qualitative trends.  
- *Metric choice:* Squared parameter distance is mathematically convenient but may not align with classification accuracy; the paper supplements with standard CL metrics.  
- *Buffer sampling:* Theory assumes uniform/random sampling; empirically, advanced methods (herding) improve performance but core `s`‑dependency patterns remain robust.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. DNN memory error never shows a U‑shape for any buffer size → the diminishing lower bound may be an artifact of the linear assumption.  
2. Adaptation error consistently decreases with `s` across all model capacities in DNNs → contradicts the overparameterized prediction.  
3. No turning point near `s = p−n` appears empirically; errors change monotonically with buffer size → undermines the λ‑based regime characterization for deep learning.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce Figure 2(b)/3(b): For fixed `p`, `n`, `T`, `σ`, and task similarity, plot `E[A]` and `E[M]` vs. `s` in both regimes. Verify U‑shape for memory and initial decrease‑then‑increase for adaptation in the overparameterized case.  
2. Validate T=2 Formula: Simulate the linear model, compute empirical average adaptation and memory error for T=2 across a grid of `s`, `p`, and ‖w*_2−w*_1‖². Compare against closed‑form Equations (9) and (10).  
3. DNN Threshold Test: On CIFAR‑10/100, train a CNN with buffer sizes from 0% to 50% of total data. Plot adaptation and memory error; check whether adaptation error starts to rise at a specific `s` and whether memory error exhibits a minimum.

## Limitations
- The analysis assumes a linear Gaussian model; the exact parametric transition (s ≈ p−n) may not transfer to non‑linear deep networks due to implicit biases and optimization dynamics.  
- Task similarity in the theory requires near‑identical true parameters, but the operationalization in DNN benchmarks is unspecified, risking misalignment with synthetic conditions.  
- DNN training details (architecture, hyperparameters, buffer sampling) are incompletely described, hindering exact replication and possibly affecting the observed effect magnitudes.

## Confidence
- **Rehearsal impairs adaptability under overparameterization:** High confidence for the linear model (Theorem IV.1); Medium confidence for DNNs (empirical trend supported but may vary with architecture/dataset).  
- **Memory error exhibits a diminishing lower bound:** High confidence in the overparameterized, low‑noise, similar‑task regime (Theorem IV.3); Low confidence in general DNNs (U‑shape is subtle and can be masked by non‑linearities).  
- **Optimal rehearsal scale depends on model capacity:** High confidence theoretically (λ governs all errors); Medium confidence empirically (Figures 2–4 show turning points near s = p−n, though exact alignment varies).
