# Beyond ReinMax: Low-Variance Gradient Estimators for Discrete Latent Variables

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08257
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08257
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** Two new gradient estimators (ReinMax‑Rao, ReinMax‑CV) for discrete latent variables achieve lower variance and better ELBO than ReinMax on VAE training.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the high variance of the ReinMax gradient estimator for training models with discrete latent variables. ReinMax approximates the gradient of an expectation by combining two Straight‑Through terms, but its dependence on the sampled discrete variable leads to high variance. To mitigate this, the authors propose two new estimators: **ReinMax‑Rao** applies Rao‑Blackwellisation using the Gumbel‑Rao estimator to the problematic term, yielding lower variance but higher bias; **ReinMax‑CV** introduces a control variate based on the Straight‑Through Gumbel‑Softmax estimator, with its expectation estimated by Gumbel‑Rao, achieving a balance between bias and variance. Both methods integrate the Gumbel‑Softmax reparameterization and require additional computational effort (e.g., K=100 samples for conditional expectations). Experiments on variational autoencoders (VAEs) with independent categorical latent variables across six configurations (e.g., 8×4, 64×8) show that the proposed estimators consistently achieve lower ELBO than ReinMax and other baselines. Notably, on the 8×4 VAE, ReinMax‑CV reaches a training ELBO of 124.94 compared to ReinMax’s 125.08. Gradient variance is substantially reduced: the sample standard deviation drops from 7.400 (ReinMax) to 2.733 (ReinMax‑Rao). The results illustrate a clear bias‑variance trade‑off, with ReinMax‑CV offering the best overall performance.

## Method Summary
The ReinMax estimator decomposes the gradient into two Straight‑Through components, one of which uses a transformed logit vector θ_D = (θ + D/2)/τ that depends on the discrete sample D, causing high variance. **ReinMax‑Rao** replaces this component with the Gumbel‑Rao estimator, which conditions on D and marginalises over continuous Gumbel perturbations to produce a lower‑variance unbiased estimator for the expected gradient. However, the Rao‑Blackwellisation introduces bias due to the Monte‑Carlo approximation (K samples) and the omission of the Jacobian through the conditional reparameterization. **ReinMax‑CV** instead keeps the original high‑variance term but subtracts a control variate: it uses the Straight‑Through Gumbel‑Softmax estimator evaluated at θ_D, scaled by a weight η, and adds back its expectation (approximated by Gumbel‑Rao). This control variate is correlated with the target term, reducing variance without altering the expectation, provided the weight η is well‑chosen and the expectation estimate is accurate. Both ReinMax‑Rao and ReinMax‑CV rely on the Gumbel‑Softmax relaxation and require tuning of temperature τ and, for ReinMax‑CV, the control weight η. In VAE training, the gradient estimators are applied per latent dimension (assuming independence), and the overall gradient combines reconstruction and KL terms. The additional computational cost is dominated by K evaluations of the softmax Jacobian for Gumbel‑Rao.

## Key Results
- ReinMax‑CV achieves lower training ELBO than ReinMax and other baselines on VAE tasks (e.g., 124.94 vs 125.08 on 8×4 VAE).
- ReinMax‑Rao reduces gradient variance substantially (sample std dev 2.733 vs 7.400 for ReinMax on 8×4 VAE).
- The estimators exhibit a bias‑variance trade‑off: ReinMax (low bias, high variance), ReinMax‑Rao (high bias, low variance), ReinMax‑CV (balanced, best overall ELBO).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: High Variance Originates from θ_D‑Dependent Term
The ReinMax gradient estimator (Equation 12) can be decomposed as `2·∇ST,τ(D,θ_D) − ½·∇ST,τ=1(D,θ)`. The second term has fixed θ, while the first term uses θ_D = (θ + D/2)/τ, which depends on the sampled discrete one‑hot vector D. This dependence introduces additional randomness that amplifies the variance of the overall estimator across gradient samples. Empirical evidence: replacing D with argmax(θ) in θ_D (ReinMax‑Argmax) drastically reduces variance (Table 1), confirming that the θ_D term is the primary variance source.

### Mechanism 2: ReinMax‑Rao Reduces Variance via Rao‑Blackwellisation
ReinMax‑Rao applies Rao‑Blackwellisation to the high‑variance term by replacing `∇ST,τ(D,θ_D)` with the Gumbel‑Rao estimator `∇GR,τ(G,θ_D)`. Gumbel‑Rao conditions on the discrete sample D and marginalises over continuous Gumbel noise: it draws K samples Y_k from the conditional distribution p(θ+G | D) (ignoring the derivative through the reparameterization) and averages the Jacobians of the softmax at Y_k. This produces an unbiased (up to Monte‑Carlo error) estimate of the expectation of the Straight‑Through gradient, but with lower variance because the conditioning reduces variability. The trade‑off is increased bias from the finite K approximation and the omitted Jacobian term. Experiments show ReinMax‑Rao achieves the lowest variance among ReinMax variants (Figure 1, right) but sometimes higher ELBO loss, indicating higher bias.

### Mechanism 3: ReinMax‑CV Balances Bias‑Variance with Control Variates
ReinMax‑CV retains the original high‑variance term but adds a control variate to reduce its variance without changing the expectation. Specifically, it subtracts `η·∇STGS,τ_cv(G,θ_D)`, where `∇STGS` is the Straight‑Through estimator applied to the Gumbel‑Softmax sample (θ_D+G). The expectation of this control variate, `E_G[∇STGS,τ_cv(G,θ_D)]`, is estimated using the Gumbel‑Rao estimator `∇GR,τ_cv(G,θ_D)`. Thus the modified first term becomes `∇ST,τ=1(D,θ_D) − η·(∇STGS,τ_cv(G,θ_D) − ∇GR,τ_cv(G,θ_D))`. The correlation between `∇ST` and `∇STGS` and the accuracy of the expectation estimate determine variance reduction. ReinMax‑CV exhibits variance between ReinMax and ReinMax‑Rao and often achieves the best ELBO, demonstrating a successful bias‑variance trade‑off. The method requires tuning η and τ_cv; optimal values are dataset- and configuration‑dependent (Table 4).

  - **Why needed:** ReinMax and the proposed methods are modifications of Straight‑Through. Gumbel‑Softmax provides a continuous relaxation. Both are baseline approximations of the intractable gradient.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Encoder (x) → θ → Gradient Estimator Module (samples D, computes ∇θ) → Decoder (x̂) → Loss (reconstruction + KL) → Backward pass → Encoder update.

**Critical path:** The gradient from the loss to the encoder parameters passes through the estimator’s backward pass. The estimator defines how ∂Loss/∂D is transformed into ∂Loss/∂θ, and this ∂Loss/∂θ is combined with the KL gradient to update the encoder.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Bias vs variance:** ReinMax (low bias, high variance); ReinMax‑Rao (high bias, low variance); ReinMax‑CV (balanced). Choice depends on problem dimensionality and tolerance for noise.
- **Computational cost:** Straight‑Through and ReinMax have minimal overhead. ReinMax‑Rao and ReinMax‑CV require K=100 samples for Gumbel‑Rao, making them about 3× slower.
- **Hyperparameters:** Temperature τ must be tuned for Gumbel‑Softmax/‑Rao components; ReinMax‑CV also requires control weight η. Poor choices can degrade performance.
- **Scalability:** The methods assume independent categorical latent variables (row‑wise). Vectorized implementations across rows are possible but must ensure correct gradient aggregation.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Worse ELBO than simple baselines (ST/GST):** Likely due to excessive bias (e.g., using ReinMax‑Rao on small latent spaces) or mis‑tuned hyperparameters.
- **Training instability (spiking ELBO, exploding gradients):** Typically caused by high variance (e.g., low τ in STGS) or an inappropriate η in ReinMax‑CV that amplifies noise.
- **No improvement over ReinMax:** Could indicate insufficient correlation between control variate and target estimator, perhaps due to the ignored Jacobian in Gumbel‑Rao.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Variance diagnosis on minimal VAE:** Train a tiny VAE (e.g., 8×4 latent) with each estimator. For a fixed batch and model, sample 1024 gradient vectors and compute their sample standard deviation (variance) and cosine similarity to the exact gradient (computed by enumerating all 8⁴=4096 combinations). Expected ordering: variance: ReinMax > ReinMax‑CV > ReinMax‑Rao; bias (1 − cosine similarity): ReinMax‑Rao > ReinMax‑CV > ReinMax.
2. **Hyperparameter grid search for ReinMax‑CV:** For a medium‑sized VAE (e.g., 8×16 latent), sweep over τ_cv ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1.0} and η ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}. Train for 50 epochs and record final training ELBO. Expect an optimal region around τ_cv=0.5–1.0 and η=1.5 (as suggested by Table 4).
3. **Cross‑configuration performance:** Train VAEs across all six latent configurations (8×4, 8×8, 8×16, 16×12, 32×12, 64×8) for 160 epochs using the best hyperparameters found. Compare final training and test ELBOs. Expect ReinMax‑CV to achieve the lowest ELBO on most configurations; ReinMax‑Rao may excel on high‑dimensional categorical spaces (e.g., 64×8); basic ST/GST should perform best on the smallest configuration (8×4) where bias dominates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Does the Gumbel‑Rao estimator require including the derivative through the conditional reparameterisation, and how does this affect its bias and variance?
  - *Basis:* Prior work (Paulus et al., 2021) is ambiguous; current implementations exclude this derivative, likely increasing bias.
  - *Why unresolved:* Systematic study of this design choice is lacking.
  - *Resolution evidence:* Theoretical analysis of the gradient contribution and empirical comparison across tasks.
- **Question 2:** Can alternative numerical integration methods yield gradient estimators for discrete latents with better bias‑variance trade‑offs without additional computational cost?
  - *Basis:* The authors state that more accurate approximations require a different toolkit of numerical methods and remain challenging.
  - *Why unresolved:* Higher‑order methods (e.g., Simpson’s rule) require non‑categorical evaluations or Hessians, which are impractical.
  - *Resolution evidence:* Discovery of a new rule that uses only first‑order derivatives and categorical samples, validated theoretically and empirically.
- **Question 3:** Why do high‑bias low‑variance estimators outperform low‑bias high‑variance ones in high‑dimensional discrete latent spaces?
  - *Basis:* Empirical observation: low‑bias high‑variance estimators are more effective in simple low‑dimensional settings, while high‑bias low‑variance estimators excel in complex high‑dimensional settings.
  - *Why unresolved:* The underlying cause of this dimensional dependence is not explained.
  - *Resolution evidence:* Analytical scaling of variance with dimension or controlled experiments isolating factors like sparsity.

## Limitations
- **Multi‑dimensional latent handling:** The paper assumes row‑wise independent categorical latents but provides limited detail on vectorized implementation of ReinMax‑CV’s control variate across rows, raising potential shape mismatches or incorrect gradient aggregation.
- **Gumbel‑Rao derivative omission:** Ignoring the Jacobian through the conditional reparameterization (as noted in a footnote) may weaken the correlation between control variate and target in ReinMax‑CV, increasing bias.
- **Hyperparameter inconsistencies:** Table 4 lists configuration‑specific τ values for ReinMax‑Rao/CV, while the text states that the ST/ReinMax terms use τ=1; it is unclear whether τ_cv overrides all terms or only the Gumbel‑Rao/STGS parts.
