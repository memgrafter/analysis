# Adaptive Subspace Modeling With Functional Tucker Decomposition
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.25530
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25530
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Functional Tucker decomposition with RKHS continuity constraints enables adaptive subspace modeling that maintains classification accuracy under domain shift where standard Tucker fails.

## Executive Summary
Traditional tensor decompositions, such as Tucker, rely on fixed discrete bases and thus lose continuous information when data originates from smooth processes. This becomes problematic when training and test samples are drawn from different sampling domains (domain shift). The paper introduces functional Tucker decomposition (FTD), which incorporates continuity constraints via reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). By representing each mode as a function without committing to a fixed basis, FTD can adapt to arbitrary sampling points, preserving the underlying functional structure.

FTD is evaluated on three classification tasks: semi‑synthetic digits, NYC taxi trips, and mango hyperspectral imaging. When training and test domains match, FTD and HOSVD perform similarly. However, under domain shift, FTD remains stable (e.g., ~0.7 accuracy on taxi, up to 0.61 on hyperspectral) while HOSVD degrades seriously (drops to ~0.3 on taxi, near chance on hyperspectral). These results highlight the advantage of functional adaptability for robust subspace modeling in classification.

## Method Summary
FTD extends Tucker decomposition to functional data by modeling each mode as a function living in an RKHS. The decomposition solves a regularized optimization problem: minimize reconstruction error plus RKHS norms (smoothness penalties) of the factor functions. The optimization alternates between updating the core tensor (with fixed factors) and updating each factor function (with fixed core). Because the factor representations are basis‑free, they can be evaluated at any point in the domain, naturally handling test data with different sampling locations. This avoids the discretization error that plagues standard tensor decompositions under domain shift.

## Key Results
- In matched domains, FTD and HOSVD achieve comparable classification accuracy.
- Under domain shift, FTD's accuracy remains stable (e.g., ~0.7 on NYC taxi data, up to 0.61 on mango hyperspectral data), while HOSVD drops dramatically (~0.3 on taxi, near chance on hyperspectral).
- The improvement demonstrates that continuity constraints via RKHS enable adaptive functional modeling essential for domain transfer in tensor‑based classification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
RKHS provides a function space where smoothness is naturally controlled by the norm. By representing factor modes as RKHS functions, FTD enforces continuity without fixing a basis. Consequently, the learned factors can be evaluated at any location in the input domain. When test data come from a shifted sampling grid, these functions still produce valid representations because they are defined continuously. In contrast, a discrete basis (e.g., HOSVD) is tied to the training grid, causing severe information loss when applied to a shifted domain. Thus, the functional, continuity‑constrained nature of FTD is the key to its robustness.

## Foundational Learning
- **Tucker Decomposition**: Why needed: Classical multi‑way decomposition that FTD generalizes to functional data. Quick check: Factorizes a tensor into a core tensor and factor matrices.
- **Functional Data Analysis**: Why needed: Treats each mode as a smooth function, preserving continuity. Quick check: Analyzes data where observations are curves, surfaces, or other functions rather than scalars.
- **Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)**: Why needed: Provides a framework to work with functions and enforce smoothness via norms. Quick check: A Hilbert space of functions where point evaluation is a continuous linear functional; kernels define the inner product.
- **Domain Shift/Adaptation**: Why needed: Real‑world scenarios where training and test sampling distributions differ; FTD directly addresses shift in measurement points. Quick check: Mismatch between training and test data distributions, often due to different sampling conditions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Raw functional tensor → RKHS representation of each mode → Alternating least‑squares optimization (core update ↔ factor updates) → Learned factor functions & core tensor → Feature extraction → Classifier training/evaluation.

**Critical path**:  
1. Initialize factor functions (e.g., from HOSVD on a discretized version).  
2. With factors fixed, solve linear system for the core tensor.  
3. With core fixed, update each factor function by solving a regularized problem in its RKHS (involves kernel Gram matrix operations).  
4. Repeat steps 2–3 until convergence. The factor update step is the computational bottleneck due to kernel matrix inversions.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Adaptivity vs. cost*: Basis‑free RKHS representation allows adaptation but requires solving kernel systems that scale with the number of design points.  
- *Smoothness control*: Kernel type and bandwidth, plus regularization strength, must balance fidelity and smoothness; their selection is nontrivial and problem‑dependent.  
- *Precomputation*: Computing and storing Gram matrices can accelerate repeated updates but increases memory usage.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Violation of smoothness assumption (highly discontinuous data) leads to poor reconstruction and classification.  
- Inappropriate kernel bandwidth (too small or too large) causes over‑ or under‑fitting, reducing robustness under domain shift.  
- Numerical issues (e.g., near‑singular Gram matrices) may cause instability or slow convergence.

**First experiments**:  
1. *Synthetic validation*: Apply FTD to a known low‑rank functional tensor (e.g., Gaussian process‑generated) and verify recovery accuracy.  
2. *Reconstruction under shift*: Train on one grid, test reconstruction on a shifted grid; compare FTD with HOSVD.  
3. *Classification sensitivity*: On the semi‑synthetic digits dataset, vary kernel bandwidth and regularization, measuring accuracy in both matched and shifted domains.

## Limitations
- **Kernel sensitivity**: The impact of kernel type, bandwidth, and regularization strength on performance is not systematically evaluated.
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only three datasets (one semi‑synthetic) are used, restricting generalizability to other functional tensor domains.
- **Baseline comparisons**: The sole comparison is with HOSVD; other smoothness‑aware or domain adaptation methods are omitted, leaving the uniqueness of FTD's advantage unclear.

## Confidence
- Methodological soundness of FTD (RKHS formulation yields a valid adaptive functional decomposition): Medium
- Comparability in matched domains (FTD and HOSVD perform similarly when training and test domains align): High
- Robustness under domain shift (FTD maintains stable accuracy while HOSVD degrades): Medium
- Attribution of benefit to functional adaptability (continuity constraints are the primary cause of robustness): Low‑Medium
