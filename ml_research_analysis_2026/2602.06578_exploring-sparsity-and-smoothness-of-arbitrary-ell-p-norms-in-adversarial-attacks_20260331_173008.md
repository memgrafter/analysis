# Exploring Sparsity and Smoothness of Arbitrary $\ell_p$ Norms in Adversarial Attacks

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.06578
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06578
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The optimal $\ell_p$ parameter for achieving a favorable trade-off between sparsity and smoothness in adversarial perturbations lies in the range [1.3, 1.5], with lower values (~1.3) for convolutional networks and higher values (~1.4–1.5) for Transformers; standard choices $p=1$ and $p=2$ are suboptimal, and adversarial training shifts the optimum towards higher $p$ while reducing sparsity.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically investigates how the $\ell_p$ norm parameter $p \in [1,2]$ affects the structural properties—sparsity and smoothness—of adversarial perturbations. Through extensive experiments across four image datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Flowers102, GTSRB) and eight model architectures (ResNet, VGG, ViT, Swin), and using two attack algorithms ($\ell_1$-APGD and AFW), the authors calibrate perturbation budgets to ensure equal attack effectiveness and then compute five normalized measures (two sparsity, three smoothness). They define the optimal $p$ as the largest value where all measures exceed a common threshold. Their key finding is that the best trade-off occurs for $p \in [1.3,1.5]$, with CNNs favoring the lower end and Transformers the higher end. Adversarial training further increases optimal $p$ and reduces sparsity. The work also highlights algorithmic differences between $\ell_1$-APGD and AFW and raises questions about the non‑monotonic sparsity behavior near $p=1$.

## Method Summary
The experimental pipeline begins by calibrating, for each model–dataset combination, the perturbation budget $\varepsilon_p$ for every $p$ such that the attack reduces test accuracy to one‑third of the clean accuracy, thereby equalizing effectiveness across norms. Adversarial perturbations are then generated: $\ell_1$-APGD is used for $p=1$, and AFW for $p>1$ (including a proxy $p=1.01$ to approximate $\ell_1$). For each perturbation, five structural measures are computed: Gini Index and Hoyer measure (sparsity), and three smoothness metrics based on Gaussian smoothing, low‑pass filtering, and first‑order Taylor approximation. Raw measures are normalized to $[0,1]$ per model/dataset/algorithm to enable cross‑$p$ comparison. The optimal $p$ is identified as the largest threshold $\beta_{\text{opt}}$ such that all normalized measures exceed $\beta_{\text{opt}}$ at that $p$; the set of all $p$ satisfying this forms $M_{\text{opt}}$. The study aggregates these optimal $p$ values across models and datasets, both normally and adversarially trained, to derive the reported ranges.

## Key Results
- Standard $\ell_p$ norms ($p=1,2$) are suboptimal for the joint sparsity‑smoothness objective; the best trade‑off occurs for $p \in [1.3, 1.5]$.
- Convolutional networks (ResNet, VGG) have optimal $p$ near 1.3, while Transformers (ViT, Swin) exhibit optimal $p$ around 1.4–1.5.
- Adversarial training increases the optimal $p$ and reduces overall perturbation sparsity; the gap between $\ell_1$-APGD and AFW in sparsity measurements is pronounced.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1**: The $\ell_p$ norm parameter $p$ directly shapes the geometry of the constraint set ($\ell_p$ ball), which in turn influences how the perturbation magnitude is distributed across pixels. For $p$ close to 1 the ball is pointy, encouraging many zero entries (sparsity); for $p=2$ it is rounder, spreading magnitude more evenly. The optimization algorithm (AFW or $\ell_1$-APGD) navigates this geometry, so the perturbation’s structural properties are primarily determined by the norm constraint. *Evidence*: Sections III.A & V.B; abstract. *Break condition*: If the algorithm’s step size or stopping criteria dominate over the constraint geometry, the observed $p$‑dependence may weaken or reverse.

**Mechanism 2**: Smoothness is operationalized as stability under local smoothing operations or linear approximation. The smoothing‑based measures compute the deviation between the perturbation and its smoothed version integrated over a range of smoothing strengths; lower deviation indicates higher smoothness. The Taylor‑based measure quantifies the residual of a first‑order local approximation. This framework ensures consistent smoothness quantification across $p$‑values. *Evidence*: Section IV; abstract. *Break condition*: If the chosen smoothing kernels or neighborhood definitions do not align with human perceptual smoothness, the measures may not correlate with perceptual quality.

**Mechanism 3**: The optimal $p$ arises from the intersecting region where both sparsity and smoothness are sufficiently high. Empirically, sparsity peaks near $p=1.3$ and smoothness plateaus around $p=1.4$; the optimal $p$ is the largest value where all normalized measures exceed a threshold $\beta_{\text{opt}}$. Architecture differences likely stem from Transformers processing images as patch sequences, altering how $\ell_p$ constraints interact with attention mechanisms, leading to higher optimal $p$. *Evidence*: Section VI.B & Fig. 3; Section VI.D. *Break condition*: If normalization distorts relative importance or the thresholding method is sensitive to an outlier measure, the identified $p^*$ may not represent a true Pareto frontier.

## Foundational Learning
- **$\ell_p$ Norm Geometry**: The shape of the $\ell_p$ ball (pointier for $p \to 1$, rounder for $p=2$) dictates how the constraint distributes perturbation magnitude across coordinates, directly influencing sparsity. Understanding this geometry is key to predicting why $p=1$ favors fewer large‑magnitude pixels while $p=2$ spreads them.  
  *Quick check*: Why does an $\ell_1$ constraint tend to produce vectors with more zero entries than an $\ell_2$ constraint of the same radius?

- **Sparsity Measures (Gini Index, Hoyer)**: Since $\ell_0$ is non‑convex and discrete, Gini and Hoyer provide continuous proxies for the unevenness of coefficient magnitudes, aligning better with perceptual sparsity than simple non‑zero counts.  
  *Quick check*: What is the key difference between the Gini Index and Hoyer measure in how they weight small vs. large coefficients?

- **Smoothness as Operator Stability**: A signal is smooth if it changes little under a smoothing operator. The paper’s framework integrates the deviation $|C_\alpha(I) - I|$ over smoothing strengths $\alpha$ with exponential decay, offering a theoretically grounded smoothness metric applicable across $p$‑values.  
  *Quick check*: Would a “salt‑and‑pepper” noise perturbation score high or low on the Gaussian smoothing‑based measure? Why?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Attack generators ($\ell_1$-APGD for $p=1$; AFW for $p>1$) produce adversarial perturbations $\delta$. These feed into measure calculators that compute five metrics: Gini Index, Hoyer measure, $T_{\text{Gaussian}}$, $T_{\text{lowpass}}$, $T_{\text{Taylor}}$. The optimal $p$ finder takes the normalized measures and computes $\beta_{\text{opt}}$ and $M_{\text{opt}}$. The $\varepsilon$ calibration component loops back to ensure performance‑matched attack strengths for each $p$.

**Critical Path**:  
For each (model, dataset, training‑type) combination:  
1. Calibrate $\varepsilon_p$ for all $p \in [1,2]$ to target 1/3 of clean accuracy.  
2. Generate adversarial perturbations for all $p$ using $\ell_1$-APGD ($p=1$) and AFW ($p>1$).  
3. Compute all five measures on each perturbation.  
4. Normalize each measure to $[0,1]$ per (model, dataset, algorithm).  
5. Compute $\beta_{\text{opt}}$ and $M_{\text{opt}}$ as defined in Eq. (15)–(16).  

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Attack algorithm choice*: $\ell_1$-APGD is exact for $p=1$ but cannot be used for $p>1$; AFW approximates for $p>1$ but is iterative. Comparing $p=1$ ($\ell_1$-APGD) to $p=1.01$ (AFW) introduces a confound (see Fig. 4).  
- *Measure selection*: Smoothness measures differ; Taylor captures local linearity, smoothing captures multi‑scale coherence. Using all three is comprehensive but may over‑represent certain aspects.  
- *Normalization*: Measures are normalized per (model, dataset, algorithm) to $[0,1]$ to enable cross‑$p$ comparison, but this discards absolute scale and assumes relative changes are most informative.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Non‑monotonic sparsity curves with a peak at $p\approx1.3$ instead of monotonic increase toward $p=1$.  
- High variance in optimal $p$ for Transformer models (Fig. 3).  
- $\ell_1$-APGD and AFW at $p\approx1$ yielding divergent sparsity outcomes (Fig. 4a).  
- Adversarial training causing overall lower sparsity and an upward shift in optimal $p$ (Figs. 3, 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What explains the non‑monotonic sparsity curve with a peak near $p=1.3$? *Basis*: Figure 2 shows sparsity peaking at $p\approx1.3$; the paper attributes it partly to algorithmic differences but not the underlying cause. *Why unresolved*: Understanding the cause is necessary to predict how $p$ influences perturbation structure and to design attacks with desired sparsity. *What evidence would resolve*: Analyze optimization trajectories of AFW/$\ell_1$-APGD across $p$, examine $\ell_p$ unit ball geometry for non‑integer $p$, or derive theoretical bounds linking $p$ to coefficient distribution.

- **Open Question 2**: Why do Transformers have higher optimal $p$ (~1.4–1.5) than CNNs (~1.3) for balancing sparsity and smoothness? *Basis*: Figure 3a shows Transformers’ optimal $p$ is significantly higher and more variable; the paper remarks Transformers are more robust to sparse attacks but provides no mechanistic explanation. *Why unresolved*: Without this, we cannot generalize optimal $p$ selection to new architectures or anticipate design effects. *What evidence would resolve*: Compare gradient sparsity and frequency sensitivity between attention‑based models and CNNs; test whether the difference stems from patch processing, global context, or other inductive biases.

- **Open Question 3**: Do the reported optimal $p$ ranges (1.3–1.5) generalize to other attack algorithms and data modalities? *Basis*: The study is restricted to $\ell_1$-APGD and AFW on image classification datasets; other attacks (e.g., PGD, Square) and other data types (audio, video, 3D) are not considered. *Why unresolved*: Different optimization strategies and perceptual characteristics in other modalities may alter the sparsity‑smoothness trade‑off. *What evidence would resolve*: Replicate the systematic $p$‑sweep with diverse attacks on multiple data domains, using appropriate sparsity and smoothness definitions, and compare resulting optimal $p$ values.

## Limitations
- Smoothness measure specifications (e.g., Taylor neighborhood size, low‑pass cutoff mapping) are not fully described; the code is essential for faithful reproduction.
- The $\varepsilon$‑calibration algorithm (binary search details, attack convergence criteria) is unspecified, risking biased cross‑$p$ comparisons.
- The comparison between $p=1$ (using $\ell_1$-APGD) and $p>1$ (using AFW) introduces an algorithmic confound that may partly explain sparsity differences.

## Confidence
- **Sparsity and smoothness vary systematically with $p$**: High
- **Optimal $p$ lies in [1.3, 1.5]**: Medium
- **CNNs have lower optimal $p$ (~1.3) than Transformers (~1.4–1.5)**: Medium
- **Adversarial training increases optimal $p$ and reduces sparsity**: Medium
