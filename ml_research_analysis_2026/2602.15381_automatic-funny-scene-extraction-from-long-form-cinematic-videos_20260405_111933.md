# Automatic Funny Scene Extraction from Long-form Cinematic Videos

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.15381
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15381
- **Reference Count:** 15
- **Primary Result:** An end-to-end pipeline extracts humorous scenes from long films with 87% curator-judged accuracy and 98% localization precision.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a fully automated system for identifying and extracting humorous moments from full-length cinematic videos, bypassing the need for manual annotation. It combines multimodal scene segmentation—using visually trained shot representations and textual captions—with a humor tagging stage that fuses audio laughter cues and textual humor analysis via a modified ColBERT model. A content guardrail filters inappropriate material. Evaluated on five feature films, the system demonstrates high accuracy in both locating funny scenes and judging their comedic intent, offering a scalable tool for generating engaging previews.

## Method Summary
The proposed pipeline operates in three stages. First, it performs scene segmentation by merging visual shot embeddings (refined through guided triplet mining) with shot-level text captions. Second, a humor tagging module assesses each scene using two signals: audio-based laughter detection and a fine-tuned ColBERT model that analyzes scene transcripts for humorous patterns. Finally, a guardrail mechanism removes clips with improper content. This end-to-end approach integrates visual, audio, and textual modalities to identify comedic moments without human intervention.

## Key Results
- Scene segmentation achieves an 18.3% average precision improvement over state-of-the-art on the OVSD benchmark.
- Humor detection in long text yields an F1 score of 0.834.
- On five full-length films, 87% of extracted clips were judged as intentionally funny by professional curators, with 98% accuracy in scene boundary localization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The proposed system’s effectiveness stems from the complementary integration of visual, audio, and textual modalities:
- Scene segmentation merges visually derived shot embeddings (enhanced by guided triplet mining) with textual captions to produce coherent scene boundaries.
- Humor tagging employs two complementary signals: (1) Audio laughter detection identifies audience laughter in the audio track; (2) A fine-tuned ColBERT model analyzes scene transcripts for humorous language patterns.
- A guardrail filters out clips with inappropriate content based on visual analysis.
The multimodal fusion likely captures both explicit (laughter) and implicit (linguistic) cues, leading to high curator-judged accuracy (87%) and precise localization (98%). The paper does not provide a formal ablation study to isolate each component’s contribution, so this mechanism is inferred from the method description.

## Foundational Learning
Key learning components include:
- **Visual shot embeddings**: Learned using guided triplet mining, which encourages the model to distinguish between shots that belong to different scenes while grouping similar shots. This improves segmentation performance (18.3% AP gain on OVSD).
- **Humor detection with ColBERT**: The ColBERT model is fine-tuned on a dataset of humorous and non-humorous text (likely derived from movie transcripts). It leverages BERT’s contextual understanding to detect subtle humor cues in long text, achieving an F1 score of 0.834.
- **Audio laughter detection**: Implements a pre-trained model to detect laughter sounds, providing an audio-based indicator of humor.
- **Guardrail classifier**: A visual content filter that removes inappropriate scenes.
These foundations build upon prior work in scene segmentation (OVSD) and humor detection, though the paper does not detail the specific datasets or pre-training corpora for these components.

## Architecture Onboarding
The overall architecture follows a sequential pipeline:
1. **Scene Segmentation**: Takes a long-form video, detects shots, extracts visual embeddings (using a CNN backbone) and generates textual captions (likely via an image captioning model). The two modalities are fused to produce scene boundaries.
2. **Humor Tagging**: For each segmented scene, audio is processed for laughter detection, and the scene transcript is fed to the fine-tuned ColBERT model. The two scores are combined to decide if the scene is humorous.
3. **Guardrail**: Applies a visual classifier to each candidate humorous clip to filter out inappropriate material (e.g., violence, nudity).
**Critical path**: Video input → shot detection → visual & textual feature extraction → scene segmentation → transcript generation → humor scoring (audio + text) → guardrail filtering → output of funny clips.
**Design tradeoffs**:
- Visual + textual segmentation improves over purely visual methods but requires reliable captioning, which may fail on low-light or abstract scenes.
- Using audio laughter detection adds robustness but may misclassify non-laugh sounds; ColBERT captures linguistic humor but depends on transcript accuracy.
- The guardrail prioritizes safety, possibly discarding some benign but contextually humorous content.
**Failure signatures**:
- Missed humorous scenes when transcripts are inaccurate (e.g., due to poor ASR) or when humor is purely visual.
- False positives from canned laughter or colloquial language misinterpreted as humorous.
- Segmentation errors on films with rapid shot changes or static scenes.
**First experiments**: The paper does not recount early experiments. Given the pipeline complexity, the authors likely started with individual module baselines (e.g., segmentation on OVSD, humor detection on a held-out dataset) before integration. Iterative tuning of fusion thresholds and guardrail sensitivity probably occurred during development.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Although the paper does not explicitly enumerate research questions, its focus and limitations suggest several unanswered questions:
- How well does the system generalize to a diverse set of films (e.g., different genres, eras, languages)?
- Do professional curator judgments align with those of general audiences across cultures?
- What is the failure rate of the guardrail on borderline content, and can it be made more nuanced?
- How does the humor tagging module perform against state-of-the-art humor detection models on standardized benchmarks like HumorDB?
- Could the pipeline be adapted for live video streams or shorter user‑generated content?
- What is the computational cost and scalability of the end‑to‑end system for massive video libraries?
These questions arise from the limited evaluation scope and the need for broader validation.

## Limitations
- Evaluation was conducted on only five full-length films, limiting statistical diversity and generalizability.
- Humor judgments rely on professional curators, which may not reflect broader audience perceptions.
- Scene segmentation performance is validated only on the OVSD benchmark; applicability to other cinematic styles or languages is uncertain.
- The improper‑content guardrail lacks quantitative metrics such as false positive rates.
- The humor detector is not directly compared against recent humor recognition benchmarks.

## Confidence
- **Scene segmentation (AP +18.3% on OVSD):** High
- **Humor detection (F1=0.834 on long text):** Medium
- **Overall system (87% funny, 98% localization):** Medium
