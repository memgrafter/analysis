# Evaluating the Diagnostic Classification Ability of Multimodal Large Language Models: Insights from the Osteoarthritis Initiative

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02443
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02443
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A trained vision encoder (CLIP-OA) alone achieved 85.27% binary accuracy (AUC 0.949, AP 0.945) and 70.29% multi-class accuracy, with 92.9% sensitivity, outperforming full MLLM pipelines; LLM fine-tuning provided no improvement and often degraded performance.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates multimodal large language models (MLLMs) for knee osteoarthritis (OA) radiograph classification using the Osteoarthritis Initiative dataset. Through extensive ablation studies, the authors find that a task-specific, trained vision encoder (CLIP-OA) alone significantly outperforms full MLLM architectures that include an LLM component. Fine-tuning the LLM—even with parameter-efficient methods like LoRA—fails to improve diagnostic accuracy and frequently reduces sensitivity. Counterintuitively, training on a small, class-balanced subset (500 images) yields better results than using a larger imbalanced set (5,778 images), emphasizing data quality over scale. Baseline MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini) perform near-randomly. The work concludes that for high-certainty diagnostic classification tasks, optimizing the vision encoder is critical, while the generative LLM component is ill-suited for the core decision and better reserved for interpretation and report generation.

## Method Summary
The authors built upon the LLaVA-Med architecture, replacing its vision encoder with a CLIP model fine-tuned on knee X-rays from the Osteoarthritis Initiative. The vision encoder was trained in a supervised manner to classify Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grades (0–4) using cross-entropy loss, with text labels describing each grade. A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) connector was then trained to project the encoder’s features into the LLM’s (Mistral-7B) embedding space. The full MLLM was evaluated under various conditions: with the vision encoder frozen or fine-tuned, with the LLM frozen or adapted via LoRA, and using either a balanced (500 images) or imbalanced (5,778 images) training set. All models were assessed on a held-out test set (1,656 images) using standard classification metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, Cohen’s κ, etc.). The vision encoder-only model (CLIP-OA) served as the primary ablation baseline.

## Key Results
- **CLIP-OA (vision encoder alone)** achieved 85.27% binary accuracy (AUC 0.949, AP 0.945) and 70.29% multi-class accuracy, with 92.9% sensitivity—surpassing all full MLLM configurations.
- **LLM integration degraded performance:** Models with a trained vision encoder but a fine-tuned (LoRA) LLM showed lower sensitivity (70.7–81.7%) than the vision encoder alone (92.9%), and often produced non-convergent training or hallucinations when trained on the imbalanced set.
- **Data balance trumped scale:** LoRA fine-tuning on the small class-balanced subset (500 images) consistently outperformed training on the larger imbalanced set (5,778 images), where training curves failed to converge or produced severe hallucinations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Diagnostic accuracy is primarily driven by a task-specific, trained vision encoder.** The vision encoder learns to extract and classify disease-specific visual features (e.g., joint space narrowing, osteophytes) directly from images via a supervised objective. CLIP-OA’s strong standalone performance (85.27% binary accuracy, AUC 0.949) demonstrates that a well-defined image-to-label mapping can be solved without an LLM’s generative capabilities. This assumes the task involves a single, static image and a fixed label set; it may break down if complex textual context (e.g., patient history) must be integrated at inference.

**Mechanism 2: Integrating and fine-tuning an LLM provides no meaningful improvement and often degrades diagnostic certainty.** The LLM’s generative, next-token prediction objective introduces stochasticity and misalignment. It processes projected visual features but can alter the classifier’s implicit decision from the vision encoder pathway, reducing sensitivity (e.g., 92.9% → 70–81%). LLM fine-tuning primarily teaches a “response style” (formatting the grade) rather than improving visual interpretation. This mechanism relies on the assumption that the information bottleneck lies in visual feature extraction, not language generation; if the task evolves to require reasoning or explanation, the LLM’s value may emerge for downstream interpretation.

**Mechanism 3: A small, class-balanced dataset yields more effective MLLM training than a larger, imbalanced one.** Class imbalance in the 5,778-image set biases the connector and LLM pathways toward majority KL grades (0, 2), causing non-convergence or severe hallucinations during LoRA fine-tuning. A balanced 500-image subset ensures equal feature learning for all grades. The vision encoder appears more robust to imbalance, but the connector+LLM pipeline is highly sensitive to distribution skew. This breaks if the dataset were both large *and* perfectly balanced (or balanced via sophisticated sampling), where larger size should provide greater feature diversity.

## Foundational Learning

**Contrastive Vision-Language Pretraining (e.g., CLIP)**  
*Why needed:* The vision encoder starts from a model pretrained to align images with natural language, enabling it to learn semantic features that match text descriptions of KL grades. This pretraining explains why zero-shot classification is possible and why further fine-tuning on grade-specific text labels improves discriminative power.  
*Quick check:* How does a contrastive loss (e.g., InfoNCE) encourage a vision encoder to produce image embeddings that match the text “moderate osteoarthritis, definite narrowing of joint space”?

**Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA)**  
*Why needed:* The study uses LoRA to adapt the LLM and connector with minimal parameter changes, making it feasible to train on limited data. Understanding that LoRA learns low-rank updates to weight matrices explains how it can adjust output formatting without altering core visual representations—but also why it may be sensitive to data distribution and fail to learn new visual concepts if the vision encoder is frozen.  
*Quick check:* Why might LoRA fine-tuning on a small balanced dataset succeed for instruction formatting but fail to improve core visual classification if the underlying vision encoder remains untuned?

**Class Imbalance in Multi-Class Ordinal Tasks**  
*Why needed:* The KL grade distribution is heavily skewed (few grade 4 images), which misleads accuracy metrics and biases training. The paper’s results (e.g., poor KL-1 accuracy, training failures on imbalanced data) are direct consequences. Recognizing this explains why metrics like per-class F1 or Cohen’s κ are more reliable for evaluation.  
*Quick check:* Beyond accuracy, which metrics are most appropriate for evaluating an imbalanced, ordinal classification problem like KL grading?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Vision Encoder (CLIP-vit-large) → Connector (MLP) → LLM (Mistral-7B)

**Critical path:**  
1. Train Vision Encoder on balanced image-text pairs (KL grade descriptions) with cross-entropy loss.  
2. Train Connector on frozen Vision Encoder outputs (1 epoch, MLP only).  
3. **Stop.** Use prompt engineering with frozen LLM for output formatting.  
4. *Optional, high-risk:* Apply minimal LoRA to LLM (rank 128, α=256) if response style is incorrect, using a small balanced dataset.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Vision Encoder:* Frozen (general CLIP) yields random performance; trained (CLIP-OA) is essential but incurs compute cost.  
- *LLM:* Frozen + prompt engineering is more stable and often matches/exceeds LoRA performance; LoRA risks degradation with imbalanced data.  
- *Dataset:* Smaller (500), perfectly balanced set reliably trains connector+LLM; larger (5,778), imbalanced set causes failures.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Non-converging training loss during LoRA/MLP training.  
- Repetitive, nonsensical, or “de de de” hallucinations in LLM outputs.  
- Very low sensitivity (<80%) on minority classes (KL 1, 4) despite high overall accuracy.  
- Low Cohen’s κ/MCC between trained vision encoder and full MLLM output.  
- Grad-CAM shows no focused attention on joint spaces/osteophytes.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Vision Encoder Ablation:** Train CLIP on 5,778-image set, evaluate zero-shot classification. Expect ~70% multi-class, ~85% binary accuracy; confirm Grad-CAM focuses on relevant anatomy.  
2. **Connector Necessity Test:** With frozen CLIP-OA, train *only* MLP on 500-image balanced set. Expect similar accuracy to vision encoder alone but with LLM-compatible format.  
3. **LLM Intervention Test:** Take Exp 2 model and evaluate (a) frozen LLM with prompt engineering, (b) LoRA on 500-image set, (c) LoRA on 5,778-image set. Expect (a) ≥ (b), both worse than vision encoder in sensitivity; (c) should show non-convergence/hallucination.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability:** Are conclusions that vision encoder optimization is critical and LLM fine-tuning provides no benefit generalizable beyond knee osteoarthritis X‑ray classification? The study was limited to a single dataset and modality.
2. **Degradation mechanism:** Why does integrating an LLM into a trained vision encoder often degrade classification accuracy? The underlying cause (e.g., misalignment, generative uncertainty) is unclear.
3. **Imbalanced fine-tuning:** How can we fine‑tune the LLM component of MLLMs on class‑imbalanced medical data without training divergence and hallucinations? Effective mitigation strategies (e.g., loss weighting, oversampling) were not explored.
4. **Confidence calibration:** Can MLLMs be modified to output well‑calibrated confidence scores for their classifications? Generative text lacks explicit uncertainty quantification needed for clinical trust.

## Limitations
- **Unclear training details:** Typos (LoRA batch size “1a6”), vague early‑stopping criterion (“confusion matrix performance”), and unspecified handling of class imbalance during connector/LoRA training block faithful replication.
- **Weak external validation:** The corpus has low average neighbor FMR (0.518) and few citations, offering little corroboration for claims beyond the study’s own experiments.
- **Single‑dataset evaluation:** Results are derived solely from knee OA X‑rays; generalizability to other imaging modalities or diagnostic tasks remains unproven.
