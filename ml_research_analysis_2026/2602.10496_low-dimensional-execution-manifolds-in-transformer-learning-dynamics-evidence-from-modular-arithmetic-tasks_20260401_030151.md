# Low-Dimensional Execution Manifolds in Transformer Learning Dynamics: Evidence from Modular Arithmetic Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10496  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10496  
- **Reference count:** 7  
- **Primary result:** Training trajectories of attention-only transformers on modular arithmetic collapse onto low-dimensional (effective rank 3–4) execution manifolds, with SGD updates strongly aligned to this subspace early in training.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the geometric structure of learning dynamics in overparameterized transformer models using controlled modular arithmetic tasks. By tracking complete parameter trajectories of attention-only transformers and applying principal component analysis, the authors reveal a rapid dimensional collapse: despite the high ambient dimensionality, training dynamics concentrate onto execution manifolds of effective rank 3–4. The alignment of SGD commutators with this subspace—up to ten times higher than random baselines—early in training indicates that stochastic updates are largely confined to a few directions. Additional experiments show that sparse autoencoders capture auxiliary routing features but not the core execution, and that curriculum training influences convergence speed while task complexity (e.g., multiplication) modestly increases manifold dimension. The findings suggest that even highly overparameterized models operate within surprisingly low-dimensional subspaces when learning algorithmic tasks.

## Method Summary
The study uses synthetic marker-based modular addition as the primary task. Input sequences of length 32 contain *m* marked positions, each followed by a value token in [0, C‑1]; all other positions hold distractors. Attention-only transformers with embedding dimension *d*=128 (no MLP) are trained via SGD (learning rate 0.001, batch size 64) until perfect accuracy (~5000 steps). Full model checkpoints (all attention weight matrices) are saved every 100 steps across multiple random seeds. Post‑training, flattened weight trajectories are analyzed via PCA to compute the effective rank *r90* (number of components needed to explain 90% variance). SGD commutators—differences between gradient updates from two minibatches in swapped order—are projected onto PCA‑derived execution bases and compared to random bases to measure alignment (ρ_exec/ρ_rand). Sparse autoencoders are trained on intermediate activations to probe feature structure, and curriculum effects are tested by varying task difficulty schedules.

## Key Results
- Effective rank *r90* of attention weight trajectories collapses to 3–4, a tiny fraction of the ambient parameter dimensionality.
- SGD commutators show strong preferential alignment with the execution subspace: projection ratios ρ_exec/ρ_rand reach up to 10× the random baseline early in training, and >92% of non‑commutativity lies in orthogonal staging directions.
- Sparse autoencoder features capture auxiliary routing information but not the core execution, which remains distributed across the low‑dimensional manifold. Curriculum training accelerates convergence, and more complex tasks (e.g., multiplication) yield manifolds of dimension 4–6.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Modular arithmetic tasks admit a simple linear (affine) solution that can be represented by a small number of directions in parameter space. During training, SGD quickly discovers this solution subspace; gradient updates from different minibatches become nearly commuting within it, while the orthogonal complement remains largely stagnant. This explains the observed low effective rank and the early alignment of commutators with the top principal components: the optimizer’s stochasticity reveals that the dynamics are confined to a few dominant modes. The linear nature of the task ensures that the execution manifold is globally defined and low‑dimensional, whereas the presence of MLPs or nonlinearities disrupts this structure by introducing higher‑order interactions that spread updates across many dimensions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Execution Manifold**: The low‑dimensional subspace to which parameter trajectories converge. *Why needed*: Captures the essential degrees of freedom for solving the task. *Quick check*: Compute PCA on weight snapshots and verify that the top few components explain >90% of variance.
- **Effective Rank (r90)**: The number of principal components required to capture 90% of variance. *Why needed*: Quantifies the intrinsic dimensionality of the trajectory. *Quick check*: For a given set of weight snapshots, compute r90 and confirm it is much smaller than ambient dimension.
- **SGD Commutator**: The difference between gradient updates from two minibatches in swapped order; measures non‑commutativity. *Why needed*: Reveals whether gradient steps lie in a low‑dimensional subspace. *Quick check*: Compute the commutator, project onto the PCA basis, and compare the projection magnitude to that onto random bases of the same dimension.
- **Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**: Linear technique to find orthogonal directions of maximum variance. *Why needed*: Identifies the execution manifold. *Quick check*: Apply PCA to flattened weight matrices and check that eigenvalues decay rapidly (e.g., >90% variance in ≤5 components).
- **Sparse Autoencoder**: Unsupervised model that learns a compressed representation with sparse activations. *Why needed*: Tests whether auxiliary features (e.g., routing decisions) are captured separately from the execution subspace. *Quick check*: Train on intermediate activations and inspect feature sparsity and correlation with execution directions.
- **Curriculum Training**: A strategy where task difficulty increases over time. *Why needed*: May shape the geometry of the learning manifold. *Quick check*: Compare effective rank trajectories with and without a curriculum; a smoother or lower‑dimensional manifold may indicate a beneficial curriculum.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input tokens → Token embeddings → Attention‑only Transformer (L layers, each with Q, K, V, O projections) → Output logits → Cross‑entropy loss.

**Critical Path**  
The training loop generates batches of synthetic sequences, computes loss, performs SGD updates, and periodically saves full model checkpoints. After training, the saved trajectories are analyzed: PCA determines effective rank; commutator calculations assess alignment; sparse autoenc disentangle auxiliary features.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Attention‑only architecture simplifies the parameter space and avoids MLP‑induced nonlinearities, making low‑dimensional geometry more apparent.  
- Fixing embedding dimension *d*=128 keeps the total number of parameters manageable for full trajectory storage but limits expressivity.  
- Synthetic modular arithmetic tasks provide a clean, linear testbed but may not generalize to more complex, real‑world data distributions.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Model fails to reach 100% accuracy, indicating the task was not learned.  
- Effective rank remains close to ambient dimensionality (e.g., >1000) instead of collapsing to ~3–4.  
- Commutator projection ratio (ρ_exec/ρ_rand) stays near 1, showing no alignment with a low‑dimensional subspace.  
- Training instability or loss divergence suggests hyperparameter mismatch (e.g., learning rate too high).

**Three First Experiments**  
1. Replicate the base experiment: train a single‑layer attention‑only transformer (*d*=128) on modular addition (*m*=4, *C*=8) with SGD (η=0.001, batch=64). Confirm >99% accuracy and low effective rank (*r90*≈3–4).  
2. Disrupt the manifold by adding a single MLP layer (ff_dim=128) after attention; re‑measure effective rank. Expect increased dimensionality, confirming the sensitivity of the low‑dimensional structure to architectural changes.  
3. Explore task complexity scaling: increase markers to *m*=6 or use multiplication; measure effective rank. Expect a modest growth to 4–6 dimensions, illustrating how the manifold expands with algorithmic difficulty.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scaling laws for execution dimension**: How does manifold dimension scale with model size, task complexity, and dataset diversity?
- **Early‑training diagnostics**: Can geometric measurements early in training reliably predict eventual generalization success (e.g., grokking speed)?
- **Nonlinear extensions**: Do execution manifolds exist for tasks without global linear structure? How should intrinsic dimension be measured when manifolds are local or nonlinear?
- **Role of architecture**: Does including MLP layers fundamentally disrupt low‑dimensional structure? Under what conditions can manifolds re‑emerge?
