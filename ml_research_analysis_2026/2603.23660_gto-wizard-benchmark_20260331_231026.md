# GTO Wizard Benchmark
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23660
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23660
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: The GTO Wizard Benchmark provides a public API for evaluating HUNL agents against a superhuman AI using AIVAT, with top LLMs achieving -16 ± 3 bb/100.

## Executive Summary
The GTO Wizard Benchmark is the first standardized, publicly accessible framework for evaluating AI agents in Heads-Up No-Limit Texas Hold’em (HUNL), with a focus on large language models. It pits agents against GTO Wizard AI, a superhuman poker engine that previously defeated Slumbot by 19.4 ± 4.1 bb/100. The core methodological innovation is the integration of AIVAT, a provably unbiased variance reduction technique, which yields statistically significant results with ten times fewer hands compared to naive evaluation. In zero-shot tests, state-of-the-art LLMs such as GPT-5.3 Extra High reasoning and Claude Opus 4.6 achieve a luck-adjusted win rate of −16 ± 3 bb/100, demonstrating notable progress but remaining far from the superhuman baseline. The benchmark thus establishes a precise, quantifiable setting for probing planning and reasoning under partial observability.

Despite the progress gap, the benchmark offers a valuable tool for the community, with plans to extend to other poker variants and multi-player settings. It highlights persistent challenges in LLM‑based decision making, including representational errors and an inability to reason probabilistically over opponent ranges.

## Method Summary
Evaluation proceeds via a public API where an agent repeatedly plays fixed‑length hands against the GTO Wizard AI. For each decision point, the agent receives a textual description of the game state (including hole cards, community cards, bet history, and stack sizes) and must output a legal poker action. The opponent responds with its equilibrium action, and the hand continues until resolution. To control for luck, AIVAT reweights each hand’s outcome based on the agent’s action probabilities, yielding an unbiased estimate of expected value. The primary metric is the luck‑adjusted win rate in big blinds per 100 hands (bb/100), accompanied by a 95% confidence interval. All tested LLMs are evaluated under zero‑shot conditions without fine‑tuning on poker data.

## Key Results
- GTO Wizard AI achieved a superhuman win rate of 19.4 ± 4.1 bb/100 against the prior champion Slumbot.
- AIVAT reduces the number of hands needed for a given statistical significance by a factor of ten relative to raw outcome aggregation.
- The best LLM (GPT‑5.3 Extra High reasoning) recorded an AIVAT‑adjusted win rate of −16 ± 3 bb/100 against GTO Wizard AI, a substantial improvement over GPT‑4’s −136.2 bb/100 but still far from optimal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s effectiveness stems from three synergistic design choices. First, GTO Wizard AI provides a consistent, equilibrium opponent that cannot be exploited, serving as a stable gold standard. Second, AIVAT dramatically lowers the variance inherent in poker outcomes by reweighting hands according to the agent’s action probabilities, thus delivering precise EV estimates with far fewer hands. Third, the public API abstracts away implementation details, ensuring that evaluations are reproducible and accessible to any researcher. Together, these elements create a rigorous, efficient, and fair testbed for strategic reasoning under partial observability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Game Theory Optimal (GTO) strategy**: Why needed: Establishes a theoretical equilibrium that defines optimal play; deviations indicate exploitable weaknesses. Quick check: Compare the agent’s action frequencies in well‑studied spots (e.g., bluffing the river) to published GTO approximations.
- **AIVAT (Adaptive Importance Sampling for Variance Analysis of Trajectories)**: Why needed: Poker outcomes are high‑variance; AIVAT produces unbiased EV estimates with lower variance, making evaluation practical. Quick check: For a fixed policy, compare the standard error of the mean with AIVAT vs. raw outcomes over the same number of hands.
- **Heads‑Up No‑Limit Texas Hold’em (HUNL)**: Why needed: A benchmark game with huge state/action spaces and hidden information, stressing reasoning and planning capabilities. Quick check: Verify that the agent can handle all betting rounds and arbitrary bet sizes without producing illegal actions.
- **Large Language Model (LLM) agent**: Why needed: LLMs are being repurposed for decision‑making tasks; this benchmark tests whether their language‑based reasoning can be grounded in game theory. Quick check: Measure the proportion of agent responses that map to valid poker actions (fold, call, raise with a legal amount).
- **Expected Value (EV) mistake**: Why needed: The difference between the EV of the chosen action and the GTO action directly measures strategic soundness. Quick check: For a sample of hands, compute the EV gap using a poker calculator; the average EV mistake should correlate with overall win rate.
- **Range‑based reasoning**: Why needed: In imperfect‑information games, optimal play balances across a distribution of possible holdings; deterministic per‑hand reasoning leads to predictable, exploitable strategies. Quick check: Does the agent’s (optional) explanation mention opponent hand probabilities or mixing strategies?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: LLM agent → Action decoder → Game engine (GTO Wizard AI) → Hand outcome → AIVAT reweight → Statistics aggregator.

**Critical path**: A hand is initialized with random hole cards. At each decision point, the current game state (history, community cards, pot, stacks) is formatted into a prompt and sent to the LLM agent. The agent returns an action, which is parsed and validated. The GTO Wizard AI simultaneously chooses its equilibrium action for the opposite position (or the same state depending on role assignment). The hand proceeds through subsequent streets until showdown or fold. The raw chip change is recorded, then AIVAT adjusts it using the agent’s action propensity. The adjusted outcome contributes to cumulative metrics; the loop repeats for the desired number of hands.

**Design tradeoffs**: The authors opted for a static GTO opponent to isolate the agent’s strategic quality without interference from opponent adaptation. AIVAT introduces computational overhead but reduces required sample size, enabling faster evaluation. Zero‑shot evaluation avoids overfitting to the benchmark but likely understates what fine‑tuned models could achieve. The public API’s black‑box nature ensures ease of use but limits diagnostic access to opponent reasoning or internal state.

**Failure signatures**: Frequent invalid actions (e.g., oversized raises) that break the parser; excessively wide confidence intervals suggesting insufficient effective sample size; representational errors such as misreporting card suits, leading to nonsensical decisions; an AIVAT‑adjusted win rate worse than −200 bb/100, indicating the agent fails to follow basic strategy; or repeated API timeouts/errors indicating integration issues.

**Three first experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication**: Run the benchmark with GPT‑4 (or a known model) to reproduce the published score (e.g., −136.2 bb/100), confirming the API’s correctness and baseline behavior.
2. **Variance reduction verification**: Apply AIVAT and raw evaluation to the same GTO bot over varying hand counts; measure the sample size needed to achieve a fixed standard error (e.g., ±2 bb/100) and compare to the claimed 10× improvement.
3. **Prompt engineering sensitivity**: Vary the representation of cards (symbols vs. words), inclusion of hand strength hints, and state formatting; measure changes in representational error rates and AIVAT scores to identify best practices for LLM prompting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the GTO Wizard Benchmark be extended to evaluate frequency mistakes and exploitability, beyond current measures of expected value (EV) mistakes against a static opponent?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 4.3.1 states that the current benchmark "only evaluates EV mistakes" because GTO Wizard AI does not model or adjust to specific opponents, and calls incorporating opponent modeling to assess frequency mistakes an "important avenue for future work."
- Why unresolved: The benchmark's value lies in measuring strategic soundness, but a strategy can have low EV error yet be highly exploitable due to predictable frequencies. Without measuring exploitability, the benchmark cannot fully capture a agent's robustness against adaptive opponents.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of an evaluation protocol where agents are tested against a suite of specialized exploitative opponents (e.g., bots that over-fold to bluffs or under-call) to measure maximum exploitability, or integration of a theoretical metric like "minimax regret" over a distribution of opponent types.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What architectural or training modifications can reduce representational errors—such as suit mismatches or incorrect hand identification—in LLM-based poker agents?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 4.3 reports that all evaluated LLMs exhibited "representational errors," with GPT-5.3 mismatching suited/offsuit hands ~2% of the time, and earlier models like GPT-4 completely misidentifying holdings. These errors "likely explain some, but not all, of the performance gap."
- Why unresolved: These errors indicate a failure in precise state tracking, a fundamental requirement for sound poker strategy. The root cause—whether due to prompt design, tokenization, or lack of symbolic grounding—is not diagnosed, and no mitigation strategies are explored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies varying prompt formulations (e.g., explicit suit symbols vs. textual descriptions), incorporating external state-tracking modules, or fine-tuning on hand-annotation tasks to quantify error reduction and corresponding improvement in AIVAT score.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can LLMs be induced to reason in terms of probabilistic ranges (beliefs over opponent holdings) rather than deterministic per-hand expected value, to produce less predictable and more balanced strategies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 4.3 observes that LLMs "do not seem to consider the probability distribution of cards... when thinking about how they should act. Instead, they seem to reason on a per-hand basis," leading to "unbalanced and likely highly exploitable" play with insufficient strategic mixing.
- Why unresolved: GTO play requires mixing actions to avoid predictable patterns. The paper identifies this as a key qualitative shortcoming but does not investigate whether prompting, chain-of-thought enhancements, or architectural changes can instill probabilistic reasoning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analysis of LLM outputs for explicit probability estimates (e.g., "I call 70% of the time here"), measurement of action frequency distributions across similar decision points, and comparison of exploitability against a worst-case opponent before and after intervention.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does performance in the GTO Wizard Benchmark correlate with general reasoning capabilities or other specialized skills (e.g., mathematical calculation, long-term planning), and what does this reveal about the skills required for partial-observability games?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The benchmark is positioned as a test for "planning and reasoning under partial observability" (Abstract), and the dramatic improvement from GPT-4 (-136.2 bb/100) to GPT-5.3 (-16 bb/100) suggests scaling and reasoning enhancements transfer. However, the specific cognitive components driving success are not isolated.
- Why unresolved: The benchmark provides a single aggregate score (AIVAT). Without diagnostic subtasks or ablations, it is unclear whether strength stems from improved calculation, memory, counterfactual reasoning, or other faculties, limiting its utility as a research tool for identifying algorithmic bottlenecks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Correlating benchmark scores with performance on orthogonal reasoning benchmarks (e.g., mathematical puzzles, planning grids), or designing variant game scenarios that selectively disable certain skills (e.g., removing hidden information via " Revelation" phases) to perform skill decomposition.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Will the benchmark retain its discriminatory power as LLMs improve, or will it saturate, and what successor metrics or game variants are needed to continue pushing the frontier of robust reasoning under uncertainty?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The introduction and conclusion frame the benchmark as an "evolving ecosystem" with plans to expand to other variants (e.g., Pot-Limit Omaha) and more players. The current results show all models "remain far below the baseline," but rapid progress is noted.
- Why unresolved: If models approach GTO Wizard AI's performance, the benchmark may lose sensitivity. The paper does not define what constitutes "superhuman" relative to the benchmark's opponent or propose a roadmap for increasing difficulty while maintaining scientific rigor.
- What evidence would resolve it: Tracking the rate of improvement (bb/100 per model generation) and establishing a saturation threshold (e.g., within 5 bb/100 of GTO Wizard AI), coupled with development of harder metrics like performance against multiple diverse equilibrium opponents or in games with larger action spaces.

## Limitations
- The paper omits key methodological details, hindering reproducibility.
- Model identifiers (e.g., GPT‑5.3, Claude Opus 4.6) are ambiguous and may not correspond to publicly available versions.
- The claimed superhuman performance of GTO Wizard AI and the 10× variance reduction from AIVAT lack independent verification.
