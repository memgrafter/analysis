# Scale-invariant Gaussian derivative residual networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02843
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02843
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: Achieves strong scale generalisation on rescaled CIFAR-10 (92.7% accuracy with flat curve) and rescaled STL-10 (91.2%), outperforming prior Gaussian derivative networks by up to 13 percentage points.

## Executive Summary
Deep neural networks often fail when test images are scaled differently from training data. This paper introduces the scale-invariant Gaussian derivative residual network (GaussDerResNet), which constructs provably scale-covariant layers from scale-normalised Gaussian derivative operators and combines them with residual skip connections. Scale invariance is achieved by processing inputs in parallel through multiple scale channels (with shared weights) and pooling over scales using a permutation-invariant operation. Experiments on rescaled versions of CIFAR-10 and STL-10 show a flat accuracy curve across a wide range of scales (½ to 2×), demonstrating robust generalisation. Ablations reveal that depthwise-separable variants maintain performance with far fewer parameters, and that including a zero-order Gaussian term in higher layers benefits high-resolution datasets like STL-10.

## Method Summary
GaussDerResNet is built from layers that compute linear combinations of scale-normalised Gaussian derivative kernels. Each residual block contains two such layers plus an identity skip, preserving scale covariance. Multiple scale channels run in parallel, each with a different initial scale σ₀ but sharing all filter weights. Within a channel, scales grow geometrically (σₖ = r^{k-1}σ₀). After the final layer, spatial selection (either central pixel or max pooling) produces a feature vector per channel. Finally, a permutation-invariant pooling (max, logsumexp, or average) aggregates over the scale channels, yielding scale-invariant predictions. The architecture uses no spatial subsampling anywhere, which is essential for scale covariance.

## Key Results
- On rescaled CIFAR-10 and STL-10, multi-scale-channel GaussDerResNet achieves ~92.7% and 91.2% accuracy at the training scale and maintains a flat generalisation curve across scales ½–2×, improving over prior Gaussian derivative networks by up to 13 percentage points.
- Depthwise-separable variants reduce parameter count by ~4× while retaining comparable performance.
- Including a zero-order term in higher layers benefits the high-resolution STL-10 dataset (~2–4 percentage point gains) but not simpler lower-resolution datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper demonstrates three key mechanisms that enable scale generalisation.

**Mechanism 1: Residual blocks preserve scale covariance**  
Claim: Residual Gaussian derivative layers maintain scale covariance while enabling deeper networks.  
Mechanism: The basis functions (scale-normalised Gaussian derivatives) are provably scale-covariant; residual skip connections (identity mappings) preserve this property because they are linear and commute with scaling when scale parameters are matched (σ' = Sσ).  
Core assumption: Scaling transformation matches appropriately between layers and residual operations do not introduce non-linearities that break covariance.  
Evidence: Formal proof (Equation 14) shows output transforms correctly; experiments confirm covariance holds in practice.  
Break condition: Omitting scale normalization σ^{|α|} or using non-linear activations that do not commute with scaling would break covariance.

**Mechanism 2: Multi-scale channels with permutation-invariant pooling yield scale invariance**  
Claim: Parallel scale channels with shared weights and a permutation-invariant pooling stage allow the network to handle inputs at any scale, not just those seen during training.  
Mechanism: Each channel processes the input at a fixed initial scale σ_i,0. The set of scales is chosen to densely cover the expected range. After spatial selection, the network pools across channels (e.g., max pooling), which is invariant to which channel activates most.  
Core assumption: The scale channels span the operational scale range so that any scaled input matches some channel’s effective scale.  
Evidence: Theoretically proven for continuous infinite channels; discrete approximations with 6 channels show flat accuracy curves on rescaled datasets (Figure 14). Scale selection histograms confirm the chosen scale is proportional to image size.  
Break condition: Too large spacing between scale channels (sparse coverage) or non-permutation-invariant pooling (e.g., a fully connected layer mixing scales) would harm invariance.

**Mechanism 3: Zero-order term benefits high-resolution datasets**  
Claim: Including a zero-order (Gaussian smoothing) term in higher layers improves accuracy on high-resolution datasets with non-centered objects.  
Mechanism: The zero-order term adds low-frequency absolute intensity information that complements derivative-based features, aiding classification when objects are not centrally located or backgrounds vary.  
Evidence: Ablation on rescaled STL-10 shows ~2 ppt improvement at scale 1 and ~4 ppt at scale 2. No benefit (or slight harm) on simpler datasets like CIFAR-10 or Fashion-MNIST.  
Break condition: On datasets with centered objects and low resolution, zero-order may be redundant or reduce derivative-only equivariance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Scale-space theory and Gaussian derivatives**: Gaussian derivative operators form a basis for multi-scale feature extraction; scale normalization (σ^{|α|}) ensures that derivatives of different orders have consistent response across scales, which is essential for scale covariance. *Quick check*: "What is scale normalization in Gaussian derivatives and why is it necessary for scale covariance?"
- **Scale covariance vs. scale invariance**: Scale covariance means the output transforms predictably under scaling (e.g., feature maps are scaled versions of each other). Scale invariance means the final prediction remains unchanged under scaling. The GaussDerResNet layers are covariance; the multi-scale-channel design achieves invariance. *Quick check*: "Explain the difference between scale covariance and scale invariance. Which part of the architecture provides each?"
- **Residual connections in deep networks**: Skip connections allow very deep networks to train without vanishing gradients while preserving scale covariance because the addition is linear and commutes with scaling when scales are matched. *Quick check*: "How do residual skip interactions preserve scale covariance? Why are they safe to use?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input → First Gaussian derivative layer → Residual blocks (each: two Gaussian derivative layers + identity skip) → Final Gaussian derivative layer → Spatial selection (central pixel or spatial max pooling) → Scale channels (N parallel copies with different initial σ_i,0, shared weights) → Permutation-invariant pooling (max/logsumexp/average) over scales → Output.

**Critical path**: The input is processed independently through each scale channel. Inside each channel, the residual stack transforms features while maintaining scale covariance. After the last layer, spatial selection reduces the feature map to a vector (e.g., by taking the central pixel or max-pooling over space). Finally, pooling across the scale channels produces a single vector used for classification.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Number of scale channels: More channels give finer coverage of the scale range but increase memory/computation; typical N=6 covers factor 4 range.
- Scale spacing: Geometric ratio (√2 or 2) balances interpolation smoothness vs. number of channels; denser spacing improves invariance but costs more.
- Spatial selection: Central pixel works for centered objects; spatial max pooling handles arbitrary object locations but is slightly more expensive and may reduce spatial precision.
- Zero-order term: Adding a Gaussian smoothing term in higher layers helps on complex high-resolution datasets (STL-10) but can hurt on simpler ones.
- Depthwise-separable variant: Reduces parameters ~4× but may require longer training or lower learning rate; standard convolutions recommended for early layers.

**Failure signatures**:
- Sharp accuracy drop on scales far from the training scale indicates insufficient scale channel coverage or mismatch in scale progression.
- Training instability or vanishing gradients often arises from mismatched residual connections (e.g., channel count mismatches without proper projection) or incorrect scale matching between layers.
- Overfitting on small datasets might be mitigated with stronger regularization (label smoothing, scale-channel dropout).
- Unexpectedly low accuracy on all scales could be due to inadvertent spatial subsampling (strided conv/pooling) breaking scale covariance.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Verify scale covariance of a single residual block: feed an input, rescale it by factor S, and check that the output matches the expected transformation (Eq. 14) with σ' = Sσ.
2. Train a single-scale-channel network on rescaled Fashion-MNIST; plot test accuracy vs. test scale factor. Expect steep drop-off away from scale 1.
3. Train a multi-scale-channel network (e.g., with N=6 channels and max pooling) and compare the scale generalisation curve; the curve should be nearly flat across the same range of scales.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Why does including a zero-order Gaussian term in higher layers improve accuracy on rescaled STL-10 but not on rescaled CIFAR-10 or Fashion-MNIST? The paper shows the empirical effect but not the underlying cause (dataset statistics, object complexity).
- What causes the training instability observed in depthwise-separable Gaussian derivative residual networks (DSGaussDerResNets), despite their comparable scale generalisation and smaller parameter count?
- How does label smoothing trade off between improved scale generalisation and less sharp scale selection histograms? The paper notes the correlation but does not investigate the mechanism.

## Limitations
- Exact layer-to-channel mapping for the 18-layer configuration and the maximum derivative order N used per dataset are not fully specified; reproducibility may require guesswork.
- Pre-training procedure details (evaluated in Appendix A.4) are incomplete (e.g., weight decay, dropout, exact transfer method).
- Generalisation beyond the three rescaled image datasets used (Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, STL-10) remains unverified; applicability to natural-scale datasets or other vision tasks is open.

## Confidence
- Scale covariance of residual blocks: **High** (formal proof and experiments).
- Multi-scale-channel scale invariance: **High** (theoretical derivation plus flat generalisation curves on two datasets).
- Zero-order term benefit: **Medium** (consistent on STL-10, but generalisability to other datasets uncertain).
- Automatic scale selection: **High** (histograms align with theoretical prediction).
