# CausalReasoningBenchmark: A Real-World Benchmark for Disentangled Evaluation of Causal Identification and Estimation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20571
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20571
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: GPT‑5 baseline correctly identifies the high-level strategy in 84.4% of cases but achieves full identification correctness in only 30.1%, revealing the bottleneck lies in nuanced design specification rather than computation.

## Executive Summary
Current causal inference benchmarks conflate identification (formulating a valid research design) and estimation (computing a numerical effect), yielding aggregate scores that obscure the source of failures. This paper introduces **CausalReasoningBenchmark**, a benchmark comprising 173 queries over 138 real‑world datasets drawn from 85 peer‑reviewed studies and four textbooks. Each query requires an agent to produce (1) a structured JSON identification specification (strategy, treatment, outcome, controls, design‑specific parameters) and (2) a point estimate with standard error. Identification is evaluated via exact‑field matching and validity checks on control sets; estimation uses absolute error, confidence interval overlap, and hypothesis agreement. Results with a GPT‑5 baseline show high strategy recognition (84.4%) but low full identification correctness (30.1%), indicating the primary difficulty lies in precise specification of research design components. Estimation errors are moderate. The benchmark enables granular diagnosis of failures in reasoning versus execution.

## Method Summary
The benchmark disentangles causal inference into two phases with separate evaluation. For each query, an agent receives a natural‑language causal question, a CSV dataset, and a metadata file describing variables and study context. The agent must output (a) a JSON identification specification adhering to a strict schema (including strategy type, treatment, outcome, control variables, and design‑specific parameters) and (b) a numerical effect estimate with standard error, optionally via generated estimation code. Identification scoring checks exact matches on strategy, causal quantity, treatment, and outcome; verifies that the control set includes the minimal sufficient adjustment set and excludes post‑treatment variables; and enforces presence of required strategy‑specific fields. Estimation metrics include absolute error, confidence interval overlap (Jaccard), hypothesis agreement, and flags for opposite‑direction effects or large standard‑error gaps. An auto‑rescaling mechanism tests candidate factors to handle unit mismatches between predicted and gold estimates.

## Key Results
- GPT‑5 baseline identifies the correct high‑level strategy in 84.4% of queries but achieves only 30.1% full identification specification correctness.
- Estimation performance is moderate: median absolute error 0.044 (15.8% relative error) with 89% confidence interval overlap.
- The large gap between strategy recognition and full specification reveals that the bottleneck in automated causal reasoning is nuanced design specification, not broad strategy categorization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Granular Diagnostic Decomposition
Separating identification and estimation allows pinpointing whether failures arise from flawed reasoning (incorrect JSON spec) or execution errors (bad estimate). The benchmark uses distinct evaluation metrics for each phase, enabling the system to distinguish failures in causal reasoning from numerical execution. Evidence: The paper’s abstract and Section 6.2 explicitly define identification correctness as a binary indicator requiring all sub‑checks (strategy, controls, fields) to pass.

### Bottleneck Revelation via High‑Level vs. Full Specification Gap
By tracking both strategy‑level correctness and full identification correctness, the benchmark exposes a critical performance gap. The 84.4% vs. 30.1% gap shows that while models grasp the broad design category, they struggle with detailed parameterization (e.g., selecting correct instruments, specifying cutoffs, avoiding post‑treatment controls). Evidence: Sections 7.1 and 7.3 report and interpret this gap, confirming the bottleneck lies in specifying detailed components.

### Real‑World Complexity as a Stress Test
Sourcing queries from peer‑reviewed replications and textbooks introduces messy, domain‑specific nuances (e.g., fuzzy regression discontinuity, imperfect variable documentation) that synthetic benchmarks may miss, providing a realistic test of applied causal inference. Evidence: Section 4 describes curation from 85 papers across political science journals and four textbooks; Section 10 acknowledges domain limits but asserts the value of real‑world sourcing.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal Identification vs. Estimation as Separate Phases**: The benchmark forces the agent to first articulate a valid research design (identification) before computing an effect (estimation). Why needed: conflating them obscures failure sources. Quick check: For an IV analysis, what must the identification JSON include that differs from what is needed to run a 2SLS regression? (Answer: The JSON must specify the instrument variable and causal quantity e.g., LATE, whereas the estimation code implements the regression.)
- **Quasi‑Experimental Design Conditions**: Each strategy (IV, RDD, DiD, etc.) has precise required parameters and assumptions. Why needed: The benchmark tests fine‑grained knowledge of these conditions. Quick check: For a Difference‑in‑Differences design, what are the minimal required variables in the JSON? (Answer: `group_variable`, `time_variable`, `treatment_variable`.) What is a “bad control” to exclude? (Answer: any post‑treatment variable.)
- **Structured Output Schema Adherence**: The agent must output a JSON that strictly conforms to a predefined schema. Why needed: Enables automated exact matching and validity checks. Quick check: In the Conditional Exogeneity schema, what does the `controls` field represent? (Answer: a set of pre‑treatment covariates to adjust for.) A common error? (Answer: including post‑treatment variables or omitting key confounders.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input Loader -> Reasoning Engine -> (parallel paths) JSON Spec -> Validator and Estimation Code -> Code Executor -> Validator -> Result Aggregator

### Critical path
The input loader parses the CSV and metadata, then passes them to the reasoning engine. The engine produces two outputs: a JSON identification spec and estimation code (with language). The JSON spec is validated directly. The estimation code is executed in a sandbox to produce an effect estimate and standard error, which are then validated. Both validation results feed into the result aggregator.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Schema Strictness vs. Flexibility**: A rigid JSON schema simplifies automated scoring but penalizes valid alternative specifications (e.g., equivalent control sets).
- **Open‑Book vs. Closed‑Book**: Providing raw data and metadata tests the agent’s ability to apply knowledge to a specific dataset, but increases computational cost.
- **Gold Standard Singularity**: A single gold specification allows objective scoring but may overlook defensible alternative identification strategies.

### Failure signatures
- **Identification logic failure**: Strategy label correct but control set includes post‑treatment variables or omits key confounders.
- **Specification completeness failure**: Missing required strategy‑specific fields (e.g., no `cutoff` for RDD).
- **Estimation mismatch**: Low confidence interval overlap despite correct identification, suggesting flawed implementation (e.g., wrong regression formula).
- **Unit/scale error**: Large absolute error that auto‑rescaling fixes, indicating output formatting issues.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Sanity Check - RCT**: Pick an RCT query. Expected: perfect identification spec and low estimation error. Tests pipeline integrity.
2. **Classic Design - DiD**: Pick a DiD query with clear pre/post periods and group indicator. Expect moderate gap between strategy and full spec correctness; checks avoidance of post‑treatment controls.
3. **Nuanced Design - IV with Encouragement**: Pick the Coppock & Green example from Section 8. Tests recognition of a randomized encouragement as an instrument and correct causal quantity (LATE). Expect frequent errors (ATE instead of LATE, misidentified instrument).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Domain generalizability**: How can the benchmark be expanded to include datasets from economics, epidemiology, and other fields beyond political science?
2. **Strategy coverage**: How can the benchmark be extended to cover additional identification strategies like synthetic control, event studies, and regression kink designs?
3. **Multiple valid specifications**: How should the evaluation framework accommodate more than one defensible identification strategy for a single query?
4. **Estimation sensitivity**: How can estimation variability arising from legitimate methodological choices (e.g., bandwidth in RDD, clustering in DiD) be incorporated to distinguish true errors from acceptable variation?

## Limitations
- **Domain specificity**: Datasets are primarily from political science, limiting cross‑disciplinary generalizability.
- **Gold standard singularity**: A single authoritative identification spec may overlook equally valid alternatives, potentially penalizing creative but correct solutions.
- **Baseline narrowness**: Evaluation only on GPT‑5; it is unknown whether the observed identification‑estimation gap holds for other models or simpler systems.

## Confidence
- Granular diagnostic decomposition: Medium
- Bottleneck in nuanced specification: High
- Real‑world complexity as stress test: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑model replication**: Evaluate GPT‑4, Claude 3, and Llama‑3 on the full benchmark to confirm whether the identification‑estimation gap persists and how its magnitude varies.
2. **Multi‑gold annotation**: Gather alternative valid identification specs from multiple experts for a stratified sample of queries; measure how often model outputs match any gold, assessing scoring robustness.
3. **Domain extension**: Construct a comparable subset of queries from economics and epidemiology; test whether the bottleneck gap remains and whether error patterns differ, probing generalizability.
