# The Challenge of Out-Of-Distribution Detection in Motor Imagery BCIs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13324
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13324
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** MC Dropout achieved the highest median AUROC (0.597–0.737) for OOD detection in MI-BCI, significantly outperforming six other methods.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection in Motor Imagery Brain-Computer Interfaces (MI-BCIs), a critical capability for safety and reliability when users perform untrained tasks. The high inherent noise (aleatoric uncertainty) in EEG signals poses a unique challenge, as it can mask the epistemic uncertainty that typically signals OOD samples. Using a Leave-One-Class-Out (LOCO-OOD) paradigm across three public BCI datasets, the authors benchmark seven OOD detection methods—including Bayesian (MC Dropout, Deep Ensembles), density-based (Energy, DDU), and distance-based (DUQ, d-KNN) techniques—with a common EEGNeX CNN backbone. Key findings show that Bayesian methods, particularly MC Dropout, provide the most robust OOD separation, with performance strongly correlated with on-task classification accuracy and varying systematically by the specific OOD class. Contrary to prior work in vision, ReAct optimization offered no benefit, underscoring the need for BCI-specific OOD strategies.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a LOCO-OOD experimental design: for each subject, one motor imagery/execution class is held out as OOD, while the model is trained only on the remaining in-distribution (ID) classes. After preprocessing (bandpass filtering 7–35 Hz, z-score normalization), an EEGNeX CNN is trained on the ID data. Post-training, seven OOD detection methods are applied to the fixed test set (containing both ID and OOD samples at a 1:1 ratio). Each method produces an OOD score (e.g., prediction entropy for Bayesian approaches, energy, negative log-density, or distance metrics). Performance is measured via AUROC for separating OOD from ID trials. Datasets include BNCI2014 (9 subjects, 22 channels), Schirrmeister2017 (12 subjects, 44 motor cortex channels from 128), and Stieger2021 (62 subjects, 64 channels).

## Key Results
- MC Dropout achieved the highest median AUROC scores (0.597–0.737), significantly outperforming all other methods (p < 0.001).
- OOD detectability is class-dependent: "Rest" and "Both hands" are easier to detect than unilateral hand movements across all datasets.
- OOD detection performance correlates strongly with on-task (ID) classification accuracy (Spearman’s ρ up to 0.830 for MC Dropout).
- ReAct, a method effective in vision, provided no significant improvement, likely because ID EEG activations often exceed those of OOD samples.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MC Dropout’s superior OOD detection performance in MI-BCIs stems from its ability to approximate Bayesian model uncertainty, which better captures the high aleatoric noise in EEG signals.
- **Mechanism:** By applying dropout at inference with multiple forward passes, MC Dropout estimates predictive entropy from the distribution of outputs. For OOD samples, the model’s parameter posterior leads to higher and more consistent uncertainty across passes because the input lies in a region of the feature space not well-represented during training. For ID samples, despite inherent EEG noise (high aleatoric uncertainty), the learned decision boundaries provide more consistent predictions, resulting in lower estimated entropy on average.
- **Core assumption:** The variance in predictions from stochastic forward passes correlates with epistemic uncertainty relevant to OOD detection, even when aleatoric uncertainty is high.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "MC Dropout achieved the highest median AUROC scores (0.597–0.737), significantly outperforming others."
  - [section 2.2.1] "MC Dropout... approximates the posterior predictive distribution over the outputs. We measure the uncertainty using the entropy of the mean probabilities."
  - [section 4.1] MC Dropout significantly outperforms other methods (p < 0.001) in pairwise comparisons.
  - [corpus] The neighboring paper on UQ for MI-BCI [arxiv:2507.07511] supports that Bayesian methods like MC Dropout are promising for uncertainty in EEG, but the specific OOD performance gap is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If the ID data itself has extremely high aleatoric uncertainty such that prediction variance from MC Dropout for ID samples overlaps completely with OOD samples, the separation fails. The paper notes this occurs for "many subjects" where "uncertainty for in-distribution classes can still be higher than for out-of-distribution classes."

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** OOD detectability is class-dependent because different motor imagery/execution tasks elicit distinct neural activation patterns and signal-to-noise ratios, affecting their feature space proximity to the ID class clusters.
- **Mechanism:** The LOCO-OOD paradigm treats one class as unseen. Classes with neural signatures that are more physiologically or spectrally distant from the combination of ID classes (e.g., "Rest" with minimal motor cortex activity, "Both hands" with summed bilateral activation) will have feature embeddings that lie further from the ID class centroids or have lower density under the ID distribution. This increases the measured uncertainty (e.g., distance, entropy) for those specific OOD classes compared to others (e.g., unilateral hand movements).
- **Core assumption:** Neural representations for different MI/ME tasks are separable in the learned feature space, and the degree of separability varies systematically with the similarity between tasks.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2] "Rest" and "Both hands" are easier to detect than unilateral hand movements across datasets (e.g., Stieger2021: Rest vs. Right hand p=2.670·10⁻⁴).
  - [section 5.2 Interpretation] "Both hands likely have double the activations compared to using one hand, and the Rest class has none or little."
  - [section 1.2] The LOCO-OOD setup explicitly tests per-class OOD detectability.
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus support; neighboring papers focus on general OOD or other BCI aspects, not class-dependent MI OOD effects.
- **Break condition:** If all OOD classes produce neural patterns that are equidistant or similarly ambiguous within the learned feature space (e.g., due to poor model capacity or highly overlapping class distributions), the class-dependent effect vanishes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** OOD detection performance is non-linearly correlated with on-task (ID) classification performance because both rely on the quality of the learned feature representation; a model that separates ID classes well creates more distinct cluster boundaries, making OOD samples (from a missing class) appear as outliers in the feature space.
- **Mechanism:** High ID classification AUROC indicates the model has learned discriminative features that effectively separate the known classes. In a well-separated feature space, an OOD sample from an unseen class is less likely to fall into the high-density region of any ID class cluster and more likely to be in a low-density region or equidistant from all class centroids. This increases uncertainty estimates (e.g., entropy, distance to nearest neighbor) for OOD relative to ID. The correlation is non-linear because once ID performance is very high, OOD detection may plateau or become more sensitive to the specific OOD class.
- **Core assumption:** The feature representations learned for ID classification are versatile enough that their geometry (cluster compactness, inter-cluster separation) also governs the detectability of samples from entirely absent classes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.3] "We found a non-linear relation... where the OOD detection performance increases with the classification performance." Spearman’s ρ up to 0.830 for MC Dropout on Stieger2021.
  - [figure 5 & A1] Scatter plots show increasing OOD AUROC with On-Task Macro AUROC, especially for Bayesian and density methods.
  - [section 1.1] "we show that high in-distribution classification performance predicts high OOD detection performance."
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence; the neighboring paper on "Learning After Model Deployment" [arxiv:2510.17160] discusses OOD in open-world learning but not the classification-performance link specific to BCI.
- **Break condition:** If the model achieves high ID performance through overfitting to spurious correlations or noise (poor generalization), the feature space may not have meaningful geometry for OOD detection, decoupling the two metrics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Uncertainty Quantification (Aleatoric vs. Epistemic)**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper’s core premise is using model uncertainty to detect OOD samples. Understanding that EEG data has high *aleatoric* (data noise) uncertainty is key to interpreting why standard OOD methods struggle. *Epistemic* (model knowledge) uncertainty is what should increase for OOD.
  - **Quick check question:** If a model shows high entropy on both ID and OOD MI EEG trials, is this more likely due to aleatoric or epistemic uncertainty? What would a Bayesian method like MC Dropout need to show to still separate them?

- **Concept: LOCO-OOD (Leave-One-Class-Out OOD) Paradigm**
  - **Why needed here:** This is the experimental design defining what "OOD" means in this BCI context: samples from a motor imagery/execution class never seen during training. It simulates a user performing an untrained task.
  - **Quick check question:** In a 4-class MI experiment using LOCO-OOD, if the model is trained on Left, Right, and Tongue, which trial labels in the test set are considered ID and which are OOD? What does a perfect OOD detector’s score distribution look like?

- **Concept: EEG Signal Characteristics for MI/ME**
  - **Why needed here:** The high noise, low signal-to-noise ratio, and inter-subject variability of EEG are cited as primary reasons OOD detection is harder in BCIs than in vision. This contextualizes the performance ceiling.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might the model’s uncertainty for the "Rest" class (no movement) sometimes be lower than for a novel "Feet" OOD class? What EEG properties could cause this inversion?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Input -> Preprocessing (bandpass, z-score) -> Feature Extractor (EEGNeX CNN) -> OOD Scorer (method-specific: entropy, energy, density, distance) -> AUROC Evaluation.
  For Bayesian methods (MC Dropout, Deep Ensemble), the OOD Scorer operates over multiple stochastic forward passes or model agregates. For density/distance methods (DDU, DUQ, d-KNN), the scorer accesses stored training embeddings or fitted distributions.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Preprocess & split data for one subject and one OOD-class configuration.
  2. Train EEGNeX feature extractor on ID data (75% train, 10% val).
  3. For each UQ method: Initialize/build its head/storage using the trained feature extractor.
  4. Run inference on the fixed test set (15% ID + all OOD class samples) to get OOD scores.
  5. Compute AUROC. Repeat for all subjects and all OOD classes.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Computational Cost vs. Performance: MC Dropout (50 forward passes) and Deep Ensembles (5 models) are expensive. d-KNN and DUQ have storage/hyperparameter costs. Energy and Softmax are cheapest but poorest performers.
  - Training Complexity: Deep Ensemble and (to a lesser extent) DUQ require significant retraining/pipeline complexity. MC Dropout, Energy, d-KNN, DDU can use a single pre-trained feature extractor.
  - Method Sensitivity: DUQ and d-KNN performance is highly sensitive to k and hyperparameters (Table D1), requiring per-OOD-class tuning. MC Dropout is more turnkey.
  - Interpretability: Distance/Density methods offer more direct geometric interpretation vs. the approximate Bayesian variance of MC Dropout.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - System 1 (Random/Inverse Performance): AUROC ≈ 0.5 or worse. Indicates the OOD scorer cannot separate ID/OOD. Likely causes: (a) Feature extractor failed to learn discriminative features (low ID accuracy), (b) Aleatoric uncertainty dominates, (c) OOD class is too similar to ID classes.
  - System 2 (High Variance across subjects/classes): Large IQR in AUROC plots (Figure 3). Suggests method stability is poor across EEG variability. MC Dropout shows more consistent performance.
  - System 3 (ReAct Degradation): AUROC decreases after applying ReAct. Means OOD samples did not have activations exceeding the ID percentile cutoff; clamping removed signal.
  - System 4 (Method-Specific Collapse): DUQ/DDU performing significantly worse on Stieger2021 (Table 3). Could indicate their parametric assumptions (GMM, RBF) are violated by the feature distribution of that dataset.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate Baseline: Train EEGNeX on 3 ID classes of BNCI2014. Compute Softmax maximum probability OOD score on test set (1:1 ID:OOD). Expect AUROC ~0.55 (baseline in Table 3).
  2. Test Class Dependency: Using the same trained model from Exp 1, compute MC Dropout entropy for OOD score. Run LOCO for all 4 OOD classes. Verify "Rest" and "Tongue" yield higher AUROC than "Left" and "Right" (Figure 4a).
  3. Compare Bayesian vs. Non-Bayesian: Train two feature extractors with same hyperparams but different seeds. For one, apply MC Dropout (50 passes) for OOD score. For the other, use its Softmax maximum. Compare AUROC distributions across all subjects/classes. Expect MC Dropout median to be higher and less variable.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does incremental model training across multiple BCI sessions affect OOD detection performance, and what training schedules optimize both on-task accuracy and OOD robustness?
2. What are the operational definitions and performance benchmarks for OOD detection tailored to specific BCI applications beyond unfamiliar classes (e.g., artifact detection, non-task cognitive states)?
3. Can participant-specific training protocols (e.g., guided rehearsal of unfamiliar movements) reduce EEG signal uncertainty and thereby improve model OOD detection capability?
4. Why do in-distribution EEG samples often produce higher activation magnitudes in penultimate layers than out-of-distribution samples, contrary to assumptions from vision domains, and how does this impact OOD detection methods like ReAct?
5. How do dataset-specific characteristics (number of channels, sampling frequency, trial length, sample size) causally influence OOD detection performance across methods, and can performance be normalized or adapted to these variations?

## Limitations
- EEG's high aleatoric noise severely bounds performance; median AUROC peaks at 0.737, with several methods near random (0.5). Modest gains may not ensure reliable real-world BCI operation.
- Findings are restricted to within-subject LOCO-OOD splits; cross-subject or session-shift OOD detection remains unexplored.
- EEGNeX architecture specifics (e.g., filter counts) and hyperparameter tuning for DUQ/d-KNN rely on subject-specific validation, risking overfitting and limiting reproducibility.

## Confidence
- **MC Dropout superiority:** Medium. Strong empirical advantage (p < 0.001) across datasets, but absolute AUROC remains modest; mechanism (Bayesian uncertainty) is inferred, not formally validated via aleatoric/epistemic decomposition.
- **Class‑dependent detectability:** High. Consistent, statistically significant differences across three datasets (e.g., “Rest” and “Both hands” easier than unilateral movements).
- **Correlation with classification accuracy:** High. Robust non‑linear correlation (Spearman ρ up to 0.830) observed, though potential confounders (shared feature quality) cannot be ruled out.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-subject OOD evaluation:** Train on multiple subjects within a dataset and test on held-out subjects (same class set) to verify whether MC Dropout’s advantage persists across subject variability.
2. **Feature-space density analysis:** Fit kernel density estimates on penultimate embeddings of ID training data; compute density scores for each OOD class and correlate with per-class AUROC to test the hypothesis that easier-to-detect OOD classes occupy lower-density regions.
3. **Aleatoric uncertainty separation:** Augment MC Dropout with a heteroscedastic output head to explicitly model data noise; re‑evaluate OOD detection to determine whether epistemic uncertainty alone drives performance.
