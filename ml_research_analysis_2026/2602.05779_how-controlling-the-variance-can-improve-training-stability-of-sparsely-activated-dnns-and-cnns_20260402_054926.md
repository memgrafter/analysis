# How Controlling the Variance can Improve Training Stability of Sparsely Activated DNNs and CNNs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05779
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05779
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Increasing the initialization variance q* (from 1 to 2–3) stabilizes training of deep sparsely activated networks at up to 90% sparsity, enabling near‑full accuracy (e.g., 91% vs 79% on MNIST at 85% sparsity).

## Executive Summary
Deep neural networks with sparsity‑inducing activations (e.g., CReLU, CST) become unstable when sparsity exceeds ~80–85%. This work shows that the Edge‑of‑Chaos initialization parameter, the Gaussian process variance q*, is critical for stability. Raising q* above the conventional value of 1 flattens the variance map V(q) and reduces the sensitivity of the perturbation factor χ1(q), which together improve gradient propagation. Experiments on 100‑layer fully connected networks (MNIST) and 50‑layer CNNs (CIFAR‑10) confirm that q* values of 2–3 yield stable training, faster convergence, and high accuracy even at 90% sparsity. For 85% sparsity, test accuracy jumps from 79% to 91% on MNIST when increasing q* from 1 to 3.

## Method Summary
The paper modifies the Edge‑of‑Chaos initialization by tuning the Gaussian process variance parameter q*. Typically set to 1, q* governs the variance of pre‑activations in the infinite‑width Gaussian process limit. For sparsely activated networks, the authors hypothesize that a larger q* (2 or 3) reduces the slope and curvature of the variance map V(q) and lowers the perturbation factor χ1(q), making the network more robust to the aggressive thresholding caused by sparsity. This change is applied only at initialization; training proceeds with standard optimizers (e.g., SGD) and no extra regularisation. The method is evaluated on deep fully connected (100 layers) and convolutional (50 layers) models using CReLU and CST activations on MNIST and CIFAR‑10.

## Key Results
- Increasing q* to 2–3 stabilizes training at sparsity levels up to 90%, while the traditional q*=1 often leads to divergence.
- For 85% sparsity on MNIST (100‑layer DNN), test accuracy improves from 79% (q*=1) to 91% (q*=3).
- Comparable gains are observed for CNNs on CIFAR‑10, demonstrating broad applicability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
A larger q* flattens the variance map V(q) (reducing its slope and curvature) and decreases the sensitivity of the perturbation factor χ1(q). These effects prevent gradient explosion or vanishing when activations are sparse, effectively preserving signal amplitude through many layers. The result is a more stable training dynamic that tolerates very high sparsity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Edge‑of‑Chaos Initialization**: *Why needed*: To avoid vanishing/exploding gradients by keeping activation variance constant across layers. *Quick check*: Does the variance of activations remain stable during forward propagation?
- **Variance Map V(q)**: *Why needed*: Captures how layer‑wise variance evolves; a flatter map indicates robustness to perturbations. *Quick check*: Is V(q) ≈ 1 for all q (i.e., depth‑invariant)?
- **Perturbation Factor χ1(q)**: *Why needed*: Measures sensitivity to small perturbations; high values indicate chaotic behaviour and gradient explosion. *Quick check*: Is χ1(q) below a critical threshold (e.g., 1) across the expected range of q?
- **Sparsity‑Inducing Activations (CReLU, CST)**: *Why needed*: Achieve computational efficiency by zeroing out a large fraction of activations. *Quick check*: What fraction of activations are zero under the target sparsity level?

## Architecture Onboarding

*DNN*: Input → Dense (Edge‑of‑Chaos init, variance q*) → CReLU → Dense → CReLU → ... (repeat 100 times) → Output  
*CNN*: Input → Conv2d (Edge‑of‑Chaos init) → BatchNorm → CReLU → Conv2d → BatchNorm → CReLU → ... (repeat 50 times) → Global average pooling → Dense → Output

**Critical path**: Weight initialization (setting q*) → Forward pass with sparse activations → Loss computation → Backpropagation. The interplay between q* and sparsity determines gradient stability.

**Design tradeoffs**: Higher q* improves stability and accuracy at extreme sparsity but may interact with learning rate; an overly large q* is not studied and could potentially cause other issues (e.g., over‑large initial activations). Depth amplifies both the benefits and risks.

**Failure signatures**: Loss rapidly becomes NaN, gradient norms explode, training diverges within a few epochs, or accuracy stalls at chance level.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis assumes infinite‑width networks and Gaussian activations; its validity for finite, practical architectures is unproven.
- Experiments are limited to MNIST and CIFAR‑10 with CReLU/CST; generalization to larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet) and other model families (e.g., Transformers) remains uncertain.
- The study only tests q* up to 3; optimal or saturation points beyond this are unexplored, and interactions with learning rate or batch size are not investigated.
