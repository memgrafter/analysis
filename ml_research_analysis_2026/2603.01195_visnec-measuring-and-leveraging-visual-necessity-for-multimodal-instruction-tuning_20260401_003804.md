# VisNec: Measuring and Leveraging Visual Necessity for Multimodal Instruction Tuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01195
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01195
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Using only 15% of LLaVA-665K, VisNec achieves 100.2% of full-data performance across 10 benchmarks, and on Vision-Flan-186K the same 15% subset outperforms full-data training by 15.8%.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal instruction tuning datasets often contain samples that are visually redundant or misaligned, resulting in inefficient use of compute and weakened visual reasoning. VisNec introduces a data selection method that quantifies the visual necessity of each training instance by comparing the model's predictive loss with and without the image. By discarding samples with non‑positive scores and using semantic clustering to maintain task diversity, VisNec selects a small yet highly effective subset. Experiments show that training on just 15% of LLaVA‑665K yields performance equivalent to (100.2%) full‑data training on average across 10 benchmarks, and on Vision‑Flan‑186K the same subset even surpasses full‑data results by 15.8%. The approach is computationally cheaper than prior selection methods and generalizes across model scales from 3B to 32B parameters.

## Method Summary
VisNec is a data selection framework for multimodal instruction tuning. Given a pre‑trained multimodal model and a large instruction dataset, it first computes a per‑sample Visual Necessity Score. This score is defined as the difference between the model's loss when predicting the response with the image present (\(L_{MM}\)) and with the image masked (\(L_{Blind}\)). A positive score indicates that the image reduces uncertainty, i.e., the sample is vision‑critical. The scoring requires two forward passes per sample, with the blind pass simulating a text‑only context. After scoring, VisNec removes all samples with \(S \leq 0\), filtering out redundant (text‑solvable) and misaligned (contradictory) instances. To preserve diversity across task types, the remaining instruction questions are embedded and clustered via K‑means. Within each cluster, the top‑\(r\)% samples by \(S\) are selected. This stratified selection ensures broad skill coverage while focusing on visually indispensable examples. The final subset is then used for fine‑tuning the multimodal model.

## Key Results
- On LLaVA‑665K, training with a VisNec‑selected 15% subset achieves 100.2% of the performance obtained with the full dataset across 10 benchmarks.
- On Vision‑Flan‑186K, the identical 15% selection outperforms full‑data training by 15.8%.
- The method maintains effectiveness across model scales (3B to 32B) and requires less computational overhead than previous data selection techniques.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The Visual Necessity Score effectively quantifies the marginal contribution of visual input, enabling identification of samples that truly require cross‑modal reasoning.
- **Mechanism**: Two forward passes per sample: a “blind” pass (text‑only, image tokens masked) computes loss \(L_{Blind}\); a standard multimodal pass computes loss \(L_{MM}\). The score \(S_{VisNec} = L_{Blind} - L_{MM}\) measures how much the image reduces predictive uncertainty. Positive scores indicate vision‑critical samples.
- **Core assumption**: The relative ordering of \(\Delta L\) remains valid despite the blind pass being out‑of‑distribution, because any systematic bias cancels out in intra‑cluster ranking.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] “By comparing predictive loss with and without visual context, VisNec identifies whether a training instance is vision‑critical, redundant, or misaligned.”
  - [section 3.1] “We define the VisNec score \(S_{VisNec}\) as the discrepancy between the blind loss and the multimodal loss… A larger \(\Delta L\) indicates higher visual necessity.”
  - Corpus evidence is indirect; neighboring papers (e.g., ScalSelect, COINCIDE) use different selection signals and do not measure visual necessity via counterfactual loss.
- **Break condition**: If the model’s text‑only predictions become extremely erratic (e.g., due to severe distribution shift), \(L_{Blind}\) may dominate and yield uninformative scores, undermining ranking reliability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Semantic clustering preserves task diversity, preventing VisNec’s ranking from over‑concentrating on specific high‑gain tasks and ensuring broad skill coverage.
- **Mechanism**: Instruction questions are embedded and clustered via K‑means. Within each cluster, samples with \(S_{VisNec} > 0\) are ranked, and the top‑\(r\)% are selected. This stratifies selection across task intents.
- **Core assumption**: Clustering by semantic intent groups samples with similar visual dependency distributions, and intra‑cluster top‑\(r\)% selection yields a balanced subset.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “To preserve task diversity, we combine VisNec with semantic clustering and select high‑necessity samples within each cluster.”
  - [section 3.2] “We perform K‑Means clustering… Within each cluster, we first remove samples with \(S_{VisNec} \leq 0\)… then rank by VisNec scores.”
  - Corpus: Other works (e.g., COINCIDE) use clustering for coverage, but VisNec uniquely couples it with a visual necessity signal; no direct corpus validation of this specific combination.
- **Break condition**: If clustering fails to separate distinct skills (e.g., due to poor embedding quality or overly coarse \(K\)), the selected subset may still lack diversity, leading to imbalanced capability profiles.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Filtering out samples with non‑positive VisNec removes redundant (text‑solvable) and misaligned (harmful) instances, improving training efficiency and robustness.
- **Mechanism**: Samples with \(S_{VisNec} \leq 0\) are discarded before clustering. Redundant samples (\(S_{VisNec} \approx 0\)) offer no visual supervision; misaligned samples (\(S_{VisNec} < 0\)) present contradictory image‑text pairs that degrade learning.
- **Core assumption**: Such samples provide minimal or negative value for multimodal instruction tuning, and their removal sharpens the learning signal.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.3] Case study: low VisNec examples show annotation errors or text‑only solvable questions; high VisNec examples require visual grounding.
  - [section 4.4] Ablation: Using only VisNec (with positive scores) achieves 100.2% Rel, outperforming text‑only (95.6%) and multimodal‑only (94.0%) selection.
  - Corpus: IFD (text‑only loss) is a baseline that may retain redundant samples; VisNec’s contrastive filtering addresses this limitation, though corpus does not provide direct evidence for the thresholding rule.
- **Break condition**: A strict zero threshold might discard borderline samples that become useful later (e.g., with more training) or where loss estimates are noisy, potentially harming completeness.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Loss Computation Module → Score Calculator → Filter → Semantic Clustering Module → Selector

- **Loss Computation Module**: Runs two forward passes (blind and multimodal) through the MLLM, computes token‑level loss on response tokens only (ignoring image positions).
- **Score Calculator**: Computes \(S_{VisNec} = L_{Blind} - L_{MM}\) per sample.
- **Filter**: Removes samples with \(S_{VisNec} \leq 0\).
- **Semantic Clustering Module**: Encodes instruction questions (e.g., with a text encoder or LLM) and applies K‑means to assign cluster labels.
- **Selector**: For each cluster, sorts remaining samples by \(S_{VisNec}\) and selects top \(r\)% (rounded to nearest integer).

### Critical Path
1. Load a pre‑trained MLLM (e.g., LLaVA‑v1.5) in evaluation mode.
2. For each sample \((image, instruction, response)\):
   - Prepare blind input: replace image tokens with pad tokens, set image attention masks to zero.
   - Compute \(L_{Blind}\) by averaging negative log‑likelihood over response tokens.
   - Compute \(L_{MM}\) via standard forward pass.
   - \(S_{VisNec} = L_{Blind} - L_{MM}\).
3. Filter: keep only samples with \(S_{VisNec} > 0\).
4. Extract question from instruction (strip system prompts), generate embeddings.
5. Run K‑means on embeddings to get cluster IDs.
6. For each cluster, sort samples by \(S_{VisNec}\) and take top \(r\)% (rounded to nearest integer).
7. Output selected subset (paths to images, instruction, response) for fine‑tuning.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Two forward passes**: Doubles scoring cost but ensures fair counterfactual comparison; could be mitigated by caching multimodal features.
- **Clustering features**: Using question‑only vs. full instruction; question‑only focuses on task intent, avoiding bias from system prompts.
- **Selection ratio \(r\)**: Smaller \(r\) increases efficiency but risks underfitting; paper finds 15% optimal on LLaVA‑665K, may vary by dataset.
- **Blind pass distribution shift**: Model never saw text‑only inputs during training; but since ranking is relative, absolute loss scale does not matter.

### Failure Signatures
- Selected subset underperforms random or full‑data baselines on key visual benchmarks (e.g., VQAv2, GQA).
- Cluster selection is highly imbalanced (e.g., one cluster contributes >40% of samples).
- Average \(S_{VisNec}\) of selected subset is near zero, indicating scoring may be flawed.
- Fine‑tuned model exhibits increased hallucination or poor performance on vision‑critical tasks.
- Scoring time is excessive due to inefficient batching or lack of mixed precision.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Does the VisNec score remain robust when the scoring model and fine‑tuning model are different architectures (e.g., scoring with LLaVA but fine‑tuning with Qwen‑VL)?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper tests cross‑scale transfer within the same model family but not cross‑architecture transfer. In Table 3, they evaluate Qwen2.5‑VL models where the scores are computed using the same Qwen model for selection and fine‑tuning. They do not explore using a different model for scoring.
- **Why unresolved**: This limitation affects the practical utility of VisNec; computing scores requires running the full model on the entire dataset, which may be prohibitive if one does not have the target model. A method to transfer scores across architectures would reduce computational burden and enable broader adoption.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An experiment where VisNec scores are computed on one architecture (e.g., LLaVA‑7B) and then used to select a subset for fine‑tuning a different architecture (e.g., Qwen‑VL‑7B), measuring the relative performance compared to scores computed on the target architecture.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How sensitive is VisNec to the choice of text encoder used for semantic clustering, and could alternative clustering strategies improve diversity and performance?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper states “we extract the question component \(q_i\) from each textual instruction \(t_i\) (filtering out system prompts) and map it into a semantic embedding space. We then perform K‑Means clustering”. It does not specify which embedding model is used, implying a generic choice. Ablation on the number of clusters (\(k\)) is provided, but not on the embedding method or clustering algorithm.
- **Why unresolved**: Different sentence embeddings capture different granularities of semantic similarity, potentially altering cluster composition and thus the diversity of the selected subset. This could limit reproducibility and generalizability across domains or languages.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments varying the embedding model (e.g., SBERT, CLIP text encoder, BERT) and possibly using other clustering techniques (hierarchical, DBSCAN) while keeping VisNec scoring unchanged, and evaluating the impact on benchmark performance and cluster balance.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does the out‑of‑distribution nature of the blind forward pass (masked image tokens) introduce biases that compromise the reliability of VisNec rankings for certain types of queries?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors acknowledge that “the blind input lies outside the MLLM’s training distribution, this does not undermine the validity of VisNec as a selection signal: since data selection operates via intra‑cluster ranking rather than absolute score thresholds, any systematic bias introduced by the out‑of‑distribution input cancels out in the relative ordering.” This is an assumption not fully justified with empirical analysis.
- **Why unresolved**: If the bias interacts with query type or image content non‑uniformly, relative rankings within a cluster could be distorted, leading to suboptimal selection. The claim that bias cancels out needs verification, especially for queries with high visual dependency or subtle details.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments where the blind forward pass is replaced with a synthetic distribution more similar to the training distribution (e.g., using a blurred image or a mean‑image context) and compare resulting rankings and downstream performance; or analyze correlation between blind loss and query characteristics (e.g., length, visual presence) to detect systematic bias.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can the computational overhead of calculating VisNec scores be reduced without sacrificing quality, for example through score approximation using a smaller proxy model or by early‑stopping the forward passes?
- **Basis in paper**: The cost analysis shows selection takes 12 GPU‑hours on LLaVA‑665K using a 7B model. While less than many baselines, this overhead may still be significant for massive datasets (millions of samples). The paper does not explore cost‑reduction techniques.
- **Why unresolved**: Lowering the cost would make VisNec practical for very large‑scale data curation and broaden its applicability. An efficient approximation could preserve the core benefit of identifying visually indispensable samples.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Propose an approximation (e.g., use a frozen vision encoder with a shallow LM head, or Monte Carlo sampling of tokens, or early‑stopping based on loss convergence) and evaluate by measuring correlation between approximate and full VisNec scores, and by comparing downstream performance when using the approximate scores for selection.

## Limitations
- Blind pass OOD shift: The blind forward pass uses masked image tokens, which is out‑of‑distribution; this can cause erratic \(L_{Blind}\) and potentially distort \(S_{VisNec}\) rankings.
- Zero threshold rigidity: Discarding all samples with \(S \leq 0\) may remove borderline cases that could become valuable with more training or different models.
- Clustering dependence: The diversity guarantee relies on quality of instruction embeddings and the choice of cluster count \(K\); poor choices may lead to imbalanced task coverage.

## Confidence
- **VisNec score ranking utility**: Medium confidence (empirical support but theoretical assumption unverified).
- **Clustering for diversity**: High confidence (ablations consistently show benefit).
- **Filtering non‑positive scores**: Medium confidence (case studies positive but threshold sensitivity unknown).
- **Overall efficiency claim (15% subset matches full performance)**: High confidence (validated across multiple benchmarks and model scales).

## Next Checks
1. Blind loss stability test: Evaluate \(S_{VisNec}\) rankings when scoring with models having varying exposure to text‑only inputs to assess robustness.
2. Threshold sensitivity analysis: Sweep \(S_{VisNec}\) cutoffs from 0 to higher percentiles and measure downstream performance to find optimal thresholds.
3. Cross‑dataset transfer: Compute VisNec scores on one dataset (e.g., LLaVA‑665K) and use them to select subsets for fine‑tuning on a different dataset (e.g., COCO Captions) to test generalizability.
