# Learning to Select: Query-Aware Adaptive Dimension Selection for Dense Retrieval
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03306
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03306
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** Learns a query-aware predictor to mask unhelpful embedding dimensions, yielding consistent NDCG@10 gains at 20–40% retention over full-dimensional baselines and PRF methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses dimensional redundancy in dense retrievers by learning a query-specific dimension selector. It introduces a method to distill oracle importance scores from supervised relevance labels by contrasting aggregated relevant and hard-negative document embeddings. A lightweight predictor is then trained to map query embeddings to these scores. At inference, the predictor identifies a subset of dimensions to retain in the query vector (masking the rest), while document embeddings remain unchanged. Experiments across multiple retrievers and datasets show that pruning 20–40% of dimensions improves retrieval effectiveness and efficiency, outperforming pseudo-relevance feedback and supervised adapter baselines.

## Method Summary
The approach comprises three stages. First, during training, oracle importance scores are constructed for each query. Given a query embedding, the centroid of relevant documents (weighted by normalized gains) and the mean of hard-negative document embeddings are computed. A raw discrimination score per dimension is the product of the query’s coordinate and the difference between positive and negative centroids. This is temperature-scaled and softmaxed to yield a target distribution π_q. Second, a single fully connected layer predictor takes the frozen query embedding and outputs logits, which are trained via KL divergence to match π_q. Third, at inference, the predictor produces an importance distribution; the top-k dimensions are retained in the query vector (others zeroed), and similarity (dot product) with unchanged document embeddings is computed for retrieval.

## Key Results
- Consistently improves NDCG@10 over full-dimensional baseline, PRF-based masking (DIME, Eclipse), and supervised adapter methods across seven model–dataset pairs.
- Performance typically peaks when retaining 20–40% of embedding dimensions, demonstrating effective removal of unhelpful dimensions.
- Outperforms baselines that apply global or heuristic test-time dimension selection, confirming the benefit of query-aware learned masking.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Contrasting aggregated positive and hard-negative document embeddings yields informative per-dimension importance scores for a query.
- **Mechanism:** For a query embedding **e_q**, compute centroid **p** of relevant documents and mean **n** of hard negatives. For each dimension *j*, compute discrimination score **r_q(j) = e_q,j (p_j - n_j)**, then apply temperature-scaled softmax to get distribution **π_q**. This assigns higher probability to dimensions where the query coordinate aligns strongly with the positive–negative difference.
- **Core assumption:** Dimensions that exhibit a large difference between positive and negative document embeddings, when multiplied by the query's own value, are predictive of relevance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...distills oracle importance scores from supervised relevance labels (by contrasting aggregated relevant and hard-negative document embeddings)"
  - [section 3.1] "For each dimension *j* ... compute a raw discrimination score as the positive–negative alignment difference on that coordinate, *rq(j) = e_q,j (p_j - n_j)*"
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (DIME, Eclipse) also use contrastive estimation between positives/negatives, but with pseudo-labels; this paper uses supervised labels.
- **Break condition:** If hard negatives are not truly non-relevant (e.g., due to poor retrieval), the negative centroid **n** may be uninformative, leading to noisy discrimination scores.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A lightweight predictor trained to map query embeddings to oracle importance distributions can generalize to unseen queries, enabling query-aware dimension selection without test-time feedback.
- **Mechanism:** A fully connected layer **f_θ** takes the frozen query embedding **e_q** and outputs logits **ℓ = f_θ(e_q)**. After log-softmax, predicted distribution **π̂_q** is obtained. Training minimizes **KL(π_q || π̂_q)** where **π_q** is the oracle distribution from Mechanism 1. At inference, the predictor produces importance scores for any query.
- **Core assumption:** The query embedding alone contains sufficient information to predict useful dimensions; the predictor can learn the mapping from embedding semantics to importance patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...learns a predictor to map query embeddings to these scores"
  - [section 3.2] "The predictor takes the frozen query embedding *eq* as input, and outputs logits *ℓ = f_θ(eq)*"
  - [corpus] Prior methods (DIME, Eclipse) rely on heuristic test-time estimation with pseudo-relevance feedback; this work replaces it with an offline trained predictor.
- **Break condition:** If training queries lack diversity or predictor capacity is insufficient, generalization to new queries may fail, resulting in misaligned importance scores.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Pruning a large fraction of embedding dimensions (20–40%) based on query-aware importance improves retrieval effectiveness by removing noise and focusing on salient features.
- **Mechanism:** At inference, select top‑*k* dimensions from predicted importance **π̂_q** and mask the rest in **e_q** (set to zero). Compute similarity (dot product) between masked query **e_q^{(k)}** and original document embeddings **e_d** only on those coordinates.
- **Core assumption:** Many dimensions in dense embeddings are irrelevant or detrimental for a given query; selecting a small, query-specific subset enhances signal-to-noise ratio.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...only a subset of dimensions is consistently helpful for ranking"
  - [section 5.2] "effectiveness reaches a pronounced peak and plateau when retaining roughly 20–40% of the dimensions"
  - [section 2] "DIME-style approaches ... improving retrieval effectiveness"
  - [corpus] Related work on Matryoshka and adapters targets efficiency via truncation, but here the primary goal is effectiveness through denoising.
- **Break condition:** If *k* is too small, critical information is lost; if too large, the pruning benefit diminishes. Also, inaccurate importance predictions can discard useful dimensions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Dual‑Encoder Dense Retrieval  
  *Why needed here:* The method assumes a frozen encoder that produces L2‑normalized query and document embeddings; masking operates on these vectors without re‑encoding documents.  
  *Quick check question:* Why is it safe to zero out dimensions in the query vector without re‑normalizing?

- **Concept:** Contrastive Signals for Dimension Discrimination  
  *Why needed here:* Oracle importance scores rely on the difference between positive and negative centroids to highlight dimensions that separate relevant from non‑relevant documents.  
  *Quick check question:* What property of *(p_j - n_j)* makes it a useful indicator of dimension importance?

- **Concept:** Knowledge Distillation via KL Divergence  
  *Why needed here:* The predictor is trained to mimic the oracle distribution **π_q**, which serves as a "teacher" derived from supervised labels.  
  *Quick check question:* Why use KL divergence instead of mean‑squared error between raw scores?

- **Concept:** Hard Negative Mining  
  *Why needed here:* The quality of the negative centroid **n** depends on sampling challenging non-relevant documents; this yields a stronger discrimination signal.  
  *Quick check question:* How might the choice of hard‑negative pool size *K* and in‑batch negatives *M* affect the stability of **π_q**?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Query Encoder (frozen) -> Dimension Predictor (FC + softmax) -> Masking Module (top-k selection) -> Similarity Scorer (dot product)  
  Oracle Constructor (training only) computes π_q from positives/negatives; Loss Module computes KL divergence.

- **Critical path (inference):**  
  Query -> Query Encoder -> Dimension Predictor -> Top‑k selection -> Masking -> Similarity -> Ranking.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Predictor capacity: a single linear layer is efficient but may limit expressiveness; deeper networks could help but increase latency.
  - Temperature **τ**: controls sharpness of **π_q**/ **π̂_q**; too low overfits to few dimensions, too high yields uniform distribution.
  - Retained ratio *k/D*: trade‑off between efficiency (smaller *k*) and effectiveness (too small loses information). Optimal range 20–40% per results.
  - Hard negative sampling: larger *K* and *M* improve oracle quality but increase compute; defaults (*K*=1000, *M*=64) balance cost and performance.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Predictor overfitting:** training KL low but test NDCG poor; remedy with stronger regularization (dropout, weight decay) or more training data.
  - **Poor generalization:** predictor outputs near‑uniform or identical importance across queries; check entropy of **π̂_q** and diversity of selected dimensions.
  - **Degradation at extreme *k*:** NDCG drops sharply when *k* < 10% or plateaus/declines when *k* > 80%.
  - **Inconsistent gains with strong base retrievers:** when an adapter already homogenizes dimension importance (Table 5), our method may provide marginal benefit.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Oracle ablation (Section 6.1):** Validate that adding positive weighting and hard negative mining improves the upper bound (oracle performance) on SciFact and NFCorpus.
  2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity (Section 6.2, Appendix A.1):** Sweep temperature **τ**, hard‑negative pool size *K*, in‑batch negatives *M*, and epochs; identify robust defaults.
  3. **Baseline comparison (Section 5.1):** Evaluate against Full, Cutoff, Norm, DIME‑PRF, Eclipse‑PRF, and Adapter across seven model‑dataset pairs; show consistent gains, especially at 20–40% retention.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can query-aware dimension selectors be trained without supervised relevance labels, particularly for low-resource domain-specific datasets?
- **Basis in paper:** The first limitation states the method "relies on supervised relevance signals to construct oracle dimension-importance scores." Section 6.4 further shows that LLM-generated pseudo-queries only improve performance on large web collections (MS MARCO) but yield marginal gains on SciFact and NFCorpus.
- **Why unresolved:** Pseudo-relevance feedback methods use noisy pseudo-labels, and supervised adapters learn global transformations. No effective label-free approach exists for small datasets.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fully unsupervised or weakly supervised training method that achieves comparable NDCG@10 improvements on SciFact/NFCorpus as the supervised predictor.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the dimension selector and dense encoder be jointly optimized to surpass the effectiveness ceiling of the base retriever?
- **Basis in paper:** The second limitation: "our method only operates at the level of per-query dimension selection over frozen embeddings and cannot improve the underlying encoder itself, so its overall effectiveness is inherently bounded by the quality of the base dense retriever."
- **Why unresolved:** The current pipeline freezes the encoder, restricting gains to the selector module only. Joint training could shape embeddings to be more amenable to query-aware masking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An end-to-end trained model where encoder parameters update alongside the selector, demonstrating higher NDCG@10 than the two-stage approach on the same encoder architecture.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do supervised search adapters reduce heterogeneity in selected dimensions on SciFact but not on MS MARCO?
- **Basis in paper:** Table 5 shows that adding an adapter increases Jaccard similarity of selected dimensions substantially on SciFact (e.g., Qwen-8B: 0.34→0.50) but only mildly on MS MARCO (0.37→0.39). The authors speculate this indicates the adapter makes the router's notion of important dimensions more homogeneous on SciFact.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding this could inform when adapters alone suffice versus when combined with query-aware selection, and may reveal properties of the embedding space that drive dimension importance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis linking adapter transformations (e.g., singular vectors) to dataset characteristics (size, topical diversity) or controlled experiments on synthetic data showing when adapters homogenize importance.

## Limitations
- Model checkpoints and predictor details (bias, dropout placement) are not specified, impeding exact replication.
- Hard negative mining for large corpora is ambiguous; approximations may diverge from the reported method.
- Positive centroid weighting (g_d = 2·y−1) lacks theoretical justification, especially for multi-level relevance labels.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (contrastive oracle scores): Medium – depends strongly on hard‑negative quality, which is not fully analyzed.
- **Mechanism 2** (predictor generalization): Medium – a simple linear layer may limit adaptability to diverse queries; overfitting risk is not ruled out.
- **Mechanism 3** (pruning improves retrieval): High – consistent NDCG@10 gains across 7 model–dataset pairs, with clear peak at 20–40% retention.

## Next Checks
1. **Oracle upper bound:** Apply true π_q masks (no predictor) on test queries; measure NDCG@10. This quantifies the maximum possible gain and confirms the core premise.
2. **Cross‑dataset transfer:** Train predictor on MS MARCO and evaluate directly on SciFact/NFCorpus without fine‑tuning. Success demonstrates robustness of the learned mapping.
3. **Hard‑negative sensitivity sweep:** Systematically vary hard‑negative pool size K and per‑query sample M; record NDCG@10 and rank correlation of resulting π_q distributions. This identifies breakpoints and justifies the chosen defaults.
