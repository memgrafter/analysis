# Auditing Fairness under Model Updates: Fundamental Complexity and Property-Preserving Updates
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05909
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05909
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Proposes a PAC auditing framework via an Empirical Property Optimization (EPO) oracle for fairness under model updates, introducing the SP dimension for weak auditability and the prospect ratio for strong auditability, with theoretical sample-complexity bounds and experimental validation on COMPAS and Student Performance datasets.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the problem of auditing group fairness (statistical parity) when a model owner can update a black‑box model to a new hypothesis class while preserving the audited property. The authors develop a Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) auditing framework centered on an Empirical Property Optimization (EPO) oracle: given a strategic class \(F\) and limited query access to the original model \(f^*\), the auditor obtains a labeled sample \(S\) and searches within \(F\) for a model whose empirical property value matches that of \(f^*\). Weak auditing finds a single such model; strong auditing estimates the proportion of all models in \(F\) that satisfy the property (the prospect ratio). For statistical parity, they introduce the SP dimension—a combinatorial measure of the capacity of \(F\) to realize different group‑wise label patterns. Finite‑class auditing scales as \(O((1/\varepsilon^2)\log(|F|/\delta))\); for infinite classes, weak auditability is possible iff the SP dimension is finite, with upper bounds \(O(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)\varepsilon^2)\max\{\log(2/\delta), 2\;SP(F)\log(32e/\varepsilon^2)\})\) and a matching lower bound \(\Omega(SP(F)/\varepsilon^2)\). Strong auditability adds logarithmic factors. Concentration guarantees are provided for prospect‑ratio estimation. Experiments on COMPAS and Student Performance data—using logistic regression as the black‑box and updating to multi‑layer perceptrons or random forests—demonstrate accurate property estimation and high coverage of the prospect class.

## Method Summary
The core method is the EPO oracle: the auditor draws an i.i.d. sample \(S\) from the black‑box model, ensuring sufficient examples from each protected group. For any candidate model \(h\in F\), the auditor computes the empirical property \(\hat{\mu}_S(h)\) (for statistical parity, the absolute difference in positive rates between groups). Then it solves \(\min_{h\in F} |\hat{\mu}_S(h)-\hat{\mu}_S(f^*)|\) using an empirical risk minimization (ERM) solver. The returned model \(\hat{h}\) is the weak audit result. For strong audit, the auditor also estimates the prospect ratio \(r(\varepsilon)=\Pr_{h\sim\nu}[|\mu_D(h)-\mu_D(f^*)|\leq \varepsilon]\) by sampling \(n\) models from a distribution \(\nu\) over \(F\) and counting how many satisfy \(|\hat{\mu}_S(h)-\hat{\mu}_S(\hat{h})|\leq \varepsilon\). Uniform convergence arguments link the empirical estimate to the true ratio, yielding sample‑complexity bounds that depend on the SP dimension (which counts distinct group‑trace patterns realizable by \(F\)). The framework is agnostic to the property; for statistical parity the SP dimension serves as a capacity measure analogous to VC dimension but tailored to group structure.

## Key Results
- Introduces a generic PAC auditing framework via an EPO oracle, reducing property‑preservation checks to ERM over the strategic class.
- Defines the SP dimension for statistical parity and shows it precisely characterizes weak auditability: finite SP dimension is necessary and sufficient for infinite classes, with sample complexity upper bound \(O(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)\varepsilon^2)\max\{\log(2/\delta), 2\,SP(F)\log(32e/\varepsilon^2)\})\) and lower bound \(\Omega(SP(F)/\varepsilon^2)\); for finite classes the bound becomes \(O((1/\varepsilon^2)\log(|F|/\delta))\).
- Develops the prospect ratio as a quantitative measure for strong auditability, proving concentration bounds that enable reliable estimation from finitely many models.
- Provides experimental evidence on two real‑world datasets (COMPAS, Student Performance) showing that the auditor can accurately estimate the black‑box’s statistical parity and, for common model families (MLP, random forest), achieve high prospect‑class coverage even with modest sample sizes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The EPO oracle reduces auditing to empirical risk minimization over the strategic class.
- **Mechanism**: The auditor obtains a labeled sample \(S\) from the black‑box model. It computes the empirical property \(\hat{\mu}_S(f)\) for the black‑box and then solves \(\min_{h\in F} |\hat{\mu}_S(h)-\hat{\mu}_S(f)|\) using an ERM oracle. The solution \(h^*\) is the weak audit prospect; for strong audit, the \(\varepsilon\)-ball around \(\hat{\mu}_S(f)\) in \(F\) defines the empirical prospect class.
- **Core assumption**: The property \(\mu\) can be estimated reliably from finite samples and the optimization over \(F\) is tractable (or approximately solvable).
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “We propose a generic framework for PAC auditing based on an Empirical Property Optimization (EPO) oracle.”; [section 3, Algorithm 1] explicit ERM on the empirical risk for \(\mu\); [corpus] the Active Fourier Auditor (Ajarra et al.) also uses empirical property estimation, supporting the EPO principle.
- **Break condition**: If uniform convergence of \(\hat{\mu}_S\) to \(\mu_D\) over \(F\) fails (e.g., infinite SP dimension) or ERM is computationally hard, the empirical optimum may not generalize.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The SP dimension characterizes the sample complexity of weak auditing for statistical parity and is easier than learning (SP‑dim ≤ VC‑dim).
- **Mechanism**: The SP dimension counts the number of distinct group‑wise labeling patterns (traces) that \(F\) can induce on a set split into protected groups. Sample‑complexity bounds are derived by covering the property class via uniform convergence; the combinatorial SP dimension governs the covering number. The lower bound shows necessity.
- **Core assumption**: The property is binary statistical parity; the analysis holds for any distribution on \(X\times Y\) without further assumptions.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 4.1] Definitions 4 (group‑traces) and 5 (SP dimension); Theorem 4 gives matching upper and lower bounds. Proposition 6 shows VC dimension ≥ SP dimension. [corpus] “Fluids You Can Trust” uses property‑preserving learning for incompressibility, illustrating the paradigm.
- **Break condition**: Infinite SP dimension (e.g., infinite VC by Proposition 9) makes weak audit impossible. The factor \(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha))\) inflates sample needs when one protected group is rare.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The prospect ratio, estimated via sampling, enables strong auditability for infinite classes by quantifying the proportion of models in \(F\) that preserve the property.
- **Mechanism**: The true prospect class is \(\{h\in F: |\mu_D(h)-\mu_D(f^*)|\leq \varepsilon\}\). Since \(F\) may be infinite, the auditor draws \(n\) models from a measure \(\nu\) over \(F\) and estimates the fraction satisfying \(|\hat{\mu}_S(h)-\hat{\mu}_S(f^*)|\leq \varepsilon\). Theorem 8 guarantees concentration of this estimator provided both the property estimates (with sample size \(m\)) and the number of sampled models \(n\) are large enough.
- **Core assumption**: The sampling distribution \(\nu\) gives positive mass to the prospect class (\(r(\varepsilon)>0\)). Empirical property estimates must be accurate uniformly over the sampled models.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 4.2] Definitions 6 (prospect class), 7 (prospect ratio), Theorem 8 (concentration). [section 5] Experiments on COMPAS and Student Performance show accurate prospect‑ratio estimation for MLP and random forest strategic classes. [corpus] “Quantitative Auditing of AI Fairness with DP Synthetic Data” estimates fairness via synthetic data, supporting property estimation under constraints.
- **Break condition**: Extremely small \(r(\varepsilon)\) requires huge \(n\) for reliable estimation. Large error in property estimation (small \(m\)) degrades concentration.

## Foundational Learning

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Inputs (strategic class \(F\), black‑box model \(f^*\), property \(\mu\), tolerance \(\varepsilon\), confidence \(\delta\), optional distribution \(\nu\)) → Sampler (produces labeled sample \(S\) with group splits) → Property evaluator (computes \(\hat{\mu}_S(h)\) for any \(h\)) and EPO optimizer (finds \(\hat{h} = \arg\min_{h\in F} |\hat{\mu}_S(h)-\hat{\mu}_S(f^*)|\). For strong audit, Model sampler draws \(n\) models from \(\nu\); Property evaluator evaluates each; Prospect ratio estimator aggregates the fraction satisfying \(|\hat{\mu}_S(h)-\hat{\mu}_S(\hat{h})|\leq \varepsilon\). Outputs: weak audit (\(\hat{h}\), \(\hat{\mu}_S(\hat{h})\)); strong audit (estimated prospect ratio).

### Critical path
1. Collect sample \(S\) from \(f^*\) with sufficient points from each protected group (\(\min(m_0,m_1)\geq O(1/\varepsilon^2\log(1/\delta))\)).
2. Compute empirical property \(\hat{\mu}_S(f^*)\).
3. Run EPO optimization over \(F\) to obtain \(\hat{h}\).
4. For strong audit: sample \(n\) models from \(\nu\), compute their empirical property values, count those within \(\varepsilon\) of \(\hat{\mu}_S(\hat{h})\), and average to estimate prospect ratio.
5. Return results.

### Design tradeoffs
- Weak audit requires a single optimization (lower computational cost). Strong audit adds the expense of sampling and evaluating many models.
- Choice of sampling distribution \(\nu\): uniform over parameters works if the prospect class is not extremely small; otherwise adaptive or importance sampling may be needed (not discussed).
- ERM optimization may be non‑convex (e.g., neural nets), potentially leading to suboptimal \(\hat{h}\); multiple random restarts are advisable.
- Tolerance \(\varepsilon\): larger \(\varepsilon\) enlarges the prospect class, making it easier to find/cover models but reduces the precision of the audit.

### Failure signatures
- EPO returns a model with high \(|\hat{\mu}_S(\hat{h})-\hat{\mu}_S(f^*)|\), suggesting \(F\) lacks models that preserve the property.
- Strong audit yields an estimated prospect ratio near zero or highly variable across runs, indicating either a tiny true ratio or a mismatched \(\nu\).
- On a held‑out test set, \(\mu(\hat{h})\) differs significantly from \(\mu(f^*)\), signaling overfitting due to insufficient samples \(S\) or poor uniform convergence.
- For infinite‑capacity classes (e.g., unbounded neural nets), the SP dimension may be infinite, making weak audit theoretically impossible; if error does not decrease with more data, the class may be too complex.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Finite‑class sanity check**: Use a small finite hypothesis class (enumerate all functions). Run weak and strong audit; compare outputs with exact ground truth to validate the EPO oracle and prospect‑ratio estimator.
2. **Linear models**: Set \(F\) to linear classifiers in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Vary the sample size \(m\) and observe the estimation error’s scaling as \(1/m\). Approximate the SP dimension by counting distinct group‑trace patterns on random subsets.
3. **Cross‑model update on COMPAS**: Train \(f^*\) via logistic regression. Define \(F\) as a multi‑layer perceptron (e.g., two hidden layers with 50 ReLU units). Perform weak audit to find an MLP with similar statistical parity, and strong audit to estimate the proportion of MLPs that preserve the parity. Repeat for varying MLP widths to see how the prospect ratio changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What are the sequential, property‑aware complexity measures that characterize the sample complexity of interactive auditing settings where queries are adaptive?
2. How can model architectures be designed to directly embed audited fairness properties so that updates automatically preserve these properties?
3. What combinatorial complexity measures (like the SP dimension for statistical parity) characterize auditability for other properties such as prediction error and robust risk?
4. How can the SP dimension be generalized to handle multiple protected attributes and intersecting groups, and what are the resulting auditability conditions?

## Limitations
- The experimental implementation details for the strategic classes (exact MLP architecture, hyperparameters; exact random forest settings) are omitted, hindering faithful replication.
- The sampling distribution \(\nu\) used for strong auditing is unspecified; results may depend on the choice of \(\nu\) and could be biased.
- The analysis assumes i.i.d. queries and fixed protected groups; it does not consider distribution shifts or the complexity of multi‑group fairness.
- Theoretical guarantees for infinite‑VC classes are negative (non‑auditable); practical model families may still have high capacity that negatively impacts sample complexity.
