# Learning Self-Interpretation from Interpretability Artifacts: Training Lightweight Adapters on Vector-Label Pairs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10352
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10352
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Scalar affine adapters boost SAE feature labeling to 71% (from 63% and 49% baselines) and contrastive topic identification to 94% recall@1, with the bias vector alone accounting for ~85% of the improvement.

## Executive Summary
Self-interpretation methods, where language models describe their own internal states, often suffer from hyperparameter sensitivity and unreliability. This work addresses that issue by freezing the language model and training a lightweight adapter on interpretability artifacts—pairs of activation vectors and human-provided labels (e.g., from sparse autoencoders or contrastive activation vectors). The adapter, a scalar affine transformation with only $d_{\text{model}}+1$ parameters, learns to map activation directions to natural language descriptions. At the 70B scale, the method achieves a 71% generation scoring on SAE feature labeling, outperforming the original label baseline (63%) and an untrained baseline (49%). On contrastive vector topic identification, it reaches 94% recall@1 compared to 1% for the untrained baseline. The bias term alone contributes approximately 85% of the performance gain, and simpler adapters generalize better than full-rank counterparts. Performance improves with model scale, narrowing the gap to a Taboo knowledge baseline, and the approach also decodes implicit bridge entities in multi-hop reasoning without chain-of-thought prompting.

## Method Summary
The authors collect two types of interpretability artifacts: (1) feature vectors from sparse autoencoders (SAE) paired with human-written descriptions, and (2) contrastive activation vectors paired with topic labels. They freeze a pretrained language model and attach a scalar affine adapter that computes $s = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$, where $\mathbf{x}$ is an activation vector and $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$, $b \in \mathbb{R}$ are the only trainable parameters. The adapter’s scalar output $s$ is used to create a continuous prompt (by scaling a fixed embedding), which is injected into the language model’s input to generate a natural language interpretation. Training minimizes a standard cross-entropy loss between the generated text and the target label, while keeping the base model unchanged. This decouples interpretation from model updates and drastically reduces the number of task-specific parameters.

## Key Results
- Scalar affine adapter achieves **71%** generation scoring on SAE feature labeling (70B), surpassing original label baselines (**63%**) and untrained (**49%**).
- On contrastive vector topic identification, adapter reaches **94%** recall@1 vs **1%** for untrained.
- **Bias vector alone** accounts for ~85% of the improvement.
- **Simpler adapters** (lower rank) generalize better on SAE data; full-rank adapters overfit but succeed on contrastive vectors.
- Self-interpretation performance improves with model scale (7B → 72B), narrowing the gap to a Taboo knowledge baseline.
- The method decodes implicit bridge entities in multi-hop reasoning without chain-of‑thought prompting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The adapter works because the frozen model’s activation space already encodes linearly separable information about the targeted concepts. A simple linear probe (the scalar affine transformation) can extract this signal. The dominance of the bias term suggests that the activation vectors themselves carry rich prior information about label distributions; the weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ provides task‑specific adjustments. The extreme parameter efficiency prevents overfitting to noise in the artifacts, leading to more robust interpretations. As model scale increases, representations become richer and better aligned with human‑understandable concepts, further boosting adapter accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
**Self‑interpretation** – *Why needed:* Enables models to explain their internal states without external supervision. *Quick check:* Can the model produce coherent descriptions of its own neuron activations given a probe?

**Sparse Autoencoders (SAE)** – *Why needed:* Provide feature vectors and interpretable descriptions that serve as training artifacts. *Quick check:* Do SAE features capture disentangled, reusable concepts aligned with human semantics?

**Contrastive Activation Vectors** – *Why needed:* Offer a different modality of interpretability signals, focusing on discriminative directions between classes. *Quick check:* Are vectors from contrastive methods linearly separable for known topics?

**Scalar Affine Adapter** – *Why needed:* Extremely parameter‑efficient mapping from high‑dimensional activations to a scalar used for interpretation generation. *Quick check:* Does a randomized adapter perform at chance while a trained one shows clear improvement?

**Overfitting vs Generalization** – *Why needed:* Determines the optimal adapter capacity for different artifact types. *Quick check:* Does increasing adapter rank consistently improve validation performance, or does it eventually degrade?

**Model Scale Effects** – *Why needed:* Larger models may have more structured representations, affecting adapter performance. *Quick check:* How does adapter accuracy change when moving from 7B to 72B parameter models?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input text → Frozen LM → Activation vector $\mathbf{x}$ → Scalar affine adapter ($s = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$) → Scaled fixed prompt ($p = s \cdot \mathbf{e}_{\text{fixed}}$) → Frozen LM (generates interpretation) → Output description

**Critical path:**  
The activation vector is extracted from the frozen LM, transformed into a scalar, scaled to form a continuous prompt token, and reinjected into the LM which decodes the final natural language interpretation. Training updates only $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$, preserving the base model.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Adapter rank: scalar (most efficient) vs. full‑rank (higher capacity, risk of overfitting); found scalar sufficient on SAE, full‑rank helped on contrastive data.  
- Fixed prompt vs. learnable: using a fixed embedding for scaling drastically reduces parameters; a learnable prompt would add cost but may improve performance.  
- Dataset size: Small artifacts may limit adapter generalization; scaling laws show benefits from larger training sets.  
- Model scale: Larger base models improve adapter accuracy but increase computational overhead for activation extraction and generation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Collapse to a single frequent description (high bias, low variance in outputs).  
- Sharp performance drop on out‑of‑distribution concepts not present in training artifacts.  
- Divergence during training when learning rate is too high (scalar amplification effect).  
- Weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ shrinks to near zero, indicating the model relies solely on bias and may lack specificity.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Untrained baseline:** Evaluate an adapter with randomly initialized $\mathbf{w},b$ to establish chance performance.  
2. **Ablation of weight vector:** Train with $\mathbf{w}=0$ (bias‑only) and compare to full scalar adapter; also train a full‑rank adapter to assess overfitting on SAE vs. contrastive data.  
3. **Data scaling:** Train adapters on increasing fractions of the artifact dataset (e.g., 10%, 50%, 100%) to measure data efficiency and learning curves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can adopting a Q&A framing with diverse training objectives (e.g., self‑supervised context prediction) substantially improve the capabilities and generalization of lightweight self‑interpretation adapters?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Discussion: "Adopting the Q&A framing and diverse training objectives from concurrent work... could substantially expand our adapters’ capabilities while preserving their simplicity."  
**Why unresolved:** The current approach uses a single "what is the meaning of" template; the potential benefits of diverse objectives remain untested.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train adapters on multiple question types or self‑supervised tasks, evaluate across a suite of interpretation benchmarks (e.g., SAE labeling, topic retrieval, bridge entity detection), and compare performance and robustness to the single‑template baseline.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can lightweight adapters be effectively extended to auditing applications, such as recovering hidden objectives or detecting behavioral changes from fine‑tuning, with performance comparable to fine‑tuning‑based methods?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors propose in the Discussion: "The auditing applications these methods demonstrate (recovering hidden objectives, detecting behavioral changes from fine‑tuning) represent compelling targets that lightweight adapters might address with appropriate extensions."  
**Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on interpretation tasks; auditing applications are suggested but not implemented or evaluated.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement adapters for specific auditing tasks (e.g., extract fine‑tuned secrets, identify jailbreak triggers), benchmark against fine‑tuning‑based interpretability methods, and measure accuracy, efficiency, and faithfulness to the original model’s representations.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can reinforcement learning from internal rewards (RL from internal rewards) optimize models to produce verifiable and accurate self‑reports, reducing confabulation in self‑interpretation?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors introduce in the Discussion: "We call this direction RL from internal rewards: optimizing models to accurately report their own computations, using the same paradigm now driving capability gains."  
**Why unresolved:** This is a conceptual proposal without empirical validation; it is unknown if RL can enhance verifiability or if internal rewards provide a stable learning signal.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Develop an RL framework where self‑interpretation accuracy (e.g., via generation scoring or external verification) serves as reward, train adapters or models, and assess improvements in truthfulness, consistency across scales, and resistance to generating plausible but ungrounded descriptions.

## Limitations
- Generalizability beyond SAE and contrastive activation vectors remains untested; performance on other artifact types (e.g., causal mediation vectors, concept activation vectors from different frameworks) is unknown.
- Robustness of adapter training (sensitivity to learning rate, dataset curation, random seeds) is not fully explored; the method may introduce new hyperparameter dependencies.
- The theoretical reason why the bias vector alone captures ~85% of the improvement is not explained; it may be dataset‑specific rather than fundamental.
- The significance of approaching the Taboo knowledge baseline with scale is unclear; the Taboo baseline itself is a non‑standard reference.
- The claim of decoding implicit bridge entities in multi‑hop reasoning lacks controlled ablations (e.g., prompt‑only or random‑adapter baselines) to isolate the adapter’s contribution.

## Confidence
- **High:** Empirical performance numbers on held‑out SAE labeling and contrastive topic ID tasks (directly reported results).
- **Medium:** Claim that "simpler adapters generalize better" (observed pattern but causal reason not established).
- **Low:** Generalizability to new interpretability artifact types; scalability claims beyond 72B; broad assertion that the method reliably "improves self‑interpretation"; conclusion from multi‑hop decoding experiment without rigorous ablation.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑artifact generalization test:** Train and evaluate the identical scalar adapter on at least two different interpretability artifact sources not used in the paper (e.g., feature descriptions from a different SAE regime, causal mediation vectors). Measure relative improvement over an untrained baseline.
2. **Hyperparameter robustness sweep:** For the best‑performing adapter configuration, perform a thorough sensitivity analysis on key training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, dataset size). Report performance variance and compare to the original self‑interpretation method’s reported hyperparameter sensitivity.
3. **Ablated multi‑hop evaluation:** Re‑run the multi‑hop reasoning experiment under three conditions: (a) original prompt + adapter, (b) original prompt only, (c) prompt with a randomly initialized adapter. Measure bridge entity accuracy to isolate the adapter’s specific contribution from the frozen model’s inherent reasoning capability.
