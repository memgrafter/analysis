# Understanding Image2Video Domain Shift in Food Segmentation: An Instance-level Analysis on Apples

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08491  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08491  
- **Reference count:** 19  
- **Primary result:** Food segmentation models maintain high per-frame mIoU (~73–74%) on video but suffer severe temporal instability (ID switches 27–34, track fragmentation 16–21), causing counting errors and revealing that image-centric metrics grossly overestimate video reliability.

## Executive Summary
This paper uncovers a critical domain shift when applying image-trained food segmentation models to video. Although frame-wise accuracy remains high, instance-level tracking metrics expose substantial temporal inconsistency: masks flicker, identities fragment, and ID switches proliferate due to illumination changes and texture ambiguities. Consequently, apple counting errors are significant (e.g., true count 12 predicted as 15–18). Standard image evaluation masks these failures. Post-hoc temporal smoothing and self-supervised consistency losses only partially mitigate the problem, pointing to image-centric training objectives—not model capacity—as the root cause of the video deployment gap.

## Method Summary
The study trains modern segmentation models (CNN, Transformer, foundation-based) on static food segmentation datasets (FoodSeg103, UECFoodPix). At inference, frames from YouTube videos (apples in motion, varying lighting) are processed to produce per-frame instance masks. A tracking-by-matching module (IoU-based greedy association) links masks across consecutive frames, yielding temporal metrics (ID switches, track fragmentation). Two remedies are evaluated: (1) post-hoc temporal smoothing (e.g., median filtering of mask sequences) and (2) self-supervised temporal consistency loss added during fine-tuning on unlabeled video. The pipeline isolates why image-trained models fail in video by contrasting frame-wise mIoU with tracking stability.

## Key Results
- Frame-wise mIoU on video remains comparable to image test performance (~73–74% vs. ~74%), giving a false sense of reliability.
- Tracking metrics reveal high instability: ID switches range 27–34 and track fragmentation 16–21 per video across all models.
- Counting errors directly track fragmentation; e.g., a true count of 12 apples becomes 15–18 estimated.
- Post-hoc smoothing reduces ID switches (e.g., 29→14) and fragmentation but does not eliminate them; self-supervised consistency yields similar partial gains.
- The persistent gap between image and video performance demonstrates that conventional segmentation metrics drastically overestimate real-world video deployment quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** High frame-wise segmentation accuracy (mIoU) does not ensure temporal instance consistency in video.
- **Mechanism:** Image-trained models optimize for spatial appearance cues per frame without learning to associate the same physical object across time. When video introduces appearance variations (illumination, reflection, motion blur), the per-frame instance embeddings or mask predictions become unstable, causing the tracking-by-matching step (using IoU) to fail in associating masks of the same object across frames. This results in mask flickering and identity fragmentation.
- **Core assumption:** The association score (simple IoU) is sensitive to small geometric and appearance shifts that are tolerated by human vision but destabilize pixel-level matches.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Abstract: "high frame-wise segmentation accuracy does not translate to stable instance identities over time"  
  Section 3.2: association score `S(mi_t, mj_t+1) = IoU(mi_t, mj_t+1)`  
  Section 5.5: "Despite maintaining high frame-wise mIoU on individual video frames, all models exhibit substantial temporal instability."
- **Break condition:** If videos contain no significant appearance variation (static scene, constant lighting), the IoU-based tracking may remain stable even with an image-trained model.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Post-hoc temporal smoothing and self-supervised consistency objectives can partially mitigate temporal instability but do not fully close the video deployment gap.
- **Mechanism:** Temporal smoothing (e.g., short-window mask filtering) reduces high-frequency flickering by enforcing local smoothness. Self-supervised temporal consistency losses encourage frame-to-frame prediction similarity. These act as regularizers on the output space, compensating for missing temporal coherence in the training objective. However, they are applied after the core segmentation model is frozen or trained with a weak auxiliary loss, so they cannot fully relearn robust spatio-temporal representations.
- **Core assumption:** Instability primarily manifests as high-frequency noise and inconsistent assignments rather than fundamental mis-segmentation of object parts, so output-space regularization can help.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Section 3.5: "post-hoc temporal regularization and self-supervised temporal consistency objectives"  
  Section 5.7: "both reduce identity switches and track fragmentation. However, these improvements remain partial and do not fully close the gap"  
  Table 4: improvement from 29→14 ID switches but not to zero.
- **Break condition:** If the image-trained model's per-frame errors are severe (e.g., systematic mis-segmentation of whole objects), output-space smoothing may degrade performance by propagating errors.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The root cause of failure is image-centric training objectives that ignore temporal coherence, not insufficient model capacity.
- **Mechanism:** Standard segmentation losses (per-pixel cross-entropy/Dice) treat each frame independently. The learned features and decision boundaries are optimized for static appearance statistics, not for the temporal manifold where an object's appearance evolves smoothly. This creates a representation that is brittle to the natural variations within a video sequence, even though the same model may generalize well across *different static images*.
- **Core assumption:** The model capacity is sufficient to learn temporal coherence if the training objective explicitly requires it; the deficiency is in the objective function, not the architecture's expressive power.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Section 1: "the root cause of failure lies in image-centric training objectives that ignore temporal coherence, rather than model capacity"  
  Section 5.7: "These findings suggest that the root cause of failure lies in image-centric training objectives that neglect temporal coherence, rather than insufficient model capacity"  
  Section 2.2: contrasts with VIS methods that incorporate temporal modules, implying architecture could help but isn't the primary bottleneck here.
- **Break condition:** If video data with temporal annotations were used during training, the same model architectures would likely achieve temporal stability, contradicting the capacity claim.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Instance Segmentation & Tracking-by-Matching**  
  Why needed here: The paper reformulates video segmentation as an instance tracking problem to expose temporal failures invisible to semantic segmentation metrics. Engineers must understand how to convert per-frame masks into object tracks (e.g., via IoU matching) and the associated failure modes (ID switches, fragmentation).  
  Quick check: How would you detect if a single apple in a video is being tracked as multiple identities using only mask outputs per frame?

- **Concept: Temporal Consistency Metrics (ID Switches, Track Fragmentation)**  
  Why needed here: Standard mIoU is frame-wise and blind to identity preservation. These metrics, adapted from multi-object tracking, are the evidence for the paper's core claim. Engineers must know how they are computed (e.g., number of times a track's identity changes).  
  Quick check: What is the difference between an ID switch and track fragmentation in terms of what they measure about temporal stability?

- **Concept: Domain Shift (Image-to-Video)**  
  Why needed here: The core problem is a distribution shift not from one image dataset to another, but from static images to temporal video streams. Engineers need to reason about covariate shift introduced by motion, illumination change, and frame dependency.  
  Quick check: Why might a model generalize well across different food image datasets but fail on a simple video of the same food category?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Base Segmentation Model -> Tracking-by-Matching Module -> (Optional) Temporal Remedy  
- **Critical path:** Training (images only) → Inference on video frames → Extract masks per frame → Apply tracking-by-matching → Compute temporal metrics (ID switches, fragments) → (Optional) Apply temporal remedy → Re-evaluate.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Simplicity vs. Performance:* The IoU-based tracking is simple and isolates segmentation model failures but is brittle to appearance change. More sophisticated trackers (embedding-based) might be more robust but confound analysis.  
  - *Remedy Scope:* Post-hoc smoothing is non-invasive but only masks high-frequency noise. Self-supervised consistency requires modifying training and may affect frame-wise accuracy.  
  - *Evaluation Choice:* Relying solely on mIoU hides failures; must include tracking metrics for deployment assessment.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High frame-wise mIoU (>70%) coupled with high ID switches (>25) and track fragmentation (>15).  
  - Counting error proportional to fragmentation (e.g., true count 12, predicted 15-18).  
  - Failure correlates with video segments containing illumination changes or specular highlights on surfaces (apples).  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Image vs. Video mIoU:** Quantify the drop (or lack thereof) in per-frame mIoU when moving from image test set to video frames. Expect minimal drop, hiding the problem.  
  2. **Tracking Metric Breakdown:** Run the tracking-by-matching pipeline on video outputs. Report ID switches and track fragmentation for each model. Expect all models to show high values.  
  3. **Remedy Efficacy:** Apply post-hoc smoothing and self-supervised consistency separately. Measure reduction in ID switches/fragments and any change in frame mIoU. Expect partial improvement without full gap closure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can temporal coherence be integrated into food segmentation training using only static image annotations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors conclude that image-centric objectives ignore temporal coherence, and self-supervised consistency objectives are incomplete fixes.
- **Why unresolved:** Current approaches do not achieve full temporal stability, indicating a need for novel training paradigms.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduction and empirical validation of temporal-aware loss functions on video benchmarks, demonstrating significant improvements in tracking metrics.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What evaluation metrics are necessary for video food segmentation to predict real-world reliability, especially for instance counting?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Frame-wise mIoU is shown to overestimate video performance, and tracking-based metrics are proposed but not standardized.
- **Why unresolved:** Lack of consensus on video-centric evaluation; current image-based benchmarks are inadequate.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlative studies between temporal metrics and counting errors across diverse videos, leading to adopted evaluation standards.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the temporal instability patterns (e.g., due to illumination) generalize to food categories beyond apples?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study focuses solely on apples, assuming they are representative, but generalizability is untested.
- **Why unresolved:** Different foods may have distinct visual characteristics affecting temporal robustness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extension of the analysis to multiple food categories in video, comparing failure modes to identify common or category-specific issues.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can post-hoc temporal regularization be made fully effective for video food segmentation, and what are its inherent limitations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Post-hoc smoothing reduces but does not eliminate ID switches and fragmentation (Table 4).
- **Why unresolved:** Simple smoothing may not capture complex temporal dynamics; integration might be needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Advanced post-hoc methods, such as adaptive temporal filters, evaluated for complete mitigation of temporal failures without frame accuracy loss.

## Limitations
- **Video data specifics:** Exact YouTube sources, resolution, frame rate, and preprocessing steps are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- **Tracking parameters:** IoU matching threshold, association window, and handling of new/disappearing instances are not detailed; results may be sensitive to these choices.
- **Generalizability:** Study limited to apples; other food categories with different textures or reflectance properties might exhibit different failure modes.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (High frame-wise mIoU ≠ temporal consistency):** High  
- **Mechanism 2 (Remedies partially help):** High  
- **Mechanism 3 (Root cause: image-centric training, not capacity):** Medium

2. **Cross-category generalization:** Collect 2–3 videos each for two other food categories (e.g., oranges, bananas) with similar motion and lighting variations. Apply the same pipeline to verify whether the frame mIoU vs. temporal stability discrepancy holds broadly.
