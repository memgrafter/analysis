# Diagnosing Non-Markovian Observations in Reinforcement Learning via Prediction-Based Violation Scoring
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.27389
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27389
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: A prediction-based Markov violation score (MVS) can diagnose non-Markovian observations in RL without requiring causal graphs.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning (RL) typically assumes that observations satisfy the Markov property—the future is independent of the past given the present. In practice, real-world sensors often introduce correlated noise, latency, or partial observability that violates this assumption. This paper introduces the Markov Violation Score (MVS), a two-stage method for detecting such non-Markovianity. The approach first fits a flexible random-forest model to predict the next observation from the current state and action; it then uses ridge regression to test whether past observations can explain the residuals of this prediction. If historical observations provide additional predictive power, the current state-action representation is insufficient, indicating a violation. The authors evaluate MVS on six classic control benchmarks (CartPole, Pendulum, Acrobot, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d) with three RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, SAC) under synthetic AR(1) noise at six intensities and ten seeds. Results show mixed performance: in high-dimensional locomotion tasks, 7 out of 16 environment-algorithm pairs exhibit a significant positive monotonic relationship between noise intensity and MVS (maximum Spearman ρ = 0.78). Conversely, in low-dimensional tasks (CartPole, Pendulum, Acrobot), the score inversely correlates with noise, suggesting that the random forest can absorb the violation pattern. A targeted experiment on CartPole with masked velocities confirms that MVS correctly identifies the non-Markovian condition (0.42 vs. 0.000) and that augmenting the policy with a history of observations recovers near-optimal performance (from 43.1 to 500). The study demonstrates both the promise and the pitfalls of a purely predictive diagnostic for non-Markovianity.

## Method Summary
The proposed MVS pipeline consists of two stages applied to a trajectory of observations, actions, and (optionally) states. Stage 1 uses a random forest regressor to learn the mapping from the current state-action pair (s_t, a_t) to the next observation o_{t+1}. The prediction residuals r_t = o_{t+1} - \hat{o}_{t+1} capture any aspects of the next observation not explained by the current state-action. Stage 2 trains a ridge regression model to predict these residuals from a history of past observations (e.g., o_{t-h: t}). The improvement in predictive performance—quantified via the coefficient of determination (R^2) on held-out data and bounded between 0 and 1—forms the Markov Violation Score. A higher MVS indicates that past observations contain information about the future beyond what is already captured by the present state-action, thus diagnosing non-Markovianity. The method requires no causal graph and can be applied post-hoc to logged RL trajectories.

## Key Results
- **Detection monotonicity in high-dimensional locomotion**: 7 of 16 environment-algorithm pairs (mainly HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d) show a significant positive monotonic relationship between AR(1) noise intensity and MVS (Spearman ρ ≤ 0.78), confirming that the score scales with violation severity.
- **Inversion in low-dimensional tasks**: In CartPole, Pendulum, and Acrobot, MVS decreases as noise intensity increases, revealing that the random forest can absorb the violation pattern, scrubbing the residuals of the signal.
- **Utility in a controlled non-Markovian setting**: On CartPole with velocities masked (a known non-Markovian observation), MVS was 0.42 while a Markovian baseline scored 0.000. Switching to an LSTM policy that incorporates observation history restored the expected return from 43.1 to 500.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core intuition is that if observations are truly Markovian, the optimal predictor of the next observation given the current state and action should leave residuals that are conditionally independent of the past. Any remaining temporal structure in the residuals that is predictable from past observations implies that the current state-action representation does not contain all state information required for predicting the future—a hallmark of non-Markovianity. The random forest in Stage 1 acts as a powerful, non-linear function approximator that attempts to capture as much of the dynamics as possible. The subsequent ridge regression on residual history provides a linear, regularized test for leftover predictive information. By assessing the proportion of residual variance explained by the past (bounded between 0 and 1), the MVS yields a scalar measure of violation strength that is comparable across tasks and does not rely on known causal relationships.

## Foundational Learning
- **Markov property**  
  Why needed: The detection target is a deviation from the standard RL assumption that given the current state (or observation), the future is independent of the past.  
  Quick check: Formally define the Markov condition for an MDP transition as P(s'|s,a) independent of history.

- **Residual analysis**  
  Why needed: After Stage 1, residuals should contain only noise if the model is perfect. If non-Markovian structure remains, it may be detectable in residuals via past observations.  
  Quick check: Given a predictor f and target y, what does the residual series represent? Under classical assumptions, what must be true for residuals to be i.i.d.?

- **Random forest**  
  Why needed: Provides a flexible, non-parametric model for the potentially complex dynamics mapping (s,a) -> o', without requiring feature engineering.  
  Quick check: How does the ensemble approach of random forests reduce variance relative to a single decision tree?

- **Ridge regression**  
  Why needed: Used in Stage 2 to linearly relate residual history while avoiding overfitting through L2 regularization.  
  Quick check: How does the ridge penalty affect the magnitude of coefficients and the bias-variance tradeoff?

- **Non-Markovian observations**  
  Why needed: The method aims to detect real-world sensor imperfections such as colored noise, latency, or missing dimensions that break the Markov assumption.  
  Quick check: Give an example of an observation process (e.g., o_t = s_t + ε_t with ε_t correlated over time) that violates the Markov property.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Trajectory Data → Random Forest (Stage 1) → Residuals → Ridge Regression (Stage 2) → MVS

**Critical path**  
1. Collect trajectories (observations, actions, states) from an RL agent.  
2. Train random forest to predict o_{t+1} from (s_t, a_t).  
3. Compute residuals r_t on held-out data.  
4. Aggregate historical observations (e.g., concatenate o_{t-h}, ..., o_t).  
5. Train ridge regression to predict r_t from historical observations.  
6. Compute R^2 of ridge on test residuals; clip to [0,1] to obtain MVS.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Random forest flexibility*: High capacity can fit true dynamics but may overfit in low-dimensional settings, absorbing non-Markovian signals and causing inversion.  
- *Linear second stage*: Simpler and robust to overfitting, but may miss non-linear residual structures.  
- *History window size*: Too short may omit relevant past; too long increases dimensionality and collinearity, weakening test.  
- *No causal graph*: Makes the method broadly applicable but sacrifices interpretability about which past variables are responsible.

**Failure signatures**  
- Inversion: MVS decreases as known violation intensity increases (observed in low-dimensional tasks).  
- Near-zero MVS despite obvious non-Markovianity (e.g., masked state dimensions) suggests Stage 1 already captured the violation.  
- High variance of MVS across random seeds indicates instability, possibly due to insufficient data or overfitting.

**First experiments**  
1. **CartPole velocity-masking replication**: Generate two sets of trajectories—one with full state (Markovian) and one with velocity components masked (non-Markovian). Compute MVS for both; expect near-zero for Markovian and elevated score for masked. Then train an LSTM policy on the masked environment and show that using history recovers high returns (≈500).  
2. **Random forest capacity sweep**: On CartPole with AR(1) noise, vary max_depth (e.g., 2, 5, 10, None) and n_estimators (10, 100, 500). For each, compute MVS vs. noise intensity. Observe whether limiting capacity mitigates inversion.  
3. **Stage1 model comparison**: Replace random forest with a linear regression in Stage 1 for both low-D and high-D tasks. Check whether monotonicity improves in low-D and whether detection power in high-D diminishes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the two-stage scoring pipeline be adjusted to prevent signal absorption in low-dimensional environments where random forests fit the violation pattern? (Section 5.3 identifies inversion root cause; Section 6 suggests mitigations like capacity limits, linear first stage, ensembles, but no empirical validation.)  
2. Does the prediction-based score generalize to non-AR(1) non-Markovian observation structures such as sensor latency, frame stacking, or partial observability beyond autoregressive noise? (Section 6 explicitly states AR(1) was the sole violation type tested and calls generalization an open question.)  
3. What are the statistical properties (Type I error, power) of the violation score under the Markov null and various non-Markov alternatives? (Section 6 notes lack of theoretical guarantees, making it hard to set thresholds or assess reliability.)

## Limitations
- Mixed performance: Only 7/16 high-dimensional pairs show positive monotonicity; inversion occurs in low-dimensional tasks, likely due to random-forest overfitting; no hyperparameter analysis confirms this.  
- Narrow evaluation: Experiments restricted to six benchmarks, three RL algorithms, and AR(1) noise; generalizability to image domains, diverse algorithms, other noise types, or real-world tasks remains unknown.  
- Limited validation: MVS not compared to alternative non-Markovianity metrics (e.g., causal discovery); the scale of [0,1] is empirical. History-augmented benefits demonstrated only on CartPole; scalability untested. Necessity of the two-stage design not examined.

## Confidence
- Diagnostic validity: Medium (CartPole manipulation confirmed but inconsistent elsewhere)
- Monotonicity in high-dimensional locomotion: Medium (observed in most pairs, but not universal)
- Inversion in low-dimensional tasks: High (robust pattern across three environments)
- Reward degradation under noise: High (significant in 13/16 pairs)
- Recovery via history-augmented policies: Low (only CartPole evidence)

## Next Checks
1. Hyperparameter sweep: Vary random-forest depth/n_estimators and ridge λ across all environments to quantify impact on MVS and test overfitting in low-dimensional tasks.  
2. Broader benchmarks: Test MVS on image domains (Atari, Control Suite vision) with diverse RL algorithms (value-, model-based) and noise types (colored, missing) to assess generalizability.  
3. Causal baseline: Compute non-Markovianity scores via causal discovery (e.g., PCMCI) on same synthetic POMDPs, correlate with MVS to validate convergent validity.
