# On the Role of Batch Size in Stochastic Conditional Gradient Methods
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21191  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21191  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Under a fixed token budget, the optimal batch‑sequence product scales as \(BS \sim T^{2/3}\), yielding a non‑monotonic error regime; the resulting BST scaling rule improves token efficiency and final validation loss in language model training.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the impact of batch size and sequence length on the efficiency of stochastic conditional gradient (SCG) methods, specifically the momentum variant Scion, when training under a fixed token budget. Under the μ‑KL inequality condition and with noise variance scaling inversely with the batch‑sequence product (BS), the authors derive a three‑term convergence bound. This bound reveals three distinct regimes as BS varies: an iteration‑starved regime where error decreases with BS, a noise‑limited regime where error scales as \(T^{-1/3}\) independent of BS, and a noise‑dominated regime where error increases with BS. Balancing the first two terms yields the optimal scaling \(BS \propto T^{2/3}\) and a step‑size rule \(\beta \propto 1/K\), while momentum \(\alpha\) remains constant. Empirical experiments on the NanoGPT model across different sizes and token budgets confirm the non‑monotonic effect and show that adhering to the BST scaling rule improves token efficiency and reduces final validation loss. Moreover, hyperparameter transfer from small to large models works well, demonstrating the practical utility of the derived rule.

## Method Summary
The method centers on analyzing the Scion algorithm, a stochastic conditional gradient method with momentum, for training language models. The authors impose key assumptions: smoothness (A1), bounded domain (A2), μ‑KL inequality (A3), and noise scaling where mini‑batch variance scales as \(1/(BS)\) (A4). Under these, they derive a convergence bound (Theorem 4.1) that involves three competing terms involving BS, total iterations K, and token budget T. By analyzing the product \(BS\) under fixed T, they identify three regimes and an optimal operating point where two terms balance, leading to the scaling rule \(BS \sim T^{2/3}\) and \(\beta \sim 1/K\), while momentum \(\alpha\) is shown to be approximately constant under this scaling. To apply the rule, one first tunes a small proxy model to obtain baseline hyperparameters, then uses the formulas (7) and (8) to compute scaled hyperparameters for larger token budgets or model sizes, while keeping \(\alpha\) and the layer‑wise radii \(\eta\) fixed.

## Key Results
- Non‑monotonic effect of batch‑sequence product \(BS\): optimization error first decreases with \(BS\), plateaus at \(\sim T^{-1/3}\), then increases.
- Derived BST scaling rule: \(BS \propto T^{2/3}\), stepsize \(\beta \propto 1/K\), and momentum \(\alpha\) constant. This rule reduces final validation loss by \(\approx 0.01\) on NanoGPT and generalizes across model sizes.
- Exceeding the optimal \(BS\) degrades performance noticeably (additional 0.005–0.04 loss); hyperparameter transfer tests confirm accurate prediction of \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Token Budget-Induced Non‑Monotonicity
Under the fixed token budget \(T = K B S\), the convergence bound for Scion (Corollary 4.1) can be expressed as
\[
\varepsilon = \max\left\{ \frac{L B S}{\mu^2 T}, \left(\frac{L \rho^2 \sigma_\star^2}{\mu^4 T}\right)^{\!1/3}, \frac{\rho \sigma_\star}{\mu (T^2 B S)^{1/6}} \right\},
\]
up to logarithmic factors. The three terms dominate in different regions of the effective batch‑sequence product \(BS\):
1. **Iteration‑starved** (large \(BS\) → small \(K\)): undersmoothing increases error proportionally to \(BS\).
2. **Noise‑limited** (intermediate \(BS\)): the second term becomes independent of \(BS\) and governs the rate \(\sim T^{-1/3}\).
3. **Noise‑dominated** (small \(BS\)): high variance from few samples per iteration makes the third term dominant.
Balancing the first two terms yields the optimal operating point \(BS \propto T^{2/3}\), which is the basis of the BST rule.

### Mechanism 2: Validity of μ‑KL in Language Model Training
The theoretical analysis relies on the μ‑KL inequality (Assumption 3.3), which relates the dual gradient norm to the suboptimality: \(\|\nabla f(x)\|_* \ge \mu (f(x)-f_\star)\). Empirically, for NanoGPT the authors observe that once the training loss drops below approximately 5, the dual gradient norm and loss exhibit a clear linear relationship with slope \(\mu \approx 3.1\) (Figure 1). This validates the μ‑KL condition in the relevant training regime and ensures that the derived error bound correctly captures the optimization dynamics.

### Mechanism 3: Constant Momentum Transfer via BST Rule
Theorem 4.1 specifies the momentum parameter as \(\alpha = \min\left\{1, O\!\left((\varepsilon \mu)^2/(\rho\sigma)^2\right)\right\}\). Substituting the optimal error \(\varepsilon \sim T^{-1/3}\) and the scaling \(BS \propto T^{2/3}\) cancels all problem‑ and budget‑dependent quantities, leaving \(\alpha\) approximately constant. This prediction is confirmed experimentally: under different token budgets and model sizes (124M to 1.4B), the optimal \(\alpha\) remains near 0.09 (Figures 6 and 7). Hence, when scaling hyperparameters according to the BST rule, only \(B\), \(S\), and \(\beta\) need adjustment while \(\alpha\) can be fixed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Token Budget Composition**: Understanding that the total token budget \(T\) factorizes as \(T = K \times B \times S\) is essential because the three‑regime analysis stems from varying \(BS\) while keeping \(T\) fixed. *Quick check:* With \(T = 10^{12}\) tokens and a target of \(K = 10^5\) updates, the required product is \(BS = T/K = 10^7\).
- **Kurdyka‑Łojasiewicz (KL) Inequality**: This property (Assumption 3.3) provides a polynomial relationship between the gradient norm and the suboptimality in non‑convex settings, enabling the derivation of explicit convergence rates. *Quick check:* The function \(f(x) = |x|^p\) with \(p \in (1,2]\) satisfies a μ‑KL inequality; the resulting rates are polynomial.
- **Stochastic Conditional Gradient with Momentum**: The Scion algorithm replaces the projection step of SGD with a linear minimization oracle (LMO) and uses a momentum‑based gradient estimator. This structure is key to the analysis. *Quick check:* In SCG, the LMO solves \(\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}} \langle \nabla f(x), x\rangle\) over the constraint set \(\mathcal{C}\), unlike a projection which finds the closest point in \(\mathcal{C}\).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Inputs: total token budget `T`, model size `D`, and tuned small‑model hyperparameters `(B0, S0, β0)` (with fixed momentum `α` and layer‑wise radii `η`). Optional: problem constants `L, μ, ρ` (estimated or power‑law fits). Process: compute the product `B1·S1` and step size `β1` using the BST scaling formulas (equations 7 and 8). Output: new hyperparameters `(B1, S1, β1)` for the larger training run; `α` and `η` remain unchanged.

**Critical path:**
1. **Proxy tuning:** Train a small model (e.g., 124M) with budget `T0`. Sweep `B0, S0, β0` to minimize validation loss; fix `α=0.1` and `η` from prior work.
2. **Estimate constants** (optional): Measure smoothness `L`, KL constant `μ`, norm‑equivalence `ρ` for the small model (Appendix B.2). Fit power‑law scaling if model size will change.
3. **Compute new hyperparameters:** Use formulas (7) and (8) to compute `B1S1` and `β1`. If only `T` changes, ignore `L, μ, ρ` ratios.
4. **Apply:** Train the larger model with the computed hyperparameters from scratch or restart after `T0` tokens.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- \(B S\) product: Too low leads to variance‑dominated error (third term); too high leads to undersmoothing (first term). The BST rule targets the intermediate plateau.
- Batch vs. sequence: When context extension is unnecessary, increasing batch size \(B\) while keeping sequence length \(S\) fixed often yields slightly better computational efficiency (Finding 3).
- Step size \(\beta\): Must scale as \(1/K\) to maintain the theoretical regime; using a fixed \(\beta\) couples to the µP regime and is suboptimal.

**Failure signatures:**
- Validation loss higher than expected (>0.005) suggests \(B S\) is too large; reduce \(B\) or \(S\).
- High variance or instability early in training indicates \(B S\) is too small; consider increasing it or using a warm‑up.
- Divergence or extremely slow progress points to an incorrectly scaled \(\beta\); adjust according to formula (8).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Assumption verification:** Sweep batch size \(B\) and sequence length \(S\) for a fixed model; confirm that gradient noise variance scales as \(1/(B S)\) and that the dual gradient norm is linearly related to suboptimality (μ‑KL).
2. **Regime mapping:** Train the base model with varied \(B S\) products; locate the plateau where validation loss becomes independent of \(B S\); verify the predicted transition points match the three‑regime structure.
3. **Transfer test:** Increase token budget \(T\) or model size; compute new hyperparameters via BST; compare token efficiency and final loss against baselines with fixed hyperparameters (e.g., µP); expect improved performance and accurate prediction of \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### 1. Extension to generalized smoothness
- **Question:** Can convergence analysis of SCG be extended to generalized smoothness (e.g., (L0,L1)-smoothness)?
- **Basis:** Related Works states: "Extending our analysis to these generalized smoothness settings is a future direction."
- **Why unresolved:** L‑smoothness is too restrictive for many practical problems.
- **Needed evidence:** Convergence proof under generalized smoothness plus experiments confirming broader applicability.

#### 2. Minimal hyperparameter set for reliable BST scaling transfer
- **Question:** What is the minimal hyperparameter set that ensures reliable scaling transfer from a small proxy to a large training run?
- **Basis:** Limitations: "Determining the minimal set ... remains an important open question."
- **Why unresolved:** Only batch size, sequence length, and step size were tuned; other hyperparameters (e.g., learning‑rate schedule) may also affect scaling accuracy.
- **Needed evidence:** Systematically vary the tuning set and measure prediction error of final loss across model sizes and token budgets.

#### 3. Momentum transfer and scaling
- **Question:** Why does the optimal momentum \(\alpha\) shift with model size, and can the BST rule be refined to predict it accurately?
- **Basis:** Limitations note that momentum transfer requires investigation, observing that optimal \(\alpha\) moves lower when scaling.
- **Why unresolved:** The BST rule assumes \(\alpha\) constant, but empirical drift suggests the underlying assumption (e.g., constant \(\rho\) or \(\sigma_\star\)) may not hold.
- **Needed evidence:** Large-scale empirical study of \(\alpha\) across model sizes and a theoretical extension that incorporates token‑budget effects into the momentum selection.

## Limitations
- **Assumption dependence:** The μ‑KL inequality and noise scaling (\(\sigma^2 \propto 1/(BS)\)) are only empirically validated for NanoGPT up to 1.4B parameters and loss < 5; their validity for larger models or different architectures remains uncertain.
- **Scaling constant estimation:** The BST rule requires constants \(L, \mu, \rho\) whose scaling with model size is inferred from only three data points (124M–1.4B); extrapolation to much larger scales is speculative.
- **Momentum transfer:** While the rule predicts a constant \(\alpha\), experiments show some drift with model size; the constancy assumption may need refinement for broader applicability.

## Confidence
- **Convergence bound derivation (theoretical):** High confidence (sound under stated assumptions)
- **μ‑KL condition empirical validity:** Medium confidence (validated for NanoGPT up to 1.4B, limited to loss < 5)
- **Hyperparameter transfer across model sizes and token budgets:** Medium confidence (successful for 124M→1.4B; untested on non‑GPT architectures)
- **Scaling of constants L, μ, ρ:** Low confidence (based on three data points; extrapolation uncertain)

## Next Checks
- **Validate noise scaling broadly:** For a fixed model, sweep \(B\) and \(S\) over a wide range; measure gradient norm variance to confirm \(\sigma^2 \propto 1/(BS)\) holds beyond the regimes tested in the paper.
- **Test μ‑KL on larger/diverse models:** For models >1B parameters (e.g., Llama) and non‑GPT architectures (e.g., T5), empirically verify the linear dual norm–suboptimality relationship and estimate \(\mu\).
- **Evaluate BST on encoder‑decoder tasks:** Apply the scaling rule to a CNN‑based or encoder‑decoder model (e.g., T5) and compare token efficiency against baseline schedules; if performance degrades, analyze which assumption (e.g., smoothness, KL) breaks.
