# Evaluating randomized smoothing as a defense against adversarial attacks in trajectory prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10821
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10821
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Adding Gaussian noise to trajectory inputs and averaging predictions consistently reduces Average Displacement Error (ADE) under kinematically constrained adversarial attacks by up to ~50%, while maintaining or improving accuracy on clean data; optimal noise level is σ=0.25m, with strategy (position vs. control) depending on the prediction model.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates randomized smoothing—a test-time defense that adds Gaussian noise to inputs and averages model outputs—as a method to protect trajectory prediction models from adversarial attacks. Evaluated on the L-GAP and rounD datasets using Trajectron++ and ADAPT predictors, smoothing significantly improves robustness against attacks that respect vehicle dynamics, reducing ADE by up to half at a maximum perturbation of 1.0m. The approach requires no retraining, is computationally lightweight, and can even enhance performance on unattacked data through implicit regularization. The optimal noise level is around σ=0.25m, and whether noise is applied to positions or control inputs varies by model architecture.

## Method Summary
The defense wraps a pre-trained trajectory predictor `f_Pθ` with a randomized smoothing layer. At inference, for an input `X` (past trajectory), `N` noisy variants are created by injecting i.i.d. Gaussian noise (variance σ²) either directly into position states (`pos`) or into control inputs (`ctrl`) which are then integrated through a dynamics model `Φ` to produce kinematically feasible perturbed trajectories. The base predictor generates `N` output trajectory samples, which are averaged element-wise to produce the final smoothed prediction `g_N(X)`. Two training strategies are considered: `eval` (smooth only at test time) and `train&eval` (train with noise augmentation). Attacks are generated via PGD in the control space under kinematic constraints.

## Key Results
- Smoothing reduces ADE under attack dramatically (e.g., ADAPT on L-GAP: baseline 2.952m → smoothed 1.428m at d_max=1.0m).
- Optimal noise level is σ=0.25m; higher σ sometimes harms benign accuracy.
- Strategy preference is model-dependent: position-based smoothing works best for Trajectron++, control-based for ADAPT.
- Smoothing maintains or improves accuracy on unattacked data, acting as a regularizer.
- The method is effective without any model retraining (`eval` strategy).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gradient Smoothing via Input Neighborhood Averaging
- **Claim:** Randomized smoothing reduces model sensitivity to adversarial perturbations by averaging predictions over a noisy neighborhood of the input.
- **Mechanism:** The smoothed predictor `g_N(x) = (1/N) Σ f(x+ε_i)` approximates the expected output. This aggregation dampens the effect of any single adversarial perturbation because the output becomes an average over many slightly different inputs, flattening the local prediction landscape an attacker exploits.
- **Core assumption:** Adversarial attacks rely on steep, high-norm input gradients. Averaging over a Gaussian neighborhood (variance σ²) smooths these gradients.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section II-B] "smoothed regressors... become less sensitive to inputs associated with high-norm gradients." [section IV-B] "lowering the correlation between the perturbation limit d_max and the resulting ADE."
- **Break condition:** The attack norm `‖δ‖` exceeds the certified robustness radius, which scales with σ and the base model's local Lipschitz constant. If the base model already has nearly constant gradients, smoothing offers little marginal gain.

### Mechanism 2: Kinematic Feasibility Preservation via State-Space Noise Injection
- **Claim:** Control-based smoothing (Eq. 7) injects noise into control inputs `u` rather than positions `p`, producing perturbed trajectories that are more likely to remain dynamically feasible under the model's assumed dynamics `φ`.
- **Mechanism:** Noise is added to `U` (initial state + controls) before applying the dynamics model `Φ`. This generates trajectories where perturbations accumulate through integration (e.g., acceleration noise → velocity → position), avoiding kinematically impossible sharp turns that pure position-space noise might introduce.
- **Core assumption:** The adversary's attack is applied to control inputs, and the dynamical model `φ` is accurate enough that noise in `u` propagates plausibly. The model is more robust to inputs respecting its learned dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section III-B] "resulting trajectories are less likely to display dynamically infeasible inputs." [section IV-B] Tables II & IV show ADEPT best with control smoothing, Trajectron++ best with position smoothing.
- **Break condition:** The assumed dynamics `φ` poorly matches true vehicle dynamics or the base model is insensitive to dynamic feasibility. Noise in `u` leads to rapidly diverging trajectories from the training data manifold.

### Mechanism 3: Implicit Regularization Enhancing Benign Performance
- **Claim:** Injecting noise during evaluation (or training) acts as a regularizer, reducing overfitting and improving generalization even on unperturbed data.
- **Mechanism:** The noise transforms the prediction problem into a stochastic estimation of an expected output, akin to Tikhonov regularization, forcing the model to produce consistent predictions under small input variations.
- **Core assumption:** The base predictor has a tendency to overfit or produce brittle predictions. The noise-induced variance reduction improves average-case accuracy.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "maintaining or improving accuracy in non-adversarial settings." [section IV-B] "randomized smoothing can function as a form of regularization... even improved baseline performance."
- **Break condition:** The noise level σ is too large relative to the smoothness of the true underlying function, causing excessive bias (oversmoothing) that harms benign accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Randomized Smoothing (Distributional Robustness)
  - **Why needed:** It is the core defense mechanism. Understanding that it provides *empirical* robustness by averaging over perturbed inputs, not by modifying model weights, is crucial.
  - **Quick check:** How does adding noise to inputs at test time make a regressor more robust, and what is the key trade-off (clean accuracy vs. robustness)?
- **Concept:** Kinematically Constrained Adversarial Attacks
  - **Why needed:** The defense is evaluated against *realistic* attacks. Attacks producing physically impossible trajectories are not meaningful for robustness evaluation.
  - **Quick check:** Why is it insufficient to perturb trajectory positions arbitrarily, and how do constraints on control inputs ensure feasibility?
- **Concept:** Trajectory Prediction Model Architecture (e.g., Trajectron++, ADAPT)
  - **Why needed:** The smoothing strategy's effectiveness is model-dependent (pos vs. ctrl). This stems from architectural differences in input representation and dynamic modeling.
  - **Quick check:** What are key architectural differences between a GNN-based predictor (Trajectron++) and a transformer-based one with explicit scene adaptation (ADAPT), and why might one be more sensitive to position noise vs. control noise?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Original Input X -> Noise Injection Module (Position noise ε_p or Control noise ε_c + Dynamics Φ) -> Perturbed Inputs X_i -> Base Predictor f_Pθ (Trajectron++/ADAPT) -> Prediction Samples bY_i -> Averaging Mechanism -> Smoothed Output g_N(X)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Take original past observations X for all agents.
  2. For i=1..N: Generate noise ε_i. For pos: X_i = X + ε_i. For ctrl: U_i = U + ε_c; X_i = Φ(U_i).
  3. Run base predictor f_Pθ on each X_i to get samples bY_i.
  4. Average the N prediction sets element-wise to produce g_N(X).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **σ magnitude:** Low σ (0.25m) balances robustness and benign accuracy; high σ (1.0m) may oversmooth.
  - **Smoothing space:** Position-based (simpler) vs. Control-based (maintains dynamic feasibility). Choice is model-dependent; no universal winner.
  - **Training integration:** `eval` (smooth only at test) is cheap. `train&eval` may regularize further but requires noise during training.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High σ causing large ADE increase on d_max=0 (oversmoothing).
  - Mismatched strategy: e.g., control-based on Trajectron++ yielding higher ADE under attack than position-based.
  - Dynamics model Φ inaccuracy causing trajectory divergence and high errors.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline establishment:** Evaluate base model on d_max=0; compare against smoothed models with various σ to check for degradation/improvement.
  2. **Robustness curve:** For fixed σ (0.25m), run attacks with increasing d_max (0.25, 0.5, 1.0m). Plot ADE vs. d_max for base vs. smoothed; expect flatter slope for smoothed.
  3. **Strategy comparison:** On each model/dataset, compare pos(σ=0.25) and ctrl(σ=0.25) across d_max; identify which yields lower ADE consistently.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can certified robustness (radius bounds) from randomized smoothing be adapted to trajectory prediction, and does α-smoothing improve these guarantees? The current work only evaluates empirical robustness; theoretical certification for high-dimensional sequential outputs remains unexplored.
- **Open Question 2:** Is the variance of the randomized predictor a reliable proxy for the base model's uncertainty, and can it be used by motion planners? No analysis links smoothing variance to predictive uncertainty or planning performance.
- **Open Question 3:** How generally do the robustness benefits hold across more trajectory prediction models, datasets, and attack methods? Evaluation was limited to two models, two datasets, and kinematically constrained white-box attacks.
- **Open Question 4:** Why do different models show a preference for position- versus control-based smoothing, and can a hybrid approach be universally beneficial? The paper observed model-dependent trends but did not investigate underlying causes or test combined strategies.

## Limitations
- Attack hyperparameters (PGD step size, iterations, feasible set) from Schumann et al. [22] are not fully described; reproduction relies on assumptions or reimplementation.
- Control-based smoothing requires a dynamics model Φ; the paper does not specify the exact formulation for Trajectron++ and ADAPT, risking implementation drift.
- Evaluation covers only two datasets and two models; findings may not generalize to other predictors, rule-based agents, or longer horizons.

## Confidence
- Smoothing reduces ADE under kinematically constrained attacks: **Medium** (experimental evidence strong, but attack specifics and certified radius absent).
- Smoothing preserves/improves benign accuracy: **High** (consistent across models/datasets, matches regularization intuition).
- Optimal σ ≈ 0.25 m: **Medium** (optimal seems dataset/model-specific; limited σ sweep).
- Model-dependent strategy (pos vs. ctrl): **High** (clear pattern aligns with architectural differences).
