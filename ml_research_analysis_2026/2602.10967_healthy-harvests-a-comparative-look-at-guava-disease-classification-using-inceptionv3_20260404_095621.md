# Healthy Harvests: A Comparative Look at Guava Disease Classification Using InceptionV3

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10967
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10967
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: InceptionV3 achieved 98.15% test accuracy on guava disease classification, significantly outperforming ResNet50 (94.46%).

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates deep learning for automated classification of three guava disease categories (Anthracnose, Fruit flies, Healthy). Using a dataset of 473 original images augmented to 3,784 via CutMix and MixUp, the authors fine-tuned InceptionV3 and ResNet50. InceptionV3 reached a test accuracy of 98.15%, clearly surpassing ResNet50's 94.46%. Performance was analyzed with confusion matrices and SHAP-based interpretability, which identified disease-relevant image regions such as fungal spots and oviposition marks.

## Method Summary
Transfer learning was employed with InceptionV3 and ResNet50 (ImageNet pre-trained). Images were resized to 256×256 RGB and augmented using CutMix and MixUp. The models were adapted by replacing the top layers with Global Average Pooling, a Dropout layer, and a three-unit Dense layer with Softmax. Training used Adam (lr=0.0001) and categorical cross-entropy loss, with early stopping and 20% validation data. Evaluation included per-class precision/recall/F1, confusion matrices, and SHAP/LIME explanations for interpretability.

## Key Results
- InceptionV3 test accuracy: 98.15%
- ResNet50 test accuracy: 94.46%
- SHAP analysis highlighted that models focus on disease-specific regions for classification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Multi-scale feature extraction via Inception blocks
InceptionV3 uses parallel convolutions (1×1, 3×3, 5×5) within each block, capturing features at multiple spatial scales. This is beneficial because guava disease symptoms vary in size (e.g., small fungal acervuli vs. larger fly lesions). Evidence: the paper’s abstract mentions InceptionV3’s “advanced framework” with multiple filters, and Section III.A details the parallel convolutions. A neighboring guava disease study also suggests multi-scale advantages. Break condition: If symptoms were scale-invariant, the overhead might not yield gains.

### 2. Augmentation with CutMix and MixUp for regularization
CutMix and MixUp create synthetic samples with mixed labels, encouraging robust, linearly connected representations and reducing overfitting on the small dataset. Evidence: Section IV defines these augmentations and states they enhance robustness; Table III shows α-tuning. Excessive mixing (high α) could obscure subtle disease indicators.

### 3. Global average pooling reduces overfitting
InceptionV3 replaces fully connected layers with global average pooling (GAP), drastically reducing parameters before the classification head. This structural regularization helps in the low-data regime (~3,784 images). Evidence: Section III.A describes GAP as minimizing parameters; superior accuracy is partly attributed to it. Break condition: If spatial relationships between distant regions are critical, GAP might lose necessary configuration information.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transfer Learning with Pre-trained CNNs**  
  *Why needed*: ImageNet initialization provides generic edge/texture detectors; only later layers require fine-tuning for disease patterns. Freezing all layers would cripple adaptation.  
  *Quick check*: If you freeze all layers of InceptionV3 and only train the final SoftMax layer, what outcome is most likely and why? (Poor performance; features not specialized for guava diseases.)

- **Data Augmentation for Small Agricultural Datasets**  
  *Why needed*: With only 473 original images, augmentation (CutMix, MixUp, resizing) expands data and injects invariances to lighting, scale, and occlusion common in fields.  
  *Quick check*: What is a potential downside of aggressive MixUp (α=0.6) for subtle disease symptoms? (Over-blending may dilute critical features.)

- **Evaluation Beyond Simple Accuracy for Imbalanced/Small Test Sets**  
  *Why needed*: High overall accuracy can mask poor minority-class performance; per-class metrics and confusion matrices reveal weaknesses important for real use.  
  *Quick check*: Looking at InceptionV3’s confusion matrix, which class is most confused with “Healthy,” and what might that imply? (False negatives could lead to missed treatment.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input → Resize (256×256) → Augmentation (CutMix/MixUp) → Backbone (InceptionV3/ResNet50) → GlobalAveragePooling → Dropout → Dense(3) → SoftMax. Post‑training: SHAP/LIME for saliency maps.

### Critical path
1. Data preparation and augmentation parameter tuning (α).  
2. Model selection (InceptionV3 vs. ResNet50).  
3. Training with early stopping (patience 10/15).  
4. Per‑class evaluation (confusion matrix, precision/recall).  
5. XAI validation (SHAP/LIME).

### Design tradeoffs
- **InceptionV3 vs. ResNet50**: InceptionV3 offers multi‑scale sensitivity with a complex block structure; ResNet50 provides stable residual flow but may need more data.  
- **Augmentation strength**: Moderate α (MixUp≈0.25, CutMix≈0.4) balanced robustness and feature preservation; higher α caused over‑regularization.

### Failure signatures
- Overfitting: training accuracy ~100% vs. validation ~85% and rising validation loss.  
- Class-specific weakness: e.g., Fruit Fly recall drops to 0.89 vs. 1.0, indicating feature extraction issues.  
- Misleading explanations: SHAP highlights background/stem, suggesting dataset bias.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline without augmentation**: Train both models with only resizing; expect lower accuracy and overfitting.  
2. **Ablation on augmentation hyperparameters**: Sweep α values (as in Table III) to confirm optimal regularization; monitor validation metrics.  
3. **Layer‑wise feature visualization**: Apply Grad‑CAM to the last convolutional block for misclassified images; check whether attention aligns with disease indicators.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Dataset scale and quality**: To what extent do dataset size and image quality affect model accuracy? The study uses 473 original images but does not experimentally vary these factors (explicit research question).  
- **Real‑time leaf disease detection**: What system adaptations are needed for field deployment on guava leaves? The current work focuses on fruit images and does not consider leaf‑specific challenges or real‑time constraints (explicit research question).  
- **Consistent image features**: Which specific image features are most reliably important for distinguishing Anthracnose, Fruit flies, and Healthy? SHAP/LIME outputs are anecdotal without aggregation or expert validation (inferred from interpretability section).  
- **Generalization to new diseases/contexts**: How well do models perform on unseen guava diseases or images from diverse regions? Evaluation is limited to three categories from a single dataset (inferred from limitations).

## Limitations
- Unclear train/validation/test split ratios and stratification risk data leakage and unrepresentative evaluation.
- Incomplete reporting of augmentation details (exact transforms, probabilities, final α) hinders faithful reproduction.
- Lack of ablation studies prevents isolation of architecture or augmentation contributions.
- SHAP/LIME explanations are not quantitatively validated against expert annotations, so their reliability is uncertain.

## Confidence
- InceptionV3’s reported superiority (98.15% vs. 94.46%): **Medium** (numbers lack statistical testing or independent replication).
- Multi‑scale architecture as primary driver of performance: **Low** (hypothesized but not validated).
- Augmentation regularization effect (CutMix/MixUp): **Medium** (hyperparameter sweep suggests benefit but no baseline without augmentation).
- SHAP‑based interpretability reliability: **Low** (qualitative only, no expert validation).
