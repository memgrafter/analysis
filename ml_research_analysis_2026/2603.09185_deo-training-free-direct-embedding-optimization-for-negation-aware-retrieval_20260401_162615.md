# DEO: Training-Free Direct Embedding Optimization for Negation-Aware Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09185  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09185  
- Reference count: 14  
- Primary result: DEO improves MAP@100 by +0.1028 and nDCG@10 by +0.0738 on NegConstraint using BGE-large-en-v1.5, and increases Recall@5 by +6% over OpenAI CLIP on COCO-Neg, with minimal computational overhead (20-50 optimization steps).

## Executive Summary
DEO (Direct Embedding Optimization) tackles the problem of negation and exclusion in retrieval queries without requiring any model fine-tuning. By leveraging an LLM to decompose a user query into positive and negative sub-queries, DEO then directly optimizes the initial query embedding using a contrastive loss that attracts it to positive sub-query embeddings while repelling it from negative ones. This simple yet effective approach yields substantial improvements on text and multimodal retrieval benchmarks, notably increasing MAP by 0.1028 and nDCG@10 by 0.0738 on NegConstraint, and boosting Recall@5 by 6% on COCO-Neg compared to OpenAI CLIP, all with only 20-50 optimization steps and negligible additional computational cost.

## Method Summary
DEO operates in three stages. First, an LLM decomposes the input query into a set of positive sub-queries (desired topics) and negative sub-queries (excluded concepts). Second, a frozen pre-trained encoder (e.g., BGE for text, CLIP for multimodal) generates embeddings for the original query and all sub-queries. Third, the original query embedding is used as initialization for a learnable vector that is optimized via gradient descent (Adam) for a fixed number of steps (typically 20) under a contrastive loss: it is pulled toward the average of positive embeddings and pushed away from negative embeddings, with a consistency term to anchor it to the original query. The optimized embedding is then used for nearest-neighbor retrieval against the pre-computed corpus index. No model weights are altered, making DEO training-free and applicable to any off-the-shelf encoder.

## Key Results
- On NegConstraint, DEO improves MAP@100 by +0.1028 (to 0.7327) and nDCG@10 by +0.0738 (to 0.7877) with BGE-large-en-v1.5.
- On COCO-Neg (multimodal), DEO increases Recall@5 by +6% over the baseline OpenAI CLIP, and also outperforms fine-tuned NegCLIP.
- Consistent gains are observed across multiple embedding models, with only 20-50 optimization steps and minimal computational overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Query Decomposition for Semantic Structuring
- Claim: Decomposing a negated/exclusionary query into separate positive and negative sub-queries via an LLM creates a structured semantic representation that isolates inclusion from exclusion semantics.
- Mechanism: The LLM parses the original query's linguistic negation cues (e.g., "excluding", "but not") and generates two sets of sub-queries: one that expands on the desired topic (positives) and one that explicitly lists the concepts to be excluded (negatives). This turns a single ambiguous embedding objective into a controlled contrastive problem.
- Core assumption: A capable LLM can reliably interpret the user's intent and perform this decomposition with high accuracy, generating sub-queries that are semantically complete and correctly polarized.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DEO decomposes queries into positive and negative sub-queries via an LLM."
  - [section 3.1] Detailed description and example of LLM-based decomposition into positive/negative components.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., MERLIN) also leverage LLMs for retrieval refinement, supporting the paradigm of using LLMs to interpret queries, though not specifically for negation. *Corpus evidence for this specific decomposition mechanism is indirect/weak.*
- Break condition: If the LLM generates incorrect or incomplete positive/negative sets (e.g., missing a key exclusion, adding irrelevant positives), the optimization signal becomes flawed, potentially degrading performance below the baseline.

### Mechanism 2: Contrastive Embedding Optimization
- Claim: Directly optimizing the initial query embedding with a contrastive loss, using embeddings of the decomposed sub-queries as attractors/repulsors, repositions it in the embedding space to better satisfy the composite negation constraint.
- Mechanism: Starting from the frozen model's original query embedding `e_o`, gradient-based updates (e.g., Adam) adjust a copy `e_u` to minimize `L(e_u)`. The loss pulls `e_u` toward the average of positive sub-query embeddings (`ep_i`) and pushes it away from negative sub-query embeddings (`en_j`), while a consistency term (`λ_o * ||e_u - e_o||`) prevents drift from the original query's core semantics. This shifts the query vector toward regions of the embedding space correlated with desired documents and away from regions correlated with excluded documents.
- Core assumption: The pre-trained embedding model's space has a meaningful geometry where semantic similarity is captured by vector proximity, and moving a query vector via gradient steps on a contrastive objective can improve its relative positioning without retraining the entire model.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "optimizes the query embedding with a contrastive objective... attracting it to positive embeddings while repelling it from negatives."
  - [section 3.2] Formal loss function definition (Equation 4) and optimization procedure description.
  - [section 5.2] PCA visualization shows `e_u` trajectory moving toward ground-truth and positive sub-queries while moving away from negative sub-queries.
- Break condition: If the embedding space is not sufficiently linear or smooth for small gradient steps, or if the positive/negative sets are not semantically coherent/representative, optimization may lead to overfitting to the sub-queries, causing the embedding to leave the manifold of meaningful representations and degrade retrieval.

### Mechanism 3: Modality-Agnostic Application via Frozen Encoder
- Claim: Because DEO operates solely on the embeddings produced by a frozen encoder and does not alter model weights, it is inherently modality-agnostic and can be applied to any encoder that maps inputs to a comparable vector space (text or multimodal).
- Mechanism: The same pipeline—LLM decomposition (for text queries), embedding generation via a frozen model, vector optimization—is used regardless of whether the query and corpus items are both text (e.g., BGE) or one is text and the other is an image (e.g., CLIP). The contrastive loss operates in the encoder's output space. For CLIP, the text query embedding is optimized while image embeddings remain fixed.
- Core assumption: The shared embedding space of a multimodal model like CLIP is amenable to the same contrastive geometry manipulation as a unimodal space, and semantic concepts (both positive and negative) from the decomposed text query can be faithfully encoded into vectors that are meaningfully close/far from image vectors in that space.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "DEO is model-agnostic... generalizing across diverse embedding models and retrieval settings... improves Recall@5 by +6% over OpenAI CLIP."
  - [section 3.3] Explicitly states applicability to "various embedding models, including multimodal encoders such as CLIP."
  - [section 4.2.2] Experimental results on COCO-Neg with multiple CLIP variants, including a fine-tuned negation model (NegCLIP), show consistent gains.
  - [section 5.2] PCA visualization on CLIP space (Figure 6) shows the same attractive/repulsive trajectory pattern as in text space.
- Break condition: If the modality gap in a multimodal model is too large or the text encoder's representation of negative concepts does not align well with image regions/corpus embeddings representing those concepts, the repulsion force may be misdirected, harming retrieval.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Contrastive Learning (e.g., InfoNCE, triplet loss).  
  Why needed here: The core optimization loss (Equation 4) is a form of contrastive learning. Understanding its goal—to increase similarity for positive pairs and decrease it for negative pairs—is essential to grasp why DEO's loss function is designed as it is and how hyperparameters (`λ_p`, `λ_n`) affect the trade-off.  
  Quick check question: In DEO's loss, what is the effect of increasing `λ_n` relative to `λ_p` on the final query embedding's position in vector space?
- Concept: Embedding Space Geometry & Cosine Similarity.  
  Why needed here: DEO assumes retrieval is performed via cosine similarity in a dense vector space. The mechanism relies on the idea that moving a vector changes its similarity relations to other vectors. Knowing that distance/similarity metrics define retrieval is key.  
  Quick check question: If DEO used Euclidean distance instead of cosine similarity for retrieval, would the optimization objective (Equation 4) need to change? Why or why not?
- Concept: Large Language Model (LLM) Prompting for Structured Output.  
  Why needed here: The entire method hinges on the LLM's ability to perform reliable query decomposition. Understanding prompting strategies, in-context learning, and potential failure modes (e.g., hallucination, incomplete lists) is critical for assessing DEO's robustness.  
  Quick check question: How might you design a prompt to improve the LLM's accuracy in identifying *all* exclusionary phrases in a complex query?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input Query Parser → LLM Decomposer → Embedding Encoder → Optimizer → Retriever → Hyperparameter Manager

Critical path: Query → LLM Decomposition → Encode all sub-queries → Initialize e_u = e_o → Optimization loop (compute loss from ep, en, e_o, backprop to e_u) → Retrieve with e_u.

Design tradeoffs:
- LLM Choice vs. Cost/Accuracy: Using a powerful model (GPT-4.1-nano) yields better decomposition but incurs API cost/latency; a smaller local model (Qwen2.5-1.5B) is cheaper but less accurate.
- Optimization Steps T: More steps (20-50) allow convergence but increase latency; >100 steps can lead to over-optimization and performance drop.
- Consistency Weight λ_o: Balances adherence to original query vs. influence of sub-queries. For text (NegConstraint), lower λ_o (0.2) works best; for CLIP (COCO-Neg), λ_o=1.0 is optimal.

Failure signatures:
- Poor decomposition: LLM output contains irrelevant, redundant, or incorrectly polarized sub-queries. Validate by sampling decompositions (as in Section 5.1's 91.76% accuracy check).
- Vanishing gradients / No movement: If e_u doesn't change significantly during optimization, check gradient norms; may indicate λ_p and λ_n are too low or sub-query embeddings are too similar to e_o.
- Degeneration on multimodal: On CLIP, if e_u moves into a region where image-text alignment breaks down (e.g., optimizing for a text-only concept that has no visual correlate), Recall@5 will drop. Monitor similarity distributions.

First 3 experiments:
1. Decomposition Sanity Check: Run the LLM decomposer on 10-20 sample queries from your target domain. Manually inspect if positive/negative sets are complete and correctly polarized. This validates the first, critical dependency.
2. Optimization Trajectory Visualization: For a single query with known relevant/non-relevant documents, run DEO and plot e_u in a 2D PCA projection of the corpus (replicate Figure 5/6). Confirm it moves toward positives/ground truth and away from negatives.
3. Hyperparameter Sweep on Validation Set: For your specific encoder and dataset, run a minimal grid search over λ_p (0.5-2.0), λ_n (0.5-2.0), λ_o (0.1-1.0), and T (10-50). ## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we enhance the robustness and accuracy of LLM-based query decomposition for negation-aware retrieval, especially for complex negation expressions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitation states: "it relies on the ability of LLMs to correctly decompose user queries... Future work could explore enhancing query decomposition with more robust LLMs."
- Why unresolved: Current decomposition errors propagate to retrieval; improving decomposition is crucial but challenging due to linguistic variability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop and evaluate decomposition methods using larger or specialized LLMs, or improved prompting on a diverse benchmark; correlate decomposition quality with retrieval metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can we design an adaptive optimization strategy that automatically determines the optimal hyperparameters (λo, λp, λn) and optimization steps per query?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitation suggests "incorporating adaptive optimization strategies that automatically select loss balancing parameters per query."
- Why unresolved: Fixed hyperparameters may not suit all queries; adaptive control could further boost performance and reduce manual tuning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Propose a meta-controller that predicts hyperparameters based on query features; validate on benchmarks showing improved robustness over fixed settings.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent does DEO generalize to other multimodal retrieval tasks (e.g., text-to-audio, text-to-video) where negation semantics differ?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitation recommends "extending DEO to diverse multimodal datasets beyond images, such as audio."
- Why unresolved: Current experiments are limited to image-text retrieval; other modalities have different data characteristics and negation expressions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Apply DEO to audio/video retrieval benchmarks with negation constraints; compare with baselines; analyze modality-specific challenges.

## Limitations
- Missing critical specifications: exact LLM prompt, Adam optimizer hyperparameters (learning rate, etc.), and CLIP token pooling method hinder exact reproduction.
- Decomposition accuracy only validated on NegConstraint; generalizability to diverse negation types or domains is uncertain.
- Hyperparameters are dataset/modality-specific; the provided defaults may not transfer to new settings.
