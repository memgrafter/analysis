# Steering Frozen LLMs: Adaptive Social Alignment via Online Prompt Routing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15647
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15647
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CCLUB achieves a 10.98% higher cumulative reward and a 14.42% lower offline suboptimality gap compared to strong baselines, with a theoretical sublinear regret bound \(O(d\sqrt{M T \log T})\).

## Executive Summary
Static alignment policies for large language models (LLMs) are rigid and cannot adapt to evolving safety threats or diverse societal norms. This paper presents CCLUB, an inference-time governance framework that dynamically routes user queries to suitable system prompts. CCLUB leverages a conservative consensus clustering mechanism that intersects utility- and safety-based similarity graphs, thereby preventing unsafe generalization while maintaining adaptability.

Experiments on the Qwen3-0.6B model show that CCLUB achieves a 10.98% higher cumulative reward and a 14.42% lower offline suboptimality gap compared to strong baselines. The method is further supported by a theoretical sublinear regret bound \(O(d\sqrt{M T \log T})\), confirming its sample efficiency.

## Method Summary
CCLUB assumes a fixed pool of candidate system prompts. For each incoming query, its embedding is computed and matched to the nearest prototypes in two offline-constructed graphs: one capturing utility similarity (queries with similar utility outcomes) and another capturing safety similarity (queries with similar safety outcomes). The sets of prompts recommended by each graph are intersected to form a conservative candidate set. A multi-armed bandit algorithm (e.g., UCB) then selects a prompt from this set, balancing exploration and exploitation. After the LLM generates a response, a reward model (for utility) and a guard model (for safety) provide feedback that updates the bandit’s estimated values. This online process allows the framework to adapt to changing query distributions without retraining the frozen LLM.

## Key Results
- 10.98% higher cumulative reward relative to strong baselines.
- 14.42% lower offline suboptimality gap.
- Theoretical regret bound: \(O(d\sqrt{M T \log T})\), guaranteeing sublinear growth.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The conservative intersection of utility and safety candidate sets ensures that only prompts satisfying both objectives are considered, eliminating those that might be utility-optimal but unsafe. This design directly targets the risk of unsafe generalization. Meanwhile, the bandit selection adapts to observed rewards and safety signals, gradually favoring prompts that perform well in both dimensions. The theoretical analysis further shows that even with the conservative constraint, the algorithm can achieve sublinear regret, confirming its efficiency.

  *Why needed:* Enable online learning from limited feedback while balancing exploration and exploitation.

- **Graph-based Clustering**  
  *Why needed:* Capture similarity structure in query space with respect to utility and safety objectives.  
  *Quick check:* Verify that nearest prototypes in each graph correlate with similar reward/safety outcomes.

  *Why needed:* Prevent selection of prompts that excel in one objective but fail in the other.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Query → Feature Extractor → {Utility Matcher → Utility Candidates; Safety Matcher → Safety Candidates} → Intersector → Candidate Set → Bandit → Prompt Injector → Frozen LLM → Response. Reward & Safety Models evaluate Response and feed back to Bandit.

**Critical Path**  
The inference loop begins with a user query, proceeds through embedding and matching, yields a candidate prompt set via intersection, selects a prompt using the bandit, generates a response, and updates the bandit with feedback.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Fixed prompt pool limits adaptivity to novel threats but simplifies deployment.  
- Conservative intersection improves safety at potential cost of utility.  
- Bandit exploration may temporarily select suboptimal or unsafe prompts, requiring careful regularization.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Empty intersection leads to fallback to a default prompt (possible safety lapse).  
- Rising regret indicates poor adaptation.  
- Increased safety violations after deployment signal distribution shift or adversarial attacks.

3. Scale to a larger model (e.g., Llama-7B) using identical CCLUB parameters to test generalization beyond the 0.6B scale.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the fixed-pool assumption in CCLUB be relaxed to allow dynamic candidate generation of system prompts during deployment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Conclusion and Limitation section states: "Future work will focus on relaxing the fixed-pool assumption via dynamic candidate generation."
- Why unresolved: The current framework relies on a pre-computed set of prompts, which cannot automatically address novel or evolving safety threats that emerge after deployment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop an online mechanism to synthesize new prompts (e.g., using an LLM guided by clustering statistics) and integrate it with CCLUB; evaluate adaptability on a benchmark that simulates emerging jailbreak patterns and compare cumulative reward and safety-utility trade-offs with a static pool.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can CCLUB be enhanced to remain robust under distribution shifts by leveraging richer feedback signals beyond scalar utility and safety scores?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Conclusion and Limitation section also states: "Future work will focus on ... enhancing robustness against distribution shifts using richer feedback signals."
- Why unresolved: Dependence on a single reward model and guard model may lead to misalignment or bias when the query distribution changes over time; richer modalities (e.g., natural language explanations, human judgments) could provide more stable guidance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Incorporate multi-source feedback (e.g., multiple judges, textual rationales, or human-in-the-loop) into the CCLUB objective and demonstrate improved performance under simulated distribution shifts, e.g., on varying safety-critical benchmarks such as HarmBench with perturbed toxicity levels.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can CCLUB maintain its theoretical guarantees and safety assurances when faced with non-i.i.d. or adversarially crafted query sequences?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The analysis (Assumption 4.2) assumes i.i.d. prototype arrivals; however, the Impact Statement and Appendix D discuss risks from "adversarial manipulation of the routing policy" and "sequential attacks," implying that real-world queries may be adaptive and violate independence.
- Why unresolved: The sublinear regret bound relies on i.i.d. contexts; adaptive adversaries could degrade convergence or induce unsafe exploration by exploiting the bandit’s learned policy.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extend the theoretical analysis to a dependent process (e.g., mixing or oblivious adversarial models); and conduct empirical tests with adaptive attack strategies (e.g., queries that probe system prompts to find over‑refusal or compliance patterns) to measure safety violations and regret under attack.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Does CLLUB propagate and amplify biases present in the offline reward and safety models, and how can equitable behavior be enforced?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The Impact Statement explicitly acknowledges "the propagation of biases from reward models" as a risk, yet the paper’s evaluation does not examine demographic or group‑level fairness.
- Why unresolved: If feedback models encode societal biases, the clustering and pooling mechanism in CCLUB could systematize and worsen those biases, leading to discriminatory prompt routing across user groups.
- What evidence would resolve it: Audit CCLUB on bias‑sensitive datasets (e.g., BBQ, HANS) measuring disparate impact; develop fairness‑aware regularization or clustering constraints that balance safety and utility across subgroups and report trade‑offs.

## Limitations
- **Model Scope:** Evaluation solely on Qwen3-0.6B; generalization to larger, widely-used LLMs (e.g., Llama 3, GPT series) is unknown.
- **Benchmark Specificity:** The nature and difficulty of the safety/utility benchmarks are unspecified; performance may not transfer to standard, diverse suites like SafetyBench or RealToxicityPrompts.
- **Theoretical Assumptions:** The sublinear regret bound relies on idealized assumptions (e.g., graph structure, reward Lipschitzness); its tightness under real-world noise and distribution shifts is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Generalization to diverse LLMs: **Low**
- Robustness across standard safety benchmarks: **Medium**
- Tightness of regret bounds under noise and shifts: **Medium**
