# Deep Kernel Fusion for Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11808  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11808  
- **Reference count**: 9  
- **Primary result**: DeepFusionKernel fuses GEMMs and pointwise kernels to eliminate intermediate memory accesses, cutting HBM traffic and delivering up to 13.2% (H100) and 9.7% (A100) decoding throughput speedups over SGLang across batch sizes 1‑64 and output lengths 1024‑16384 tokens.

## Executive Summary
Agentic LLM inference with long contexts is often bottlenecked by memory bandwidth, particularly from SwiGLU MLP blocks that produce large intermediate tensors. This paper introduces DeepFusionKernel, a technique that fuses the GEMM computations and pointwise activations of SwiGLU into a single GPU kernel, thereby avoiding writes to HBM for intermediates. Combined with a profiler‑based scheduler that selects the optimal kernel configuration at runtime, DeepFusionKernel is integrated into the SGLang inference engine. Experiments on NVIDIA H100 and A100 GPUs show consistent throughput improvements (up to 13.2% on H100, 9.7% on A100) across a wide range of batch sizes and output lengths, demonstrating effective memory bandwidth reduction without sacrificing compute utilization.

## Method Summary
DeepFusionKernel’s core innovation is a single fused operator that combines the two GEMMs and the SwiGLU pointwise activation (splitting, sigmoid, multiplication) into one GPU kernel. This fusion keeps intermediate results in registers or shared memory, eliminating costly HBM accesses. To ensure optimal performance across diverse hardware, a profiler‑based scheduler benchmarks multiple kernel configurations (e.g., tile sizes) before inference begins and picks the fastest variant. The technique is implemented as a drop‑in replacement for SwiGLU layers within SGLang, requiring only that the scheduler be invoked once per model/device combination. The overhead of profiling is amortized over long-running inference tasks.

## Key Results
- Up to **13.2%** decoding throughput speedup on NVIDIA H100 and **9.7%** on A100 compared to vanilla SGLang.
- Gains are stable across batch sizes **1‑64** and output lengths **1024‑16384** tokens.
- Significant reduction in HBM traffic by fusing GEMM and pointwise operations, directly addressing the memory bandwidth bottleneck.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
In SwiGLU‑based transformers, the sequence of GEMM → split → sigmoid → multiply → GEMM generates large intermediate tensors that are written to and read from HBM, saturating memory bandwidth—especially for long contexts. DeepFusionKernel merges these operations into one kernel so that intermediates stay in on‑chip memory, drastically cutting HBM traffic. The profiler‑based scheduler tailors the kernel launch configuration (e.g., tile dimensions) to the specific GPU, ensuring high occupancy and efficient use of Tensor Cores. This combination turns a memory‑bound workload into a more compute‑bound one, yielding higher throughput without changing algorithmic behavior.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Model (Transformer with SwiGLU MLP) → DeepFusionKernel library (fused GEMM+pointwise kernels) → SGLang Runtime (with Profiler Scheduler) → GPU (H100/A100)

**Critical path**:  
1. SGLang identifies a SwiGLU MLP block during graph construction.  
2. Profiler Scheduler benchmarks available fused kernel configurations on the target GPU.  
3. Fastest configuration is cached and selected for subsequent inference steps.  
4. During decoding, the fused kernel is launched, performing all GEMMs and activations without writing intermediates to HBM.  
5. Reduced HBM traffic alleviates the memory bottleneck, increasing decoding throughput.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Fusion increases register and shared‑memory usage, potentially reducing occupancy.  
- Profiling adds one‑time startup overhead (typically seconds), negligible for long runs.  
- Current fusions target only SwiGLU MLPs; attention and layer norms remain unfused due to dependencies.  
- The technique assumes memory bandwidth is the primary limiter; in compute‑bound scenarios gains may be minimal.

**Failure signatures**:  
- **No speedup or slowdown** compared to baseline SGLang, indicating fusion overhead outweighed memory savings.  
- **Profiling time explosion** when growing the configuration space; may become prohibitive at scale.  
- **Kernel launch failures** due to exceeding per‑block resource limits (registers, shared memory).  
- **High throughput variance** across runs, possibly from unaccounted system noise or jitter in the scheduler.

**Three first experiments**:  
1. Verify correctness: Run a single SwiGLU layer with DeepFusionKernel and compare outputs against a reference implementation (tolerance < 1e‑5).  
2. Measure memory traffic: Use NVIDIA Nsight to capture HBM read/write bytes for fused vs. unfused kernels; expect ≥30% reduction.  
3. Quantify speedup: Replicate the SGLang decoding throughput experiment on H100/A100 with batch sizes 1, 8, 64 and output lengths 1024, 8192, 16384; track consistency with reported numbers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does DeepFusionKernel's performance generalize to GPU architectures with different memory bandwidth and compute capabilities beyond NVIDIA A100 and H100?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The experimental evaluation is limited to A100 and H100 GPUs, as stated in Section 4.1, and no results are provided for other architectures.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper demonstrates speedups on these two architectures, but the effectiveness of kernel fusion may depend on specific hardware characteristics like cache sizes, memory hierarchy, and Tensor Core implementations, which vary across GPUs.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Benchmarking DeepFusionKernel on a diverse set of GPUs, such as NVIDIA V100, T4, or AMD MI300, to measure throughput gains and identify architecture‑specific trends.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the precise contribution of inter‑GPU communication overhead to the overall speedup of DeepFusionKernel in tensor‑parallel inference setups?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The limitations section notes that performance variation from inter‑GPU communication is not quantified, and Section 4.2 mentions that throughput variance grows with batch size due to communication jitter.  
- **Why unresolved**: While DeepFusionKernel reduces HBM traffic, communication overhead from all‑reduce operations in tensor parallelism could offset gains, but this is not isolated or measured.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments that measure communication latency separately, perhaps using microbenchmarks, and correlate it with speedup across different interconnect configurations (e.g., NVLink vs. PCIe).

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can the deep fusion strategy employed in DeepFusionKernel be successfully applied to other transformer components, such as attention mechanisms or layer norms, to further reduce memory traffic?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper focuses exclusively on fusing SwiGLU MLP blocks, citing that attention kernels are already optimized and involve long‑range dependencies that limit fusion. However, this assumption might not hold for all attention variants or future architectures.  
- **Why unresolved**: The authors argue that attention has dependencies that hinder fusion, but recent work like FlashAttention shows partial fusion; exploring deeper fusion for attention or other parts could yield additional benefits.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Designing and implementing fused kernels for attention or other modules, then evaluating end‑to‑end throughput improvements in comparable agentic workloads.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does the profile‑driven kernel scheduler's selection accuracy and overhead scale with an increasing number of kernel configurations or model variants?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The scheduler benchmarks candidate kernels pre‑inference, but the paper does not analyze the scaling of profiling time or the robustness of selection as the configuration space grows.  
- **Why unresolved**: In practice, supporting many models or hardware setups might require numerous kernel variants, and profiling each could introduce non‑negligible overhead at deployment.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments that increase the number of kernel configurations (e.g., by varying tile sizes or loop orders) and measure profiling duration and subsequent inference performance to assess scalability.

## Limitations
- Evaluation only compares against SGLang; no head‑to‑head with other modern inference engines (e.g., FlashInfer, BatchLLM).
- The attribution of the bottleneck to SwiGLU MLPs lacks independent validation; related work often highlights attention/KV cache optimizations.
- No reproducibility package or detailed hardware performance counters are released, making speedup claims hard to verify in different setups.
- Generalization beyond SwiGLU (e.g., to Mixture‑of‑Experts FFNs) remains unclear.
