# Localized Concept Erasure in Text-to-Image Diffusion Models via High-Level Representation Misdirection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19631
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19631
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** HiRM achieves ~96% unlearning accuracy while preserving >89% in-domain and cross-domain retention, and transfers to Flux without retraining.

## Executive Summary
Text-to-image diffusion models can generate harmful content, and existing fine-tuning methods for concept erasure are computationally expensive and often degrade unrelated generations. This paper introduces High-Level Representation Misdirection (HiRM), a targeted approach that updates only the first transformer block of the CLIP text encoder while steering the final block’s semantic representations toward designated vectors. By decoupling the location of updates from the target semantics, HiRM removes specific concepts (styles, objects, NSFW) with minimal collateral damage. Evaluations on the UnlearnCanvas benchmark show strong erasure (>96% accuracy) and high retention (>89%). The method demonstrates robustness against adversarial attacks and transfers to the Flux architecture without retraining, offering a modular and efficient solution for content moderation.

## Method Summary
HiRM fine‑tunes solely the parameters of the first transformer block (θ¹) of the CLIP text encoder while keeping all subsequent blocks frozen. During training, for prompts containing the target concept, a loss is applied to the final‑block output hᴸ, pulling it toward a misdirection vector. Two variants are proposed: HiRM‑R uses per‑token random unit vectors, while HiRM‑S uses semantic vectors derived from a guided concept (e.g., "Painting" for the style "Van Gogh"). The loss is a mean squared error scaled by a steering coefficient (c=500 for HiRM‑R, c=1 for HiRM‑S). This updates the low‑level building blocks that encode visual attributes without directly perturbing later layers responsible for non‑target semantics. Because only the text encoder is modified, the resulting patch is lightweight (≈1 s, 1.6 GB) and can be directly swapped into any diffusion model that shares the same CLIP encoder, ensuring transferability.

## Key Results
- Unlearning Accuracy (UA) ~96% on UnlearnCanvas for styles and objects.
- In‑domain (IRA) and Cross‑domain (CRA) Retention both >89%.
- Robustness: Ring‑A‑Bell adversarial attack ASR reduced to ≤1.05%.
- Utility: COCO CLIP score ~0.306, indicating preserved generative quality.
- Transferability: HiRM‑R applied to Flux1.dev without retraining yields competitive NSFW removal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** By confining weight updates to the first transformer block while steering the final‑block representations, HiRM decouples the *location* of erasure from the *target* semantics, enabling precise concept removal without collateral damage.

**Mechanism:**  
HiRM fine‑tunes only the parameters of the first block (θ¹) of the CLIP text encoder. For a prompt containing the target concept, the loss is defined on the final‑block output ℎ⁽ᴸ⁾ (high‑level semantics) and pulls it toward a designated vector (random or semantic). This updates the low‑level building blocks (the “bag of concepts”) responsible for visual attributes, but the supervision signal comes from the fully integrated semantics, avoiding direct perturbation of later layers that encode non‑target information.

**Core assumption:**  
Early layers contain the causal states for visual attributes, whereas the final block holds the coherent concept representation that directly conditions the U‑Net (Basu et al., 2023; Toker et al., 2024).

**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “HiRM updates only these first‑layer weights while steering the high‑level semantic representations of the target concept in the final encoder layer toward random or semantic vectors. This decouples the location of updates from the target of erasure.”  
- [Section 3.2] Formal definition of ℒ_HiRM‑R and ℒ_HiRM‑S; updates restricted to θ¹.  
- [corpus] Neighboring works (e.g., *Receler*, *MACE*) highlight the need for localized erasure, supporting the design choice.

**Break condition:**  
If the target concept’s representation is strongly scattered across both early and late layers, updating only θ¹ may leave residual semantics in the final output; conversely, if the misdirection vectors are poorly aligned, over‑erasure of non‑target content may occur.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Steering the *final* transformer block’s representation is more effective for erasing abstract or high‑level concepts than editing early layers directly.

**Mechanism:**  
Unlike Diff‑QuickFix, which applies a closed‑form edit to the first block’s output, HiRM defines the erasure loss on ℎ⁽ᴸ⁾. By pulling ℎ⁽ᴸ⁾ toward random or semantic vectors, the method attacks the semantic composite the U‑Net actually sees, while the early layers remain adaptable to retain the encoding of unrelated visual primitives.

**Core assumption:**  
The final block integrates distributed information into coherent, semantically meaningful tokens that directly drive cross‑attention (Toker et al., 2024).

**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “Our method ... misdirects high‑level semantic representations ... while updating only early layers that contain causal states of visual attributes.”  
- [Section 3.1] Direct fine‑tuning of early layers (Diff‑Q*) degrades generation quality (Table 1).  
- [Section 5.1] Ablation (Table 12) shows that using the final block’s W_out as target yields the best erasure‑retention trade‑off across styles and objects.

**Break condition:**  
If the final representation is highly entangled with many non‑target concepts, steering it could unintentionally suppress those concepts as well. However, the empirical results show minimal retention loss, indicating that the early‑layer decoupling protects non‑target semantics.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Modifying only the CLIP text encoder makes HiRM modular, computationally cheap, and transferable across diffusion architectures without retraining.

**Mechanism:**  
Since the text encoder is a shared component in many T2I models (SD1.5, Flux), updating its first block weights produces a plug‑and‑play erasure patch. The method does not touch the U‑Net, so training cost is low (≈1 s, 1.6 GB) and the erased encoder can be directly swapped into other models that use the same CLIP encoder.

**Core assumption:**  
The conditioning signal fed to the U‑Net originates from the CLIP text encoder; altering its weights uniformly changes that signal regardless of the denoising backbone.

**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “This design ... avoids any modification to the U‑Net, thereby ensuring modularity and transferability across model variants.”  
- [Section 5.3] HiRM‑R achieves competitive nudity removal on Flux1.dev without any fine‑tuning on that architecture (Table 4).  
- [Section 5.4] Combining HiRM with U‑Net‑based erasures (ESD, CA, EraseAnything) yields synergistic robustness, confirming orthogonality.

**Break condition:**  
Models that rely on a different text encoder (e.g., T5 in SD3) or use multiple encoders may not benefit from CLIP‑only changes; also, if the model heavily weights non‑CLIP embeddings, the effect could be limited.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Causal Localization of Visual Attributes
**Why needed here:** HiRM’s update confinement to the first transformer block rests on the finding from causal tracing that visual attributes (e.g., style, object features) are localized in that block. Without this insight, updating only early layers would be an arbitrary guess.
**Quick check question:** According to Basu et al. (2023), which component of the CLIP text encoder is the sole causal state for various visual attributes in T2I diffusion models?

### Concept: Cross‑Attention Conditioning in Diffusion Models
**Why needed:** The text encoder’s output is injected into the U‑Net via cross‑attention layers. Understanding this flow clarifies why modifying the encoder can influence image generation and how ℎ⁽ᴸ⁾ acts as the conditioning vector.
**Quick check question:** In Stable Diffusion, how are the text embeddings from the CLIP encoder used by the U‑Net during the denoising process?

### Concept: Representation Misdirection for Machine Unlearning
**Why needed:** HiRM steers high‑level representations toward random or semantic vectors rather than simply deleting weights; this idea adapts representation misdirection from LLM unlearning to the T2I domain and underpins the loss design.
**Quick check question:** What is the key difference between “representation misdirection” (e.g., RMU) and conventional fine‑tuning for concept erasure?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Tokenizer & token embedding -> Transformer Block 1 (trainable θ¹) -> Transformer Blocks 2-L (frozen) -> Final output hᴸ. Loss modules (HiRM‑R or HiRM‑S) compute loss on hᴸ and backpropagate to update only θ¹.

**Critical path:**
1. Forward pass: tokens -> embed -> block 1 (modified) -> blocks 2…L (frozen) -> ℎ⁽ᴸ⁾.
2. During training, compute ℒ by comparing ℎ⁽ᴸ⁾ to misdirection vectors R̂ or Ŝ.
3. Backpropagate only through θ¹ to update first‑block weights.
4. Inference: use the entire updated encoder; no special handling needed.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Random vs. semantic misdirection: Random vectors are simple but risk degrading image quality; semantic vectors (e.g., super‑categories) require a suitable guided concept but preserve coherence.
- Keyword vs. template gating: Concrete concepts (styles, objects) can be targeted with explicit keywords; abstract NSFW attributes need template estimation for better coverage.
- Single‑ vs. multi‑concept fusion: Simple averaging of LoRA modules for multiple styles works but hurts in‑domain accuracy (Table 9); smarter fusion (e.g., weighted) is an open direction.

**Failure signatures:**
- Under‑erasure: Target concept still appears (high classifier accuracy on target prompts).
- Over‑erasure: Significant drop in IRA/CRA or COCO CLIP/FID; visual artifacts in non‑target images.
- Adversarial vulnerability: High ASR under Ring‑A‑Bell or MMA‑Diffusion indicates robustness failure.
- Transfer failure: Applying HiRM‑R to Flux yields performance comparable to SD (Table 4) – if degraded drastically, model‑specific text encoder differences break the assumption.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. UnlearnCanvas benchmark – evaluate style and object erasure (UA, IRA, CRA, AA) against prior methods.
2. Adversarial/NSFW robustness – test on Ring‑A‑Bell, MMA‑Diffusion, I2P, and COCO utility (CLIP/FID).
3. Transferability – apply HiRM‑R to Flux1.dev and LoRA‑tuned SD1.5 without retraining; report ASR and CLIP scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can HiRM incorporate selective or weighted token representation guidance to avoid suppressing non‑target concepts?
2. How can HiRM be extended to accurately erase concepts in compositional prompts and multi‑concept scenarios?
3. Why does HiRM’s effectiveness vary across concept categories (e.g., style vs. object vs. NSFW), and what modifications can improve erasure of abstract concepts like nudity?
4. What key factors enable HiRM to transfer to unseen diffusion architectures (e.g., Flux) without retraining, while other text‑encoder methods (e.g., Diff‑QuickFix) exhibit limited transferability?

## Limitations
- Training hyperparameters (batch size, optimizer details, scheduler) are omitted, preventing exact replication.
- Guided‑concept prompt selection for HiRM‑S is ad‑hoc with no systematic rule.
- Token alignment between target and misdirection prompts is unspecified; differing token lengths require handling.
- Transferability claims are limited to CLIP‑based models; not tested on non‑CLIP encoders (e.g., T5 in SD3).
- Fixed steering coefficient may require tuning for some concepts to balance erasure and retention.

## Confidence
- Efficacy: High (consistent >95% UA & >89% retention across styles/objects).
- Adversarial Robustness: Medium (Ring‑A‑Bell ASR ≤1.05%, but only one attack type evaluated).
- Cross‑Architecture Transfer: Medium‑High (Flux1.dev results positive, but limited to one alternative).
- Computational Efficiency: High (training ~1 s, 1.6 GB; no U‑Net modifications).
