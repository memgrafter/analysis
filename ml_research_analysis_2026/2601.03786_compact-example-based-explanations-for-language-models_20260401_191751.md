# Compact Example-Based Explanations for Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03786
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03786
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: The proposed facility location-based selection strategy improves selection relevance by up to 9 dB over naive selection and achieves top aggregate scores (e.g., 29.03 dB AUC for LESS estimator).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the largely overlooked role of selection strategies in example-based explanations for language models, where only a small subset of training data is presented. It introduces a retraining-free metric called selection relevance (ξSR) that quantifies how well the gradients of a selected set of training examples linearly reconstruct the gradient of a test instance. A higher ξSR indicates that the selected examples compactly and non‑redundantly represent the gradient information relevant to the test input. The authors also propose a facility location–based selection strategy that balances influence (via a cost term) and representativeness (coverage in gradient space). They find that naive ranking strategies (e.g., top‑k most influential) often perform worse than random selection, while the proposed method significantly improves ξSR and correlates with an alternative fine‑tuning–based measure of relevance. Validation experiments confirm that sets with higher ξSR tend to reinforce the model’s original predictions after a single fine‑tuning step, especially when ξSR > 0 dB.

## Method Summary
The evaluation framework uses three small language models (OLMo2‑1B, Llama‑3.2‑1B, Qwen2.5‑0.5B) fine‑tuned on the Tülu3 supervised‑finetuning mixture. For each model, a random 10% of the training set (~86.6k examples) serves as the attribution pool, and 1,000 disjoint test instances are sampled. Influence estimators (DataInf, LESS, and BM25) produce per‑example scores for a given test input. From the estimator ranking, a shortlist (top N=100) is taken to bound computational cost. Selection strategies then pick k examples from this shortlist: naive (most/least influential), DIVINE, AIDE, or the proposed facility‑location (FL) with various λ values. The core selection relevance (ξSR) is computed by solving a non‑negative, simplex‑constrained least‑squares problem that approximates the test gradient as a linear combination of the selected gradients. The score is reported in dB as 10·log₁₀(‖G‖² / ‖G − A·t‖²). For validation, one‑step fine‑tuning (learning rate 1e‑5) on the selected set yields two metrics: prediction support ξ⁺ (difference in log‑likelihood of the original output vs. a random set) and prediction shift ξ_JSD (ratio of Jensen‑Shannon divergences). The pipeline is: influence estimation → shortlisting → selection → scoring; the fine‑tuning validation is run separately for correlation analysis.

## Key Results
- Naive top‑k selection (most influential) often underperforms random selection in terms of ξSR, especially for gradient‑based estimators, highlighting the risk of directly using raw influence scores.
- The facility‑location strategy with λ ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} significantly improves ξSR, up to 9 dB over the naive “most influential” baseline; for the LESS estimator it achieves an AUC of 29.03 dB across budgets k ∈ {1,5,10,25}.
- Selection relevance (ξSR) correlates positively with fine‑tuning support (ξ⁺) when restricted to sets with ξSR > 0 dB (Spearman ρ = 0.58). A simple heuristic—select sets with ξSR > 0 dB—predicts that fine‑tuning will reinforce the original prediction in most cases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Selection Relevance Score (ξSR)
The score treats an explanation as a gradient reconstruction task: given a test gradient G, we seek non‑negative coefficients t that sum to 1 such that a linear combination of selected training gradients A reproduces G. The ratio of the expected squared norm of a randomly sampled training gradient to the expected reconstruction error (in dB) measures how well the set captures the relevant gradient directions. A higher ξSR indicates a compact, non‑redundant representation of the test gradient. Evidence: Formal definition in Equation 1 (Section 3.1); validation via fine‑tuning shows that high‑ξSR sets tend to support the original prediction (Section 4.3).

### Facility Location‑Based Selection
Naive ranking fails because the most influential examples are often outliers or redundant. The proposed strategy extends the classic max‑cover (facility location) objective by adding a cost term derived from influence scores. The marginal gain for adding example j to set S is Δλ(j|S) = (Δ(j|S)+1)^λ / c_j^(1‑λ), where Δ(j|S) is the increase in coverage (sum of max cosine similarities to already selected examples) and c_j is the influence‑based cost. Greedy selection from a top‑100 shortlist balances coverage (representativeness in gradient space) and low cost (high influence). Evidence: t‑SNE visualizations (Section 4.1) show improved coverage; quantitative results (Table 1, Figure 4) show FL (λ>0) often outperforms pure influence (λ=0) and random.

### Correlation with Fine‑Tuning Support
If a selected set is truly relevant to explaining the test instance, using it for a small fine‑tuning step should increase the model’s confidence in the original output more than a random set would, while causing less distributional shift. This is formalized via ξ⁺ and ξ_JSD. Empirically, a strong positive correlation between ξSR and ξ⁺ emerges only when ξSR > 0 dB, suggesting that positive reconstruction quality is a necessary condition for the set to act as a “supporting” explanation. Evidence: Section 4.3 computes Spearman correlations; Figure 6 illustrates the threshold effect.

## Foundational Learning
### Influence Functions & Gradient‑Based Data Valuation
Why needed: Influence estimators (DataInf, LESS) are the backbone of the selection framework; they provide the initial rankings from which shortlists are drawn. Understanding that these methods approximate the effect of a training example on model parameters or predictions via Hessian‑inversed gradients is essential.  
Quick check: What is the key limitation of using raw influence scores (e.g., IF) for selecting a small explanation set, according to Barshan et al. (2020) as cited? *Answer: Raw influence scores can be dominated by outliers and are not necessarily diverse, leading to redundant or unrepresentative selections.*

### Submodular Optimization for Diverse Selection
Why needed: The facility location strategy and baselines (DIVINE, AIDE) rely on submodular maximization to ensure diversity while maintaining theoretical guarantees (greedy algorithm provides a (1‑1/e)‑approximation for monotone submodular functions).  
Quick check: What does the λ parameter in the Δλ(j|S) objective control, and what are the two extreme cases (λ=0, λ=1)? *Answer: λ trades off coverage (Δ) against cost (influence). λ=0 reduces to pure influence ranking (cost‑only); λ=1 reduces to pure coverage (max‑cover).*

### LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) for Efficient Fine‑Tuning & Attribution
Why needed: The paper fine‑tunes 1B‑parameter models with LoRA to manage computational costs and restricts influence estimation to LoRA layers. This impacts the gradient space used for selection and must be understood for reproduction.  
Quick check: Why might restricting influence estimation to LoRA layers (instead of all model parameters) bias the resulting influence rankings or selection relevance scores? *Answer: LoRA layers capture only a subset of the model’s learned information; gradients from these layers may not fully represent the influence of training examples on the full model, potentially omitting important interactions and leading to biased or incomplete influence estimates.*

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Influence Estimator → Shortlist → Selection Strategy → Scoring (Validation separate)
- **Critical path:** The runtime path for evaluating a new selection strategy is: estimator produces rankings → shortlist takes top‑N → selection strategy picks k → scoring computes ξSR.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Computational cost vs. accuracy*: Shortlisting (N=100) makes greedy facility location feasible but may exclude globally optimal examples.  
  *Gradient space vs. semantic space*: ξSR is computed in gradient space where influence operates, yet user explanations are in text/embedding space; high ξSR does not guarantee semantic coherence.  
  *Selection budget (k)*: Smaller k magnifies the impact of each individual choice; selection strategies may vary in performance across k.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  • ξSR < 0 dB: selected set is less informative than the trivial baseline (using no examples), indicating a flawed selection logic.  
  • Facility location not outperforming naive: If λ=0 (pure influence) yields higher ξSR than λ>0, the top‑100 shortlist likely lacks diverse, high‑influence examples.  
  • Low correlation (ρ ≈ 0) between ξSR and ξ⁺/ξ_JSD: suggests either ξSR does not capture “support” or the one‑step fine‑tuning protocol (learning rate, batch size) is mismatched.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Sanity check validation**: Fine‑tune on the test instance itself (S = {test}) and verify ξ⁺ > 0 and ξ_JSD < 1 in >95% of cases, confirming the validation metrics behave as expected.  
  2. **Main scoring benchmark**: For each estimator‑model pair, compute ξSR for all selection strategies (naive, DIVINE, AIDE, FL) across k ∈ {1,5,10,25}. Identify strategies that consistently beat random selection and achieve top aggregate AUC.  
  3. **Correlation analysis**: Compute Spearman’s ρ between ξSR and (a) ξ⁺, (b) ξ_JSD across all selections. Test whether re‑ranking with FL significantly alters the correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can explanation faithfulness be evaluated for example‑based explanations at human‑interpretable selection budgets? The paper notes a need for dedicated evaluations of fidelity or plausibility beyond proxy metrics.
- **Open Question 2:** Why do gradient‑based influence estimators (DataInf, LESS) underperform random selection in selection relevance, while BM25 (token overlap) often succeeds? The root cause—whether due to LoRA restriction, data saturation, or inherent properties of influence functions—remains unclear.
- **Open Question 3:** Does the selection relevance framework remain effective for large language models (>10B parameters) without LoRA approximations? Experiments were limited to 0.5–1B models for computational reasons; scalability to larger models is untested.
- **Open Question 4:** How can the trade‑off parameter λ in the facility location strategy be automatically tuned? The method currently requires manual selection from a set of λ values; a principled automatic tuning would enhance practicality.

## Limitations
- ξSR assumes that gradients can be linearly reconstructed; this may fail if gradients are saturated or the model’s local behavior is highly non‑linear.
- Facility location gains depend on the top‑100 shortlist, the λ hyperparameter, and the gradient similarity measure; robustness to different choices is uncertain.
- The correlation between ξSR and fine‑tuning support is weak except when ξSR > 0 dB, raising questions about the sufficiency of one‑step fine‑tuning as a validation protocol.
- Reproducibility is hindered by vague details (e.g., exact random projection distribution, gradient extraction specifics, DIVINE hyperparameter ranges).

## Confidence
- Claim 1 (ξSR relevance): Medium – the linear reconstruction assumption is clear but not fully verified.
- Claim 2 (FL improves relevance): High – empirical gains are consistent across estimators and budgets.
- Claim 3 (ξSR predicts fine‑tuning support): Low – correlation is weak and conditional on ξSR > 0 dB.

## Next Checks
1. Non‑linear reconstruction: Replace linear least‑squares with kernel methods and compare resulting selection rankings to see if non‑linear modeling improves ξSR or alignment with fine‑tuning support.
2. Sensitivity analysis: Vary shortlist size (N=50–500) and λ values to verify that facility location gains are robust across these hyperparameters.
3. Human evaluation: Conduct a user study comparing high‑ξSR versus low‑ξSR selections to assess perceived relevance and faithfulness of explanations.
