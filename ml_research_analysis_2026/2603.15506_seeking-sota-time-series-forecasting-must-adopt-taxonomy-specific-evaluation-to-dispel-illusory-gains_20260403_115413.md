# Seeking SOTA: Time-Series Forecasting Must Adopt Taxonomy-Specific Evaluation to Dispel Illusory Gains

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15506
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15506
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Simple classical methods (e.g., AR) and ultra-lightweight models (<1k parameters) match or outperform state-of-the-art transformer architectures on canonical long-horizon TSF benchmarks due todataset periodicity bias, exposing illusory gains from complexity.

## Executive Summary
This paper exposes a critical evaluation flaw in long-horizon time-series forecasting (TSF). The nine canonical benchmark datasets (ETTh1/2, ETTm1/2, Traffic, Electricity, Exchange-Rate, Weather, ILI) exhibit strong, persistent periodicities. Consequently, simple linear and statistical models—which explicitly capture seasonality—achieve performance comparable to or better than sophisticated transformer-based SOTA, despite having orders of magnitude fewer parameters. The apparent superiority of complex deep learning models is largely illusory, stemming from benchmark bias and inadequate baseline comparisons. The authors urgently call for taxonomy-specific evaluation protocols, the retirement or augmentation of current datasets, and mandatory reporting of robust classical baselines to ensure genuine, computationally justified progress.

## Method Summary
The study conducts a systematic empirical comparison between classical statistical baselines (AR, ARIMA, ETS), simple linear models (DLinear), and minimalist deep learning architectures (SparseTSF) against prominent transformer-based TSF models (e.g., Informer, Autoformer). The evaluation spans the nine standard long-horizon datasets, using standard chronological splits and common metrics (MSE, MAE). The core methodology involves re-implementing key models and running them under consistent conditions, supplemented by analysis of autocorrelation functions (ACF) to diagnose periodicity, and decomposition of aggregated metrics to reveal horizon-specific error patterns.

## Key Results
- **Classical competitiveness:** Well-tuned AR and Auto-AR models achieve MSE/MAE scores on par with the best transformers across all datasets and forecast horizons, requiring minimal computational resources.
- **Ultra-lightweight sufficiency:** SparseTSF (<1k parameters) performs competitively, demonstrating that the marginal gains from massive transformer architectures are not justified by predictive accuracy on these benchmarks.
- **Benchmark bias confirmed:** ACF analysis reveals dominant, persistent seasonal cycles (e.g., lag 24 for hourly data) in all benchmark series, which simple models exploit effectively, while the residual component left for deep models is small and noisy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The mainstream LTSF benchmarks contain strong, persistent periodicities that simple linear or statistical models can capture effectively, so the apparent advantage of complex deep learning models is illusory.
- **Mechanism:** Periodic signals cause high autocorrelation at seasonal lags (e.g., lag 24 for daily cycles in hourly data). Simple models — such as seasonal AR, ETS, or linear regression with seasonal dummies — directly encode these cycles. Deep models, despite large capacities, end up learning similar periodic patterns because the residual (non-periodic) component is small and noisy, so the extra complexity does not yield lower out-of-sample error.
- **Core assumption:** The dominant signal in the benchmark time series is repetitive and stationary across the train/test split; any leftover variation is essentially random or low-signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "We demonstrate that these ‘standard’ datasets often exhibit dominant auto‑correlation patterns and seasonal cycles that can be effectively captured by simpler linear or statistical models..."
  - [section] (PAGE 3): "SparseTSF effectively decompose a time series X(t) into its periodic component P(t) ... By isolating or accounting for the highly regular P(t), the model is left to primarily learn a mapping function f:X(t)→T(t), a task often achievable with a simple MLP layer."
  - [corpus]: ACF plots (Appendix E) show clear peaks at lag 24 in all ETTh1 features, persisting in train and test splits.
- **Break condition:** If benchmarks start including series with weak or disrupted seasonality (structural breaks, drifting volatility), the relative performance of simple models may drop and complex models could show genuine gains, provided those gains exceed the added computational cost.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Aggregating errors over long horizons and across many series hides horizon‑specific weaknesses of deep models and allows scale‑dependent biases to exaggerate their performance.
- **Mechanism:** MSE and MAE are typically averaged over all forecast steps and all series. This collapses a multi‑dimensional evaluation (by horizon and by series) into a single number. For periodicity‑dominated data, deep models may have similar short-horizon errors but accumulate more error as horizon increases, yet the average may look comparable. Moreover, averaging across series without normalization gives disproportionate weight to series with larger variance, which can favor models that fit high-variance series better.
- **Core assumption:** The evaluation relies on scalar summaries that do not expose the distribution of errors across horizons or properly account for scale differences among series.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...rendering complex deep‑learning architectures frequently no more performant than their classical counterparts for these specific data characteristics, and raising questions as to whether any marginal improvements justify the significant increase in computational overhead and model complexity."
  - [section] (PAGE 7): "Researchers often naïvely average metrics across series of vastly different scales [39], implicitly giving more weight to larger‑scale series."
  - [section] (PAGE 8): "Inappropriate metric choices can thus yield distorted performance rankings."
  - [corpus]: "ModelRadar" argues that averaging performance over all samples dilutes relevant information, especially for long‑horizon forecasts where error trajectories matter.
- **Break condition:** Adopting horizon‑stratified metrics (e.g., MSE at each lead time) and scale‑free measures like MASE would reveal whether deep models truly generalize across the full forecast range; if their errors increase faster with horizon, the aggregated illusion would break.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The frequent omission of robust classical baselines in published deep learning TSF papers creates a false impression that sophisticated models are necessary for good performance.
- **Mechanism:** Authors often compare their new deep architecture only against previous deep models (a "SOTA chasing" cycle). When a simple, well‑tuned ARIMA or ETS model — which no one would claim is a strong competitor for general sequential prediction — is finally included, it frequently matches or beats the deep models on these periodic benchmarks. This selection bias inflates the perceived value of complexity.
- **Core assumption:** The community’s reviewing and benchmarking standards do not mandate inclusion of appropriate naïve or classical methods; authors choose baselines that make their contributions look good.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "We call on the community to ... require every deep‑learning submission to include robust classical and simple baselines..."
  - [section] (PAGE 8): "Exclusion of naïve, relevant statistical baseline methods. For instance, Bergmeir et al. recount ... never against a naïve baseline – which in fact outperformed all those advanced models."
  - [section] (PAGE 8, Table 3): The consolidated results show AR and Auto‑AR achieving MSE values comparable to the best transformers, despite being vastly simpler.
  - [corpus]: "Tsururu" library promotes comparison of many strategies, implicitly supporting the need for comprehensive baselines.
- **Break condition:** If conference and journal guidelines require explicit reporting of performance against a set of standard classical baselines (e.g., ARIMA, ETS, naive seasonal), and those baselines consistently match or exceed deep models, the field will recognize that many claimed advances are not substantive.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Time Series Decomposition**
  - **Why needed here:** Recognizing that many forecastable series can be split into trend, seasonal, and residual components explains why models that explicitly handle seasonality (e.g., ETS, SARIMA) are strong competitors in benchmarks with strong periodicities. It also shows that deep models may be inadvertently learning these components.
  - **Quick check:** "A monthly retail series shows a yearly peak every December. Which forecasting method would most directly capture this pattern, and why?"

- **Concept: Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Seasonal Identification**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper uses ACF plots to demonstrate persistent periodicity across train/test splits in the LTSF datasets. Understanding how to read ACF to identify seasonal lags is essential to diagnose whether a dataset has the characteristics that favor simple models.
  - **Quick check:** "Given the ACF of a daily series, significant spikes at lags 7, 14, 21 suggest what? How would you set the seasonal period in a SARIMA model?"

- **Concept: Evaluation Metric Properties (Scale‑dependence, Horizon Sensitivity)**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper criticizes the exclusive use of MSE/MAE. Understanding the trade‑offs among metrics (scale‑dependent vs. scale‑free, sensitivity to outliers, horizon aggregation) is crucial for designing fair comparisons and avoiding misleading conclusions.
  - **Quick check:** "Why might MASE be a better metric than MAE when comparing forecast accuracy across different time series with different units?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Data Preparation (raw files -> normalized train/val/test splits) -> Classical Baselines (AR/ARIMA/ETS) & Deep Models (DLinear/SparseTSF/Transformers) -> Metrics Computation (MSE/MAE/MASE per series/horizon) -> Aggregation & Analysis (tables, plots)
- **Critical path:**
  1.  **Data preparation:** Download datasets; normalize each series using training statistics (mean/std); ensure no leakage. Save fixed train/val/test indices.
  2.  **Classical baselines:** For each dataset, fit auto_arima on the training data (including exogenous if desired; simplest is univariate). Use `stepwise=True` to speed up. Forecast each horizon; store predictions.
  3.  **Deep models:** Train DLinear and SparseTSF on each dataset with official hyperparameters (lookback = 96 or 512 depending). Early stop on validation. Generate test forecasts.
  4.  **Evaluation:** Calculate MSE, MAE, MASE for each model, per horizon and averaged.
  5.  **Comparison:** Recreate a table like Table 3, adding classical results. Highlight that AR and Auto‑AR are competitive.
  6.  **Analysis:** Plot per‑horizon error curves for a representative dataset to show error accumulation patterns.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Univariate vs. multivariate classical:** ARIMA traditionally models single series; to use exogenous, switch to ARIMAX or `auto_arima` with `X` argument. This adds complexity but may improve accuracy if covariates are informative. However, using only past target is a fairer comparison since many deep models also only use past covariates; future covariates are unknown.
  - **Normalization:** Deep models typically standardize inputs; classical models can handle raw data but may benefit from similar preprocessing. Must apply inverse transform for fair error calculation on original scale.
  - **Computation:** Classical fitting is fast (seconds per series). Deep models require hours on GPU. For large multivariate datasets (Traffic with 862 channels), one may forecast each target separately or use a single multivariate model. Simpler: follow the paper’s setting: target is ‘OT’ and input includes all other channels as exogenous? The paper’s setup often uses all channels as input and forecasts the target. Classical AR with exogenous would then use all channels at time t to predict target at t+1. That is feasible via linear regression with lags.
  - **Metric choice:** MSE/MAE are scale‑dependent; MASE allows cross-dataset comparison. Use both.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Classical baseline unexpectedly poor:** Check that seasonal period `m` is set correctly (e.g., 24 for hourly ETTh). Verify that training data is not over‑differenced. Ensure auto_arima explores seasonal orders.
  - **Deep model underperforms relative to paper:** Could be due to different splits, normalization, or hyperparameters. Align exactly with published splits and preprocessing (e.g., use the same data files from the official repo).
  - **Extreme error accumulation:** If a model’s error grows super‑linearly with horizon, it may be overfitting short-term patterns; plot per‑horizon error to detect.
  - **Data leakage:** Normalization using test statistics inflates performance; always compute mean/std only on training set (or train+val) before applying to val/test.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Reproduce SOTA:** Run SparseTSF on ETTh2 with horizon 720 and confirm it achieves MSE ≈ 0.307 (from Table 3) with < 1 k parameters. This validates that ultra‑light models are competitive.
  2.  **Classical baseline competition:** Fit auto_arima on the entire ETT training + validation (using only training for parameter selection) and forecast the test set for all four horizons. Compare the MSE numbers to the deep models. Expect AR to be within the range of transformer results.
  3.  **Metric sensitivity test:** Compute MASE for each model on Exchange‑Rate dataset using a seasonal naive (period = 1, because no clear seasonality) as the scale denominator. Observe whether the ranking changes when using a scale‑free metric compared to MSE; this reveals whether apparent gains are merely due to scale differences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Are self-attention mechanisms in transformers fundamentally ill-suited for time-series forecasting due to their permutation invariance, and can architectural modifications overcome this limitation?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3 critiques transformers for permutation-invariant self-attention, citing Zeng et al. [68] that positional encodings only partially mitigate the loss of temporal structure. The paper questions if this inherent flaw explains why simple linear models outperform transformers on LTSF benchmarks.
  - **Why unresolved:** Despite empirical evidence (e.g., LTSF-Linear outperforming transformers), it remains unclear whether this is a universal limitation or specific to current transformer implementations. Theoretical analyses are inconclusive, and newer architectures may address this.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating the effect of permutation invariance by comparing transformer variants with and without strict temporal inductive biases (e.g., fixed positional encodings, time-aware attention) on diverse time-series taxonomies, measuring performance gaps relative to non-time-aware models. Additionally, theoretical proofs bounding the expressivity of time-aware vs. standard transformers for sequential data.

- **Question:** How can benchmark suites for long-horizon time-series forecasting be systematically augmented to include datasets with diverse non-stationarities (e.g., structural breaks, concept drift, heteroscedasticity) to ensure models generalize beyond periodic patterns?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The abstract and Section 6 call for retiring or augmenting current benchmarks with datasets exhibiting a wider spectrum of non-stationarities, less predictable dynamics, and from diverse domains. The authors argue current datasets are biased toward strong periodicity.
  - **Why unresolved:** While the need is clear, there is no consensus on which non-stationarity types are most critical, how to curate or synthetically generate such datasets with ground-truth properties, or how to balance dataset diversity with real-world relevance. Existing efforts like GIFT-Eval are mentioned but not detailed.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** A taxonomy-driven curation of new benchmark datasets, each annotated with specific non-stationarity characteristics (e.g., break points, volatility regimes). Community-wide evaluation cycles on these augmented benchmarks, tracking whether performance rankings change significantly compared to periodicity-dominated ones. Success metrics: models that excel on new benchmarks but not old ones reveal overfitting to periodicity.

- **Question:** What constitutes a robust, appropriately chosen classical baseline for a given time-series forecasting task, and how can baseline selection be standardized to enable fair comparisons across papers?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.1.2 and Appendix B mandate comparisons against naïve, classical statistical (e.g., ARIMA, ETS), and simple ML baselines, chosen according to data characteristics (e.g., seasonal naive for seasonal series). The paper criticizes the common omission of such baselines.
  - **Why unresolved:** Baseline choice is currently ad-hoc and often omitted. There's no standard protocol for which baseline to use for which data type, how to tune them (e.g., ARIMA orders), or how to report their results (e.g., using best-found parameters vs. default). This leads to incomparable evaluations.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a baseline selection guide based on time-series taxonomy (e.g., trend/seasonality strength, stationarity tests) and task horizon. Empirical studies showing that failure to include certain baselines leads to inflated SOTA claims. Community adoption via integrated benchmarking libraries that automatically recommend and run relevant baselines given dataset characteristics.

- **Question:** Under what circumstances do global time-series foundation models (trained on many series) provide genuine utility over specialized local models for individual forecasting tasks, and does aggregated SOTA on heterogeneous benchmarks misleadingly favor global models?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.2 draws an analogy to Stein's Paradox, suggesting global models may achieve high aggregated scores by "shrinking" towards a common structure, potentially sacrificing peak performance on individual series. The paper questions if aggregated SOTA reflects true task-specific utility.
  - **Why unresolved:** Most evaluations report average metrics across datasets, obscuring per-dataset or per-series performance. It's unclear whether global models are universally competitive or only on certain series types. The trade-off between generality and specialization is not quantified.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-grained evaluation reporting: per-dataset breakdowns for global vs. local models, analysis of which series benefit from global training (e.g., short, noisy series) and which suffer (e.g., long, stationary series). Studies measuring the "Stein risk" increase for outlier series when using global models. Development of evaluation metrics that balance average performance with worst-case or task-specific performance.

## Limitations
- **Dataset split ambiguity:** Exact train/val/test timestamps for non-ETT datasets (Traffic, Electricity, Exchange-Rate, Weather, ILI) are unspecified; minor deviations could alter comparative results.
- **Classical baseline tuning:** Paper lacks details on AR lag selection, ARIMA max orders, and ETS seasonality type; improper tuning could understate classical performance.
- **Generalizability constraint:** Findings are confined to nine canonical periodic datasets; performance on non-periodic, volatile, or structurally broken series remains unknown.
