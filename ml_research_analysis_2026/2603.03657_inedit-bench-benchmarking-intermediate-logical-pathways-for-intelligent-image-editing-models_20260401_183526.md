# InEdit-Bench: Benchmarking Intermediate Logical Pathways for Intelligent Image Editing Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03657
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03657
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Current image editing models fail at multi-step logical reasoning, with the best model (GPT-Image-1) achieving 81.33 overall score but only 16.75% perfect samples, and open-source models often scoring near zero on process metrics.

## Executive Summary
Current image editing models excel at static, single-step transformations but lack the ability to perform dynamic, multi-step reasoning required for complex procedural edits. This work introduces InEdit-Bench, a benchmark with 237 hand-annotated test cases across four task categories (state transition, dynamic process, temporal sequence, scientific simulation) and six evaluation metrics. The benchmark shifts evaluation from final output quality to the coherence of the entire intermediate logical pathway. Evaluation of 14 models reveals widespread deficiencies: the top model achieves a high overall score but very low perfect-sample accuracy, while open-source models lag significantly. Performance declines systematically from continuous to discrete tasks and from surface phenomena to scientific principles, exposing critical gaps in long-term dependency and causal reasoning.

## Method Summary
InEdit-Bench provides 237 test instances, each consisting of an initial image, a final target image, and a textual instruction. Models must generate a single composite grid image showing *N* intermediate stages that form a coherent transformation process. The benchmark defines six metrics: three standard visual quality metrics (Appearance Consistency, Perceptual Quality, Semantic Consistency) and three novel process-oriented metrics (Logical Coherence, Scientific Plausibility, Process Plausibility). Evaluation uses an LMM-as-a-Judge approach (GPT-4o) with task-specific prompts and, for scientific plausibility, human-curated knowledge checklists. Four task categories structure the dataset, each with distinct reasoning demands: state transition (discrete jumps), dynamic process (continuous fluid changes), temporal sequence (aligned timeline), and scientific simulation (law adherence).

## Key Results
- GPT-Image-1 achieved the highest overall score (81.33) but a perfect-sample accuracy of only 16.75%, indicating many flaws even in the best output.
- Open-source models significantly underperformed, with several scoring near zero on Logical Coherence and Scientific Plausibility.
- A clear performance gradient exists: models perform better on continuous tasks than discrete ones, and on surface phenomena than scientific principles, revealing deficits in long-term dependency capture and causal reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The benchmark exposes deficiencies in multi-step logical reasoning by requiring a coherent intermediate sequence, not just a plausible final output.
- **Mechanism**: By providing initial and final images plus an instruction, the task shifts from single-step editing to synthesizing an entire *process*. This penalizes models relying on static correspondence or memorization and rewards those maintaining cross-step consistency and causal progression.
- **Core assumption**: A model’s inability to produce a logically coherent sequence—even with a correct final image—indicates a lack of procedural understanding.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract states the benchmark shifts focus from “destination” to “intermediate logical pathways.” Section 1 argues static tasks leave “procedural reasoning largely underdeveloped.” Corpus neighbors (e.g., OmniGen2) do not evaluate intermediate reasoning pathways.
- **Break condition**: If future models achieve high final-image quality *and* perfect process metrics without architectural changes targeting recurrence/temporal modeling, the diagnostic power diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The three novel process-oriented metrics reliably measure reasoning deficits that standard image quality metrics cannot capture.
- **Mechanism**: These metrics decouple *visual fidelity* from *procedural soundness*. Logical Coherence checks adjacency transitions; Scientific Plausibility enforces domain knowledge; Process Plausibility tests ordered constraint adherence. This multi-dimensional scoring reveals whether a model is stitching semantically correct endpoints or simulating an evolution.
- **Core assumption**: High scores on appearance/perceptual/semantic consistency are necessary but insufficient for intelligence; process metrics are orthogonal and expose systematic flaws.
- **Evidence anchors**: Sections 3.2–3.4 define the six metrics, emphasizing the latter three “scrutinize the plausibility of intermediate processes” and are “novel.” The abstract notes “three novel process-oriented metrics” alongside standard ones.
- **Break condition**: If correlations between process metrics and visual metrics approach 1.0 across models, the novel dimensions may be redundant.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The four-category taxonomy systematically stratifies reasoning difficulty, revealing a performance gradient from continuous to discrete and surface to deep.
- **Mechanism**: Each category imposes distinct cognitive demands: discrete state transitions require identifying key nodes; dynamic processes demand continuous, mechanism-driven fluidity; temporal sequences need timeline-aligned phase evolution; scientific simulations mandate adherence to formal laws. This structure pinpoints underdeveloped reasoning capabilities.
- **Core assumption**: Reasoning is not monolithic; discrete logic, continuous dynamics, temporal ordering, and scientific constraints are separable skills.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.1 dedicates paragraphs to each category, outlining unique challenges (e.g., “structural reconstruction” vs. “biological principles”). The abstract states results show “performance declined systematically from continuous to discrete tasks and from surface phenomena to scientific principles.”
- **Break condition**: If models eventually perform equally across all four categories after scaling, the taxonomy loses diagnostic value.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Multi-Step Causal Reasoning in Visual Modalities
- **Why needed here**: The model must infer and generate a sequence where each visual state causally follows from the previous, not just match endpoints. This requires simulating transformations, tracking object permanence, and maintaining consistency over time.
- **Quick check**: Can the model generate a 5-stage sequence showing a piece of paper being folded into an origami crane, with each fold clearly and progressively altering the shape without skipping steps?

### Concept: Process-Oriented Evaluation vs. Outcome-Oriented Evaluation
- **Why needed here**: Traditional image editing judges only the final output’s fidelity. InEdit-Bench assesses the *entire pathway*—logical flow, absence of redundancy, and adherence to path constraints.
- **Quick check**: Given start/end images of a melting ice cube, does the model produce a grid showing gradual reduction in size and increasing water pool, without jumping from solid to puddle?

### Concept: Domain Knowledge Integration in Visual Generation
- **Why needed here**: For scientific simulation tasks, intermediate stages must obey real-world principles (e.g., diffusion, chemical reaction stoichiometry). This requires embedding explicit knowledge into generation, not just visual plausibility.
- **Quick check**: Can the model render the stages of magnesium burning in air, correctly showing the bright white flame and white ash formation in sequence, respecting the reaction’s phenomenology?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input Encoder -> Intermediate Pathway Generator -> Process Metrics Evaluator. Path Constraint Parser conditions the Generator for process plausibility tasks.

### Critical path
1. Receive initial image, final image, and instruction (with possible path constraints).
2. Encoder fuses visual and textual inputs into a joint representation.
3. Generator synthesizes a grid-image where each cell is an intermediate state; cell count *N* is determined by task category/instruction keywords.
4. For process plausibility tasks, generator is conditioned on one of two path orders.
5. Evaluation module (LMM-as-a-Judge) assesses the grid on all six metrics using separate prompts/checklists.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Grid format vs. continuous video**: Grids simplify evaluation but discretize continuous processes, potentially forcing unnatural jumps.
- **LMM-as-a-Judge vs. human eval**: Automation enables large-scale assessment but introduces evaluator bias; paper shows high correlation with human eval (r=0.96) but relies on a single proprietary model (GPT-4o).
- **Manual keyframe annotation**: For dynamic/scientific tasks, keyframes guide the model, reducing degrees of freedom but possibly constraining valid solutions.
- **Adaptive *N* determination**: Letting the model decide stage count matches process complexity but complicates fair cross-sample comparison; fixed *N* per category might simplify but lose granularity.

### Failure signatures
- **Missing/duplicate stages**: Grids with repeated or nearly identical cells indicate failure to generate meaningful transitions.
- **Inconsistent appearance**: Objects appear/disappear, change color arbitrarily, or violate preservation of irrelevant attributes.
- **Logical breaks**: Transitions between adjacent cells show regression (de-assembly), skipping, or physically impossible jumps.
- **Scientific violations**: For simulation tasks, intermediates ignore known laws (e.g., wrong phase changes, incorrect reaction products).
- **Path constraint violation**: In process plausibility, both path orders yield nearly identical grids, showing inability to follow ordering instructions.
- **Low semantic consistency**: Final grid cell does not match the target instruction even if intermediate steps are plausible.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline replication**: Run the 14 listed models on InEdit-Bench with default settings to confirm the reported score gaps, particularly the >50-point gap between proprietary and open-source models on logical coherence and scientific plausibility.
2. **Ablation of process metrics**: Evaluate models using only the three standard visual quality metrics. Compare rankings to the full six-metric evaluation to demonstrate how process metrics change perceived model capabilities.
3. **Path constraint fidelity test**: On the process plausibility subset, run each model twice with the two different path orders (Path_A and Path_B). Measure each model’s ability to generate distinct pathways adhering to each constraint versus generating the same pathway regardless of instruction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can multimodal generative architectures be redesigned to better capture long-term dependencies and perform causal reasoning across many intermediate steps, given that current models show severe performance drops on discrete and scientific simulation tasks?
2. To what extent are the reported performance gaps between proprietary and open-source models due to data scale/model size versus fundamental algorithmic limitations in handling non-deterministic, multi-path processes?
3. Is the LMM-as-a-Judge (GPT-4o) evaluation for "logical coherence" and "scientific plausibility" itself valid and unbiased, or does it reflect the evaluator's own limitations in understanding complex visual procedural reasoning?
4. What are the minimal sufficient conditions and task characteristics that make an intermediate pathway generation task truly challenging for current models, beyond the broad categories defined in InEdit-Bench?
5. Can the intermediate logical pathway generation task be reduced to or better framed as a different problem (e.g., video prediction, temporal slice interpolation, or program execution) to leverage existing techniques, or does it require a fundamentally new paradigm?

## Limitations
- **Artifact access**: Full dataset, prompts, and model outputs are not yet public; reproduction depends on their release.
- **Evaluator bias**: All process metrics rely on a single LMM (GPT-4o); while correlated with humans (r=0.96), prompt sensitivity and model-specific biases may skew scores.
- **Task discretization**: Forcing continuous processes into fixed grid stages may misrepresent truly fluid transformations, affecting process metric scores.

## Confidence
- **Diagnostic benchmark novelty**: High. Clear shift from outcome-only to pathway evaluation is well-motivated and structurally novel.
- **Process metrics reliability**: Medium. Multi-dimensional separation from visual metrics is compelling, but LMM-as-judge dependency and potential metric redundancy need broader validation.
- **Taxonomy stratification**: Medium. Empirical gradient (continuous→discrete, surface→deep) is observed, but causal links between category design and difficulty are not fully isolated.

## Next Checks
1. **Dataset release verification**: Confirm public availability of all 237 cases, instructions, and checklists; inspect image/license terms.
2. **LMM evaluator robustness test**: Run a subset with multiple LMM judges (e.g., Claude-3.5, GPT-4o-mini) and compare score distributions to detect judge-specific outliers.
3. **Grid-stage sensitivity analysis**: Re-run key samples with varying grid resolutions (e.g., 3-stage vs. 5-stage outputs) to quantify how discretization affects process metric scores.
