# 2-Step Agent: A Framework for the Interaction of a Decision Maker with AI Decision Support

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21889
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21889
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: A single misaligned prior about historical policy or covariate distribution can make ML decision support harmful, while an incorrect prior about treatment effect is corrected by Bayesian updating.

## Executive Summary
The impact of AI decision support on human choices remains poorly understood, especially when decision makers hold incorrect beliefs about the data‑generating process. This paper introduces the **2‑Step Agent**, a Bayesian framework that simulates how a rational agent incorporates machine‑learning predictions into its beliefs and then makes causal decisions. Using a synthetic medical case with linear regression as the ML model, the authors vary the agent’s priors for treatment effect, historical treatment policy, and covariate distribution. Results show that a single misaligned prior—particularly about the historical policy or covariate distribution—can degrade decision quality compared to having no decision support at all, even when the ML model is accurate and the agent is rational. In contrast, an incorrect prior for the treatment effect is naturally corrected by the Bayesian update, leading to improved outcomes. The work underscores the critical importance of user training and clear model documentation.

## Method Summary
The 2‑Step Agent operates in two phases. First, it updates its beliefs about the underlying data‑generating process (including means, variances, and treatment effect) by combining its prior with observed information: the ML model’s prediction (the OLS slope from a training sample) and, for a new patient, the covariate value $X_o$. This Bayesian inference uses a hierarchical prior and is implemented in PyMC, with the training plate collapsed via sufficient statistics for efficiency. Second, the posterior distribution is used to estimate the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). The agent then chooses a binary treatment $A\in\{10,20\}$ if the posterior expected CATE exceeds a threshold $\tau=5$. The synthetic world follows a linear structural causal model: $X\sim N(80,10)$, $A = 0.125\,X + N_A$, $Y = 12 -0.1\,X + A + N_Y$, with independent Gaussian noise $N_A\sim N(2,1)$, $N_Y\sim N(0,0.1)$. The ML model is a slope‑only linear regression of $Y$ on $X$ trained on $n=1000$ samples. Agent priors are centered at true values with small variances; misalignment experiments shift one prior mean across a grid while keeping others correct. Posterior predictive simulations then compare downstream outcome distributions with versus without ML‑DS.

## Key Results
- **Misaligned priors for historical policy ($\mu_A$) or covariate distribution ($\mu_X$) are sufficient to make ML‑DS harmful**: despite accurate ML predictions and rational Bayesian updating, decisions yield worse expected outcomes than ignoring the model.
- **An incorrect prior for the treatment effect ($N_E$) is automatically corrected** by the Bayesian update because the ML slope directly informs this parameter; the agent then makes better decisions than without support.
- The framework quantifies these effects and highlights that **user training and model documentation are essential** to align agent beliefs with what the ML model can (and cannot) convey.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The 2‑Step Agent’s Bayesian update is driven by the likelihood of the observed ML prediction (the slope $M$) and the new patient’s covariate $X_o$. Because the ML model is a slope‑only regression, $M$ encodes information about the sum of the direct effect of $X$ on $Y$ and the indirect effect via $A$ (i.e., $b+d$). However, $M$ is **uninformative about the means** $\mu_X$ and $\mu_A$ and only weakly constrains the outcome noise $\sigma_Y$. Consequently, if the agent’s prior for $\mu_A$ or $\mu_X$ is wrong, the posterior remains biased, leading to distorted estimates of the CATE and suboptimal decisions. In contrast, the treatment‑effect parameter $N_E$ (or its equivalent) is directly tied to $M$ through the structural equations; the observed $M$ tightly constrains $N_E$, so even a strongly mis‑specified prior is overwhelmed by the likelihood. This mechanism explains why only certain types of prior misalignment are harmful and provides a rigorous way to quantify those risks.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian inference**  
  *Why needed:* Enables a rational agent to combine prior beliefs with observed data (the ML prediction and patient covariates) to update uncertainty.  
  *Quick check:* Can the agent compute a posterior that properly incorporates the likelihood of the observed slope $M$ and $X_o$?
- **Hierarchical modeling**  
  *Why needed:* Allows the agent to express uncertainty over population parameters (means, variances) and capture dependencies.  
  *Quick check:* Are hyperparameters (e.g., variances) set such that the prior is proper and the model remains identifiable?
- **Linear regression (slope‑only)**  
  *Why needed:* Serves as the ML decision‑support model; the OLS slope $M$ is a sufficient statistic that the agent conditions on.  
  *Quick check:* Does the computed slope match the direct OLS estimate from the training data?
- **Structural causal model (SCM)**  
  *Why needed:* Defines the true data‑generating process against which the agent’s beliefs are compared.  
  *Quick check:* Do simulated samples obey the specified equations and independence assumptions?
- **Conditional average treatment effect (CATE)**  
  *Why needed:* The quantity that guides the agent’s treatment decision for the new patient.  
  *Quick check:* Is CATE correctly derived from the posterior parameters as $N_E \times \Delta A$?
- **Prior misalignment**  
  *Why needed:* Central to studying the impact of erroneous beliefs on decision quality.  
  *Quick check:* Does shifting a single prior mean (e.g., $\mu_A$) while holding others at truth reproduce the expected harm pattern?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
SCM (true DGP) -> Training Data (n=1000) -> ML Model (slope M) -> New Patient (X_o, pred_o) -> Bayesian Agent (priors on μ_X, σ_X, μ_A, σ_A, μ_Y, σ_Y, N_E) -> Posterior Inference (PyMC, sufficient statistics) -> CATE Estimation -> Decision Rule (A=20 if E[CATE]>5 else 10) -> Treatment -> Outcome Y

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate training data from SCM.  
2. Fit slope‑only linear regression to obtain $M$.  
3. Observe new patient’s $X_o$ and compute $pred_o = M X_o$.  
4. Bayesian agent updates beliefs using $(X_o, pred_o)$.  
5. Compute posterior expected CATE and select treatment.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Simplicity vs. realism:* Linear SCM and slope‑only model keep analysis tractable but may not capture complex real‑world interactions.  
- *Hyperprior variance:* Too narrow yields over‑confidence; too wide slows convergence and increases sensitivity to misspecification.  
- *Observation noise on $pred_o$:* A tiny variance ($10^{-3}$) is needed for a well‑behaved likelihood but must be small enough not to bias the update.  
- *Decision rule:* Using posterior mean CATE yields deterministic decisions; per‑draw stochastic decisions introduce variability that may better reflect human noise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Divergent transitions** during NUTS sampling indicate model misspecification or insufficient tuning.  
- **Low effective sample size (ESS)** for key parameters leads to noisy posterior estimates; remedy by increasing draws or reparameterizing.  
- **Incorrect sufficient statistics** (e.g., sign errors in the formulas from Appendix E) produce posteriors that do not match the direct OLS slope.  
- **Excessively large observation noise** around $pred_o$ dilutes the information from the ML model, reducing the update’s accuracy.

**First three experiments (from the paper):**  
1. Vary the prior mean for $\mu_A$ (historical treatment policy) while keeping all other priors at their true values.  
2. Vary the prior mean for $\mu_X$ (covariate distribution) with other priors correct.  
3. Vary the prior mean for $N_E$ (treatment effect) with other priors correct.  
These experiments isolate the effect of each type of misalignment and confirm the differential impact on decision quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do different forms of agent misalignment beyond priors—such as incorrect beliefs about model structure or non‑Bayesian reasoning—affect the outcomes of ML decision support?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Section "Limitations and Future work" states: "i) different types of agents (e.g. agent with incorrect internal model of the world of different kinds of uncertainties)".  
   *Why unresolved:* The current framework only considers Bayesian agents with specific prior errors; other cognitive limitations are not modeled.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Simulations with agents having wrong causal graphs or behavioral biases (e.g., anchoring) and comparing their outcomes to the Bayesian baseline.

2. **To what extent do the harmful effects of ML decision support generalize to non‑linear or high‑capacity machine learning models?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The limitations mention: "ii) more complex ML models for ML-DS".  
   *Why unresolved:* The study uses only linear regression; complex models (e.g., neural nets) may interact with agent beliefs differently.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Extend the framework to non‑linear models (e.g., Gaussian processes) and repeat the prior‑misalignment simulations.

3. **How does heterogeneity in treatment effects across individuals modulate the risk of adverse outcomes when agent priors are misaligned?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The limitations note: "iii) cases with heterogeneity of treatment effect (i.e. the effect of the action is not the same for all instances)".  
   *Why unresolved:* The SCM assumes a constant treatment effect; heterogeneity could create subgroups that are more vulnerable to prior errors.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Introduce interaction terms or varying coefficients in the SCM and analyze outcome disparities across prior misalignment conditions.

4. **What are the long‑term dynamics of agent learning and decision quality in sequential interactions with ML decision support, especially with initial prior misalignment?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The limitations list: "iv) repeated rounds of learning for the same agent".  
   *Why unresolved:* The framework considers a single decision; repeated use might allow belief correction or error accumulation.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Simulate multiple rounds where the agent updates beliefs from observed outcomes and new predictions, tracking convergence or persistent harm.

5. **How does employing a causal model for decision support, rather than a predictive model, change the interaction with agent priors and potential for harmful outcomes?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper notes: "v) using causal models for ML‑DS" and earlier mentions leaving interventional estimates for future work.  
   *Why unresolved:* The current ML‑DS is treatment‑naive; causal estimators might directly target the effect and could be more or less sensitive to prior misalignment.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Replace the slope‑only predictor with a causal estimator (e.g., double machine learning) and compare sensitivity to the same prior errors.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic‑only setting:** The use of a simple linear SCM and fixed parameters limits external validity to real‑world, high‑dimensional, or non‑linear contexts.
- **Missing specifications:** The exact value of $X_o$ is not reported; hyperprior variances for misaligned agents are assumed unchanged; the decision rule (posterior mean vs. per‑draw) is ambiguous, potentially affecting variability in results.
- **Implementation sensitivity:** The Bayesian posterior relies on correct truncated normals, exact sufficient‑statistic derivations, and extremely small observation noise ($10^{-3}$); pitfalls like divergent transitions or low ESS can bias outcomes.

## Confidence
- Framework validity (Bayesian two‑step formulation): **Medium**
- Harm from misaligned priors for historical policy or covariate distribution: **Medium**
- Automatic correction of biased treatment‑effect prior: **High**
- Implications for user training and documentation: **Low‑Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Real‑data replication:** Apply the 2‑Step Agent to an actual clinical dataset to verify that misaligned priors about policy or covariates lead to worse outcomes than no ML support.
2. **Hyperprior sensitivity:** Vary the hyperprior variances (e.g., from 0.01 to 1) while maintaining a misaligned mean to test the robustness of the harmful effects.
3. **Decision rule resolution:** Compare results when the agent uses the posterior mean CATE versus sampling per‑draw stochastic decisions to determine which better matches the reported variability and real human behavior.
