# Gender Bias in LLMs: Preliminary Evidence from Shared Parenting Scenario in Czech Family Law

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05879  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05879  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Some LLMs show gender bias in shared parenting recommendations under Czech family law, with Claude Haiku 4.5 allocating significantly more time to the female parent for identical negative factors.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates whether leading large language models (LLMs) exhibit gender bias when generating legal advice on shared parenting under Czech family law. Using an expert-designed divorce scenario, four LLMs—GPT-5 nano, Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Llama 3.3—were tested in zero-shot mode with two prompt variants: gendered parent names (Adam vs. Eve) and neutral labels (Parent1 vs. Parent2). Each parent was assigned one of nine legally relevant negative factors (e.g., substance abuse, violence) to measure the impact on recommended parenting time ratios. The results reveal systematic bias in certain models, most acutely in Claude Haiku 4.5, which allocated Eve (female) substantially more time than Adam (male) for the same adverse factor (e.g., 66.56% vs. 8.24% for child abuse). Across models, Eve generally received higher time allocations for severe factors, suggesting that LLMs may produce skewed legal guidance in sensitive family law contexts.

## Method Summary
The researchers developed a realistic divorce scenario rooted in Czech family law, focusing on joint parenting arrangements. This scenario was presented to four state-of-the-art LLMs using zero-shot prompts. Two experimental conditions were employed: one where parents were identified with gendered names (Adam and Eve) and another with neutral identifiers (Parent1 and Parent2). For each condition, one parent was systematically assigned one of nine negative factors—such as substance abuse, mental illness, child abuse, or partner violence—that are legally pertinent to parenting capacity. The LLMs’ outputs were parsed to extract recommended parenting time percentages, enabling a comparative analysis of how the same factor influenced allocations depending on the parent’s gendered name.

## Key Results
- Claude Haiku 4.5 displays stark gender asymmetry: for child abuse (factor 6), Eve receives 66.56% parenting time versus Adam’s 8.24%; for partner violence (factor 7), Eve receives 67.89% versus Adam’s 27.25%.
- Across all tested models, the female parent (Eve) tends to be allocated more parenting time than the male parent (Adam) when severe negative factors are applied.
- The pattern indicates a potential for LLMs to systematically bias legal advice in shared parenting determinations, with serious implications for their use in family law.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not investigate an explicit mechanism, but potential sources of the observed gender bias include:
- **Training data biases**: Pre-training corpora may contain societal stereotypes associating women with nurturing roles or men with negative behaviors, influencing model outputs in legal contexts.
- **Tokenization and embedding effects**: Gendered names like 'Adam' and 'Eve' might activate different semantic associations in the embedding space, leading to asymmetric processing.
- **Zero-shot prompt formulation**: The absence of explicit instructions to ignore gender may allow implicit biases to dominate the response generation.
Assumption: Bias emerges from statistical patterns in training data. Unknown: Specific internal model states or attention patterns responsible for the asymmetry.

## Architecture Onboarding
While the paper does not dissect architecture, the evaluated transformer-based models share common features where bias can propagate:
- **Component maps**: Attention layers may differentially weigh gender indicators when processing negative factors, affecting time allocation predictions.
- **Critical paths**: Input embeddings of gendered names through transformer blocks to output logits; bias could be amplified in later layers.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Model scale and training objectives (e.g., next-token prediction) may prioritize coherence over fairness, with limited bias mitigation.
- **Failure signatures**: Consistent output asymmetry for swapped gendered inputs indicates representational bias rather than random variance.
- **First experiments**: Probe intermediate representations for gender-direction vectors or use gradient-based attribution to identify influential tokens.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Based on the study's scope and results, key open questions include:
1. How generalizable is the bias to other legal systems (e.g., common law vs. civil law) or non-Western cultural contexts?
2. Would supervised fine-tuning on balanced legal datasets reduce or eliminate the observed gender asymmetry?
3. Do model architecture differences (e.g., decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder) influence bias severity in legal reasoning tasks?
4. How effective are debiasing techniques (e.g., prompt engineering, adversarial training) in this high-stakes domain?
5. What is the comparative performance of human experts versus LLMs on identical gendered/neutral scenarios, and where do discrepancies lie?

## Limitations
- Scenario limited to a single Czech family law context; generalizability to other legal domains, jurisdictions, or multi-factor cases is unknown.
- Only four specific LLM versions were tested; bias may vary across model families, sizes, or domain-fine-tuned variants.
- Zero-shot prompting exclusively; bias magnitude could differ with few-shot, chain-of-thought, or instruction-tuned legal prompts.

## Confidence
- Claude Haiku 4.5 shows pronounced gender asymmetry: **Medium** (consistent within-model pattern but single-model, single-scenario).
- Cross-model trend: Eve receives more time for severe factors: **Medium** (directional pattern across models but limited N=4 models, no statistical pooling).
- Implication of biased legal guidance: **Low** (extrapolates from narrow scenario to broad advisory context).
