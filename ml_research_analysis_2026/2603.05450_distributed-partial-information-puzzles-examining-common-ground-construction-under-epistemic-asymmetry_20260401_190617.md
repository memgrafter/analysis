# Distributed Partial Information Puzzles: Examining Common Ground Construction Under Epistemic Asymmetry
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05450
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05450
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Introduces DPIP, a multimodal common ground benchmark; DEL-based pipeline outperforms LLMs; LLMs fail to capture rich belief dynamics despite identifying absent common ground in one failed dialogue.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents the Distributed Partial Information Puzzle (DPIP), a collaborative construction task designed to study common ground formation under epistemic asymmetry. Three directors each see a different side view of a 3D Lego structure, while a builder manipulates blocks. The resulting interactions are recorded and annotated with temporally aligned speech, gesture, and actions. The authors evaluate two modeling approaches: state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) prompted to infer shared beliefs, and an axiomatic pipeline grounded in Dynamic Epistemic Logic (DEL). Using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) as the primary metric, they find that LLMs struggle to track belief states, often achieving near-zero alignment with ground-truth common ground. In contrast, the DEL-based approach yields competitive or superior performance on structure prediction. Notably, LLMs correctly detect an absence of common ground in a failed group dialogue but otherwise fail to match the nuanced dynamics captured by the annotations, positioning DPIP as a challenging benchmark for multimodal common ground tracking.

## Method Summary
The DPIP task involves three directors with distinct 2D views of a target 3×3×3 Lego structure and one builder who places blocks. The epistemic asymmetry forces directors to communicate spatial relations via speech and gestures, while the builder acts on these cues. The data collection yields a multimodal corpus with synchronized speech (transcribed and propositionalized via Whisper), gestures (annotated with GAMR), and actions (extracted from structure evolution using SAT). Cross-modality alignment merges these streams into turn-level logs, where turn boundaries are defined by updates to the common ground.

Two inference paradigms are evaluated. First, several LLMs (Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B, GPT-5) are prompted with either actions-only or fully aligned multimodal data per turn, and asked to predict the current structure or shared beliefs. Second, an axiomatic DEL pipeline implements three principles—Seeing is Believing, Acting is Believing, and Saying is Believing—to incrementally update each participant’s belief state and derive a common ground set when at least two agents accept a proposition. Predictions are compared against gold annotations using DSC at both per-turn (Average) and full-dialogue (Global) levels.

## Key Results
- LLMs exhibit poor alignment with ground-truth common ground, often achieving near-zero DSC across groups, except for correctly identifying an absence of common ground in a single failed dialogue.
- The DEL-based axiomatic approach attains competitive or superior performance on structure prediction, demonstrating the effectiveness of deterministic belief tracking.
- Despite high structure prediction accuracy in some groups (e.g., Group 9), LLMs and DEL show low overlap in predicted common ground sets, indicating they capture fundamentally different aspects of the interaction.
- DPIP establishes a challenging benchmark for multimodal common ground tracking, highlighting current limitations of large language models in handling rich belief dynamics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Task design elicits multimodal communication
The DPIP setup distributes unique side views to directors, creating epistemic asymmetry that compels participants to articulate private spatial information through speech and gestures. The builder, as the sole manipulator, must interpret these cues to place blocks. This interaction naturally generates temporally aligned multimodal signals (speech, gesture, action) that reflect belief negotiation and shared knowledge construction. The design assumes that humans naturally combine modalities when resolving spatial problems under partial information, a premise supported by prior work on embodied interaction.

### Mechanism 2: Axiomatic DEL pipeline captures belief updates
The pipeline takes temporally aligned multimodal propositions and applies three axioms—Seeing is Believing (perceptual context updates belief), Acting is Believing (embodied action reveals intention), Saying is Believing (language communicates epistemic state)—to incrementally update each participant’s belief state. When at least two participants concurrently accept a proposition, it enters the common ground. This deterministic, interpretable rule-based approach avoids the brittleness of neural model training and directly models the epistemic dynamics of the task. The axioms are inspired by formal DEL but are not proven complete; performance hinges on annotation quality and the assumption that these three update modes dominate in DPIP.

### Mechanism 3: Cross-modality alignment enables unified symbolic representation
Raw annotations from speech, gestures, and actions are normalized: color/shape terms in speech are mapped to concrete block identifiers from actions; gestures are linked to the nearest structure element; all events are timestamp-aligned. This produces a consistent turn-level sequence of propositional updates that resolves referential ambiguity across modalities. The alignment is critical for both the DEL pipeline and LLM prompts; if it fails (e.g., due to occlusions or low gesture IAA), the fused representation becomes noisy and downstream inference degrades.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data collection (Unity, Kinect Azure, microphone) -> Annotation (Whisper ASR + human correction -> proposition extraction; GAMR in ELAN; SAT for structure evolution -> action extraction) -> Alignment module (normalization, block reference resolution, timestamp merging) -> Turn segmentation (CG-update based) -> CG inference (DEL axioms) or LLM interface (prompt generation & inference) -> Evaluation (DSC calculation).  

**Critical path**: Video recording → speech transcription & correction → gesture annotation → structure annotation → action extraction → cross-modality alignment → turn segmentation → (DEL) run CG axioms OR (LLM) construct prompts & generate outputs → DSC evaluation.  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Modality selection: Action-only input simplifies but discards Speech/Gesture cues; full multimodal context may improve global DSC but adds noise for per-turn inference.  
- Deterministic vs neural: DEL offers interpretability and stable performance but depends on annotation quality; LLMs are flexible but brittle to long context and often yield near-zero DSC.  
- Proposition granularity: Fine-grained relations capture detail but require high inter-annotator agreement; coarser summaries may be more robust but lose nuance.  
- Turn boundary definition: Using CG updates to bound turns introduces subjectivity; alternative segmentation (e.g., speaker turns) would alter dynamics.  

**Failure signatures**:  
- Gesture IAA (intersection SMATCH) drops below ~0.8 → cross-modal alignment likely inconsistent.  
- LLM produces many zero DSC scores → model fails to extract meaningful information from actions or aligned data.  
- DEL returns very small/empty common ground sets early on → axioms not firing due to missed events or misaligned timestamps.  
- Large gap between Average DSC (per-turn) and Global DSC for a model → poor long-horizon belief tracking.  

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Actions → Structure**: Input builder’s put/remove/move actions per turn; prompt LLM to predict board state. Tests inference from physical actions alone.
2. **Aligned → Structure**: Provide LLM with aligned annotations (speech, gesture, actions) per turn; ask to predict structure. Measures multimodal fusion benefit.
3. **CGC → Structure**: Run DEL-based CG inference on aligned annotations to obtain shared beliefs, then materialize a structure from those beliefs. Evaluates axiomatic approach against ground truth.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How do occlusions arising from annotation from a single camera angle impact the reliability of spatial relation annotations and subsequent common ground inference in DPIP?  
*Basis*: The paper acknowledges that despite three camera angles, annotations were done from a single view, risking occlusions.  
*Why unresolved*: The effect on annotation quality or model performance is not measured.  
*Resolving evidence*: Compare annotations and model outputs using central view versus multi-view fused annotations; quantify discrepancies in spatial relations and impact on DSC.

**Open Question 2**: Why do LLMs correctly infer an absence of common ground in the failed group dialogue (Group 7) but generally fail to match the rich belief dynamics in successful groups?  
*Basis*: The paper notes the phenomenon but does not analyze its cause.  
*Why unresolved*: No investigation into underlying reasons; likely differences in evidence patterns.  
*Resolving evidence*: Qualitative error analysis comparing LLM outputs on Group 7 versus a high-performing successful group; probe LLMs with controlled synthetic dialogues isolating specific belief update patterns.

**Open Question 3**: Are the three axiomatic grounding principles (Seeing, Acting, Saying is Believing) sufficient to capture all forms of common ground emergence in DPIP, or are additional epistemic rules needed?  
*Basis*: The axioms yield competitive performance but vary across groups; future work may explore additional axioms.  
*Why unresolved*: Axioms are not derived from exhaustive analysis; low DSC in some groups suggests gaps.  
*Resolving evidence*: Ablation studies removing/modifying each axiom, measuring DSC changes; human evaluation of failure cases to identify missing belief update types (e.g., indirect inferences, meta-communication).

**Open Question 4**: How does the robustness of common ground tracking compare between the axiomatic DEL pipeline and LLMs when faced with noise in gesture annotations, given moderate inter-annotator agreement for gesture occurrence?  
*Basis*: The paper reports moderate gesture IAA (union avg. 0.495, intersection avg. 0.845) but does not test noise sensitivity.  
*Why unresolved*: No robustness analysis; both pipelines may degrade differently.  
*Resolving evidence*: Artificially introduce noise (random gesture additions/deletions) into aligned annotations, run both pipelines, measure DSC degradation; correlate per-group IAA with model performance.

**Open Question 5**: Why do LLMs and the DEL axiomatic approach yield low overlap in predicted common ground sets even on groups where both achieve relatively high structure prediction accuracy (e.g., Group 9)?  
*Basis*: The paper observes low overlap (<0.5) even in high-accuracy groups, indicating different aspects captured.  
*Why unresolved*: No analysis of the nature of discrepancies; DEL produces explicit belief membership while LLMs may encode beliefs implicitly.  
*Resolving evidence*: Detailed turn-wise analysis of CG labels from DEL versus LLM outputs, classifying proposition types where they agree/disagree (e.g., block placements, spatial relations, meta-beliefs); probe LLMs for explanations of their CG predictions.

## Limitations
- Limited dataset scale (10 groups) restricts statistical power and generalizability across diverse interactions.
- Implementation details (exact LLM prompts, axiom logic) are underspecified, relying on supplementary material and hindering full reproducibility.
- Corpus ecological validity is uncertain; neighboring work has low FMR and zero citations, suggesting the task may be niche or nascent.
