# Detecting Toxic Language: Ontology and BERT-based Approaches for Bulgarian Text

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.01745  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01745  
- **Reference count**: 28  
- **Primary result**: A BERT-based classifier for Bulgarian toxicity detection achieves a 0.89 F1 macro score, significantly outperforming prior Bulgarian systems and an ontology-based approach.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses false positives in toxic language detection for Bulgarian, where non-toxic content like medical terms or minority group references is mistakenly blocked. The authors introduce two Bulgarian-specific resources: ToxicOntoBg, an ontology that models four contextual categories (toxic, medical, non-toxic, minority), and ToxicBertBg, a BERT-based classifier fine-tuned on a manually annotated dataset of 4,384 Bulgarian sentences.

Evaluations show ToxicBertBg achieves a 0.89 F1 macro score, significantly outperforming the ontology-based approach and prior Bulgarian systems (0.72 and 0.39 F1, respectively). However, when applied to large language model outputs, both systems degrade; the ontology, though less accurate overall, shows relative strength in detecting niche toxic content such as human trafficking terms. The work highlights the promise of contextual and data-driven methods while underscoring needs for larger domain-balanced datasets, multilingual support, and adaptation to LLM-generated text.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a manually annotated dataset of 4,384 Bulgarian sentences, curated to include diverse toxic and non-toxic examples. They developed ToxicOntoBg, an ontology that assigns each lexical item to one of four contextual categories (toxic, medical, non-toxic, minority) to enable context-aware disambiguation. For the neural approach, they fine-tuned a Bulgarian BERT model (ToxicBertBg) on the dataset for binary toxicity classification. Both systems were evaluated on a held-out test set and further tested on responses from Bulgarian-focused large language models (BgGPT, Gemma 2). The BERT model significantly outperformed the ontology and prior art, though performance dropped on LLM-generated text.

## Key Results
- ToxicBertBg achieves 0.89 F1 macro on the test set, a substantial improvement over prior Bulgarian systems (0.72 and 0.39).
- The ontology-based approach, while less accurate overall, demonstrates strengths in identifying niche toxic expressions (e.g., human trafficking terms) that are underrepresented in the training data.
- Both systems suffer notable performance degradation when applied to LLM-generated Bulgarian text, indicating a domain shift challenge.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ToxicBertBg leverages the contextual power of BERT, which captures word meaning through self-attention over the entire sentence. This allows it to disambiguate terms that are toxic in some contexts but benign in others (e.g., medical jargon). Pre-training on large Bulgarian corpora (or multilingual BERT) provides strong linguistic foundations, while fine-tuning on the annotated dataset adapts it to the specific task. The ontology, though simpler, encodes explicit knowledge about term categories, enabling rule-based decisions that are interpretable and can cover rare or domain-specific toxic terms that might be missed by a data-driven model with limited examples. The complementary strengths suggest that a hybrid approach could further improve robustness.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Toxicity detection**: The task of identifying text that is offensive, harmful, or inappropriate. *Why needed*: Enables automated content moderation to protect users. *Quick check*: Is the word "idiot" always toxic? (No, depends on context.)
2. **Contextual word embeddings**: BERT produces embeddings that vary with surrounding context, unlike static embeddings. *Why needed*: Resolves ambiguity (e.g., "sick" meaning ill vs. cool). *Quick check*: How does BERT represent "bank" in "river bank" vs. "bank account"? (Different vectors.)
3. **Ontology engineering**: Building a structured knowledge base of concepts and relationships. *Why needed*: Provides a formal, machine-readable way to encode nuanced linguistic knowledge for disambiguation. *Quick check*: What are the four categories in ToxicOntoBg? (toxic, medical, non-toxic, minority.)
4. **Dataset annotation**: Manual labeling of text for supervised learning. *Why needed*: Creates gold-standard data for training and evaluating models. *Quick check*: Why might two annotators disagree on whether a sentence is toxic? (Because toxicity is subjective and influenced by personal experience.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### ToxicBertBg (BERT-based Classifier)
- **Component map**: Input Bulgarian sentence → Bulgarian BERT tokenizer → BERT-base-bulgarian (12 layers, 768 hidden) → [CLS] token vector → Fully connected layer with sigmoid activation → Toxicity probability.
- **Critical path**: Tokenization → Embedding lookup → Sequential transformer self-attention layers → Classification head. The transformer layers are the computational core, modeling contextual interactions.
- **Design tradeoffs**: High accuracy (0.89 F1) but large model size (~110M parameters) demands GPU for efficient inference; may be overkill for simple cases. Training on only 4,384 sentences risks overfitting; mitigated by using a pre-trained model and dropout regularization. The ontology alternative trades accuracy for interpretability and low resource use.
- **Failure signatures**: Performance drops on out-of-domain text (e.g., LLM-generated responses, formal news articles) due to vocabulary and style mismatch. The model fails on niche toxic domains (human trafficking) where training data is sparse. It may also produce false positives on medical/minority terms if context is insufficiently captured.

### ToxicOntoBg (Ontology-based Classifier)
- **Component map**: Input sentence → Tokenization and part-of-speech tagging → Matching against the ontology lexicon (4,000+ entries) → Contextual rule application (e.g., negation detection, category constraints) → Toxicity decision.
- **Critical path**: Lexicon lookup → Category determination → Rule-based inference. The ontology's four categories enable fine-grained disambiguation.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Interpretable and low computational cost; can be updated with new terms without retraining. However, limited coverage and difficulty handling complex context (e.g., long-range dependencies) lead to lower overall accuracy. It excels at niche toxic terms due to curated entries.
- **Failure signatures**: High false positive rate for medical/minority terms when context is ambiguous; inability to process negation or sarcasm; struggles with out-of-vocabulary slang.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question**: How can the training dataset be expanded with diverse examples from specific domains (e.g., human trafficking, adult content) to improve the BERT model's detection of nuanced toxic language?  
   **Basis**: Future Work point 1: "To bolster the machine learning algorithm’s capacity to detect toxic content in specific domains, such as adult content and human trafficking, it is imperative to incorporate more diverse and representative training data."  
   **Why unresolved**: The current dataset lacks sufficient examples from these domains, causing the BERT model to underperform on such content as evidenced by poor detection in LLM-generated responses.  
   **Resolving evidence**: A larger, domain-balanced dataset with annotated examples from human trafficking and adult content, followed by retraining ToxicBertBg and demonstrating significantly improved F1 scores for these categories on a held-out test set.

2. **Question**: What additional lexical and contextual annotations (e.g., slang vs. formal usage, word meanings) should be added to the ontology to enhance its context-awareness and reduce false positives for non-toxic terms?  
   **Basis**: Future Work point 2: "The instances of the ontology can be additionally annotated with their meanings, as well as whether they are part of the official (Bulgarian) language or slang language."  
   **Why unresolved**: The current ontology lacks semantic and register distinctions, leading to limitations like inability to handle negation or context-dependent meanings, resulting in false positives (e.g., medical terms flagged as toxic).  
   **Resolving evidence**: An enriched ToxicOntoBg with annotations for word sense, register, and context constraints, evaluated on a test set containing ambiguous terms, showing higher precision for non-toxic categories without compromising toxic detection.

3. **Question**: How can the ToxicOntoBg ontology be extended to a multilingual framework to effectively detect toxic code-switching and mixed-language expressions in online Bulgarian communication?  
   **Basis**: Future Work point 3: "To improve the detection of toxic content in multilingual contexts, the ontology should be expanded to support multiple languages."  
   **Why unresolved**: Online content often involves code-switching (e.g., Bulgarian-English), but the current Bulgarian-only ontology cannot handle such cases, limiting applicability in real-world multilingual settings.  
   **Resolving evidence**: A multilingual extension of ToxicOntoBg covering common languages used in Bulgarian online discourse (e.g., English, Russian), validated on a dataset of code-switched Bulgarian text, demonstrating improved recall for toxic spans across languages.

4. **Question**: What adaptations are necessary to transfer the classifiers (ontology-based and BERT-based) from forum-trained models to effectively detect toxicity in LLM-generated text, given the observed performance degradation?  
   **Basis**: Section 6.3: Performance on LLM responses (BgGPT, Gemma 2) is significantly lower than on forum test data; ontology better recognizes niche toxic content like human trafficking terms, but both struggle with LLM-specific patterns.  
   **Why unresolved**: The classifiers were trained on Bulgarian forum data, but LLM outputs differ in structure, language (e.g., Llama 3 answering in English), and content style, causing domain shift and reduced effectiveness.  
   **Resolving evidence**: Fine-tuning or domain-adapting the classifiers on a corpus of LLM-generated Bulgarian text (with manual annotations), followed by evaluation showing restored or improved F1 scores on LLM response datasets like MultiJail.

5. **Question**: How can annotator subjectivity and generational differences in perceiving toxicity be systematically addressed to improve the reliability and consistency of annotated datasets for Bulgarian toxic language detection?  
   **Basis**: Limitations 7.2: "The annotation of the Flores lexicon and the train and test corpora can be subjective. While annotating the words in the ontology, we noticed a shift in the perception of the level of toxicity of some words between annotators from different generations."  
   **Why unresolved**: Subjective annotations introduce noise and bias, affecting model training and evaluation; generational differences may lead to inconsistent labeling of evolving slang or sensitive terms.  
   **Resolving evidence**: Development of detailed annotation guidelines with examples, conducting inter-annotator agreement studies (e.g., Cohen's kappa) on a diverse annotator pool, and creating a consensus dataset that mitigates individual biases, resulting in higher agreement and more stable model performance.

## Limitations
- The manually annotated dataset contains only 4,384 sentences, which may be insufficient to capture the full variability of Bulgarian toxic language across domains and styles.
- Annotation quality metrics (e.g., inter-annotator agreement) are not reported, leaving the reliability of the gold labels uncertain.
- Evaluation is confined to a single in‑sample test split; no external validation or cross‑domain robustness has been demonstrated.

## Confidence
- ToxicBertBg achieves 0.89 F1 macro, significantly beating prior Bulgarian systems: **High**
- ToxicOntoBg models contextual categories and reduces false positives: **Medium**
- The ontology better recognizes niche toxic content in LLM outputs: **Low**
