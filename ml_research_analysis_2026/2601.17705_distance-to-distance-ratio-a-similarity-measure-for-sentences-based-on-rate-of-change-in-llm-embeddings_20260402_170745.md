# Distance-to-Distance Ratio: A Similarity Measure for Sentences Based on Rate of Change in LLM Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.17705  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17705  
- **Reference count:** 12  
- **Primary result:** The Distance-to-Distance Ratio (DDR) consistently outperforms centroid and end-of-sequence (EOS) cosine similarity on controlled lexical edits, achieving substantially higher Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) and lower Pearson correlation between semantically similar (synonym) and dissimilar (random) sentence pairs.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Distance-to-Distance Ratio (DDR), a novel sentence similarity metric that measures the rate of change in token-level distances before and after processing by an LLM. Inspired by Lipschitz continuity, DDR compares the maximum chordal distance between pre‑context embeddings (input layer) to that between post‑context embeddings (final hidden states). Experiments on 500 sentences perturbed with 1–3 synonym or random word substitutions show that DDR provides far stronger discrimination between semantically similar and dissimilar edits than standard centroid- or EOS‑based cosine similarity. The advantage persists as edits accumulate, suggesting DDR captures local semantic continuity more effectively than aggregated representations.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a dataset of 500 sentence excerpts from *The Hacker Crackdown* and *Dracula*. For each source, two types of variants are generated: synonym‑substituted (preserving meaning) and random‑substituted (degrading meaning), with 1, 2, or 3 word replacements while keeping token counts identical. For any sentence pair (source vs. variant), the pipeline extracts token embeddings both at the input layer (pre‑context) and after full LLM forward pass (post‑context).  

- **Centroid/EOS methods:** Sentence embeddings are obtained by mean‑pooling or using the EOS token; similarity = cosine between the two sentence vectors.  
- **DDR:** For each aligned token position, chordal distance (scaled Euclidean on normalized vectors) is computed between the two tokens. The maximum pre‑context distance across positions is `din`; the maximum post‑context distance is `dout`. DDR = `din / dout`.  

For each edit depth and substitution type, 500 scores are collected and compared using Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) between the synonym and random distributions, and Pearson correlation between paired per‑sentence synonym and random scores.

## Key Results
- For single edits, DDR yields an EMD of ~0.623, whereas centroid and EOS scores are near zero (∼0.00006).  
- Pearson correlation between synonym and random scores is ≈0.473 for DDR, compared to >0.94 for centroid/EOS.  
- As the number of edits increases, DDR maintains a large EMD gap (e.g., 0.28 at three edits) while centroid/EOS discriminative power degrades rapidly, indicating DDR’s robustness to multiple perturbations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 – Relative contraction/expansion discriminates on‑ vs. off‑manifold edits.** DDR computes `din/dout`, where `din` is the maximum token distance before the LLM and `dout` after. The LLM is locally contractive on the natural‑language manifold: synonym substitutions (on‑manifold) cause substantial compression (`dout << din`), yielding high DDR; random substitutions (off‑manifold) lead to little compression or even expansion, yielding lower DDR. This relies on the assumption that the LLM transformation smooths semantically coherent texts more strongly. The mechanism fails if token positions misalign or the LLM exhibits chaotic, non‑smooth behavior.

**Mechanism 2 – Ratio form enhances stability.** Absolute distances in high‑dimensional spaces can be noisy and scale‑dependent. Taking a ratio allows common noise and scaling factors to cancel, leveraging statistical results that density‑ratio estimation often converges faster than density estimation. DDR inherits this robustness; the chordal distance ties directly to cosine similarity, preserving geometric interpretability. Stability diminishes if the pre‑ and post‑embedding spaces have vastly different noise characteristics or the ratio surface is highly irregular.

**Mechanism 3 – Max aggregation preserves local change signals.** By reducing distances with the `max` operator over token positions, DDR is highly sensitive to even a single edited token. Centroid and EOS methods average over all tokens, diluting local changes. The max‑based aggregation ensures that minimal edits, which affect only a few tokens, directly influence the score. This works as long as edits are not uniformly distributed across all tokens (in which case meaning changes drastically and should be detected by any method).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Tokenizer → Token embedding layer (pre‑context) → LLM transformer → Final hidden states (post‑context) → Per‑position chordal distance calculator → Max aggregator → DDR (din/dout) → Evaluation (EMD, Pearson, CDF)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Input two same‑length strings.  
  2. Tokenize both; obtain pre‑context embeddings from the model’s embedding matrix.  
  3. Run full forward pass to get post‑context hidden states.  
  4. For each aligned token position, compute chordal distance between the two strings’ vectors (separately for pre and post).  
  5. Reduce pre distances with `max` → `din`; reduce post distances with `max` → `dout`.  
  6. Compute `DDR = din / dout`.  
  7. Repeat over many pairs under controlled edits; collect scores for synonym and random groups.  
  8. Compute EMD between the two score distributions and Pearson correlation between paired scores.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Max vs. average:* Max yields high sensitivity to single‑token changes but may be noisier; averaging would be smoother but could miss subtle edits.  
  - *Equal‑length requirement:* Restricts input pairs; padding/truncation introduces biases.  
  - *Chordal distance:* Computationally cheap (Euclidean on normalized vectors) and linked to cosine similarity, but scaling factors affect ratio interpretability.  
  - *Cost:* Requires two independent forward passes per pair; could be optimized by batching or shared caching.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Length mismatch → alignment error.  
  - Constant post‑context embeddings → `dout ≈ 0` leads to division‑by‑zero or infinite DDR.  
  - All tokens edited uniformly → `max` may not differentiate well (though meaning still changes drastically).  
  - Numerical instability when `din` and `dout` are both very small, producing volatile ratios.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. One‑word substitution: replace one word with a synonym and with a random word, compute DDR, centroid‑cosine, and EOS‑cosine for 500 source sentences.  
  2. Two‑word substitution: same as (1) but with two replacements per sentence.  
  3. Three‑word substitution: repeat with three replacements; then for each edit depth and method, compare distributions via EMD and scatter plots with Pearson correlation to assess discriminative power.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to variable‑length sequences**  
   The current DDR formulation requires equal token lengths. Extending it to handle arbitrary lengths (e.g., via alignment or set‑based distances) without sacrificing theoretical guarantees remains an open problem.

2. **Generalization beyond lexical swaps and literary domain**  
   Experiments are limited to synonym/random word swaps on two novels. It is unknown whether DDR retains its advantage for other edit types (paraphrasing, negation, entailment) or in diverse domains (social media, technical text).

3. **Cross‑model robustness**  
   The study uses unspecified “GPT‑style” embeddings. DDR’s performance may depend on architecture, size, or training objective; its behavior on encoder‑decoder models, multilingual models, or instruction‑tuned variants is untested.

4. **Direct empirical verification of the on‑manifold/off‑manifold hypothesis**  
   The paper’s explanatory intuition—that synonym edits stay on a low‑dimensional manifold while random edits go off‑manifold—is not directly measured. Manifold properties (intrinsic dimension, curvature, local density) could be analyzed to confirm or refute this claim.

5. **Impact on downstream tasks**  
   Although DDR is proposed for applications like near‑duplicate detection or adversarial robustness evaluation, no empirical validation in such tasks has been performed. It is unclear whether DDR’s discriminative power translates into practical improvements in retrieval, classification, or security settings.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified model and tokenizer:** The exact LLM (e.g., GPT‑2, GPT‑3, Llama) and tokenizer details are not provided, making faithful replication difficult.  
- **Ambiguous pre‑context definition:** It is unclear whether “pre‑context embeddings” refer to raw token‑embedding layer outputs or the first transformer layer; this affects the computed distances.  
- **Incomplete perturbation protocol:** Details on synonym source (e.g., WordNet), random word sampling, and handling of multi‑token replacements are missing, as are rules for special tokens (BOS/EOS/PAD) in centroid/EOS pooling and DDR alignment.

## Confidence
- **DDR discriminates effectively between synonym and random edits:** High  
- **Lipschitz‑inspired ratio mechanism explains the results:** Medium  
- **Ratio form provides stability relative to absolute distances:** Low  
- **Max aggregator confers sensitivity to single‑token changes:** High  

## Next Checks
1. **Model specification replication:** Run the full pipeline with a specific GPT‑2 variant (e.g., `gpt2`), record exact EMD and Pearson values, and compare against the paper’s reported numbers.  
2. **Pre‑context definition ablation:** Compare DDR using raw token‑embedding layer outputs versus first‑layer hidden states to assess robustness to this ambiguous choice.  
3. **Aggregation function sweep:** Replace the `max` operation in DDR with `mean` and `median`, then re‑evaluate discriminative power across edit depths to understand the contribution of extreme‑value aggregation.
