# Approximate equivariance via projection-based regularisation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05028  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05028  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Projection-based regularisation enforces approximate equivariance with 2× higher throughput and up to 2.7 dB PSNR gain over sample-based methods on SO(2), fluid dynamics, and CT tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces projection-based regularisation for approximate equivariance in neural networks. Instead of augmenting data or penalising pointwise deviations, the method directly penalises the non-equivariant component of each linear layer via its orthogonal projection onto the equivariant subspace. For continuous compact groups, this projection is computed exactly and efficiently through Fourier-domain masking, avoiding explicit group integration. Evaluated on SO(2) toy tasks, turbulent fluid dynamics with relaxed symmetries, and CT metal artifact reduction (C4 rotation), the approach consistently improves accuracy and reduces training/inference time compared to sample-based penalties.

## Method Summary
The method adds a per-layer penalty $\lambda_\perp \|W - P(W)\|_F^2$ to the training loss, where $W$ is a linear layer’s weight and $P(W)$ is the orthogonal projection onto the space of $G$-equivariant linear maps. For finite groups $G$, $P(W) = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G} \rho_{\text{out}}(g)^*\,W\,\rho_{\text{in}}(g)$. For continuous compact groups (e.g., SO(2)), $P(W)$ is derived via the Peter–Weyl theorem: transform the spatial kernel to the Fourier basis, mask all off-diagonal irrep blocks, average within each diagonal block using a closed-form Bochner integral, then inverse transform. This operator-level regularisation enforces symmetry across the full group orbit without data augmentation. The projection is differentiable and integrates seamlessly into standard training pipelines.

## Key Results
- On CT metal artifact reduction, the method improves PSNR by up to 2.7 dB over sample-based penalties and achieves 5–12 vs 2.5–4 samples/GPU-s throughput (≈2× speedup).  
- It matches or exceeds prior approaches (sample-based, relaxed group convolutions) across SO(2) toy classification, fluid dynamics prediction, and CT reconstruction.  
- The Fourier-domain projection for continuous groups is exact and avoids the sample complexity of integrating over the group.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Partitioning a linear layer’s weight tensor into an equivariant component (via orthogonal projection) and its complement enables operator‑level regularisation over the full group orbit. The Reynolds operator $P(T) = \int_G \rho_{\text{out}}(g)^* T \rho_{\text{in}}(g) \,d\lambda(g)$ computes the orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace of $G$-equivariant maps. Penalising $\|T - P(T)\|$ directly restricts non‑equivariant capacity at the weight level. The key assumption is that the hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$ (e.g., all linear layers) is closed under $P$, which holds for finite‑dimensional linear spaces of operators. The bound $\Delta_g(T) \leq C \|T - P(T)\|$ links the per‑layer penalty to the equivariance defect.

### Mechanism 2
For continuous compact groups (e.g., SO(2), SO(3)), the projection can be computed exactly and efficiently via masking in the Fourier (spectral) domain. The Peter–Weyl theorem decomposes $\mathcal{L}^2(G)$ into irreducible representations; an equivariant linear operator is block‑diagonal in this basis. The projection $P$ therefore zeroes all off‑diagonal blocks and, for each diagonal block, averages within the irrep using a closed‑form Bochner integral. This yields a simple masking operation on Fourier coefficients: FFT → mask → average → IFFT. The assumption is that the group action aligns with a regular grid enabling a discrete Fourier transform. This avoids costly numerical integration over the group.

### Mechanism 3
The defect‑based bound (Lemma 3.3) shows that regularising each linear layer’s projection error improves the network’s worst‑case equivariance defect, scaled by downstream Lipschitz constants. For a network $f_k \circ \cdots \circ f_1$, the global defect is bounded by a weighted sum of per‑layer defects: $\mathcal{E}(T) \leq C \sum_\ell \|W^{(\ell)} - P(W^{(\ell)})\|_F$. Thus minimising the per‑layer $\|W - P(W)\|$ controls overall non‑equivariance. The assumption is that all intermediate feature maps lie in $G$-modules with unitary representations and that activations are $G$‑equivariant and Lipschitz.

  Why needed: The entire method relies on formalising symmetries as group actions on vector spaces (modules). The Reynolds operator, orthogonal decomposition, and Fourier analysis all assume unitary representations.

- **Haar measure and averaging**  
  Why needed: The projection $P(T) = \int_G \rho_{\text{out}}(g)^* T \rho_{\text{in}}(g) \,d\lambda(g)$ is defined via the normalized Haar integral. It is the optimal least‑squares projection onto the equivariant subspace.

  Why needed: Provides the spectral decomposition where equivariant operators become block‑diagonal, enabling efficient masking. The Fourier transform turns convolution into multiplication.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input feature tensors with $G$-module structure → Linear layer weight $W$ → Projection operator $P$ (analytical: group averaging or FFT masking) → Penalty term $\lambda_\perp \|W-P(W)\|_F^2$ combined with task loss → Network output.

**Critical path:**  
1. Define group $G$ and unitary irreducible representations $\rho_{\text{in}}$, $\rho_{\text{out}}$ for input/output feature types.  
2. For each linear layer, implement $P(W)$:  
   - Finite $G$: loop over group elements, accumulate $\rho_{\text{out}}(g)^* W \rho_{\text{in}}(g)$, divide by $|G|$.  
   - Continuous $G$: compute FFT of spatial kernel, mask off‑diagonal irrep blocks, average within each block (Eq. 19), inverse FFT.  
3. Add $\|W - P(W)\|_F^2$ penalty to loss (optionally with $\lambda_G \|P(W)\|_F^2$).  
4. Backpropagate through $P(W)$ (analytical gradient depends on $\rho$ and group structure).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Norm choice:** Frobenius norm $\|\cdot\|_F$ is default (differentiable, efficient). Spectral norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ may align better with operator capacity but requires SVD (costly).  
- **$\lambda_G$ vs $\lambda_\perp$:** $\lambda_\perp$ dominates the effect; setting $\lambda_G=0$ is typical. Too high $\lambda_\perp$ forces strict equivariance and hurts fit.  
- **Group implementation:** For large finite groups, precompute $\rho(g)$ matrices. For continuous groups, ensure FFT axes align with group action (e.g., angular FFT for SO(2) on 2D grid).  
- **Representation alignment:** Input/output channels must be organised into $G$-modules (e.g., orientation channels for $C_4$, harmonic orders for SO(2)).

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Slow projection:** Using naive integration for continuous groups (no spectral masking) kills throughput vs sample‑based methods.  
- **Poor equivariance:** $\|W - P(W)\|_F$ stays high → check irrep block structure (off‑diagonals not zeroed), verify kernel alignment with group action.  
- **Degenerate solution:** If $\lambda_\perp$ too high relative to task loss, model ignores task and projects weights to zero (or purely equivariant subspace that cannot fit data).  
- **Mismatched representations:** If input feature map is not in a $G$-module (e.g., missing orientation channels), the $\rho(g)$ used in $P$ is invalid → broken equivariance.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **SO(2) toy classification** (Section 4.1): Validate that tuning $\lambda_\perp$ controls the trade‑off between fitting data and enforcing rotational invariance. Decision boundaries become smoother circles as $\lambda_\perp$ increases.  
2. **Smoke plume prediction with relaxed symmetries** (Section 4.2): Compare to sample‑based regularisers (Bai et al.) and relaxed group convolutions (Wang et al.). Our method achieves lower pixel‑MSE in rotation/scaling regimes where symmetry only partially holds.  
3. **CT metal artifact reduction** (Section 4.3): Apply $C_4$ projection to convolutional kernels. Outperforms sample‑based penalty in PSNR/SSIM and gains 2× throughput by avoiding per‑rotation forward passes. Table 2 shows consistent ≈0.5–1.2 dB PSNR gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
How can projection‑based regularisation be extended to more complex group structures, such as product groups or semi‑direct products (e.g., SE(3)), that go beyond the simple continuous SO(2) and finite C4 cases tested?  
*Basis:* "[explicit] current experiments only evaluate the proposed method for relatively simple groups. In future work, we plan to extend this approach to more complex group structures consisting of several subgroups with applications in e.g. material sciences."  
*Why unresolved:* The theoretical framework assumes compact groups and a uniform grid enabling a fast Fourier transform. For many physically relevant groups (e.g., SO(3) combined with translations), an efficient global FFT does not exist; the cost of handling Wigner D‑matrices or similar transforms is unclear.

**Open Question 2**  
Can the derivation of the projection penalty be automated so that it can be applied to any linear layer and group representation without manual effort?  
*Basis:* "[explicit] A limitation of the proposed approach is that the penalty term needs to be derived anew for each model architecture and group operation."  
*Why unresolved:* Manual derivation for each new architecture (e.g., transformers, graph convolutions) and group is a barrier to widespread adoption. The paper provides closed‑form expressions only for fully connected and standard convolutional layers under C4/SO(2) actions; extending to, say, spectral parameterisations or mixed discrete‑continuous groups requires fresh algebra.

**Open Question 3**  
What is a principled way to set the hyperparameters $\lambda_G$ and $\lambda_\perp$ without costly manual searches, ideally in a manner that adapts to the degree of symmetry present in the data?  
*Basis:* "[inferred] The paper manually tunes these parameters on a grid; no automatic selection method is discussed."  
*Why unresolved:* Grid search is expensive and often heuristic; the right trade‑off between equivariance bias and task performance is unknown a priori. A theoretical link (e.g., between $\lambda$ and a data‑dependent symmetry metric) is missing.

**Open Question 4**  
For groups that lack a simple fast Fourier transform (e.g., non‑abelian groups like SU(2) or non‑compact groups), can the projection‑based penalty still be computed efficiently, or must approximation be introduced?  
*Basis:* "[explicit] whenever an efficient Fourier transform is available (e.g. on regular grids) or the model is already parameterised spectrally ... imposing equivariance reduces to diagonalising the relevant linear operators."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper analyses asymptotic cost only for finite groups and continuous groups with an FFT (i.e., product of circles). For groups requiring a sum over all irreducible representations with non‑trivial dimension (e.g., SO(3) with Wigner D functions), the summation could be prohibitively expensive compared to sample‑based penalties that use a few group elements.

## Limitations
- **Break condition:** If the representation theory for $G$ is unknown or the Fourier transform is inefficient on the data domain (e.g., non‑uniform sampling), the projection may not be computable in closed form, forcing reliance on sample‑based approximations.  
- **Reproducibility:** Exact hyperparameters (λ values, network widths) for smoke and CT experiments are missing; baseline code unavailable.  
- **Scalability:** Extension to high‑dimensional continuous groups (e.g., SO(3)) not explored; memory usage and computational cost unclear.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical basis:** High (rigorous orthogonal projection and defect bound).  
- **Efficiency claim:** Medium (throughput comparison lacks low‑level profiling; FFT overhead not fully characterised).  
- **Effectiveness:** Medium (three tasks but with limited baseline diversity).
