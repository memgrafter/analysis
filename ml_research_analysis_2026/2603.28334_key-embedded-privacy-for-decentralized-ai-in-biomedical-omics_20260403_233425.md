# Key-Embedded Privacy for Decentralized AI in Biomedical Omics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28334
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28334
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** INFL embeds a secret key into client model architectures via implicit neural representations, enabling strong privacy without utility loss across multiple biomedical omics tasks.

## Executive Summary
Privacy-preserving federated learning in biomedical omics faces a trade-off: cryptographic methods introduce heavy overhead, while differential privacy degrades model performance. This paper introduces INFL, a lightweight alternative that uses Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) to cryptographically lock models. By replacing standard linear layers with coordinate-conditioned INR modules, INFL encrypts the model itself—without the secret permutation key, the model collapses. Only the small INR parameters are aggregated via FedAvg, allowing seamless collaboration across heterogeneous sites. Validated on bulk proteomics cancer subtyping, single-cell perturbation prediction, and spatial multi-omics integration, INFL matches or surpasses centralized and other federated baselines, while ablation studies confirm its strong, controllable privacy.

## Method Summary
INFL introduces `INRLinear` modules that substitute standard linear layers in any backbone model. Each module computes an output as a blend: `y' = α(Wx+b) + (1-α)Δ`, where `Δ_i = Φ_θ(γ(C(π(i)))`. Here, `π` is a secret permutation key, `γ` a sinusoidal positional encoding, and `Φ_θ` a small MLP (the INR). During federated training, clients update both backbone and INR parameters on private data; only INR parameters `θ` are uploaded to the server and averaged via FedAvg. At inference, authorized clients use the correct `π` to generate meaningful `Δ`; attackers with a wrong `π` receive scrambled outputs because high-frequency INRs yield weakly correlated Jacobians at distinct coordinates, causing a high-variance error. The approach is plug-and-play, works across heterogeneous backbones, and avoids the noise injection that harms differential privacy.

## Key Results
- INFL achieves top accuracy, macro-F1, and macro-AUROC on bulk proteomics cancer subtyping; lowest MSE and highest Pearson correlation on single-cell perturbation prediction; highest ARI, AMI, and NMI on spatial multi-omics integration.
- Model performance collapses without the correct secret key, providing strong, verifiable privacy.
- INFL matches or exceeds centralized training benchmarks and outperforms DP-based FL baselines, demonstrating no utility loss from its privacy mechanism.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cryptographic lock via coordinate-conditioned INRs
INR modules act as a lock: the secret permutation `π` determines coordinates fed to the INR. Without `π`, the modulation `Δ` becomes a permutation of the correct vector `Δ*`, scrambling the output. This relies on high-frequency INRs whose Jacobians at distinct coordinates are weakly correlated, ensuring the error term has non-vanishing variance. Formal proof (Section 5.3) derives error variance `E[ϵ²] ≈ 2(1-α)²σ²Δ` and gradient mismatch `E[‖g−gₐ‖²] ≳ 2(1-α)²σ²J Σ E[(∂ℓ/∂y′ᵢ)²]`. If the INR is not high-frequency (low `σ²Δ`) or the permutation space is small (small `N_out`), the lock weakens.

### Mechanism 2: Seamless aggregation via plug-and-play meta-learners
INR modules are small, adaptable networks that learn site-specific corrections while the backbone learns shared representations. Only INR parameters `θ` are aggregated via FedAvg; the global `θ` then works with any client’s backbone under its own `π`. This succeeds because the INR’s capacity is limited so that site-specific adaptations average to a useful global modulation. Experiments across heterogeneous data (Dirichlet α=0.3) and three different base models confirm the approach. Extreme heterogeneity beyond this test range may cause the averaged `θ` to lose meaning.

### Mechanism 3: Utility preservation via architectural (non-additive) privacy
Unlike differential privacy, which injects noise into updates and degrades convergence, INFL’s privacy is purely architectural—it affects only inference without the key. Training proceeds on a clean loss landscape, allowing the model to reach high accuracy that is “unlocked” at inference. This avoids the utility cost of noise-based methods, as shown by consistent superiority over DP baselines across all tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Local Learner (Backbone) → INR Modules (INRLinear) → Federated Orchestrator (FedAvg server). Key Manager separately distributes secret permutation π to authorized clients.

**Critical path**  
1. Initialize global backbone M₀ᴳ and client-specific secret π.  
2. Server sends current (M, θ) to selected clients each round.  
3. Client forward: backbone + INR (using its π) on local data; compute loss; update backbone and θ via backprop.  
4. Client uploads only θ to server.  
5. Server averages received θ → θᵗ; redistributes to clients.  
6. Repeat steps 2–5 until convergence.  
7. Inference: authorized client uses (M, θ, π) to compute correct Δ; attacker with wrong π gets scrambled output.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- INR size (hidden dim/layers): larger Φθ increases correction capacity but risks overfitting/attack if low-frequency. Default: hidden=8, layers=3.  
- Blending factor α: α→1 eases training but weakens lock; α→0 strengthens lock but may destabilize training. Default: α=0.5.  
- Permutation scale: Security grows with number of output neurons N_out (larger permutation space) but also increases modulation variance.

**Failure signatures**  
- Training instability: α too low or INR too large may prevent convergence of local loss.  
- Poor aggregation: Using different backbone architectures across clients makes FedAvg of θ meaningless.  
- Weak privacy: Shallow/low-frequency INR yields small gradient mismatch, enabling reconstruction attacks.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Proteomic cancer subtyping** – Replace final linear layers of an MLP with INRLinear; FedAvg across 5 clients (ProCan data); evaluate classification metrics; ablation: remove INR at inference → collapse.  
2. **Perturbation prediction (GEARS)** – Insert INRLinear into decoder’s gene-specific layers; FedAvg across 10 clients on Adamson/Norman scRNA-seq; evaluate MSE/Pearson; check hit rate.  
3. **Spatial multi-omics mosaic integration (SpaMosaic)** – Add INRLinear to embedding projection; FedAvg across 3 clients with overlapping RNA/ADT slices (10x Visium); evaluate ARI/AMI/NMI on clustered embeddings; validate marker genes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimizing INR for efficiency**: How can the INR architecture be refined to minimize computational and communication overhead in FL without compromising privacy?
- **Robustness against practical attacks**: Does INFL provide strong protection against gradient inversion or membership inference in real-world biomedical FL, beyond theoretical guarantees?
- **Secure key management**: How can key distribution and revocation be achieved for INFL in large, dynamic federations of institutions?

## Limitations
- Exact client data splits and full architectures for GEARS/SpaMosaic are not released, hindering faithful replication.
- Security assumes INRs are trained to high frequency; under-regularization may smooth the modulation, weakening privacy against coordinate-guessing attacks.
- Aggregation requires consistent backbone architectures across clients; mixing different model types would break the FedAvg of INR parameters.

## Confidence
- Privacy lock mechanism: **High** (formal proof) but **Medium** in practice (depends on INR frequency training, permutation scale).  
- Utility preservation vs DP: **High**.  
- Seamless aggregation across heterogeneity: **Medium** (tested up to Dirichlet α=0.3; extreme non-IID untested).  
- Overall claim strength: **Medium**.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate proteomics subtyping** using public code with a standardized non-IID split (Dirichlet α=0.3) and verify both performance matching and collapse when using a wrong key.
2. **Stress-test privacy lock** by deliberately training INRs with low-frequency activations (e.g., reduced sinusoidal frequencies) and small permutation spaces (`N_out` ≤ 32); measure whether outputs remain corrupted without the key.
3. **Evaluate aggregation under extreme heterogeneity** by assigning clients highly divergent data distributions (Dirichlet α < 0.1) and random initial seeds; check if FedAvg of INR parameters converges or diverges.
