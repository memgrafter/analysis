# Circuit Tracing in Vision-Language Models: Understanding the Internal Mechanisms of Multimodal Thinking
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20330
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20330
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces the first framework for transparent circuit tracing in VLMs, revealing hierarchical integration and distinct visual circuits; validated via causal interventions.

## Executive Summary
Vision-language models (VLMs) combine visual and textual information but are often treated as black boxes, limiting our understanding of their multimodal reasoning. This paper introduces a novel framework for transparent circuit tracing in VLMs, enabling detailed inspection of internal computations. The approach uses transcoders to replace MLP layers, decomposing activations into sparse, interpretable features; attribution graphs to map causal influence from inputs through features to outputs; and attention analysis to interpret visual tokens from the vision encoder. Applied to Gemma-3-4B (SigLIP + Gemma), the framework uncovers a hierarchical integration of visual and semantic concepts in higher layers, identifies separate circuits for simple arithmetic (e.g., counting fingers) and associative visual triggers (e.g., Mars activating space-related features), and validates these circuits via steering and patching interventions. Transcoders achieve low Fraction of Variance Unexplained (FVU), especially in mid-layers, demonstrating effective decomposition. This work provides essential tools for debugging, interpreting, and improving VLM systems.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on training per‑layer transcoders—encoder‑decoder models with TopK sparsity—to mimic each MLP’s input‑output behavior while producing a sparse latent representation. Transcoder training uses a multimodal dataset combining text (SmoLIM2), image‑text pairs (ImageNet), and QA data (Cauldron). The encoder maps activations to a high‑dimensional latent space; the decoder reconstructs the MLP output. The reconstruction error, measured by Fraction of Variance Unexplained (FVU), serves as a quality metric, with low FVU indicating faithful decomposition.

With transcoders in place, the model is linearized around a specific input; nonzero gradients are stop‑graded at nonlinearities to preserve local linearity. Attribution edges are computed as virtual weights: the product of the source feature’s activation and the Jacobian from source to target, scaled by the decoded vectors. A full graph is built, then edges whose cumulative influence falls below a threshold (e.g., ϵ=0.8 or 0.98) are pruned, yielding a sparse interpretable circuit. Visual features are interpreted via attention rollout over the SigLIP encoder: averaging low‑entropy attention heads across final layers, multiplying by identity residuals, and reshaping into spatial heatmaps. Human experts group related features into nodes, extract subgraphs, and validate causality through steering (setting feature activations) and circuit patching (overwriting features).

## Key Results
- Introduced the first transparent circuit tracing framework for VLMs, enabling feature‑level decomposition and causal attribution.
- Discovered hierarchical integration of visual and semantic concepts in higher layers and identified distinct circuits for arithmetic reasoning (e.g., counting fingers) and associative visual triggers (e.g., Mars → space shuttle).
- Demonstrated controllability of identified circuits via steering and patching; transcoders achieved low FVU (median ~0.15 in mid‑layers), confirming effective multimodal decomposition.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Transcoders replace MLP layers to decompose polysemantic neural activations into sparse, approximately monosemantic features, enabling feature‑level circuit analysis.
- **Mechanism:** For each MLP, a transcoder (encoder‑decoder with TopK sparsity) is trained to match the MLP’s input‑output behavior. The encoder maps activations to a higher‑dimensional latent space; the decoder reconstructs the MLP output. This replacement expresses the model’s computation as a linear combination of sparse features, with a residual term capturing reconstruction error.
- **Core assumption:** The latent features learned by transcoders correspond to human‑interpretable concepts (monosemanticity), and the reconstruction residual is small enough that the linearized approximation captures the essential computation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "utilizing transcoders to decompose neural activations into interpretable features"
  - [section 3.1] Details transcoder architecture, training, and FVU metric; notes low FVU in mid‑layers indicates effective decomposition.
  - [corpus] Corpus shows active research on circuit tracing in LLMs and VLMs (e.g., "CircuitProbe," "Uncovering Graph Reasoning"), supporting the broader methodological context, but does not directly validate transcoder monosemanticity for VLMs.
- **Break condition:** High FVU or evidence that features remain polysemantic (e.g., top‑activating examples show mixed concepts) would undermine the interpretability claim; failure to train transcoders for lower layers (high dead latents) suggests limitations in decomposing early visual representations.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Attribution graphs linearly decompose the model’s computation to trace causal influence from input tokens through features to output logits, identifying the minimal subgraph (circuit) responsible for a behavior.
- **Mechanism:** With transcoders installed and nonlinearities frozen at their prompt‑specific values, the model becomes locally linear. Virtual weights (local Jacobian) compute edge attributions, showing how much each earlier feature contributes to later activations. Pruning low‑attribution edges yields a sparse, interpretable graph.
- **Core assumption:** The linear approximation around the fixed input is sufficiently accurate to capture the true causal pathways; the circuit identified for one prompt generalizes to the underlying algorithmic mechanism.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "attribution graphs to map causal relationships"
  - [section 3.2] Derives the attribution formula \(A_{s→t} = a_s w_{s→t}\) and explains the Jacobian factorization; notes that residuals are included as separate nodes.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Uncovering Graph Reasoning in Decoder‑only Transformers" validate circuit tracing in LLMs, but direct evidence for VLMs is confined to this paper’s validation experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the pruned graph fails to reproduce the model’s output when ablated (e.g., patching disrupts behavior unpredictably), or if the linearization error (measured by reconstruction residual magnitude) is large, the attributed edges may not reflect true causal structure.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Attention‑rollout maps over the vision encoder’s self‑attention layers localize which image regions influence specific visual token embeddings, enabling interpretation of visual features in the circuit.
- **Mechanism:** For the SigLIP vision encoder, attention matrices from the last K focused heads are averaged, combined with identity residuals, and multiplied across layers to produce a rollout matrix. Rows corresponding to the \(N_v\) visual tokens are reshaped into spatial heatmaps over the input image grid.
- **Core assumption:** The aggregated attention patterns correlate with task‑relevant visual regions; the vision encoder’s attention is a reasonable proxy for which pixels shaped the embeddings that trigger features in the LLM.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "attention analysis to interpret visual tokens"
  - [section 3.3] Describes the rollout computation, head selection (lowest entropy), and spatial mapping process; notes no additional refinements are applied.
  - [corpus] Related work (e.g., "CircuitProbe") uses attention for visual semantics, but this paper’s method is novel for VLM circuit tracing; corpus does not provide independent validation.
- **Break condition:** If attention maps are diffuse, fail to highlight semantically meaningful regions (e.g., for abstract shapes), or if vision encoder uses non‑attentional architectures (e.g., pure CNNs), this method becomes uninformative.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) and Transcoders**  
  **Why needed here:** The entire circuit tracing pipeline depends on decomposing MLP activations into interpretable features. Understanding SAE training (sparsity via L1 or TopK), latent space expansion, and the distinction between SAEs (reconstruct activations) and transcoders (mimic MLP input‑output) is essential.  
  **Quick check question:** Why does using TopK sparsity instead of an L1 penalty potentially yield more stable training for transcoders in this work?

- **Concept: Causal Attribution and Linearization in Transformers**  
  **Why needed here:** Attribution graphs rely on locally linearizing the model. Prerequisite knowledge includes how attention and LayerNorm introduce nonlinearities, why stop‑gradients are used on softmaxes, and how virtual weights compose decoder and encoder vectors with Jacobians.  
  **Quick check question:** In the attribution formula \(w_{s→t} = f(s)_{dec}^\top J_{s→t}^{\downarrow} f(t)_{enc}\), what does the downward arrow on the Jacobian signify, and why are nonlinearities stop‑gradient?

- **Concept: Vision‑Language Model Architecture (SigLIP + LLM Decoder)**  
  **Why needed here:** To understand how visual tokens are generated (896×896 image → 256 patches) and integrated into the LLM’s residual stream, and why bidirectional attention over image tokens matters for circuit tracing.  
  **Quick check question:** How does the SigLIP vision encoder’s output (256 tokens) interface with the Gemma‑3 language model, and what implication does this have for attributing visual influence?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Vision encoder (SigLIP) -> LLM decoder (Gemma‑3‑4B) -> Per‑layer transcoders -> Attribution graph builder -> Feature analyzer -> Circuit discovery interface -> Intervention engine

**Critical path:**
1. Input image + text → vision encoder produces visual tokens.
2. Visual + text tokens stream through LLM layers; transcoders replace MLPs, emitting sparse features.
3. Attribution graph computed for a specific prompt, tracing influence from tokens through features to final logits.
4. Graph pruned; human annotator groups semantically similar features into circuit nodes.
5. Interventions (steering/patching) run on transcoder‑replaced model to test circuit causality.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Per‑layer vs. cross‑layer transcoders: Per‑layer is simpler but cannot capture cross‑layer superposition (noted as a limitation). Cross‑layer might better disentangle visual features but increases complexity.
- FVU vs. sparsity: Lower FVU requires larger expansion factor (N_latents), but too many latents increase dead features and manual annotation burden.
- Attention map fidelity vs. interpretability: Raw rollout maps are unfiltered but often noisy; adding denoising could improve localization but might obscure true model signals.

**Failure signatures:**
- High dead latent percentage in early layers (>70%) leading to poor feature coverage.
- Vision encoder attention maps fail to localize relevant image regions, making visual feature interpretation ambiguous or incorrect.
- Inability to find features for basic concepts (e.g., "six" in finger counting) → suggests missing circuits or encoder failure to encode relevant information.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Train a transcoder on a single mid‑layer (e.g., Layer 15) using the multimodal dataset; verify FVU < 0.2 and that top‑activating examples are visually coherent.
2. Compute an attribution graph for a simple VQA prompt (e.g., "What animal is this?" with a cat image); identify the top 5 features contributing to the correct logit and validate via attention maps.
3. Perform feature steering on a mid‑layer visual feature (e.g., "planet") in a Mars prompt; observe if output shifts to related concepts (e.g., "space shuttle"), confirming associativity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How can vision-encoder attention maps be improved to reliably localize relevant image regions for feature interpretation?**  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "the vision-encoder attention maps used for interpreting visual features can be difficult to read: they sometimes fail to localize relevant regions or provide meaningful contextual cues"  
  *Why unresolved:* Unreliable attention maps impede accurate feature annotation, a cornerstone of circuit tracing for VLMs.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* New attention visualization techniques that demonstrate high correlation with human-annotated salient regions across diverse images and tasks.

- **Can cross-layer transcoders effectively capture cross-layer superposition in VLMs, and do they yield more monosemantic features than per-layer transcoders?**  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "our use of per-layer transcoders cannot capture cross-layer superposition" and "we frequently observe many near-duplicate visual features firing in attribution graphs"  
  *Why unresolved:* Cross-layer superposition may lead to feature redundancy and ambiguous circuits, limiting interpretability.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Implementation of cross-layer transcoders, with comparative analysis showing reduced feature duplication and improved circuit coherence on VLM benchmarks.

- **What methods can automate and scale circuit tracing for VLMs to reduce computational cost and human effort?**  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "circuit discovery currently requires substantial human effort" and "computational cost... have risen significantly," with calls for automation.  
  *Why unresolved:* Manual processes and high costs hinder application to larger models and systematic improvement.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Efficient algorithms for feature activation analysis and circuit extraction that scale to larger VLMs, validated by reduced runtime and maintained interpretability.

## Limitations
- Transcoder monosemanticity rests on qualitative top‑activation examples; early‑layer dead latents may leave important low‑level features unexplained.
- Attribution graphs assume local linearity; no error bounds or comparison to non‑linear ablations are provided.
- Attention‑rollout parameters (layers K, head fraction q, pooling b) are underspecified, and map quality lacks quantitative validation.

## Confidence
- **Transcoder decomposition:** Medium – FVU metrics are strong, but monosemanticity remains anecdotal.
- **Causal attribution:** Medium‑Low – steering/patching suggest causality, yet linearization assumptions are not rigorously tested.
- **Attention‑based visual localization:** Low – method described but no independent validation of spatial accuracy.
- **Discovered circuits (hierarchical, arithmetic, associative):** Low – expert grouping yields plausible stories but lacks inter‑annotator agreement or cross‑dataset generalizability.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑prompt feature steering:** For a set of learned features (e.g., “planet”, “six”), apply identical steering perturbations across multiple prompts and measure output distribution shifts to quantify effect‑size robustness.
2. **Linearization error quantification:** Compute Jacobian‑based edge attributions and compare them to brute‑force single‑edge causal effects (e.g., via patching) across a sample of layers; report correlation and error statistics.
3. **Automated dataset curation:** Replace expert‑selected “curated small datasets” with a clustering‑based protocol (e.g., k‑means on activation spectra) and evaluate whether feature interpretability (top‑k examples) remains stable.
