# RIE-Greedy: Regularization-Induced Exploration for Contextual Bandits

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11276  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11276  
- **Reference Count:** 33  
- **Primary Result:** RIE-Greedy replaces explicit exploration mechanisms with pure-greedy action selection on an estimator regularized via early stopping using random validation splits; it matches Thompson Sampling allocation in theory and outperforms ε-greedy, FALCON, and KL-EXP on a large real-world dataset in both stationary and non-stationary settings.

## Executive Summary
Contextual bandit problems often rely on black-box reward estimators (e.g., gradient boosting) that make traditional exploration strategies like Thompson Sampling or UCB difficult to apply. RIE-Greedy addresses this by using early stopping—a standard regularization technique—via random cross-validation splits. The stochasticity in the validation split acts as a natural source of exploration, and early stopping functions as a hypothesis test that accepts new boosting iterations only when validation loss improves. This induces Thompson Sampling–like behavior without requiring explicit exploration bonuses.

The authors prove that in a simplified two‑armed non‑contextual setting, the probability of selecting the worse arm equals the one‑sided p‑value from a normal hypothesis test, mirroring Thompson Sampling. Empirically, on a 200k‑instance email marketing dataset with 113 contextual features and 50 offers, RIE‑Greedy achieves equal or higher cumulative reward than several baselines. Crucially, when reward distributions drift, the early‑stopping oracle automatically reduces the number of boosting iterations, increasing exploration without manual re‑tuning. Adding explicit exploration on top often degrades performance, suggesting that carefully tuned regularization alone can suffice.

## Method Summary
RIE‑Greedy trains an iterative model (e.g., gradient‑boosted trees) on a sliding buffer of historical interactions. For each training epoch: 1) split the buffer randomly into training and validation sets; 2) fit the model iteratively, monitoring validation loss; 3) stop when validation loss fails to improve for `n_wait,max` consecutive rounds, returning the best model `F_best`. At decision time, for a new context `x`, predict rewards for every action `a` using `F_best(x,a)` and select the action with the highest prediction (pure greedy). No explicit exploration (e.g., ε, TS) is added. In non‑stationary environments, a sliding window ensures the buffer contains only recent data, and the early‑stopping depth automatically adjusts to changes in the reward function.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical:** In a two‑armed bandit, the allocation probability of RIE‑Greedy equals the posterior probability used in Thompson Sampling (Proposition 5.1, Theorem 5.2).
- **Empirical (Stationary):** On a real email marketing dataset, RIE‑Greedy matches or exceeds the cumulative reward of ε‑greedy, FALCON variants, and KL‑EXP.
- **Empirical (Non‑Stationary):** During reward drift, the average stopping iteration declines automatically, increasing exploration; RIE‑Greedy outperforms baselines and adding explicit exploration to RIE‑Greedy usually harms performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Random validation splits during early stopping introduce stochasticity that induces exploration analogous to Thompson Sampling.  
**Mechanism:** For each boosting iteration, a new base learner is accepted only if it improves validation loss. The random split makes this acceptance probabilistic. The probability of continuing training is higher when the signal (difference in empirical means between arms) is strong and consistent with validation data. This mirrors TS’s principle of acting proportionally to the posterior probability that an action is optimal.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] states the stochasticity induces TS‑like exploration; [section 5] explains early stopping as a hypothesis test; theoretical proof in the two‑armed case (Proposition 5.1, Theorem 5.2).  
**Break condition:** The proof is restricted to a simplified two‑armed non‑contextual setting; extension to multi‑armed contextual bandits is unproven and may require different analysis.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** In high‑dimensional, diverse contexts, passive exploration from the natural coverage of the action space by a greedy policy can be sufficient.  
**Mechanism:** When context features are rich, different contexts lead to different greedy actions, implicitly sampling suboptimal arms without explicit exploration.  
**Evidence:** [section 7.2] shows RIE‑Greedy without early stopping already performs well in the stationary simulation, indicating passive exploration.  
**Break condition:** Passive exploration is inadequate in low‑context diversity or non‑stationary settings, as noted in prior work and demonstrated in the paper’s non‑stationary experiments.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** During reward distribution shifts, early stopping automatically increases exploration because conflicting signals reduce the frequency of validation loss improvements, causing shallower models.  
**Mechanism:** In a sliding buffer, mixing old and new data during drift leads to poorer validation improvements for additional iterations, triggering early stopping sooner. The resulting lower‑complexity model has higher variance and less reliance on possibly outdated patterns, effectively increasing exploration.  
**Evidence:** [section 7.2] shows a drop in average stopping iteration during drift and a corresponding faster adaptation; also, explicit exploration on top of RIE‑Greedy often hurts performance.  
**Break condition:** Ineffective if the sliding window is too small to provide stable estimates or if drift is too abrupt/gradual for the buffer to capture a conflicting signal.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Buffer → Random Split → Iterative Training → Early Stopping Decision → `F_best` → Greedy Action Selection → Reward → Buffer Update

**Critical path:** The buffer supplies historical data; a random split creates training/validation sets; the iterative model is trained; early stopping picks the best model based on validation loss; that model is used to greedily select actions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Validation split ratio:** Larger validation reduces variance but leaves less training data; smaller increases stochasticity and exploration but may be noisy.  
- **Patience (`n_wait,max`):** Higher values lead to deeper models (less exploration); lower values cause earlier stopping (more exploration).  
- **Sliding window size:** Larger windows give stable estimates but adapt slowly to non‑stationarity; smaller windows adapt faster but with higher variance.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High regret in non‑stationary setting: window too large or patience too high, causing slow adaptation.  
- Repeated selection of the same arm in low‑context‑diversity scenarios: passive exploration insufficient; need explicit exploration.  
- Erratic stopping iterations and unstable policy: validation split too small, making the decision noisy.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce the theoretical two‑armed equivalence: implement a simple two‑armed boosting setup with fixed data, show that the selection probability of the worse arm matches the one‑sided p‑value and approximates TS (as in Fig. 1).  
2. Test on a simplified stationary contextual bandit: use a simulated dataset with 5 action features and 2 contextual features; compare RIE‑Greedy (early stopping) against pure greedy (fixed iterations) and ε‑greedy/FALCON; verify competitive or better reward without tuning exploration parameters.  
3. Test non‑stationary adaptation: implement the paper’s non‑stationary simulation (linear interpolation between two reward functions); plot average stopping iterations over time (like Fig. 7) to confirm a drop during drift; compare cumulative reward of RIE‑Greedy against baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Motivation:** Can the theoretical equivalence between early stopping and Thompson Sampling be extended to contextual bandit settings with multiple arms and high‑dimensional context features? The current proof is restricted to the two‑armed case; extending the framework would provide formal regret guarantees for real‑world applications.  
- **Generalization:** How do other regularization techniques (e.g., L1/L2 penalties, dropout, pruning) compare to early stopping in inducing effective exploration? The paper suggests this is a promising direction for guiding pure‑greedy action selection.  
- **Robustness:** In environments with minimal contextual diversity or extremely sparse rewards, does RIE‑Greedy’s intrinsic exploration provide sufficient coverage, or are additional strategies required? Existing work suggests passive exploration may fail in such settings, but RIE‑Greedy’s automatic adaptation has not been tested under extreme sparsity or low context dimensionality.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis is limited to a two‑armed non‑contextual setting; extension to general multi‑armed and contextual bandits remains unproven.  
- Empirical validation relies on a proprietary email marketing dataset; independent replication on other public datasets is needed to assess external validity.  
- Sensitivity to hyperparameters (validation split ratio, patience, sliding window size) is not analyzed; performance may degrade if these are poorly chosen.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical guarantees** (equivalence to Thompson Sampling) generalize to multi‑armed and contextual settings: **Low**  
- **Empirical superiority** over baselines across diverse domains: **Medium**  
- **Mechanism validity** (cross‑validation randomness induces exploration): **Medium**  
- **Hyperparameter robustness** (performance insensitive to validation split, patience, window size): **Low**
