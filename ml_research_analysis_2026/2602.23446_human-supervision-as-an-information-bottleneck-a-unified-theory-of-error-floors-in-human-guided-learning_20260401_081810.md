# Human Supervision as an Information Bottleneck: A Unified Theory of Error Floors in Human-Guided Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23446
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23446
- Reference count: 26
- Primary result: Persistent errors in human-supervised LLMs stem from the human supervision channel acting as an information bottleneck; hybrid supervision with auxiliary channels can reduce or eliminate this error floor.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the Human-Bounded Intelligence (HBI) theorem, proving that a strictly positive excess-risk floor exists when human supervision is insufficient to capture the latent target. This floor decomposes into annotation noise, preference distortion, and semantic compression. Supported by six mathematical frameworks (operator theory, PAC-Bayes, information theory, causal inference, category theory, game theory), the theory shows non-sufficiency yields positive lower bounds. Empirical experiments on real preference data, synthetic tasks, and GSM8K/HumanEval confirm that human-only supervision hits a performance ceiling, while hybrid supervision with auxiliary signals (e.g., program execution) strictly improves performance and can collapse the floor when the auxiliary is sufficiently informative, with up to +0.059 pairwise accuracy gains.

## Method Summary
The authors validate the HBI theory through experiments comparing human-only and hybrid supervision. Real-world preference data from Dahoas/full-hh-rlhf is used, where human preferences are mixed with auxiliary scores from a TinyLlama model. Synthetic tasks with a known linear reward function allow precise measurement of alignment error and distortion norm. GSM8K and HumanEval benchmarks provide pairs of correct/incorrect solutions, using binary correctness as an auxiliary signal. Reward models (BERT-tiny, DistilRoBERTa-base, RoBERTa-base) are trained via the Bradley-Terry loss on pairwise comparisons. Hybrid supervision mixes human and auxiliary signals via a hyperparameter α (and scaling λ), sweeping α∈{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1} and λ∈{0.5,1.0,2.0}. Training size and label corruption are also varied. Evaluation measures pairwise accuracy on held-out pairs and, for synthetic tasks, alignment error and distortion norm.

## Key Results
- Human-only supervision exhibits a persistent error floor: pairwise accuracy stagnates despite scaling.
- Hybrid supervision with auxiliary channels strictly reduces error, improving pairwise accuracy by up to +0.059 points.
- When the auxiliary signal is sufficient (e.g., binary correctness on synthetic tasks), the error floor collapses completely and distortion norms become zero.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The HBI theorem formalizes that the human supervision channel imposes an irreducible error floor when it cannot convey enough information about the true target. Three complementary mechanistic views explain this:

1. **Information-Theoretic View**: The data-processing inequality limits mutual information between the true target and the learned model to that of the supervision channel. If the channel capacity is below the rate-distortion bound for zero excess risk, a positive distortion floor persists.

2. **Causal/Identifiability View**: The human channel is typically non-invertible: distinct true outputs can produce the same supervision distribution. This non-identifiability means any predictor based on supervision alone cannot distinguish such cases, yielding a Bayes risk floor.

3. **Operator-Theoretic View**: In infinite-width or kernel regimes, the learned operator converges to the human supervision operator. If this operator differs from the ideal target operator (i.e., the channel is insufficient), the excess risk is lower-bounded by a constant proportional to the operator difference.

In all cases, the bound vanishes only when the supervision channel becomes sufficient—i.e., it can perfectly encode the target under the given distortion measure.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Human supervision channel P_H generates S_H from (X, Y*); Auxiliary channel P_A generates S_A from (X, Y*); Model f_theta generates S_M from X; Hybrid combiner F mixes (S_H, S_M, S_A) into S_mix; Learner trains f_theta by minimizing loss L(f_theta(X), S_mix). Data flow: (X, Y*) -> P_H -> S_H; (X, Y*) -> P_A -> S_A; X -> f_theta -> S_M; (S_H, S_A, S_M) -> F -> S_mix; (X, S_mix) -> Learner -> f_theta.
- **Critical path**: 1. Collect dataset with (X, human preferences) and optional auxiliary signals. 2. Define F (with α, λ). 3. Compute S_mix = α·S_H + (1−α)·λ·S_A. 4. Train f_theta with Bradley-Terry loss on pairwise comparisons using S_mix as target. 5. Evaluate on held-out pairs against ground-truth Y*.
- **Design tradeoffs**: α balances human vs auxiliary reliance; too high risks floor, too low may overfit to auxiliary. Auxiliary signals can be costly (e.g., execution). Batch normalization of scores can suppress auxiliary variance, eliminating hybrid gains. Model capacity: even infinite capacity cannot overcome insufficient auxiliary information; smaller models benefit more from hybrid.
- **Failure signatures**: Persistent stagnating performance under human-only; hybrid gain disappears (often due to normalization or degenerate auxiliary variance); distortion norm increases as α→1; reward hacking (high surrogate risk, low true risk).
- **First 3 experiments**: 
  1. *Real preference α-sweep*: Train reward models on Dahoas/full-hh-rlhf with varying α (human vs. TinyLlama auxiliary NLL), measure test pairwise accuracy.
  2. *Synthetic distortion validation*: Use linear reward R*(x,y)=wᵀφ(x,y); compute alignment error ||w_pred−w_true|| and distortion norm ||S_H−R*|| vs α.
  3. *GSM8K correctness test*: Pair correct/incorrect solutions; auxiliary S_A is binary correctness; hybrid scoring with α; expect pass@1 accuracy to reach 1.0 as α→0, showing floor collapse with sufficient auxiliary.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- *Formal model for tool-assisted supervision*: The paper suggests extending the HBI framework to incorporate tool-specific stochastic channels (e.g., program execution) to predict when auxiliary signals collapse the human bias floor. This requires developing an extended theorem and validating it with experiments that show floor collapse on tool-using tasks. (Basis: Section VII)
- *Empirical estimation of bias components*: The theory decomposes the floor into annotation noise, preference distortion, and semantic compression, but no methodology exists to measure these from real human feedback datasets. Controlled annotation studies or synthetic benchmarks with known ground truth are needed to quantify each component. (Basis: Section VII)
- *Tighter information-theoretic bounds*: The current rate-distortion analysis may yield loose lower bounds. Advanced approaches such as feedback information or multi-letter combinatorics could tighten the converse bounds, leading to a more precise characterization of supervision limits; this requires deriving stronger bounds and comparing them to empirical results. (Basis: Section VII)
- *Dynamics of multiple auxiliary channels*: While Proposition 1 addresses a single auxiliary channel, real agents may use multiple tools (e.g., retrieval plus execution). Understanding how these channels interact and optimally combine to reduce the floor demands systematic experiments with various channel combinations. (Basis: Section VII)

## Limitations
- Key implementation details for replication are missing: exact training hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs), configuration of the TinyLlama auxiliary verifier, batch normalization specifics in HumanEval, and synthetic data generation protocol.
- The empirical claim of hybrid improvement is not foolproof; normalization artifacts can suppress auxiliary signal, and auxiliary sufficiency thresholds remain unknown.
- Theoretical break conditions (channel sufficiency) are difficult to assess in practice; measuring information flow from auxiliary signals to target is non-trivial.

## Confidence
- Core theoretical mechanisms (HBI theorem and decomposition): High
- Empirical claim (hybrid supervision strictly reduces error): Medium
- Break conditions (channel sufficiency eliminates floor): Low

## Next Checks
1. *Auxiliary Sufficiency Threshold Test*: On the synthetic linear task, systematically vary auxiliary signal quality (e.g., signal-to-noise ratio) to quantify the exact information gap I(Y*;S_A) required to collapse the error floor, validating the rate-distortion condition from Theorem 4.
2. *Normalization Artifact Diagnostic*: Re-run the HumanEval experiment without batch z-score normalization. Compare hybrid performance to the normalized result to confirm whether the gain disappearance is an artifact of score scaling rather than auxiliary insufficiency.
3. *Human Channel Non-Invertibility Measurement*: On a held-out set of real preference data, estimate the fraction of (x, y*_1, y*_2) tuples where human channel outputs are identical or statistically indistinguishable (P_H(S|X,y*_1) ≈ P_H(S|X,y*_2)) to directly probe Assumption 4 and the predicted irreducible error set.
