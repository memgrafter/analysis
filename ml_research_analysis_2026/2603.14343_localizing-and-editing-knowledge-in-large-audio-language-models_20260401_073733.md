# Localizing and Editing Knowledge in Large Audio-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14343
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14343
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** The proposed speech-driven locate-then-edit framework achieves 77.60 overall audio fact editing score (95.2 efficacy) via sequential cross-modal single-layer editing.

## Executive Summary
Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs) encode factual knowledge in a static, multimodal manner that is difficult to update precisely. Existing text-based knowledge editing methods fail for speech due to continuous audio representations and cross-modal storage. This work introduces the first benchmark for audio fact editing and a locate-then-edit framework that first identifies knowledge storage locations using speech-aware causal tracing, then applies targeted parameter updates. The approach reveals that factual knowledge is jointly encoded in both the audio encoder and text decoder, with audio layers often more prominent. Single-layer editing, especially in the audio pathway, significantly outperforms fine-tuning and multi-layer edits. A sequential cross-modal strategy—editing an audio layer then a text layer—further improves performance, demonstrating fine-grained, effective knowledge control in speech AI systems.

## Method Summary
The framework operationalizes two stages: localization and editing. For localization, it adapts causal tracing to speech by using forced alignment to corrupt acoustic features precisely over subject-word spans, then measuring the Indirect Effect (IE) across audio encoder and text decoder layers to identify causally implicated layers. For editing, it employs a rank-one update derived from linear associative memory: given a key vector (mean-pooled representations over subject-aligned frames/tokens) and a target value (new object embedding), it computes a residual and solves a constrained least-squares problem to update a single layer's weights. The best performing variant first edits an audio layer identified by tracing, then projects the resulting residual into the text decoder space to guide a subsequent decoder edit, ensuring cross-modal consistency.

## Key Results
- Factual knowledge is jointly encoded across audio encoder and text decoder layers, not isolated to the text pathway.
- Single-layer audio editing (71.36 overall score) substantially outperforms text-only editing (64.10), fine-tuning (61.50), and multi-layer editing (63.52).
- Sequential cross-modal single-layer editing (audio then text) achieves state-of-the-art 77.60 overall score, with 95.2 efficacy on direct edits and 78.7 on paraphrases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Factual knowledge in LALMs is jointly encoded across both the audio encoder and the text decoder modules.
- **Mechanism:** Speech-aware causal tracing corrupts acoustic features aligned to the subject span and restores clean activations at specific layers. A high Indirect Effect (IE) score indicates the layer's representation causally contributes to the correct object prediction. Significant IE in both modalities demonstrates joint dependence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Layer-wise IE plots (section 5.1) show non-zero contributions from audio encoder layers (0-32) and text decoder layers (33-80). Abstract states "factual knowledge is jointly encoded in audio and text modules."
- **Break condition:** Near-zero IE for all text decoder layers across a broad fact set would indicate knowledge stored solely in audio, invalidating joint encoding.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Single-layer, modality-specific parameter editing (especially in audio encoder) is more effective and specific than fine-tuning or multi-layer editing.
- **Mechanism:** The locate-then-edit framework applies a constrained rank-one update (`Δ = Rkᵀ(kkᵀ+λI)⁻¹`) to a single causally traced layer. This minimally perturbs weights for the specific key `k` (derived from subject-relation) to shift output toward `v_target`, avoiding collateral damage.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports single-layer audio editing score 71.36, beating fine-tuning (61.50) and multi-layer (63.52). Table 1 shows audio single-layer NS 70.34 vs multi-layer 49.72. Top-1 traced audio layer yields 71.70 score.
- **Break condition:** If single-layer edits consistently fail (ES low) while multi-layer succeeds, facts may be too distributed for narrow updates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Sequential cross-modal editing (audio layer first, then text layer) propagates updates more robustly than unimodal editing.
- **Mechanism:** After applying `Δ_audio`, the residual between desired and current encoder output is projected (via the projection layer) into the text decoder's space. This projected residual drives a subsequent single-layer edit (`Δ_text`), grounding the text update in the modified audio representation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: sequential editing achieves 77.60 (ES 95.2, PS 78.7, NS 64.7), improving over best unimodal (71.36). Section 3.2 describes the residual projection step.
- **Break condition:** If sequential editing performs worse than the best unimodal edit, the conditioning introduces error or instability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Causal Tracing for Knowledge Localization**
  - **Why needed:** It defines the locate step: how to intervene on continuous speech (via forced alignment) and interpret IE to identify candidate layers.
  - **Quick check:** For subject "New York", corruption boundary is its aligned frame span; applying noise at a mid-level convolution layer would disrupt high-level acoustic-phonetic features critical for fact recall.

- **Concept: Rank-One Model Editing (ROME/MEMIT style)**
  - **Why needed:** It provides the mathematical basis for the edit step: the linear associative memory formulation `Wk ≈ v`, residual `R = v_target - Wk`, and update `Δ` that minimally shifts weights for one fact.
  - **Quick check:** The `λI` term (regularization) prevents overfitting to the specific key `k` by damping the update, which improves edit specificity and reduces interference with other facts.

- **Concept: Cross-Modal Representation Alignment in LALMs**
  - **Why needed:** Sequential editing relies on mapping a residual from the audio encoder's space to the text decoder's space via the projection layer, which bridges different dimensionalities and semantic subspaces.
  - **Quick check:** If audio encoder output dim=1024 and first text decoder layer expects 4096, the projection layer (linear) transforms the audio embeddings; using it for residuals risks misalignment if the two subspaces are not linearly related.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Raw waveform -> Audio Encoder (conv + Transformer) -> Projection Layer (linear) -> Text Decoder (LLM backbone) -> Output logits

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare: Generate spoken prompt `x` for fact `(s,r,o_c->o_target)`. Obtain word timestamps via forced alignment (WhisperX).
2. Localize: Run clean, corrupt (Gaussian noise on subject-span audio frames), and restore passes at each layer. Compute IE = P_restore(o_c) - P_corr(o_c). Identify top audio and text layers by highest IE.
3. Edit: For selected layer `ℓ`, extract key vector `k` (mean-pooled activations over first/last subject word frames or tokens). Compute residual `R = v_target - Wk`. Compute `Δ` and apply to `W`.
4. Evaluate: Test on direct `(s,r)`, paraphrases (PS), and neighbor facts (NS).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Single-layer vs. Multi-layer: Single-layer is simpler, more specific (higher NS), less interference. Multi-layer may boost ES but risks lower NS (Table 1: Audio multi-layer NS 49.72 vs single-layer 70.34).
- Modality choice: Audio-first single-layer editing yields unexpectedly strong ES (78.2), suggesting audio pathways are a bottleneck for spoken facts. Fine-tuning destroys specificity (NS drops).
- Sequential cross-modal: Best overall (77.60) but adds complexity (two edits, projection). Gains high ES (95.2) and good PS (78.7), but NS slightly lower (64.7).

**Failure signatures:**
- **Low ES (<50):** Wrong layer (low AIE), incorrect `k` extraction (pooling wrong spans), `λ` too large, or fact not initially known.
- **Low PS relative to ES:** Edit overfitted to exact prompt; layer too shallow (surface form) or `k` too prompt-specific.
- **Low NS (<70):** Edit interfered with unrelated facts sharing relation `r`; likely due to non-localized edit or multi-layer update without null-space protection.
- **Degradation on audio-only tasks:** Editing an audio layer harms unrelated speech QA, indicating corruption of general acoustic representations.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate causal tracing heatmap for 50 CounterFact examples; verify peak audio AIE in mid-to-late encoder (layers ~25-31) per Figure 2.
2. Perform single-layer audio editing (top-1 traced layer) on 20 facts; confirm ES > 70 and NS > 65. If NS low, check first-subject-word pooling.
3. Apply sequential cross-modal editing (audio then text) on 20 facts; expect ES > 90 and overall S > 75. If ES low, debug residual projection (dimension mismatch).

## Limitations
- Benchmark scope is limited (Known-1000, CounterFact subset); generalization to broader domains, languages, and spontaneous speech is unknown.
- Model specificity: Results only on Qwen2 + Whisper; joint encoding and editing efficacy may not hold for other architectures.
- Edit stability and scaling: Long-term stability under many sequential edits and potential interference remain untested; reliance on forced alignment for `k` extraction is a practical fragility.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Joint Encoding):** High. Consistent non-zero IE across modalities in tracing experiments.
- **Mechanism 2 (Single-Layer Efficacy):** Medium. Strong empirical results on one model, but assumes narrow storage, contradicting distributed knowledge views.
- **Mechanism 3 (Sequential Cross-Modal Benefit):** Low. Improvement is clear but residual-projection mechanism speculative; benefit might stem from simple error accumulation.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate tracing pattern:** For 100 held-out facts, rerun speech-aware causal tracing; verify peak audio AIE consistently in mid-late encoder (25-31) and non-zero text AIE.
2. **Test single-layer edit stability:** Apply single-layer audio edits (top-1 layer) for 500 facts sequentially; monitor ES/NS degradation on a held-out set after every 50 edits; NS drop >15 points indicates catastrophic interference.
3. **Ablate sequential conditioning:** Compare sequential audio-then-text vs. best unimodal audio and best unimodal text edits on 200 facts. If sequential outperforms both by >5 overall points, it supports the residual-projection mechanism; if it only matches the better unimodal, sequential may be merely combinatorial.
