# Rolling-Origin Validation Reverses Model Rankings in Multi-Step PM10 Forecasting: XGBoost, SARIMA, and Persistence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20315
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20315
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Rolling-origin validation reverses model rankings: XGBoost loses short-horizon skill against persistence while SARIMA remains skillful, revealing static splits overstate operational usefulness.

## Executive Summary
This study examines how the choice of validation protocol influences the perceived operational utility of PM10 forecasting models. Using 2,350 daily observations from a single urban background station in Elche, Spain, the authors compare XGBoost, SARIMA, and a persistence baseline under both a static chronological split and a rolling-origin evaluation that mimics real-time deployment. Under static evaluation, XGBoost appears superior across all 1–7 day horizons. However, rolling-origin validation reveals that XGBoost fails to consistently beat persistence at short leads (negative skill at h=1, near zero at h=2–4), while SARIMA maintains positive persistence-relative skill throughout. This reversal demonstrates that static splits can materially overstate a model’s operational value. The authors propose the predictability horizon (H*)—the maximum lead time with positive skill relative to persistence—as an interpretable metric for operational forecast lifetime.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses daily PM10 concentrations from an urban background station in Elche, Spain, spanning 2017–2024 (n=2,350 after quality filtering). Two evaluation protocols are compared: (1) a static chronological split, training on 2017–2022 and testing on 2023; and (2) a rolling-origin evaluation with an expanding training window and monthly origins from January 2020 to December 2023. In the rolling-origin protocol, all preprocessing (e.g., scaling, feature engineering) is performed solely on the training data at each fold to prevent temporal leakage. Models include: persistence (lag‑1 forecast), SARIMA with fixed orders (2,0,2)(0,1,1)7, and XGBoost trained on lagged PM10 values and seasonal dummy variables. Multi‑step forecasts are generated for horizons h=1 to 7 days. Performance is measured using persistence‑relative skill SS(h) = 1 – RMSE_model(h)/RMSE_persistence(h). The predictability horizon H* is defined as the maximum h for which SS(h) > 0.

## Key Results
- Under the static split, both XGBoost and SARIMA achieve H* = 7 (positive SS for all horizons), with XGBoost showing lower errors than SARIMA across all leads.
- Rolling‑origin evaluation drastically reduces XGBoost’s short‑horizon skill: SS is negative at h=1 and near zero at h=2–4, effectively collapsing its H* to less than one day; SARIMA, in contrast, maintains positive SS for all horizons and retains H* = 7.
- Consequently, the model ranking reverses: XGBoost, apparently superior in the static setting, exhibits weaker operational robustness than SARIMA under deployment‑realistic validation.

## Why This Works
### Mechanism 1: Static splits cause temporal leakage and non‑representative test periods
- **Claim:** Static chronological splits can materially overstate a model’s operational forecast skill due to temporal leakage from non‑causal preprocessing and a single, non‑representative test period.
- **Mechanism:** A static split uses one fixed training set and one fixed test set. If any preprocessing (e.g., scaling, feature engineering) uses information from the entire series or the test set is atypical, the evaluation reflects an unrealistic information set. The model’s apparent skill captures leakage artifacts rather than genuine predictive performance under sequential, information‑constrained deployment.
- **Core assumption:** Operational forecasting involves repeated model updates as new data arrives; a single static holdout does not replicate this information arrival process.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “…evaluations often use static chronological splits and omit persistence baselines, so the operational added value under routine updating is unclear.”  
  [section 2.3] “In the static setting… it does not reproduce the repeated updating process that characterizes operational forecasting systems.”
- **Break condition:** The overstatement disappears when evaluation uses a leakage‑safe, rolling‑origin protocol with train‑only preprocessing at each fold, as demonstrated by the reversal in XGBoost’s skill profile.

### Mechanism 2: Persistence is a demanding benchmark for autocorrelated environmental series
- **Claim:** Persistence (lag‑1 naive forecast) is a demanding benchmark in autocorrelated environmental series; improvements over it are necessary for operational usefulness, not just statistically significant error reduction.
- **Mechanism:** In highly autocorrelated PM10 series, the value of a forecast is its ability to beat the simplest plausible forecast that assumes no change. Absolute error metrics (RMSE, MAE) can show improvement over a trivial baseline (e.g., mean forecast) while still being worse than persistence. Persistence‑relative skill (SS = 1 – Err_model / Err_pers) directly measures added value against temporal inertia.
- **Core assumption:** Operational decision‑makers require forecasts that are *informative* relative to the most recent observation; a model that does not beat persistence provides no actionable advantage.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “…evaluate… against persistence… XGBoost fails to consistently beat persistence at short horizons…”  
  [section 2.6] “A positive value indicates an improvement over persistence… operationally interpretable.”  
  [section 1] “In highly autocorrelated environmental series, strong short‑lead performance may reflect temporal inertia more than genuine predictive skill.”
- **Break condition:** A model’s persistence‑relative skill becomes non‑positive (SS ≤ 0) at a given horizon, indicating its forecasts are no more useful than assuming today’s value.

### Mechanism 3: Predictability horizon (H*) condenses horizon‑dependent skill into an operational metric
- **Claim:** The Predictability Horizon (H*) is an operational summary of a model’s useful forecast lifetime, defined as the maximum lead time with positive persistence‑relative skill under a specified validation protocol.
- **Mechanism:** H* condenses the horizon‑dependent skill profile into a single, actionable metric. It is computed from the vector of SS(h) values: H* = max{h: SS(h) > 0}. It forces interpretation that usefulness degrades with lead time and must be sustained to count. Its value is protocol‑dependent (static vs. rolling‑origin).
- **Core assumption:** Forecast usefulness is binary (positive/negative) at each horizon relative to persistence, and the maximum horizon with positive skill is a key operational threshold for planning interventions.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “The predictability horizon (H*) is introduced as the maximum lead time with positive persistence‑relative skill…”  
  [section 2.7] Full definition and computational formula.  
  [section 3.1.2 & 3.1.4] Shows H* = 7 for both models under static split, but under rolling‑origin, XGBoost’s H* effectively shortens (negative skill at h=1) while SARIMA’s H* remains 7.
- **Break condition:** H* becomes zero or a small value, indicating the model offers no persistence‑relative advantage within the evaluated horizon range.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Temporal Causality & Leakage‑Safe Validation  
  *Why needed here:* The core finding hinges on the difference between static and rolling‑origin evaluation. Understanding that future information must never influence training or preprocessing at prediction time is essential to grasp why rankings reverse.  
  *Quick check question:* If you standardize features using the full dataset’s mean and standard deviation before creating a static train/test split, what type of leakage has occurred, and how would a rolling‑origin protocol prevent it?
- **Concept:** Persistence as an Operational Baseline  
  *Why needed here:* The paper’s thesis is that model value is defined relative to persistence, not absolute error. Without this concept, the significance of negative skill for XGBoost at short horizons is missed.  
  *Quick check question:* In an autocorrelated PM10 series with daily autocorrelation of 0.7, would you expect a persistence forecast to be more or less skillful than a climatology (monthly average) forecast for a 1‑day ahead prediction? Why?
- **Concept:** Multi‑Step Forecasting Horizons & Skill Profiles  
  *Why needed here:* The result is horizon‑specific; a model can be good at h=1 but bad at h=3. Interpreting H* requires seeing the entire Skill(h) profile, not just the terminal horizon value.  
  *Quick check question:* If Model A has Skill(1)=0.1, Skill(2)=0.05, Skill(3)=0.01 and Model B has Skill(1)=0.05, Skill(2)=0.04, Skill(3)=0.03, which has a larger H* (assuming H_max=3)? Which might be considered more *reliable* for a 2‑day forecast?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  1. Data Pipeline: Raw PM10 daily series (2017–2024) -> quality filtering -> aligned training/test windows.  
  2. Model Zoo: Persistence (lag‑1), SARIMA (fixed orders, state‑updated), XGBoost (retrained each fold with lag features).  
  3. Validation Engine: Two protocols: (a) Static split (single train/test), (b) Rolling‑origin (expanding window, monthly origin steps, train‑only preprocessing).  
  4. Scoring Module: Computes RMSE/MAE per horizon, then SS(h) = 1 – RMSE_model(h)/RMSE_pers(h).  
  5. Summary Aggregator: Computes H* = max{h: mean(SS(h)) > 0} across folds and plots full SS(h) profile.
- **Critical path:** Data -> (Static Split *or* Rolling‑Origin Loop) [Train Model -> Forecast h=1..7 -> Store errors] -> Aggregate errors per horizon -> Compute SS(h) -> Derive H* & plot profile.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - SARIMA: Fixed orders (identified once) vs. re‑identifying per fold. Paper uses fixed for consistency, but re‑identification might capture non‑stationarity.  
  - XGBoost: Feature engineering (lags, seasonal dummies) must be done *inside* the rolling‑origin loop using only training data (train‑only). This is computationally heavier but prevents leakage.  
  - Persistence benchmark: Always uses the *same origin observation* for all horizons (pers[t+h] = y_t). This is correct and highlights model deterioration.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Static‑split illusion: A model shows uniformly positive SS(h) in a single test year but fails under rolling‑origin (e.g., XGBoost’s negative SS(1)).  
  - Protocol mismatch: Comparing a model retrained every fold (XGBoost) against one with fixed parameters (SARIMA) is valid here (as in paper) but must be noted; a fairer comparison might retrain SARIMA orders too.  
  - H* misinterpretation: Reporting H* = 7 from a static split without showing the rolling‑origin profile is misleading. H* must be tied to its validation protocol.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate the static split: Train XGBoost on 2017–2022, test on 2023 (after static feature engineering). Compute SS(h) and H*. Expect positive SS across h=1–7.  
  2. Implement rolling‑origin with train‑only preprocessing: For each monthly origin from 2020–2023, use data up to t‑1 to: (a) compute preprocessing params (e.g., scaler means), (b) train SARIMA(2,0,2)(0,1,1)7 (fixed) or XGBoost, (c) generate 1–7 step forecasts. Compute SS(h) across all folds.  
  3. Compare profiles: Plot mean(SS(h)) with confidence intervals (across folds) for both models under rolling‑origin. Identify H* per model. Verify SARIMA’s SS(h) is positive for all h, while XGBoost’s is negative at h=1 and near zero at h=2–4.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Do the observed ranking reversals between XGBoost and SARIMA generalize across diverse monitoring stations, pollutants, and climatic regimes?  
  **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state the empirical analysis is based on a single station and that “future work should extend this framework across stations, pollutants, and climatic regimes, and test whether the ranking reversals observed here persist under broader spatial and temporal heterogeneity.”  
  **Why unresolved:** The study’s findings are confined to one urban background site in southeastern Spain with a specific Mediterranean climate and PM10 dynamics; patterns may differ in other environments (e.g., industrial, rural, different latitudes) or for other pollutants (e.g., NO₂, O₃) with distinct temporal behaviors.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi‑site study replicating the rolling‑origin vs. static evaluation across a network of stations with varying pollution sources, meteorological conditions, and data completeness, comparing model rankings and H* values systematically.
- **Question:** How does the predictability horizon H* and the relative skill profile change when persistence is replaced with alternative, potentially stronger naive baselines (e.g., seasonal naive, climatological averages) at longer lead times?  
  **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge a limitation: “the persistence benchmark used in this study is operationally appropriate… but it is not necessarily the strongest naive baseline at longer horizons; seasonal or climatological baselines may become increasingly competitive… future work should evaluate H* relative to multiple baseline families.”  
  **Why unresolved:** H* is defined solely relative to persistence, but operational usefulness might be better gauged against baselines that incorporate known seasonality or long‑term averages, especially beyond short horizons where persistence skill decays.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Recompute H* and horizon‑specific skill scores for XGBoost and SARIMA using seasonal naive (e.g., previous year’s same day) and climatological (e.g., long‑term daily mean) benchmarks under rolling‑origin evaluation, then compare the resulting H* values and skill trajectories.
- **Question:** Can we quantify the systematic overestimation of operational forecast utility when using static chronological splits instead of rolling‑origin validation, and does this overstatement correlate with model complexity or data characteristics?  
  **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper demonstrates that static splits overstate XGBoost’s utility but does not quantify the magnitude of this overstatement across models or horizons. The authors call for work to “quantify the extent to which apparent predictive gains are reduced when static evaluation is replaced by deployment‑realistic rolling‑origin evaluation.”  
  **Why unresolved:** While the reversal in rankings is shown, the study does not measure how much static‑skill metrics (e.g., RMSE, SS) are inflated relative to rolling‑origin metrics, nor whether this inflation is predictable from model flexibility or autocorrelation strength.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** For multiple models (including those with varying complexity) and multiple time series (with different autocorrelation structures), compute the average difference in persistence‑relative skill (SS) and H* between static and rolling‑origin evaluations; analyze whether the delta correlates with model hyperparameters (e.g., tree depth) or series properties (e.g., autocorrelation at lag 1).

## Limitations
- Data retrieval and missing‑value handling procedures for the Elche PM10 series are not fully specified, impeding exact replication.
- Critical modeling choices (XGBoost feature set, SARIMA parameter update strategy) are omitted, leaving uncertainty about result robustness.
- The evaluation is restricted to a single urban background station; applicability to other pollutants or climates remains untested.

## Confidence
- Static splits overstate operational skill: Medium
- XGBoost loses short‑horizon skill under rolling‑origin: Medium
- SARIMA maintains positive persistence‑relative skill: Medium
- Persistence‑relative skill (SS) and predictability horizon (H*) are operationally meaningful: High

## Next Checks
1. **SARIMA order adaptation:** Re‑run the rolling‑origin validation with SARIMA parameters re‑estimated at each origin (e.g., via auto‑ARIMA) and compare SS(h) profiles to the fixed‑order version.
2. **XGBoost robustness analysis:** Within the rolling‑origin framework, perform a grid search over n_estimators, max_depth, learning_rate, and lag ranges (1‑14 days); assess whether any configuration yields positive SS at h=1‑4.
3. **Multi‑station generalization:** Apply the same static and rolling‑origin protocols to at least two other PM10 stations (e.g., urban traffic and rural background) in the same network; examine whether the ranking reversal between XGBoost and SARIMA persists.
