# Computational modeling of early language learning from acoustic speech and audiovisual input without linguistic priors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08359
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08359
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Self-supervised and audiovisual models can bootstrap phonetic and lexical knowledge from raw speech, with a consistent developmental trajectory of phonemes emerging first, followed by lexicon and semantics.

## Executive Summary
This review examines computational models that simulate infant language acquisition from raw acoustic speech and audiovisual input without innate linguistic categories. It focuses on two paradigms: self-supervised learning (SSL), where models predict future audio, and visually grounded speech (VGS), where models associate speech with visual scenes. Both approaches demonstrate that predictive pressures alone can induce emergent phonemic and lexical representations. SSL models achieve robust phoneme discrimination on clean speech but struggle with realistic child-centered audio without additional inductive biases. VGS models acquire word-referent mappings and implicit segmentation, with phonemic knowledge forming first. Across paradigms, a consistent hierarchy emerges: phonemic discrimination precedes lexical acquisition, which in turn precedes semantic grounding. The findings highlight both the potential and current limitations of modeling early language learning from ecologically plausible input.

## Method Summary
The review synthesizes results from two computational frameworks. Self-supervised learning (SSL) models, such as Autoregressive Predictive Coding (APC) and Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC), are trained to predict future acoustic features or latent representations from past speech context. This generic objective forces the network to internalize speech regularities, leading to emergent phonemic and lexical categories. Visually grounded speech (VGS) models use separate speech and visual encoders trained with a contrastive objective to match spoken utterances with corresponding images. Cross-modal pressure causes speech representations to organize hierarchically, from phonemic detail in early layers to whole-word and semantic information deeper in the network, with segmentation emerging implicitly from consistent visual associations. Both paradigms are evaluated using psycholinguistic-inspired metrics like ABX phoneme discrimination and lexical spot-the-word tasks.

## Key Results
- SSL models (e.g., STELA) achieve >81% phoneme discrimination (ABX) and ~63% above-chance lexical discrimination after training on 3,200 hours of audiobooks, showing a native-language advantage.
- VGS models learn word-referent mappings and implicit segmentation without explicit mechanisms; phonemic representations emerge first, followed by lexical and semantic knowledge. Audiovisual input accelerates word learning compared to auditory-only training.
- Models trained on real child-centered audio require inductive biases (e.g., speaker separation, speech/non-speech detection) to match performance on cleaner audiobook data, underscoring challenges of ecological input.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Unsupervised phonetic and lexical learning via self-supervised audio prediction
Self-supervised learning models like APC and CPC acquire phonemic discrimination and lexical knowledge by predicting future acoustic context. This generic predictive pressure forces the network to internalize statistical regularities of speech, such as phonotactics and word boundaries, causing phonemic and lexical categories to emerge in internal representations. The core assumption is that minimizing prediction error over long sequences will induce latent linguistic structure because such structure is the most efficient predictor. Evidence: STELA achieves >81% ABX and ~63% lexical discrimination; however, on noisy child-centered audio, performance collapses unless inductive biases like speaker separation are added.

### Mechanism 2: Audiovisual cross-situational learning for word-referent mapping and implicit segmentation
Visually grounded speech models learn word-referent associations and implicitly segment speech via a contrastive objective that matches spoken utterances to images. Cross-situational regularities—where the same word co-occurs with its referent across many scenes—bootstrap word meanings. The speech encoder self-organizes hierarchically: early layers capture phonemic detail (needed to distinguish word forms), middle layers capture syllables, and deeper layers capture whole-word and semantic information. Segmentation emerges as tokens with consistent visual associations. Evidence: Phonemic information is strongest in early layers (Alishahi et al., 2017); lexical segmentation emerges without explicit mechanisms (Khorrami & Räsänen, 2021). Failure occurs with sparse naming events or static images (poor verb learning).

### Mechanism 3: Emergent developmental trajectory: phonemes before lexicon, lexicon before semantics
Across SSL and VGS, phonemic discrimination emerges earlier and more robustly than lexical discrimination, which precedes stable word-referent knowledge. This progression from local to global regularities is scaffolded: fine-grained phonetic invariance is needed to reduce noise for subsequent lexical and semantic learning. Hierarchical network architectures mirror this, with phonemic info in early layers and lexical/semantic info deeper. Evidence: The relative order of acquisition (phonemic → lexical → referential) remains consistent across training schedules (Khorrami et al., 2023) and is replicated in multiple studies (e.g., Lavechin et al., 2025; Khorrami & Räsänen, 2021).

## Foundational Learning
- **Self-supervised learning (SSL)**: Predictive objectives (e.g., future audio prediction) generate supervision from raw data, eliminating manual labels. *Why needed*: Enables learning from vast unlabeled speech. *Quick check*: What is the key architectural difference between APC (predicts acoustics) and CPC (predicts latents), and why does CPC typically yield more invariant representations?
- **Cross-situational learning**: Resolves referential ambiguity by aggregating word-referent co-occurrence statistics across scenes. *Why needed*: Allows infants to map words to meanings without explicit pairing. *Quick check*: If an infant hears "Look at the dog!" while seeing a dog and a ball, how do they eventually learn that "dog" refers to the dog?
- **Emergent representations**: Categories (phonemes, words) arise from optimization rather than being predefined. *Why needed*: Aligns with constructivist theories of infant learning. *Quick check*: Why might phonemic categories appear in early layers of a VGS model while whole-word forms appear in deeper layers?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Speech encoder (CNN/RNN) -> Autoregressive context module (RNN/Transformer) -> Prediction head (linear layer) [SSL]; plus Visual encoder (CNN) -> Associative mapping (dot/bilinear product) [VGS].
- **Critical path**: 1. Raw audio -> speech encoder -> latent sequence. 2. Past latents -> autoregressive module -> context. 3. Context -> prediction head -> predicted future latent/acoustics. 4. For VGS: speech embedding and visual embedding -> similarity score. 5. Contrastive/MSE loss computed on batch. 6. End-to-end backpropagation.
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - APC vs CPC: APC predicts raw acoustics (less invariant); CPC predicts latents (more abstract, scales better).
  - Encoder: CNNs (fast, local) vs RNNs (temporal, sequential); hybrid balances both.
  - Causality: Infant-like models must be strictly causal (no future access); non-causal (BERT-style) is less ecologically plausible.
  - Unimodal (SSL) vs multimodal (VGS): VGS accelerates lexical learning but requires paired data and may overfit to visual noise.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Phonemic: ABX ~50%, representations cluster by speaker not phoneme.
  - Lexical: Spot-the-word ~50%, no improvement with exposure.
  - Hierarchical: Phonemic info only in deep layers (opposite of expected).
  - Catastrophic forgetting: Performance drops when switching tasks/languages.
  - Overfitting: High train, low test scores on out-of-distribution speakers/noise.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Replicate STELA: Train CPC on 100h English audiobooks; expect ~75% ABX, ~55% lexical.
  2. Native-language bias test: Train separate English/French models; each should show higher cross-language ABX for its native language.
  3. Audiovisual validation: Train VGS on SPEECH-COCO; evaluate layer-wise ABX (early layer advantage), word discrimination, image-speech retrieval recall@10.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What minimal inductive biases are required for self-supervised models to learn from realistic child-centered speech? Current models need biases like speech/non-speech separation on noisy recordings, but which are essential and whether they can be learned versus must be innate remains unclear.
2. Why do current self-supervised speech models fail to replicate the developmental decline in non-native phonetic discrimination observed in human infants? Models show increasing non-native discrimination with learning, contrary to infant perceptual narrowing, suggesting missing mechanisms for competitive category learning or critical periods.
3. How does concurrent visual input modulate the emergence and ordering of phonemic, lexical, and semantic representations in VGS models? The consistent order (phonemes → lexicon → semantics) holds across auditory and audiovisual training, but the causal mechanisms—network architecture, input statistics, or prediction objectives—are not resolved.

## Limitations
- SSL models succeed on clean audiobooks but falter on noisy child-centered recordings without extra inductive biases (e.g., speaker separation, speech detection).
- VGS models rely on static images, limiting verb acquisition; sparse naming events in naturalistic data impede lexical learning.
- Training data requirements (≥3200 hours) far exceed infants' actual exposure, raising scalability and efficiency questions.

## Confidence
- *SSL phonetic/lexical bootstrap*: **Medium** — strong evidence on curated speech but fragile on ecological input.
- *Audiovisual cross-situational learning*: **Medium** — works under controlled conditions but breaks on sparse events and for verbs.
- *Developmental trajectory (phonemes → lexicon → semantics)*: **High** — consistently observed across SSL and VGS models with no reported counterexamples.

## Next Checks
1. **Ecological SSL replication**: Train a CPC model (e.g., STELA) on a large child-centered corpus without auxiliary biases. Compare phoneme (ABX) and lexical discrimination versus audiobook training to quantify the ecological gap.
2. **Dynamic vision for verb learning**: Equip VGS models with video encoders (not static images) and train on action-rich paired data. Assess noun vs. verb learning to test if motion cues alleviate the noun bias.
3. **Exposure-curve analysis**: For SSL models, vary training data from 100h to 3200h. Track emergence of phonemic, lexical, and semantic probes to see if the hierarchical order persists at lower exposure levels.
