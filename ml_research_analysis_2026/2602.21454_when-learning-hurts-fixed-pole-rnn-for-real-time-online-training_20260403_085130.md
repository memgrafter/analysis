# When Learning Hurts: Fixed-Pole RNN for Real-Time Online Training

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.21454  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21454  
- **Reference count:** 12  
- **Primary result:** Fixed-pole RNNs (with frozen recurrent dynamics and trainable linear readout) overcome the non-convex, ill-conditioned optimization landscape of standard trainable RNNs, yielding stable, faster convergence and superior performance in data-constrained real-time tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the inefficiency of learning recurrent neural network (RNN) poles via backpropagation through time (BPTT) in real‑time, data‑constrained scenarios. It proposes using fixed‑pole RNN architectures—where the recurrent dynamics are initialized and frozen, and only a linear readout layer is trained (an Echo State Network approach). Theoretically, the authors prove that optimizing pole locations creates a highly non‑convex and ill‑conditioned optimization landscape, even for linear systems. Empirically, on a wireless symbol detection task, trainable RNNs suffer from persistent gradient plateaus, rapidly increasing condition numbers (~10³), and unstable eigenvalue drift. In contrast, fixed‑pole networks (including domain‑configured variants) provide stable, well‑conditioned state representations, converge faster, and achieve lower final task loss with much reduced training complexity.

## Method Summary
The study compares three RNN architectures for real‑time wireless symbol detection under limited data: (1) a *trainable RNN* with all parameters (input, recurrent, output) optimized via BPTT; (2) a *random Echo State Network (ESN)* where recurrent and input weights are randomly initialized (spectral radius < 1) and frozen, and only a linear readout is trained via regularized least squares; (3) a *configured ESN* that uses domain knowledge to initialize the recurrent matrix (e.g., aligning poles with channel characteristics). All models have 32 recurrent units and are trained for up to 15,000 epochs using gradient descent for the trainable RNN and direct solve for the ESN readouts. Performance is measured by task loss (MSE), gradient norms, condition number of the recurrent matrix, and eigenvalue distributions.

## Key Results
- Fixed‑pole RNNs avoid the non‑convex, ill‑conditioned optimization problem inherent in pole learning.
- Trainable RNNs exhibit persistent gradient plateaus, rapidly increasing condition numbers (~10³), and unstable eigenvalue drift during BPTT.
- Fixed‑pole architectures (both random and domain‑configured) achieve lower final task loss, faster convergence, and significantly reduced training complexity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Pole learning creates a fundamentally non‑convex optimization landscape due to permutation invariance of unordered pole‑coefficient pairs.  
- **Mechanism:** The objective function’s value is identical under any permutation of the pole‑index pairing (e.g., swapping (b₁, β₁) with (b₂, β₂)). This creates multiple isolated optimal basins; gradient descent on ordered parameter vectors cannot move between them, causing stagnation.  
- **Core assumption:** The system is identifiable (sufficient samples N ≥ 2K) and parameters are in simplified form (distinct poles, non‑zero coefficients).  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section III] Lemma III.5 proves that for any non‑identity permutation σ, the convex combination λBord + (1‑λ)σ(Bord) does not correspond to the original unordered set B, breaking convexity.  
  - [section III] The discussion after Lemma III.5 extends this non‑convexity to complex poles and polar parameterizations.  
  - [corpus] Related work on RTRL and online RNN learning (e.g., papers 11557, 127718) does not address permutation‑induced non‑convexity.  
- **Break condition:** Inherent whenever K > 1; persists even if poles are constrained to the unit disk.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Gradient propagation through recurrent dynamics causes severe magnitude distortion (explosion/vanishing) based on pole location, inducing long plateaus.  
- **Mechanism:** The backpropagated error gradient ∂L/∂h[t] is a sum of terms scaled by powers of the recurrent matrix’s eigenvalues (λᵢ^(k‑t)). For |λᵢ| > 1, gradients explode backward in time; for |λᵢ| < 1, they vanish. This creates flat regions where weight updates are ineffective.  
- **Core assumption:** The RNN uses a linear or near‑linear activation so that the recurrent Jacobian is approximately constant (W_rec).  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section II] Equation (5) and the subsequent paragraph derive the gradient recursion and its spectral dependence.  
  - [section II] Equation (6) shows the gradient’s exponential scaling with pole magnitude.  
  - [section IV] Fig. 2 (left) shows gradient norm decaying to a low, non‑zero plateau while loss remains stagnant.  
- **Break condition:** Plateaus occur unless all poles are exactly on the unit circle (|λᵢ| = 1), which is unstable; realistic initialization (e.g., ρ ≈ 0.5) still causes significant distortion over typical BPTT lengths.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Optimizing pole locations ill‑conditions the state representation’s geometry, leading to “narrow valleys” in the loss surface that impede optimization.  
- **Mechanism:** The Hessian of the parameter recovery objective can become arbitrarily large in condition number as two true poles approach each other, even if they are well inside the unit disk. This creates directions in parameter space with extremely small curvature, causing slow progress.  
- **Core assumption:** The true underlying system has distinct poles; analysis considers local conditioning near a ground‑truth solution.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section III] Analysis of f(x,y) shows κ(H_f) → ∞ as c → d; Fig. 1 visualizes the “narrow‑valley” behavior.  
  - [section III] Lemma III.6 states the full Hessian’s condition number is bounded below by that of a restriction (e.g., fixing all but two poles), so ill‑conditioning in a subspace implies overall ill‑conditioning.  
  - [section IV] Fig. 2 (middle) empirically shows κ(W_rec) rising to ~10³ during trainable RNN training, correlating with stagnation.  
- **Break condition:** Ill‑conditioning becomes severe when multiple poles have similar magnitudes or locations; not prevented by standard regularization.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: State‑Space / IIR Filter Interpretation**  
- *Why needed here:* The paper’s analysis hinges on viewing an RNN neuron as a discrete‑time IIR filter with a pole at w_rec. This links neural dynamics to classical digital signal processing, enabling analysis of pole location effects on gradient flow and stability.  
- *Quick check question:* Given h[t] = w_rec h[t‑1] + w_in x[t], what is the system’s transfer function H(z), and where is its pole?

**Concept: BPTT Gradient Recursion**  
- *Why needed here:* Mechanism 2 derives from the BPTT gradient equation (5). Understanding how ∂L/∂h[t] depends on repeated multiplication by W_rec^⊤ is essential to grasp the vanishing/exploding gradient mechanism.  
- *Quick check question:* In ∂L/∂h[t] = Σ_{k=t}^{T‑1} (W_rec^⊤)^{k‑t} (W_out^⊤ ∂L/∂ŷ[k]), what happens if W_rec has an eigenvalue λ = 1.1 and (k‑t) is large?

**Concept: Echo State Network (ESN) Principle**  
- *Why needed here:* The fixed‑pole alternative is an ESN. Its core tenet—fixing recurrent dynamics and training only a linear readout—is the proposed solution. Understanding convex readout training and stable fixed dynamics is key.  
- *Quick check question:* In an ESN, how is W_out typically computed, and why does this avoid the pole learning problem?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
- **Recurrent Reservoir Layer:** Fixed random or domain‑configured weight matrix W_rec (spectral radius ρ < 1). Input matrix W_in is also fixed. State update: h[t] = f(W_rec h[t‑1] + W_in x[t]).  
- **Readout Layer:** Linear output layer W_out. Trained via regularized least squares (ridge regression) on collected state vectors.  
- **(Optional) Domain Configuration Module:** Pre‑computes a target pole distribution (e.g., matching channel zeros) and initializes W_rec accordingly using methods from cited works [8]‑[10].

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize W_rec with ρ(W_rec) < 1 (e.g., 0.5–0.9). If domain knowledge is available, configure poles to match expected system dynamics.  
2. Freeze W_rec and W_in. Do not compute or apply gradients to them.  
3. For each training sample (or mini‑batch in an online setting), run the network forward to collect states {h[t]}.  
4. Solve W_out = argmin ‖Y – W_out H‖_F² + λ‖W_out‖_F², where H is the state matrix.  
5. Deploy the fixed reservoir and trained W_out for inference.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Fixed vs. Learned Dynamics:* Fixed poles guarantee stable, well‑conditioned state evolution and convex readout training but cap representational capacity to the fixed reservoir’s echo state property. Learned poles offer theoretical capacity benefits but risk optimization failure.  
- *Random vs. Configured Reservoir:* Random initialization is simple but suboptimal. Domain configuration (using channel models) can significantly improve performance on specific tasks (e.g., wireless equalization) at the cost of requiring accurate domain models.  
- *Reservoir Size (N_h):* Larger reservoirs improve approximation power and conditioning but increase state‑collection memory and readout training time (O(N_h² T) for pseudo‑inverse).

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Training:** Gradient norms for W_rec stall at a non‑zero value (plateau) while loss decreases minimally; condition number κ(W_rec) grows large (>10²); eigenvalue spectrum of W_rec drifts outside the unit disk (|λ| > 1).  
- **Inference:** Divergent or oscillating state trajectories (h[t] → ∞ or chaotic); poor performance on held‑out data despite low training loss (readout overfitting to noisy reservoir states).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Spectral Radius & Stability:** After initialization, compute the maximum |λ| of W_rec. Confirm ρ < 1. Run a long, zero‑input sequence (x[t]=0) from a random initial state; h[t] should decay to zero, not explode.  
2. **Gradient Flow Check:** Implement a minimal BPTT pass on a fixed‑pole RNN. Verify that ∂L/∂W_rec is zero (or not computed). Check that ∂L/∂W_out yields a smooth, decreasing loss when optimized separately.  
3. **Conditioning & Performance Comparison:** On a small synthetic dataset, train a trainable‑pole RNN and a fixed‑pole ESN (same random seed for W_rec). Track κ(W_rec) and final task loss (e.g., MSE). Expect ESN to have stable, lower κ and better or comparable loss with far fewer epochs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the minimum ratio of training samples to recurrent parameters required for gradient‑based pole learning to avoid optimization plateaus and achieve performance comparable to fixed‑pole RNNs?  
- **Basis:** The paper claims pole learning requires significantly more samples but provides no quantitative threshold; empirical analysis only shows failure with limited data in one task.  
- **Why unresolved:** The exact sample complexity needed to overcome the ill‑conditioned landscape remains undefined, limiting practical guidance for when pole learning might be feasible.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying training set size and recurrent parameter count across multiple random seeds, measuring convergence speed, Hessian condition numbers, and final task loss.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the non‑convex optimization landscape and gradient pathology identified for linear RNNs persist when using common non‑linear activations (e.g., tanh, ReLU) in trainable RNNs?  
- **Basis:** The theoretical analysis and empirical validation assume linear dynamics; real‑world RNNs employ non‑linearities, which may alter gradient flow and curvature.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper explicitly restricts its analysis to linear RNNs, leaving the impact of non‑linearities on pole learning dynamics unexplored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the Hessian condition number analysis to non‑linear RNNs and/or conduct empirical comparisons of trainable non‑linear RNNs versus fixed‑pole baselines under identical data‑constrained settings.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can optimization algorithms specifically designed for ill‑conditioned problems (e.g., second‑order methods, spectral regularization) stabilize pole learning and make trainable RNNs viable for real‑time online tasks?  
- **Basis:** The paper states that “advanced optimizers offer limited improvement” but only evaluates basic gradient descent; the scope of “advanced optimizers” tested is unspecified.  
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether untried optimizers (e.g., K‑FAC, Shampoo) or regularization techniques (e.g., spectral norm constraints) can mitigate the identified ill‑conditioning and gradient plateaus.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and benchmark a suite of state‑of‑the‑art optimizers and regularization schemes on pole learning tasks, tracking convergence, eigenvalue drift, and final performance relative to fixed‑pole architectures.

## Limitations
- The exact wireless channel model (taps, Doppler, SNR) is unspecified, forcing a generic LTI approximation that may not capture the original task’s difficulty.  
- Missing training hyperparameters (learning rate, optimizer, batch size, sequence length) hinder exact reproducibility and may affect convergence dynamics.  
- The analysis assumes linear RNNs; the impact of non‑linear activations on pole learning pathologies remains unexplored.
