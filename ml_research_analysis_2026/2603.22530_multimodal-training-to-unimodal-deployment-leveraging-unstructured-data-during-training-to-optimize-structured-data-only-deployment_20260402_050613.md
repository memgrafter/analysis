# Multimodal Training to Unimodal Deployment: Leveraging Unstructured Data During Training to Optimize Structured Data Only Deployment

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.22530  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22530  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** C-CKD yields a 4.9-point absolute AUROC improvement (0.705 vs 0.656) for late-talking detection using only structured EHR at deployment.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces C-CKD, a framework for training a model that at inference relies solely on structured EHR data while exploiting unstructured clinical notes during training. This addresses the common production constraint where notes are unavailable or delayed. The framework employs a teacher model that processes clinical notes (via BioClinicalBERT embeddings) and a student model that consumes structured features. Both are trained jointly with two objectives: contrastive learning, which aligns the two modalities’ representations, and contrastive knowledge distillation, which transfers the teacher’s task-specific predictions to the student.  

In a cohort of 3,466 children for binary late-talking detection, the multimodal teacher achieves an AUROC of 0.985. The C-CKD student (structured-only) reaches AUROC 0.705, significantly outperforming a baseline structured-only model (0.656). Ablations show that knowledge distillation contributes most to the gain, though the combination of both objectives performs best.

## Method Summary
C-CKD comprises two encoders: a **teacher** that maps clinical notes to representations using BioClinicalBERT, and a **student** that maps structured EHR features (demographics, diagnosis/procedure codes) via an MLP. Each encoder outputs a representation vector fed to a prediction head producing task logits. During training, two losses are applied:  

1. **Contrastive loss** (e.g., InfoNCE) pulls together representations of the same sample across modalities while pushing apart others.  
2. **Knowledge distillation loss** (contrastive variant) encourages the student’s output distribution to match the teacher’s.  

The total loss (weighted sum) updates the student’s parameters; the teacher may be frozen or also updated. After training, only the student is deployed, requiring only structured data.

## Key Results
- Teacher (multimodal) AUROC = **0.985**.  
- C-CKD student (structured-only) AUROC = **0.705** (absolute gain of 0.049 over baseline).  
- Baseline structured-only model AUROC = **0.656**.  
- Ablation: full C-CKD (0.705) > CKD-only (0.696) > contrastive-only (0.687).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The teacher model leverages rich contextual cues from clinical notes to learn a highly predictive function. The contrastive loss aligns the student’s structured-data representation with the teacher’s, establishing a shared latent space. The distillation loss then directly transfers the teacher’s decision boundaries, allowing the student to approximate the teacher’s behavior despite having access only to structured features. This dual guidance enables the student to capture patterns that are not explicitly coded in structured EHR but are implied by the notes, all while meeting the unimodal deployment constraint.

## Foundational Learning
- **Knowledge Distillation**  
  *Why needed:* Transfers the teacher’s task-specific predictive distribution to the student, enabling the smaller student to mimic the teacher’s performance without note inputs at deployment.    
  *Quick check:* Verify high correlation (e.g., Pearson > 0.9) between teacher and student softmax outputs on a validation set.

- **Contrastive Learning**  
  *Why needed:* Aligns the representation spaces of the two modalities so that the student’s embeddings become semantically comparable to the teacher’s, facilitating effective knowledge transfer.    
  *Quick check:* For each student embedding in a batch, the corresponding teacher embedding should rank as the top-1 nearest neighbor (cosine similarity).

- **Unimodal Deployment Constraint**  
  *Why needed:* In production, clinical notes are often missing or delayed; the model must rely solely on structured EHR data that is consistently available.    
  *Quick check:* Confirm the deployed inference pipeline contains only the student encoder and prediction head; no note processing components.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Clinical Notes → Teacher Encoder (BioClinicalBERT) → Teacher Representation → Teacher Head → Teacher Logits  
Structured EHR → Student Encoder (MLP) → Student Representation → Student Head → Student Logits  
L_contrast = ContrastiveLoss(Teacher Representation, Student Representation)  
L_distill = DistillationLoss(Teacher Logits, Student Logits)  
Total Loss = α·L_contrast + β·L_distill → Optimize Student parameters (± Teacher)

**Critical Path**  
1. Preprocess notes (tokenization, BioClinicalBERT embeddings) and structured features (normalization).  
2. Initialize teacher with BioClinicalBERT and task-specific head; optionally fine-tune on notes.  
3. Initialize student MLP and head randomly.  
4. Joint training: forward pass for both, compute losses, backpropagate to update student (and possibly teacher).  
5. Early stopping based on student validation AUROC.  
6. Deployment: freeze student weights, discard teacher, serve student-only model.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Teacher capacity vs. deployment feasibility*: Teacher can be large (slow) because it runs only at training; student must be lightweight for real-time inference.  
- *Contrastive vs. task loss weighting*: Over‑weighting alignment may harm predictive accuracy; hyperparameters α, β require tuning.  
- *Distillation quality*: If the teacher overfits to note‑specific artifacts, the student may inherit these biases despite not seeing notes.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Student fails to surpass baseline: contrastive or distillation losses are mis‑scaled or teacher representations are uninformative.  
- Teacher performance degrades when jointly updated: contrastive loss pulls teacher toward weaker student space.  
- High variance across random seeds: unstable training dynamics, possibly from aggressive distillation or insufficient data.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. Train a baseline structured‑only student (no C‑CKD losses) to replicate the 0.656 AUROC gap.  
2. Run ablations (contrastive‑only, CKD‑only, full C‑CKD) to quantify each loss’s contribution.  
3. Visualize teacher and student embeddings (e.g., t‑SNE) and compute alignment metrics (CKA) to verify effective contrastive alignment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the C-CKD framework generalize to other computable phenotypes beyond late-talking detection?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "our study focused on a single late-talking cohort, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other phenotypes, age groups, or clinical settings."
- **Why unresolved:** The method was only validated on one specific task (late‑talking identification in children). Different phenotypes may have varying levels of documentation in notes and structured codes, affecting the knowledge transfer.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the study across diverse phenotypes (e.g., chronic diseases, mental health conditions) and patient populations (different ages, ethnicities, healthcare settings) to assess consistent performance gains.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under what conditions does the assumption that the note‑based teacher model provides superior or complementary information fail, potentially limiting the benefit of knowledge distillation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note: "this framework assumes that the teacher model trained on unstructured or other modality data provides superior or complementary information relative to the structured‑only model. However, in cases where the teacher model performs comparably to or worse than the student model, the knowledge distillation process may yield limited or no performance improvement."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only presents a scenario where the teacher excels (AUROC 0.985). The boundary where distillation ceases to be beneficial is not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary teacher model performance (e.g., by adding noise to note embeddings, using weaker text models, or selecting phenotypes where notes are less informative) and measure resulting student performance to identify thresholds for effective distillation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does contrastive knowledge distillation (CKD) provide a larger performance gain than contrastive alignment alone, and what specific predictive information does it transfer that alignment does not?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Ablation results show CKD model (0.696) outperforms Contrastive model (0.687). The paper suggests contrastive learning focuses on representation similarity rather than predictive alignment, but does not analyze what task‑specific signals are captured by CKD.
- **Why unresolved:** The mechanisms of what each loss component learns are not probed; the paper does not examine the teacher’s decision boundaries or how the student’s representations change.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct representational analysis (e.g., t‑SNE visualization, probing classifiers) on student embeddings from each ablation variant to identify features or patterns preserved by CKD but not contrastive learning. Compare misclassification cases between models.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What factors explain the substantial performance gap between the multimodal teacher (AUROC 0.985) and the best structured‑only student (AUROC 0.705)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The teacher nearly perfects the task, while the student’s best AUROC remains moderate. The paper attributes this to inherent limits of structured data but does not decompose the gap.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether the gap stems from information irrecoverable from structured data, limitations in the distillation process, or architectural constraints of the student.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform error analysis comparing teacher and student predictions, identify subgroups or cases where teacher succeeds but student fails, and correlate with note content (e.g., presence of contextual phrases absent in codes). Test if improved student architectures or richer structured features reduce the gap.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How robust is the C‑CKD framework to missingness or noise in the structured EHR data used at deployment, given its reliance on that modality?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework assumes structured data is "consistently accessible" at deployment, but real‑world structured data can have missing values, coding errors, or schema variations across institutions.
- **Why unresolved:** All experiments use complete, curated structured features; the model’s sensitivity to structured data quality is unassessed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulate missing structured features (e.g., drop entire categories like diagnosis codes) or inject noise (e.g., mislabel codes) in the test set and measure degradation in student model performance compared to a baseline structured‑only model trained on clean data.

## Limitations
- Single‑center cohort (3,466 children) limits generalizability to other phenotypes and settings.  
- No statistical significance testing; observed AUROC differences could be due to chance.  
- The large teacher‑student performance gap remains unexplained, possibly reflecting inherent limits of structured data or suboptimal distillation.  
- Framework depends on clinical notes during training; note preprocessing and embedding choices are not fully detailed, hindering reproducibility.

## Next Checks
1. **Statistical significance** – Apply bootstrap or DeLong tests on held‑out test predictions to obtain confidence intervals and p‑values for AUROC differences between baseline, contrastive‑only, CKD‑only, and full C‑CKD models.  
2. **External validation** – Replicate the study on an independent pediatric EHR dataset from a different health system to assess generalizability of the C‑CKD framework.  
3. **Sensitivity to teacher quality** – Replace BioClinicalBERT with alternative language models (e.g., ClinicalBERT, BioBERT) and/or deliberately degrade note quality (e.g., mask key sections, add noise) to measure the impact on student performance and identify thresholds for beneficial distillation.
