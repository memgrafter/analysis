# Politics of Questions in News: A Mixed-Methods Study of Interrogative Stances as Markers of Voice and Power
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.21823  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21823  
- **Reference count**: 28  
- **Primary result**: Interrogative sentences are rare (~2.5% of sentences) but serve primarily framing-procedural functions (52%), feature high personalization (58% person mentions), and are mostly resolved within articles monologically (80% internal answers).

## Executive Summary
This study investigates interrogative stances in French digital news, bridging pragmatic questioning theories with agenda-setting and voice allocation at scale. Analyzing 1.2 million articles from 2023–2024, the authors develop a mixed-methods pipeline: large-scale detection and classification of interrogative sentences via a teacher–student model (LLM pseudo‑labeling to fine‑tune CamemBERT), identification of textual answers through embedding similarity, and extraction of actors with NER. Findings reveal that interrogatives are infrequent (∼2.5% of sentences) but highly patterned: they predominantly serve framing‑procedural functions (52%), followed by information‑seeking (20%) and rhetorical (14%). Answerability is extremely high (95.6%), with most questions resolved internally through the journalist’s narration (80%) rather than quoted sources (15%). Interrogative contexts densely feature named individuals (58%), organizations (44%) and locations (32%), while generic publics are rarely mentioned (<3%). Variation across outlet types and thematic verticals suggests that interrogatives are a subtle but informative marker of how news frames issues and allocates voice. The work demonstrates the value of combining computational scale with qualitative triangulation to study discourse functions at scale.

## Method Summary
The authors assemble a corpus of 1.2 million French‑language news articles published between January 2023 and June 2024 from 24 outlets, including mainstream French media, Swiss local news, and thematic verticals. Sentences are segmented with Stanza, and a local context window of three preceding and three following sentences is built. Candidate interrogative sentences are identified via regular expressions (question marks, interrogative starters, question‑raising verbs/nouns). These candidates are pseudo‑labeled by the Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Instruct model for binary interrogative detection and six‑way stance classification (framing‑procedural, information‑seeking, rhetorical, leading, tag, other). Only predictions with confidence ≥0.7 are retained to train a CamemBERT‑large student model. The trained classifier is applied to all sentences, and interrogative sentences are grouped into contiguous question groups. For each group, the mean CamemBERT embedding of its sentences (±1‑sentence context) is used to search subsequent sentences for a semantically similar answer span (cosine similarity ≥0.40). Named entities in question and answer contexts are extracted with GLiNER using coarse labels. Topics are derived via BERTopic. To validate the computational pipeline and interpret macro‑functions, a stratified sample of 700 articles is manually annotated, achieving high inter‑annotator agreement (Jaccard 0.83, κ=0.78). The mixed‑methods approach enables large‑N statistical analysis while grounding the operational definitions in discourse‑analytic rigor.

## Key Results
- Interrogative sentences are rare (~2.5% of sentences) but exhibit a dominant framing‑procedural function (52%), followed by info‑seeking (20%) and rhetorical (14%).
- Nearly all interrogatives (95.6%) find textual resolution within the same article, primarily through the journalist’s own narration (80%) rather than quoted sources (15%).
- Interrogative contexts strongly personalize news: 58% mention a person, 44% an organization, 32% a location, while generic publics are barely mentioned (<3%). Variation exists across outlet types and thematic verticals.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Framing‑Procedural Dominance**  
Interrogatives in French news primarily function as framing‑procedural devices that structure discourse and highlight issues, rather than seeking direct information. Journalists deploy questions to organize article sections, introduce problems, or mark unresolved topics, thereby guiding reader attention without requiring an external response. This aligns with pragmatic theories where questions manage the “question under discussion” (QUD). Evidence: framing‑procedural questions account for 52% of interrogatives and are more prevalent in thematic verticals (sports, local news) than in geopolitics or business, where explanatory reporting is less common. If interrogative density were uniform across topics or if information‑seeking questions dominated, this mechanism would be undermined.

**Mechanism 2: Personalization Bias**  
Interrogative contexts densely foreground named individuals, organizations, and locations, while rarely addressing diffuse publics or social groups. This reflects news sourcing norms that privilege epistemically authoritative or institutionally central actors; the question design often presupposes their relevance, making them syntagmatically salient. Evidence: 58% of interrogative contexts mention a person, 44% an organization, 32% a location, while publics are below 3%. Personalization is highest in Senegalese and Canadian outlets, lower in Swiss/hyper‑local media, suggesting media‑system influences. If publics appeared as frequently as named actors, the personalization claim would weaken.

**Mechanism 3: Monologic Resolution**  
Most interrogative stances are resolved within the same article, but resolution is predominantly monologic (journalist’s narration) rather than dialogic (quoted external voices). Journalistic conventions of explanation and narrative coherence drive the completion of question‑answer pairs. The embedding‑based heuristic matches semantically similar subsequent spans, confirming that questions are typically followed by authorial interpretation. Evidence: 95.6% of interrogatives have a heuristic match; of these, 80.3% are answered internally, 15.4% via quotes. External dialogicity is higher in Swiss/Belgian outlets and hyper‑local news, lower in thematic verticals. If answerability dropped significantly or external quotes dominated, it would indicate a more dialogic, accountability‑oriented questioning culture. (The heuristic’s reliance on similarity is a limitation; true pragmatic answerhood may be lower.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Data ingestion & preprocessing → Teacher model (LLM pseudo‑labeling) → Student model (CamemBERT fine‑tuning) → Full‑corpus prediction → Answer identification (embedding search) → Entity & topic annotation (GLiNER NER, BERTopic) → Index derivation → Qualitative subcorpus (triangulation)

**Critical path**  
Raw articles → sentence splits → LLM pseudo‑labels → CamemBERT fine‑tuning → full‑corpus analysis → answer heuristic → NER/topic → indices → triangulation with qualitative sample.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Recall vs. cost in candidate detection: High‑recall heuristic reduces LLM calls but may miss implicit questions not matching regex patterns.  
- Similarity threshold: Lower values increase recall of answer spans but risk false positives; bimodal score distribution justified a fixed 0.40 cutoff.  
- Stance granularity: Six‑way classification suffers from class imbalance and pragmatic ambiguity; framing‑procedural becomes a residual category, trading fine‑grained insight for reliability.

**Failure signatures**  
- Binary classifier precision 0.76, indicating false positives from declarative sentences with question‑like phrasing.  
- Six‑way macro‑F1 0.51, with confusion among framing‑procedural, information‑seeking, and rhetorical classes.  
- Answer heuristic may match semantically related but pragmatically unresolved spans (e.g., elaboration vs. genuine answer).  
- NER coarse labels may conflate actor types (e.g., “government” as ORG vs. PUBLIC).

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Gold‑standard validation: Compute precision/recall for binary detector and six‑way classifier against the manually annotated 700‑article sample.  
2. Threshold sensitivity sweeps: Vary confidence cutoffs for interrogative predictions and answer‑similarity thresholds; confirm stability of corpus‑level patterns (interrogative index, answerability rate) across reasonable ranges.  
3. Answer span audit: Manually inspect 50 random question‑answer pairs (half predicted answered, half unanswered) to estimate true pragmatic answerhood rate and characterize heuristic failures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Broadcast vs. written news formats**: How do interrogative stance patterns vary across broadcast or spoken news, which include prosodic and interactional cues? (Basis: limitations; unresolved because study only analyzes written text. Resolution requires collecting broadcast corpora and integrating audio‑visual features.)  
2. **Pragmatic validity of answerhood**: To what extent do embedding‑based approximations correspond to truly resolved answers? (Basis: heuristic yields 95.6% answerability; unchecked. Resolution: manual evaluation of predicted answer spans against a pragmatic coding scheme.)  
3. **NER‑based voice roles**: How reliably can coarse NER mentions distinguish different voice and accountability roles (e.g., addressee, subject of scrutiny)? (Basis: NER indicates foregrounding but not functional relation. Resolution: fine‑grained role labeling layer.)  
4. **Historical and regime stability**: Do observed patterns hold across longer periods or different political regimes? (Basis: cross‑sectional design; unresolved. Resolution: longitudinal corpora and cross‑linguistic comparison.)

## Limitations
- **LLM pseudo‑label dependence**: The pipeline relies on Qwen3‑30B; variability in model choice or version could alter stance distributions and classifier performance.  
- **Answer heuristic overestimation**: The cosine‑similarity threshold likely inflates answerability; it captures topical continuity rather than guaranteed pragmatic resolution.  
- **NER salience assumption**: Proximity mentions are taken as journalistic foregrounding, but coarse entity types may conflate roles and miss implicit address (e.g., diffuse publics).
