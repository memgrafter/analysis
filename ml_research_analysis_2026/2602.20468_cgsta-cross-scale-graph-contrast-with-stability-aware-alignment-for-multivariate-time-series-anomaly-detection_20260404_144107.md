# CGSTA: Cross-Scale Graph Contrast with Stability-Aware Alignment for Multivariate Time-Series Anomaly Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20468  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20468  
- **Reference count:** 30  
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art anomaly detection on PSM (87.93% AUROC, 83.03% AUPRC) and WADI (89.67% AUROC, 55.87% AUPRC) with statistically significant gains; competitive performance on SWaT and SMAP.

## Executive Summary
Multivariate time-series anomaly detection is hampered by evolving variable dependencies and noise, which can cause graph-based methods to drift. CGSTA addresses these challenges through three innovations: (1) Dynamic Layered Graph Construction (DLGC) models variable relations at local, regional, and global scales; (2) Contrastive Discrimination across Scales (CDS) learns structure‑aware representations via intra‑scale separation and cross‑scale coherence; (3) Stability‑Aware Alignment (SAA) anchors fast‑changing graphs to a stable reference using exponential moving averages to suppress transient noise. The approach yields robust detection without requiring labeled anomalies during training.

## Method Summary
CGSTA processes sliding windows of multivariate time series. First, DLGC builds three complementary adjacency matrices per window: a local graph via attention, a regional graph via learnable clustering into functional groups, and a global graph via aggregation of regional embeddings. Graph convolutions produce scale‑specific node embeddings. Next, CDS applies self‑supervised contrast: pseudo‑anomalous views are generated by augmentations, and three losses are optimized – intra‑scale discrimination (symmetric InfoNCE within each scale), inter‑scale consistency (aligning embeddings across scales for the same window), and fusion‑level discrimination (on concatenated multi‑scale features). Concurrently, SAA maintains a per‑scale stable graph updated by EMA from the dynamic graphs. Two alignment losses enforce consistency between dynamic and stable graph embeddings and contrast dynamic normal vs. pseudo‑anomalous graphs against the stable reference. The multi‑scale embeddings are fused and passed to a conditional density estimator to produce per‑timestep anomaly scores. The total loss combines detection, CDS, and SAA terms.

## Key Results
- SOTA on PSM and WADI benchmarks, with large margins in AUROC/AUPRC; competitive results on SWaT and SMAP.  
- Ablation studies confirm the necessity of each module; removing DLGC causes the largest drop.  
- Statistically significant improvements over prior methods across multiple metrics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Multi‑Scale Relational Modeling
- **Claim:** Modeling variable dependencies at multiple granularities (local, regional, global) exposes anomalies that disrupt hierarchical relational patterns but may appear normal at a single scale.  
- **Mechanism:** DLGC generates three adjacency matrices per sliding window: (1) local via attention on raw variables, (2) regional by clustering variables into `R` functional groups using learnable centers, (3) global by aggregating regional embeddings and clustering into `G` system‑level groups. Graph convolutions then produce scale‑specific embeddings.  
- **Core assumption:** Anomalies often break consistent multi‑scale relational patterns (e.g., a sensor malfunction first disrupts local ties, then regional group dynamics).  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states “DLGC forms local, regional, and global views of variable relations”; Section 3.3 details the three construction formulas; neighboring work on multi‑scale relational modeling supports the direction.  
- **Break condition:** If variable groupings are fundamentally misaligned with process semantics, or if an anomaly simultaneously preserves all three scales, the multi‑scale signal may not emerge.

### Mechanism 2: Structure‑Aware Cross‑Scale Contrast
- **Claim:** Cross‑scale contrastive learning (CDS) makes representations structure‑aware by teaching which relational patterns to preserve (cross‑scale coherence) and which to treat as abnormal (intra‑scale separation).  
- **Mechanism:** CDS generates positive (normal) and negative (pseudo‑anomalous via augmentations) views for each window. Three losses are used: (1) *Intra‑scale discrimination* applies symmetric InfoNCE within each scale; (2) *Inter‑scale consistency* aligns embeddings of the same window across scales; (3) *Fusion‑level discrimination* applies InfoNCE on concatenated multi‑scale features.  
- **Core assumption:** Normal data exhibits discriminative structure within a scale (normals cluster, anomalies scatter) and coherent structure across scales (the same process manifests consistently). Anomalies disrupt one or both.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes “contrasts graph representations within each view and aligns the same window across views”; Section 3.4 defines losses (5)–(12); related multiview contrast papers provide context.  
- **Break condition:** If pseudo‑anomaly generation fails to simulate realistic structural deviations, or normal data is inherently noisy so cross‑scale coherence is weak, the contrastive signal may mislead.

### Mechanism 3: Stability‑Aware Graph Alignment
- **Claim:** Stability‑Aware Alignment (SAA) suppresses transient noise and prevents graph drift by anchoring fast, window‑level dynamic graphs to a slowly updated long‑horizon “stable” graph via EMA.  
- **Mechanism:** For each scale, a stable adjacency matrix `A_stable` is maintained and updated by `A_stable ← γ·A_stable + (1−γ)·A_dyn` (stop‑gradient on stable). Two losses are applied: (1) *Consistency loss* minimizes cosine distance between GCN embeddings from dynamic and stable graphs; (2) *Contrastive loss* pulls dynamic graphs of normal samples toward their stable counterpart while pushing pseudo‑anomalous dynamic graphs away.  
- **Core assumption:** Long‑term stable dependencies exist in normal operation and can be estimated without labels. Transient noise or short‑lived anomalies cause momentary `A_dyn` fluctuations; aligning toward `A_stable` filters spurious correlations.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes “maintains a per‑scale stable reference learned from normal data and guides the current window’s fast‑changing graphs toward it”; Section 3.5 gives EMA update and alignment losses (13)–(16); EMA for stability is common in representation learning but novel for graph structure.  
- **Break condition:** If training data contains unlabeled anomalies, `A_stable` may absorb anomalous patterns; if γ is too high, the stable graph lags behind legitimate concept drift.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Graph Contrastive Learning**  
  *Why needed:* CDS is the core discriminative component. Understanding InfoNCE, positive/negative pair construction, and symmetric discrimination is necessary to grasp how anomalies are separated at the graph representation level.  
  *Quick check:* If you only have multi‑scale embeddings `H_local`, `H_regional`, `H_global` (no adjacency matrices), how would you design a contrastive loss to expose structural anomalies?

- **Concept: Dynamic Graph Construction for Time Series**  
  *Why needed:* DLGC’s premise is non‑stationary variable dependencies. Knowing how adjacency matrices are recomputed per window (e.g., attention or clustering) explains why a static graph fails.  
  *Quick check:* For a sliding window `X ∈ ℝ^{N×K×L}`, what are the steps to obtain `A_local`? What role do learnable cluster centers `C_regional` play?

- **Concept: Exponential Moving Average (EMA) as a Stability Mechanism**  
  *Why needed:* SAA relies entirely on EMA to create a slow‑moving reference. This technique is fundamental for maintaining a moving target in self‑supervised learning.  
  *Quick check:* If `A_dyn` at step *t* is very noisy and `γ=0.9`, what is the effective memory horizon of `A_stable`? How does this differ from a running average over a fixed window?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Sliding window `X` → DLGC (produces three dynamic graphs `A_local`, `A_regional`, `A_global` and embeddings `H_local`, `H_regional`, `H_global`) → CDS (refines embeddings via contrastive losses) + SAA (aligns each `A_dyn` to its `A_stable` via EMA and alignment losses) → Fusion of refined multi‑scale features → Conditional density estimator → Anomaly scores `S`.  

**Critical path:** Data flows `X → DLGC (graphs + embeddings) → CDS (refined embeddings) and SAA (stability regularization on graphs) in parallel → fusion → density estimator → scores`. The total loss `L_total = L_det + α·L_CDS + β·L_SAA` combines detection, cross‑scale contrast, and stability alignment.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Multi‑scale vs. complexity:** More scales increase capacity but add compute and hyperparameters (number of regions `R`, globals `G`).  
- **EMA momentum `γ`:** Higher `γ` (e.g., 0.95) yields a very stable reference but may lag true drift; lower `γ` (e.g., 0.8) responds faster but is less noise‑robust.  
- **Contrastive weight `α`:** Balances structure‑aware learning vs. detection; too high may overshadow `L_det`.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **High false positives:** Likely if `A_stable` is poorly initialized or `β` too low, causing `A_dyn` to drift with noise unchecked.  
- **High false negatives:** May occur if CDS’s intra‑scale discrimination is weak (poor augmentations) or if cross‑scale consistency is over‑enforced (`α` too high), averaging out single‑scale anomalies.  
- **Training instability:** If `γ` is very close to 1, `A_stable` updates too slowly, potentially causing gradient issues in `L_contrast`.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Run the ablation study (Table 2/3) to verify each module’s contribution; removing DLGC should cause the largest drop.  
2. Perform a hyperparameter sensitivity sweep for `γ` (EMA momentum) on PSM; expect a peak AUROC around 0.85–0.90 per Fig 2.  
3. Conduct a case‑study visualization (Fig 4): feed an annotated attack window (e.g., SWaT index 7894) and visualize `A_dyn_local`, `A_stable_local`, and their absolute difference; expect bright bands in the difference plot on edges incident to attacked sensors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Dynamic hierarchical assignments:** How can the fixed numbers of regional and global clusters be made fully dynamic to adapt to evolving variable semantics without increasing computational overhead?  
2. **Streaming efficiency:** How can the window‑based graph recomputation be optimized for low‑latency real‑time anomaly detection?  
3. **Robustness to contamination and drift:** How can CGSTA be hardened against unlabeled anomalies in training and gradual concept drift that degrade the stable reference?

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters (`R`, `G`, α, β, γ), architecture details (hidden dimensions, density estimator), and post‑processing (score smoothing, thresholding) are unreported, hindering reproducibility.  
- Pseudo‑anomaly generation strategies (point injection, context replacement, cluster drift) are described only conceptually, lacking precise implementations.  
- Sensitivity to contaminated training data and gradual distribution shifts remains unaddressed; `A_stable` may become biased.
