# Avoid What You Know: Divergent Trajectory Balance for GFlowNets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17827
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17827
- **Reference count:** 38
- **Primary result:** ACE significantly outperforms prior exploration methods across benchmarks, achieving higher top‑K reward, lower TV distance, and larger antimicrobial peptide discovery rate.

## Executive Summary
Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) often suffer from mode collapse and slow convergence due to insufficient exploration of diverse high-reward regions. The authors propose Adaptive Complementary Exploration (ACE), a framework that trains a separate exploration GFlowNet to target under-explored, high-reward states while avoiding over-sampled regions. This is achieved via a novel Divergent Trajectory Balance (DTB) loss that explicitly suppresses probability mass on already over-allocated states, and an adaptive mixing weight that dynamically balances samples from the canonical and exploration policies. Across several benchmarks—including peptide discovery, bit sequence design, and combinatorial optimization—ACE demonstrates substantial improvements over existing exploration methods, both in terms of mode coverage and convergence speed.

## Method Summary
ACE introduces a two-network system: a canonical GFlowNet that aims to match the target distribution proportional to reward, and an exploration GFlowNet that learns to sample high-reward states that are under-allocated by the canonical net. The canonical net’s current estimate of state probabilities defines over-allocated (OA) and under-allocated (UA) sets relative to a threshold α. The exploration net is trained with the Divergent Trajectory Balance (DTB) loss, which enforces the standard trajectory balance condition only on trajectories terminating in UA, while forcing zero probability on those ending in OA. The canonical net is trained on a mixture of its own samples and the exploration net’s samples, weighted by an adaptive mixing coefficient w = Z/(Z+Z∇), where Z and Z∇ are the learned partition functions. This cooperative dynamic allows the canonical net to gradually expand its support into novel high-reward regions identified by the exploration net, thereby mitigating mode collapse and accelerating convergence.

## Key Results
- Faster identification of diverse high-reward states: higher top‑K average reward across benchmarks.
- Accelerated convergence to the target distribution: lower total variation distance (TV) compared to baselines.
- Substantially larger cumulative discovery rate of effective antimicrobial peptides in the peptide design task.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Divergent Trajectory Balance (DTB) Loss
The DTB loss explicitly forces the exploration GFlowNet to assign probability mass only to under-allocated high-reward states (UA(α)), while suppressing trajectories that terminate in over-sampled regions (OA(α)). Mathematically, for an exploration trajectory τ ending at state x, the loss is zero if x ∈ OA(α) and otherwise enforces the standard trajectory balance condition with a tempered reward R(x)^β. This mechanism relies on the canonical GFlowNet’s current estimate ˆR_g(x) to classify states. Proposition 3.4 shows that under zero loss, the exploration policy satisfies p∇_F(τ) ∝ R(x)^β I[x∈UA(α)]. The break condition occurs if α is too low (UA becomes too large, weakening repulsion) or too high (UA may become empty, causing exploration collapse).

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive Mixing Weight
The canonical GFlowNet is trained on a mixture distribution p^E_F = w p_F + (1-w) p_F^{ε,∇}, where w = Z/(Z+Z∇) is a dynamic weight derived from the estimated partition functions. This weight acts as a proxy for the reward mass captured by each net, automatically down-weighting the exploration net as the canonical net covers more of the target distribution. The mixing ensures that the canonical net integrates discoveries from the exploration net without being overwhelmed by its samples. Proposition 3.9 describes the equilibrium behavior; however, instability in Z or Z∇ can cause erratic mixing and harm convergence.

### Mechanism 3: Cooperative Two-Player Game
ACE can be interpreted as a cooperative game between the two GFlowNets. The exploration net, driven by DTB, actively seeks under-allocated high-reward states, while the canonical net incorporates these samples via the mixed training objective. This co-adaptation pushes the canonical net’s support to expand into novel modes. Proposition 3.8 shows that if both nets initially collapse into the same over-allocated set, the DTB gradient pushes the exploration net toward the complementary set, initiating diversification. The cooperative dynamic fails if the exploration net becomes too specialized or the canonical net ignores its samples.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Canonical GFlowNet (g, parameters θ) → learns forward/backward policies (p_F, p_B) and partition Z → provides estimates ˆR_g(x) → used by Region Classifier to define OA(α) and UA(α). Exploration GFlowNet (g∇, parameters ϕ) → learns policies (p∇_F, p∇_B) and partition Z∇ → trained with DTB loss L∇ that depends on OA/UA classification. Adaptive mixing weight w = Z/(Z+Z∇) (detached) combines samples from both nets to form mixture policy p^E_F for canonical training.

### Critical Path
1. Sample batch B from canonical forward policy p_F, and batch B∇ from exploration forward policy p∇_F (with ϵ-greedy).
2. Update exploration parameters ϕ by minimizing DTB loss L∇ on B∇ (pushes exploration net into UA).
3. Update canonical parameters θ by minimizing weighted TB loss on mixture of B and B∇, using mixing weight w (integrates novel modes).
4. Recompute OA(α,g) using latest ˆR_g estimates from canonical net.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Threshold α**: Smaller α → larger UA → broader exploration but less focused; larger α → narrower exploration, risk of empty UA. Default ~0.2–0.3.
- **Reward tempering β**: Higher β sharpens exploration target but can destabilize training; lower β eases navigation but may be less directed. Default ~0.25.
- **Exploration ϵ**: Too low → exploration net may not reach UA; too high → wastes samples on uniform regions. Default 0.05.

### Failure Signatures
- **Z∇ collapses to zero** → exploration net gives up (common if α ≤ 1 and canonical net has broad support). Fix: increase α.
- **TV distance plateaus early** → exploration net not finding new modes. Fix: decrease α or β; increase exploration batch size.
- **High variance in Z estimates** → unstable w, oscillations. Fix: use more particles for Z estimation; smoother EMA on Z.
- **Premature exploration collapse**: Monitor fraction of exploration trajectories classified as UA; should remain significant.
- **Canonical network ignoring exploration**: Track w; if it stays near 1, consider reducing learning rate for Z∇ or increasing weight of exploration loss.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Toy multimodal (Rings/8 Gaussians)**: Validate that L∇ forces exploration net to ignore oversampled modes and fill missing ones. Check marginal histograms.
2. **Ablation on α**: Sweep α on a simple task; expect U-shaped performance—too low/too high harms TV convergence.
3. **Mixing weight dynamics**: Log w over time; should decrease as canonical net captures more reward mass. Correlate with discovery of new high-reward states.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mechanisms of prior methods**: What are the underlying mechanisms by which Adaptive Teachers and Sibling Augmented GFlowNets fail to efficiently explore diverse high-reward regions, leading to mode collapse? The paper notes that these methods use intrinsic rewards or loss functions that are incomparable to the original reward, making their exploration-exploitation balance unclear.
2. **Extension to non-stationary rewards**: Can the DTB framework be extended to incorporate non-stationary rewards, such as curiosity-driven intrinsic rewards, to improve exploration in dynamic or partially known environments? The paper suggests this as a promising direction but notes that time-varying rewards may break equilibrium guarantees.
3. **Optimal weighting in ACE**: What is the optimal way to weight samples from the canonical and exploration GFlowNets in the ACE mixing scheme (Equation 11) to maximize sample efficiency and stability? The heuristic w = Z/(Z+Z∇) may not adapt well to training stage, reward sparsity, or network collapse.
4. **Adaptive threshold α**: How does the allocation threshold α affect the diversity-discovery trade-off across different reward distributions, and can α be set adaptively rather than manually per environment? The paper manually sets α per environment without a principled automation method.
5. **Extension to continuous spaces**: Can ACE be extended to continuous state spaces or stochastic GFlowNets while preserving theoretical guarantees and empirical benefits? The paper focuses on discrete, acyclic graphs; continuous extensions may require new mathematical tools.

## Limitations
- **α threshold sensitivity**: The choice of α (typically 0.2–0.3) is not derived from theory; its optimal value may vary across tasks and could lead to empty UA sets if set too high.
- **OA(α) estimation noise**: The method uses a single Monte‑Carlo backward sample from p_B(·|x) to estimate ˆR_g(x), which may be noisy and misclassify states, misdirecting exploration.
- **Partition function stability**: The learnable scalars Z and Z∇ can become unstable, especially early in training, causing erratic mixing weights w and possibly destabilizing the cooperative dynamics.

## Next Checks
1. **α‑sweep ablation**: Systematically vary α across a broad set of tasks (different numbers of modes, reward landscapes) and measure impact on convergence speed and mode coverage. Identify robust ranges and conditions where UA becomes too small/too large.
2. **OA estimation robustness**: Replace the single backward sample with an average over k > 1 samples or a running average of ˆR_g(x). Compare TV convergence and top‑K discovery to quantify sensitivity to estimation variance.
3. **Dynamic mixing diagnostics**: Log w, Z, Z∇, and the fraction of exploration trajectories classified as UA throughout training. Correlate these dynamics with the rate of new high‑reward state discovery; additionally, test alternative mixing proxies (e.g., empirical reward mass captured) to see if performance improves or degrades.
