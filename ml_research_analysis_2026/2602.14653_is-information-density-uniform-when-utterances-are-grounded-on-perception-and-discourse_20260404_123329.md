# Is Information Density Uniform when Utterances are Grounded on Perception and Discourse?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14653
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14653
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Visual and discourse grounding both reduce surprisal variance, with the strongest effects at discourse unit onsets, supporting a context-sensitive Uniform Information Density hypothesis.

## Executive Summary
This paper tests the Uniform Information Density (UID) hypothesis in visually grounded, multilingual settings using vision-and-language models. They estimate word-level surprisal for image captions (30 languages) and visual storytelling (13 languages) under four contextual conditions: text-only (U), image (P), discourse (D), and combined (P+D). Visual grounding consistently smoothes information density by lowering global and local surprisal variance across languages. Discourse context alone also substantially reduces variance, with additive gains from vision. The largest reductions occur at sentence and paragraph onsets, indicating that context primarily smooths density at discourse boundaries. These findings extend UID to multimodal communication and suggest that language production optimizes for uniformity by leveraging both perceptual and discourse information.

The study is computational, relying on surrogate models (PaliGemma, Gemma 3) to approximate human processing. While results are robust, limitations include potential misalignment between model and human surprisal, a language sample biased toward well-resourced families, and challenges in word-token alignment. Nonetheless, the work provides strong evidence for a context‑sensitive UID principle and opens avenues for exploring dynamic perceptual streams, information decomposition, typological diversity, and psycholinguistic validation.

## Method Summary
The authors use two vision‑language datasets. GROUND‑XM3600 comprises image‑caption pairs in 30 languages; precomputed surprisals under U and P come from PaliGemma. BLOOMVIST contains visual stories (interleaved images and paragraphs) in many languages; filtered to 13 languages (e.g., English, German, Mandarin) and truncated at 20 paragraphs. For BLOOMVIST, surprisals are computed under U (current paragraph only), P (with image), D (all preceding paragraphs), and P+D (preceding interleaved images and paragraphs plus current) using Gemma 3 4B. Word‑level surprisal is reconstructed from subword tokens by summing log probabilities and applying trailing whitespace corrections; word segmentation uses Stanza (or BNLP for Bengali).

Dependent variables are global UID (UIDv, variance of surprisal within an utterance) and local UID (UIDlv, variance between successive words). They also compute coefficient of variation. Analyses include paired Wilcoxon tests (U vs. P across languages), a Page test for ordered effects U > P > D > P+D, and linear mixed‑effects regression with random intercepts and slopes by story to examine surprisal drift with position. Additional analyses decompose surprisal by part‑of‑speech and compute surprisal reduction densities to visualize where context yields the greatest smoothing.

## Key Results
- **Visual grounding reduces surprisal variance:** Across 30 languages, image context significantly lowers both global and local UID in 28 languages (p < 0.01), indicating increased information uniformity.
- **Discourse context lowers variance, with additive effect from vision:** In BLOOMVIST, discourse (D) substantially reduces UID compared to text‑only (U); adding vision (P+D) gives further, albeit smaller, reductions. The ordering U > P > D > P+D is significant via the Page test in most languages.
- **Largest effects at discourse boundaries:** The steepest declines in surprisal occur at sentence and paragraph onsets, especially for discourse context, suggesting that contextual smoothing is concentrated at the beginnings of new discourse units.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Visual context smoothes information density by reducing surprisal variance, primarily for content words (nouns, adjectives) that refer to visible entities.
- **Mechanism:** The image provides redundant perceptual cues that constrain the next‑word distribution, lowering the surprisal of high‑information‑content words and narrowing the gap between content and function words.
- **Core assumption:** Vision‑and‑language models produce surprisal estimates that align with human processing under visual grounding (supported by Pushpita & Levy, 2024).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "grounding on perception consistently smooths the distribution of information, increasing both global and local uniformity"
  - [section 5.1] Paired Wilcoxon tests show significant UID reduction in 28 of 30 languages.
  - [section 6.2] POS analysis: adjectives, proper nouns, and numerals drive failure cases where variance increases under grounding.
- **Break condition:** Visual ambiguity, irrelevance, or poor model alignment (e.g., script‑specific tokenization issues) may prevent smoothing.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Discourse context reduces surprisal variance by building incremental common ground, with strongest effects at sentence/paragraph onsets.
- **Mechanism:** Prior text narrows the hypothesis space for upcoming words, especially at discourse boundaries where new referents/themes appear. This attenuates typical surprisal spikes, flattening the contour.
- **Core assumption:** Humans incrementally construct discourse representations that facilitate prediction; autoregressive models capture this.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "discourse context alone substantially lowers variance... largest reductions occur at onsets."
  - [section 5.2] Page test confirms ordered effect: U > P > D > P+D across languages.
  - [section G] Surprisal reduction densities peak sharply at unit onsets for ∆D.
- **Break condition:** Incoherent discourse or very short narratives may provide insufficient constraints.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Multimodal context (vision + discourse) yields additive smoothing because visual and textual constraints are complementary.
- **Mechanism:** Vision constrains referential content (concrete entities), while discourse constrains thematic progression. Their combination covers orthogonal aspects of meaning, producing greater uniformity.
- **Core assumption:** The two sources are sufficiently independent that their joint contribution exceeds individual effects.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "additional effects" from combining; "strongest reductions at onsets."
  - [section 5.2] P+D condition consistently yields lowest UID; distribution tracks D but with lower median.
  - [section 5.3] P+D slopes less steep than D, indicating more stability; ∆P+D shows longer‑tailed reduction.
- **Break condition:** High correlation between image content and discourse (redundant cues) minimizes additive gains.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  - Datasets: GROUND‑XM3600 (image‑caption pairs, 30 languages), BLOOMVIST (visual storytelling, 13 languages).
  - Models: PaliGemma (for image‑caption), Gemma 3 (for storytelling).
  - Metrics: UIDv (global variance), UIDlv (local variance), Coefficient of Variation.
  - Analyses: Paired Wilcoxon tests, Page test, linear mixed‑effects (lme4) models, POS decomposition.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Preprocess BLOOMVIST: filter stories (≥20 paragraphs), truncate at 20 paragraphs, drop paragraphs <3 words.
  2. Load model and tokenizer; ensure image preprocessing matches model expectations (e.g., resolution).
  3. For each utterance, generate surprisal under U, P, D, P+D conditions. Handle context windowing for D and P+D.
  4. Reconstruct word‑level surprisal from subword tokens (apply trailing whitespace corrections).
  5. Compute UID metrics per utterance; aggregate by language/condition.
  6. Run statistical tests (Wilcoxon per language; Page test across languages).
  7. Fit mixed‑effects models per language/unit‑level (sentence/paragraph) with random slopes by story.
  8. Compute surprisal reduction densities (∆P, ∆D, ∆P+D) and plot front‑loaded profiles.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Model choice: PaliGemma fine‑tuned on COCO (English‑centric) vs. Gemma 3 (long context, multilingual but undisclosed data). Affects language coverage and generalizability.
  - Language selection: Driven by dataset‑model intersection; BLOOMVIST includes many languages but Gemma 3 may underrepresent some. They used perplexity heuristics to filter.
  - Tokenization: Word segmentation via Stanza (most) or BNLP (Bengali) may introduce inconsistencies; subword‑to‑word aggregation requires care.
  - Narrative truncation: Limiting to 20 paragraphs may exclude longer stories but ensures context reliability.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Unexpected UID increase under grounding (e.g., Telugu, Arabic) – check model’s vision‑language alignment, script segmentation, or POS‑specific anomalies.
  - Non‑front‑loaded surprisal reduction densities – indicates poor boundary modeling or context handling.
  - Mixed‑effects model convergence issues – may need to simplify random effects structure.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce Table 4 (GROUND‑XM3600 UIDv/UIDlv U vs P) for English, German; confirm significant decreases.
  2. Reproduce Figure 3 (BLOOMVIST paragraph UIDv across conditions) for 2–3 languages; verify ordering U > P > D > P+D.
  3. Fit mixed‑effects model for English at sentence level; extract slopes for D and P+D; confirm negative and significant (as in Table 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does anchoring utterances in dynamic perceptual streams (like video) versus static images affect the distribution and temporal dynamics of information density in multimodal discourse?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors conclude by proposing a natural extension: "move beyond static images toward dynamic perceptual streams, such as video, where the temporal structure of the input can more tightly constrain linguistic predictions."
- **Why unresolved:** The current study only uses static images. Video introduces event‑level temporal structure (causality, progression) that could offer richer, time‑aligned constraints on language, potentially altering UID effects in ways not captured by static grounding.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow‑up study using video‑language models on video captioning or storytelling datasets, comparing UID metrics (variance, drift, boundary effects) against the static image baselines reported here.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent are the observed reductions in surprisal variance due to redundant versus synergistic information between visual and discourse contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors suggest using "formal decomposition into redundant and synergistic contributions (e.g., via Partial Information Decomposition)" as a future direction. Their finding that P+D yields additional smoothing over D alone hints at non‑trivial interaction, but the nature of this interaction (redundant cues vs. new synergistic constraints) is not quantified.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper reports additive but not decomposable effects. Without an information‑theoretic decomposition, we cannot determine whether the joint context provides mostly overlapping constraints (redundancy) or genuinely novel, combined predictive power (synergy) that shapes UID differently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying Partial Information Decomposition (PID) to estimate unique, redundant, and synergistic information contributions from the image and discourse contexts to word‑level surprisal, then correlating these components with UID metric changes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the front‑loaded surprisal reductions at discourse boundaries (sentences/paragraphs) driven primarily by the visual grounding of *referential* content (nouns, entities) or also by the grounding of *relational* and *discourse‑structuring* content (verbs, discourse markers)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors find "the strongest surprisal reductions occurring at the onset of discourse units" and that this is "especially pronounced for discourse context." However, the POS analysis (Section E) focuses on failure cases and shows that PROPN, NUM, and ADJ often drive *non‑reduction*. The positive reduction profile’s POS composition is not systematically broken down.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates *where* (unit onsets) reduction is strongest but not precisely *what linguistic content* is most facilitated by context at those boundaries. Understanding if verbs, connectives, or other functional elements also show front‑loaded grounding could refine models of discourse planning.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fine‑grained analysis of surprisal reduction densities (Figures 8‑20) stratified by POS tags, specifically comparing the front‑loaded profiles of content words (NOUN, PROPN) versus relational/structural words (VERB, ADV, DET, SCONJ) at sentence and paragraph onsets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the UID‑promoting effect of visual grounding generalize to languages with radically different morphosyntactic typologies (e.g., highly polysynthetic, templatic, or non‑configurational languages) that were underrepresented in this sample?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge a key limitation: "the intersection between available datasets and model capabilities effectively constrains our choice of languages... most of these fall outside the training distribution of Gemma 3, resulting in a final language set heavily biased towards Indo‑European languages." The sample includes only two families (Austronesian, Dravidian) outside Eurasia and lacks entire families like Niger‑Congo or Australian.
- **Why unresolved:** The claimed cross‑linguistic robustness of visual grounding for UID may be an artifact of sampling bias. Languages with different word formation, argument marking, or discourse conventions might exhibit different patterns of information distribution and different sensitivities to visual context.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the study on a wider typological sample using models with stronger multilingual coverage (e.g., including families like Niger‑Congo, Papuan, or Australian), or gathering new human‑produced (not machine‑translated) caption/story data for such languages and estimating surprisal with tailored models.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do the computational UID effects observed here have psycholinguistic correlates in human language production or comprehension within visually grounded, multilingual discourse?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] A core limitation is stated: "our analysis is computational, without psycholinguistic validation... no experimental data yet links these predictions to actual human processing patterns in multimodal discourse." They cite Pushpita & Levy (2024) as partial validation for single‑word reading times but not for discourse‑level UID contours.
- **Why unresolved:** Model‑based surprisal is a proxy. The paper's central finding—that grounding smooths information density in a context‑sensitive way—needs to be validated as a cognitive strategy or processing signature in humans across languages. It is unclear if speakers/writers consciously or subconsciously optimize for UID in multimodal settings, or if comprehenders show reduced effort specifically where the model predicts the strongest UID‑induced smoothing.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Behavioral experiments (e.g., production studies where speakers describe images/narratives under time pressure, measuring fluency or syntactic choices; comprehension studies with eye‑tracking or EEG in multimodal settings, testing if processing ease aligns with model‑predicted UID reductions, particularly at discourse boundaries).

## Limitations
- **Model multimodal capability:** The text‑only Gemma 3 checkpoint may not support image inputs, jeopardizing P and P+D surprisal estimation; using an incorrect model would invalidate visual context findings.
- **Language selection bias:** BLOOMVIST filtering via perplexity contrasts uses unreported thresholds and yields a set heavily skewed toward Indo‑European languages, limiting claims about cross‑linguistic universality.
- **Word‑token alignment:** Subword‑to‑word aggregation relies on language‑specific tokenizers and external word segmentation tools; mismatches can distort word‑level surprisal, especially for morphologically rich languages.

## Confidence
- **Visual smoothing (Mechanism 1):** Medium – Strong cross‑linguistic evidence but with notable exceptions and reliance on model‑human alignment.
- **Discourse front‑loading (Mechanism 2):** High – Robust ordering and onset effects across languages; less dependent on vision.
- **Multimodal additivity (Mechanism 3):** Medium – P+D yields lowest UID but incremental gain over D is small; independence assumption untested.
