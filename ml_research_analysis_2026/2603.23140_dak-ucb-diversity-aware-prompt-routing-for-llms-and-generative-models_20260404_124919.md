# DAK-UCB: Diversity-Aware Prompt Routing for LLMs and Generative Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23140  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23140  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** DAK-UCB improves diversity (higher JRKE, JKD) while preserving fidelity (CLIP-Score) in online prompt-aware selection of generative models; Mixture-DAK-UCB further boosts diversity via prompt-dependent model mixtures.

## Executive Summary
DAK-UCB addresses the problem of prompt-aware selection of generative models, where prior methods maximize only fidelity (e.g., CLIP-Score) leading to diversity collapse and biased outputs. The proposed framework is a contextual bandit that incorporates a diversity term alongside fidelity. It uses prompt-aware diversity scores—Joint Kernel Distance (JKD) and Joint Rényi Kernel Entropy (JRKE)—that decompose into a two-sample expectation, enabling unbiased online estimation from a single generated sample. The algorithm extends Kernelized-UCB with separate kernel ridge regression predictors for fidelity and diversity, combining them via an Upper Confidence Bound that balances exploration and exploitation. Mixture-DAK-UCB further learns prompt-dependent mixtures of models to enhance diversity. Evaluations on text-to-image (MS-COCO with three models) and LLM tasks show that DAK-UCB improves diversity metrics while maintaining fidelity, with theoretical regret guarantees.

## Method Summary
DAK-UCB formalizes the task as a contextual multi-armed bandit: each generative model is an arm, prompts are contexts, and the reward combines fidelity \(s_g(t)\) and diversity \(D_g(t)\). The core innovation lies in the diversity metrics (I-JRKE, JKD) that admit a two-sample expectation decomposition (Proposition 1), allowing a single generated sample \((t, x)\) to produce an unbiased stochastic label \(y^{(D)} = \psi_g(t, x; \mathcal{H})\) that depends on the current history \(\mathcal{H}\). For each arm \(g\), two independent kernel ridge regression (KRR) models are maintained: one for \(s_g\) and one for \(D_g\). At each round, given a prompt \(t\), the algorithm computes per-arm UCB scores: \(\hat{J}_g^{\text{UCB}}(t) = (\hat{s}_g(t) + \beta^{(s)}\hat{\sigma}_g^{(s)}(t)) + \lambda (\hat{D}_g(t) - \beta^{(D)}\hat{\sigma}_g^{(D)}(t))\), where \(\hat{\sigma}\) are uncertainty estimates. The arm with the highest UCB is selected, its output is generated, and the KRR models are updated with the new labels. Mixture-DAK-UCB replaces deterministic selection with a quadratic program that finds mixture weights \(\alpha(t)\) maximizing \(\alpha^\top \hat{s}^{\text{UCB}}(t) - \lambda \alpha^\top \widehat{M}^{\text{UCB}}(t) \alpha\), where \(\widehat{M}^{\text{UCB}}(t)\) estimates the cross-model diversity matrix (Proposition 2). This yields smoother, more diverse outputs by exploiting complementary collapse modes of different models.

## Key Results
- DAK-UCB significantly improves diversity metrics (Joint-RKE, JKD) compared to fidelity-only baselines (PAK-UCB, Random) while preserving CLIP-Score on MS-COCO with three image generators.
- Mixture-DAK-UCB further enhances diversity, demonstrating the benefit of prompt-dependent model mixtures.
- Theoretical regret bound is \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{G T \Gamma_s^T} + \lambda \sqrt{G T \Gamma_D^T})\), scaling with the cumulative information gains for fidelity and diversity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Two-Sample Expectation Decomposition for Online Diversity
The proposed diversity metrics (I-JRKE and JKD) are defined as expectations over two independent prompt–output pairs. Proposition 1 shows that under a product kernel \(k_{\text{joint}}([t,x],[t',x']) = k_T(t,t')k_X(x,x')\) and a known reference prompt distribution \(P_T\), these expectations can be rewritten as the expectation of a per‑sample function \(\psi(t, x)\) that depends on a single \((t, x)\) and the history \(\mathcal{H}\). This yields an unbiased stochastic label \(y^{(D)} = \psi_g(t, x; \mathcal{H})\) from a single interaction, making online learning feasible. The decomposition hinges on the product‑kernel structure and the two‑sample form of the underlying diversity metrics (MMD‑type or Rényi entropy).

### Mechanism 2: Kernel Ridge Regression for Faithful Reward Modeling with Confidence Bounds
DAK‑UCB maintains separate KRR predictors for each arm’s fidelity \(s_g\) and diversity \(D_g\). KRR operates in an RKHS induced by a positive‑definite kernel on prompts (e.g., RBF on CLIP embeddings). Given historical data, KRR provides a posterior mean \(\hat{\mu}(t)\) and a deviation \(\hat{\sigma}(t)\) that captures uncertainty. The per‑arm UCB combines the mean with a confidence radius \(\beta \hat{\sigma}\), balancing exploration and exploitation. The two separate predictors allow independent control of exploration for fidelity and diversity via \(\beta^{(s)}\) and \(\beta^{(D)}\). This design assumes the true reward functions lie in the RKHS (bounded norm) and noise is sub‑Gaussian, enabling the confidence bounds to be valid and leading to the regret guarantee.

### Mechanism 3: Mixture‑DAK‑UCB for Prompt‑Dependent Model Mixtures
Instead of selecting a single model, Mixture‑DAK‑UCB optimizes a mixture distribution \(\alpha(t) \in \Delta^G\) for each prompt. Using the product kernel, the I‑JRKE diversity metric for a mixture takes a quadratic form \(\alpha(t)^\top M(t) \alpha(t)\), where \(M(t)\) is a positive‑semidefinite cross‑model matrix (Proposition 2). The algorithm solves a concave quadratic maximization problem: maximize \(\alpha^\top \hat{s}^{\text{UCB}}(t) - \lambda \alpha^\top \widehat{M}^{\text{UCB}}(t) \alpha\) subject to \(\alpha\) being \(\epsilon\)-Lipschitz in \(t\) (ensuring smoothness and an \(\epsilon\)-approximation guarantee). This yields prompt‑adaptive mixtures that can combine the strengths of different generative models, further boosting diversity by mitigating model‑specific collapse modes.

**Concept: Kernel Methods and RKHS**  
*Why needed:* DAK‑UCB models reward functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) to generalize across prompts. The kernel encodes similarity; KRR provides posterior means and deviations.  
*Quick check:* What is the reproducing property of an RKHS? How does the kernel trick avoid explicit feature mapping?

**Concept: Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)**  
*Why needed:* UCB is the exploration strategy that adds a bonus proportional to uncertainty. DAK‑UCB adapts UCB to two simultaneous objectives (fidelity and diversity) with separate confidence radii.  
*Quick check:* In linear UCB, why is the confidence radius often proportional to \(\|\phi(t)\|_{(X^\top X + \alpha I)^{-1}}\)? What does this term capture?

**Concept: Diversity Metrics for Generative Models**

*Quick check:* How does Joint Rényi Kernel Entropy (JRKE) differ from unconditional RKE? Why is the inverse (I‑JRKE) used as a penalty in the objective?

**Concept: Two‑Sample Expectation Structure**  
*Why needed:* This structural property is the key innovation: it allows estimating a population‑level diversity metric from a single generated sample per prompt, making online learning computationally feasible.  
*Quick check:* What does it mean for a statistical functional to have a two‑sample expectation form? How does this enable an unbiased label from one sample?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Prompt Encoder → Generative Models Pool → (Fidelity Scorer, Diversity Scorer) → KRR Modules (one per arm for fidelity & diversity) → UCB Combiner → Selection Policy → History Archive.  
(Mixture Optimizer is added for Mixture‑DAK‑UCB, interfacing with cross‑model diversity KRR and the Selection Policy.)

### Critical Path
1. Sample prompt \(t_i \sim P_T\).  
2. For each model \(g\), retrieve KRR predictors for \(s_g\) and \(D_g\) and compute \(\hat{s}_g(t_i)\), \(\hat{\sigma}_g^{(s)}(t_i)\), \(\hat{D}_g(t_i)\), \(\hat{\sigma}_g^{(D)}(t_i)\).  
3. Form \(\hat{J}_g^{\text{UCB}}(t_i)\) and either choose deterministic \(\arg\max_g\) (DAK‑UCB) or solve quadratic program for mixture weights \(\alpha^*(t_i)\) (Mixture‑DAK‑UCB).  
4. Generate output \(x_i = G_{g_i}(t_i)\).  
5. Compute fidelity label \(y_i^{(s)} = \text{fidelity}(t_i, x_i)\) (e.g., CLIP‑Score).  
6. Compute diversity label \(y_i^{(D)} = \psi_{g_i}(t_i, x_i; \mathcal{H}_i)\) using the two‑sample formula and current history.  
7. Update KRR predictors for arm \(g_i\) with \((t_i, y_i^{(s)})\) and \((t_i, y_i^{(D)})\).  
8. Append \((t_i, x_i, g_i)\) to history \(\mathcal{H}\).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **λ (diversity coefficient):** Larger λ increases diversity but may reduce fidelity; requires tuning.
- **Kernel choice:** Prompt and output kernels (e.g., RBF on CLIP embeddings) affect similarity measurements; bandwidth selection matters. Normalization is critical (Assumption 1).
- **Confidence radii β:** Larger β encourages exploration but slows convergence; smaller β risks premature convergence.
- **Mixture vs deterministic:** Mixture-DAK‑UCB can achieve higher diversity but adds quadratic optimization cost and needs more data to estimate cross‑model diversity reliably; introduces stochasticity.
- **Two‑sample requirement:** Only metrics with the decomposition (I‑JRKE, JKD) support single‑sample online estimation; other metrics would need multiple samples per prompt, breaking efficiency.

### Failure Signatures
- No diversity improvement (JRKE/JKD plateau): Check correctness of \(\psi_g\) implementation; possibly \(\beta^{(D)}\) mis‑set.
- Fidelity collapse (CLIP‑Score drops): Reduce λ or verify fidelity KRR accuracy.
- High regret: May indicate violated assumptions (functions not in RKHS, heavy‑tailed noise, non‑stationarity).
- Mixture instability (oscillating or extremely sparse weights): Ensure \(\widehat{M}^{\text{UCB}}(t)\) is PSD (eigenvalue check), verify Lipschitz constant \(\epsilon\), and ensure sufficient exploration of all arms.
- Memory blow‑up with large T: Consider kernel approximations (e.g., random Fourier features) or buffering.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Text‑to‑image on MS‑COCO:** Evaluate DAK‑UCB and Mixture‑DAK‑UCB with three image generators (Kandinsky, SDXL, GigaGAN) on 1000 prompts from MS‑COCO clusters. Report Joint‑RKE, JKD, and CLIP‑Score; DAK‑UCB improves diversity while preserving fidelity (Figure 2).
2. **Simulated animal generation:** Three arms: two animal‑specific (“cat”, “dog”) and one unconditional animal generator. DAK‑UCB (using I‑JRKE) correctly favors the diverse arm; fidelity‑only PAK‑UCB does not (Figure 3).
3. **Prompt‑relevant diversity:** Four expert arms, each specialized for one cluster (cat, dog, car, cake) but producing irrelevant content for others. DAK‑UCB learns to assign each expert to its relevant cluster, avoiding irrelevant generations (Figure 4). This shows diversity gains do not sacrifice topic relevance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extending to other modalities:** How can DAK‑UCB be adapted to protein, molecular, and graph generative models?
- **Incorporating scores into generation:** Can JKD/JRKE be used as differentiable objectives to directly guide generative processes (e.g., diffusion guidance) for improved diversity?
- **Generalization to non‑generative bandits:** Under what conditions can the two‑sample decomposition be applied to other bandit settings beyond generative model selection?
- **Robustness to assumption violations:** How do violations of normalized kernels, sub‑Gaussian noise, or bounded RKHS norms affect regret and practical performance?

## Limitations
- **Theory‑practice gap:** Regret analysis assumes ideal conditions (known hyperparameters, bounded RKHS norms, sub‑Gaussian noise) that may not hold in practice.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Performance depends on λ, β, kernel bandwidths, and mixture feasibility parameters (ε, ρ) which are not fully specified and require tuning.
- **Implementation opacity:** Exact details for reference prompt distribution \(P_T\), JKD reference model \(Q\), prompt sampling schedule, Mixture‑DAK‑UCB panel rate ρ, and Lipschitz constant ε are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.

## Next Checks
1. **Validate ψ_g implementation:** On a held‑out prompt set, compute the empirical average of the one‑sample diversity label \(y_i^{(D)}\) and compare to the offline‑computed true \(D_g(t)\). Significant bias indicates a flawed two‑sample decomposition.

3. **Sensitivity sweep for λ:** Systematically vary λ across orders of magnitude (e.g., 0.01 to 100) on MS‑COCO. Plot CLIP‑Score vs. Joint‑RKE to locate the Pareto frontier and verify the reported balanced operating point.
