# Emergency Preemption Without Online Exploration: A Decision Transformer Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22315
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22315
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Emergency vehicle travel time reduced by 37.7% (88.6 s vs 142.3 s) using a Decision Transformer with return conditioning, achieving lowest civilian delay and EV stops among all baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a Decision Transformer (DT) approach to emergency vehicle (EV) signal preemption that operates entirely offline during training and enables dispatchers to adjust corridor aggressiveness via a single scalar target return at test time. The method learns to generate signal control sequences from a fixed dataset of traffic trajectories, eliminating the need for risky online exploration. Evaluated on a 4×4 grid in the LightSim traffic simulator, the proposed DT reduces EV travel time by 37.7% compared to fixed-timing preemption, while also minimizing civilian disruption. A multi-agent extension with graph attention (MADT) scales to larger 8×8 grids, and a constrained variant (CDT) offers a second knob to cap civilian delay. The approach trains in minutes and infers in milliseconds, offering a safe, controllable, and efficient solution for emergency preemption.

## Method Summary
The authors frame emergency corridor optimization as a sequence modeling problem. They collect an offline dataset of 5,000 traffic episodes in LightSim, each containing states (per-intersection features including phase, incoming queue densities, EV distance, and time), actions (phase selections), rewards (a weighted sum of EV progress, queue penalties, and arrival bonus), and undiscounted return-to-go. A Decision Transformer architecture takes a context window of interleaved return-to-go, state, and action tokens, embeds them, and processes them through a causal transformer to predict the next action. At training time, the model learns to forecast actions conditioned on desired future returns; at inference, the dispatcher sets an initial target return G* that reflects the desired trade-off between EV speed and civilian delay. The model then autoregressively generates control actions, updating the remaining return after each step. For multi-intersection coordination, MADT adds a graph attention network to propagate information across intersections. The Constrained DT introduces a second cost-to-go token to heed civilian disruption budgets.

## Key Results
- On a 4×4 grid, DT (G*=0) reduces EV travel time to 88.6 s, a 37.7% improvement over fixed-timing (142.3 s), with civilian delay 11.3 s/veh and only 1.2 EV stops—the best among all methods, including online RL baselines.
- A return-conditioning sweep (G* from 100 to -400) yields a smooth trade-off: EV travel time increases from 72.4 s to 138.2 s while civilian delay decreases from 16.8 to 5.4 s/veh, without retraining.
- MADT scales to an 8×8 grid, achieving a 45.2% reduction in EV travel time compared to fixed-timing. The DT trains in ~12 minutes (A100) and infers in 2.3 ms per step.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Decision Transformer succeeds because it avoids the pitfalls of online reinforcement learning in safety-critical domains. By training solely on a static dataset, it eliminates the risk of disruptive exploration during real traffic. The return-to-go conditioning decouples policy behavior from the reward weights used during data collection, allowing a single trained model to realize a spectrum of behaviors simply by varying G*. This trade-off control is crucial for dispatchers who must balance EV urgency against civilian delay. Moreover, the transformer architecture naturally captures long-range dependencies across intersections via self-attention, enabling coordinated corridor formation without explicit communication protocols. The model’s ability to “stitch” together high-quality subsequences from diverse training trajectories—including suboptimal ones—produces emergent strategies that even surpass the best single expert episode.

## Foundational Learning
- **Decision Transformer** – Transforms reinforcement learning into a sequence modeling problem by conditioning on desired returns; core method enabling offline, controllable policies.
- **Offline Reinforcement Learning** – Learns from a fixed dataset without environment interaction; essential for safety and deployability in traffic control.
- **Return-to-Go Conditioning** – Uses cumulative future reward as a control knob; allows a single model to represent multiple policies along a performance trade-off curve.
- **Trajectory Stitching** – Combining optimal segments from different trajectories; explains why DT can outperform the best training episode.
- **Graph Attention Network (GAT)** – Facilitates decentralized coordination among intersections in MADT by exchanging messages with neighbors.
- **Constrained Reinforcement Learning via Cost Conditioning** – Adds a cost-to-go token to influence behavior relative to a disruption budget, approximating safety constraints.

## Architecture Onboarding
The Decision Transformer pipeline follows this component map:  
Dataset (trajectories) -> Tokenization (R, S, A) -> Embedding layers -> Causal Transformer -> Action head -> Argmax/ sampling.

Critical path: During training, random context windows from the dataset are fed through the transformer to predict next action; loss is cross-entropy. During inference, the dispatcher sets initial return G*, the model issues the first action, the environment returns new state and reward, and G* is decremented by the received reward; the loop continues.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Context length (C)**: Longer contexts capture more history but increase memory; ablations show C=30 is beneficial vs C=5.
- **Dataset diversity**: Mixing expert, random, and noisy data broadens the return distribution and enables return conditioning; expert-only data leads to poor generalization.
- **Model size**: More layers/heads improve capacity but require more compute; chosen L=4, d=128, NH=4 balances performance and training time.
- **Multi-agent coordination**: MADT replaces centralized attention with local GAT; reduces compute and scales, but limits global information flow.

Failure signatures:
- **Training loss diverges** → likely too high learning rate, missing warmup, or no gradient clipping. Remedy: lower lr, add warmup, clip grads.
- **Poor evaluation performance** → dataset lacks low-return episodes or is too narrow; recompute dataset statistics, ensure stratified sampling, and verify mixed-quality ratios.
- **Mismatched state dimension** → changes in feature extraction break the model; always use repository’s state encoder; check dimension = K*(P+6).
- **Constraint violations in CDT** → cost token may not strictly enforce budgets; monitor cumulative cost vs C* and consider additional safety layers.

First three experiments to run:
1. **Baseline DT replication**: Train DT on 4×4 mixed dataset (5k episodes) and evaluate with target return 0; expect ETT ≈ 88.6 s, ACD ≈ 11.3 s/veh.
2. **Return sweep**: Vary target return {100, 50, 0, -50, -100, -200, -400} and record ETT and ACD to reproduce the smooth trade-off curve.
3. **MADT scalability**: Train MADT on 8×8 grid and evaluate; confirm ~45.2% ETT reduction over fixed-timing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Transfer to real-world networks**: How does the DT perform on realistic, irregular topologies and microscopic dynamics (e.g., SUMO, CityFlow) beyond synthetic grids?
2. **Constrained DT violation rates**: What is the empirical frequency of civilian delay budget exceedance under CDT, and how does it compare to formal safe RL methods?
3. **Mechanistic understanding of trajectory stitching**: Which specific signal decisions are most often stitched from different training episodes, and when does this yield the largest benefit?
4. **Joint routing and signal optimization**: Can DT be extended to simultaneously optimize EV route and signal control, and can the return interface coherently handle both?
5. **Scaling MADT to city-scale**: How do graph attention mechanisms behave on networks with thousands of intersections, and what architectural modifications (e.g., hierarchical GATs) are needed to maintain coordination?

## Limitations
- Experiments confined to synthetic grid networks (4×4, 8×8) with uniform demand; real-world traffic complexity remains unvalidated.
- Offline dataset limited to 5,000 trajectories, potentially lacking coverage of rare congestion patterns or distribution shifts.
- Baseline comparisons rely on reimplemented baselines; independent verification is required to confirm claimed superiority.
- Constrained DT’s safety guarantees are preliminary; violation rates not reported.
- Efficiency metrics measured on an NVIDIA A100; performance on other hardware may vary.
