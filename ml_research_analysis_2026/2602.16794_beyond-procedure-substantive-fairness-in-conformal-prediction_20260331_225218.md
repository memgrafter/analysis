# Beyond Procedure: Substantive Fairness in Conformal Prediction

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.16794
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16794
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Label-clustered conformal prediction achieves lower disparity in downstream decision accuracy (maxROR) than standard, Mondrian, and group-clustered CP by controlling set size parity, while equalizing coverage often correlates with increased unfairness.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the predominant focus on procedural fairness metrics like equalized coverage in conformal prediction (CP). It argues that the true measure of fairness is substantive: equity in the outcomes of downstream decisions made using CP sets. The authors theoretically decompose the disparity in prediction set sizes between protected groups, showing how label-clustered CP—which clusters labels by similar score distributions—controls the method-driven sources of unfairness. To evaluate substantive fairness at scale, they introduce an LLM-in-the-loop evaluator that simulates human decision-making across vision, text, audio, and tabular tasks, a method validated against human experiments. Empirical results consistently show that minimizing set size gaps, rather than coverage gaps, correlates with improved substantive fairness, cautioning against demographic conditioning in CP design.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates conformal prediction methods through the lens of substantive fairness, measured by *maxROR* (disparity in the odds ratio of decision accuracy improvement compared to a no-prediction baseline). A new LLM-in-the-loop evaluation framework is central: for each test instance, an LLM is prompted with the input, a prediction set from a CP method, and the coverage guarantee; it then makes multiple label guesses. The proportion correct is modeled via a logistic Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) that accounts for task difficulty and adoption (fraction of LLM guesses within the set), enabling the estimation of group-specific odds ratios and *maxROR*. The core algorithmic contribution is label-clustered CP, which uses *k*-means on score quantiles to group labels with similar difficulty, then computes cluster-specific conformal thresholds. Theoretical analysis provides an upper bound on set size disparity that decomposes into components related to intra-cluster heterogeneity, cross-cluster spread, and residual group differences, clarifying the mechanism by which label-clustering reduces unfairness.

## Key Results
*   Label-clustered CP achieves the lowest *maxROR* on three of four tasks (FACET, BiosBias, ACSIncome), outperforming Mondrian, group-clustered, standard, and Backward CP.
*   Across all methods and tasks, smaller set size gaps between protected groups strongly correlate with lower *maxROR* (better substantive fairness), while smaller coverage gaps correlate with *higher* *maxROR* (worse fairness).
*   Theoretical decomposition shows label-clustered CP's advantage stems from keeping the intra-cluster heterogeneity and cross-cluster spread terms small, whereas Mondrian's group-conditional thresholds inflate the residual group difference term.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Decomposing Set Size Disparity via Label-Clustering
*   **Claim:** Label‑clustered conformal prediction reduces expected set size disparity between protected groups by controlling interpretable components derived from an upper bound.
*   **Mechanism:** Theorem 4.1 expresses the set size gap Δ<sub>a,b</sub> as a sum of three terms: (I) intra‑cluster label heterogeneity, (II) cross‑cluster spread, and (III) intra‑label cross‑group disparity. Clustering labels by score similarity makes (I) and (II) small by ensuring labels in a cluster have similar expected set sizes. Using a single threshold per cluster, rather than per group, avoids inflating (III) compared to group‑conditional methods like Mondrian.
*   **Core assumption:** The clustering function *h* is fixed and learned from score distributions; conditional expectations of set size given labels/groups exist; the score function produces meaningful non‑conformity scores.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “derive an upper bound decomposing prediction‑set size disparity into interpretable components…"; [section 4.1] formal statement and proof of Theorem 4.1; discussion of terms (I)–(III).
*   **Break condition:** Bound becomes loose if clusters are too fine (large *K* → unstable quantiles, large (II)) or too coarse (*K*=1 → large (I)); also if clustering fails to group labels by score similarity.

### Mechanism 2: LLM-in-the-Loop for Scalable Substantive Fairness Evaluation
*   **Claim:** Large language models (LLMs) can serve as a scalable proxy for human subjects when evaluating substantive fairness of conformal prediction sets across modalities.
*   **Mechanism:** For each test instance *x* and CP method *t*, the LLM receives the input, a prediction set (or none), and the coverage guarantee, then produces *M* label guesses. The proportion correct *R<sub>jt</sub>* is modeled with a logistic GEE including: treatment *t*, protected group *g*, task difficulty (marginal set size), and *adoption* (fraction of LLM guesses inside the set). *maxROR* measures disparity in improvement versus no‑set baseline.
*   **Core assumption:** LLM decision‑making patterns regarding reliance on prediction sets and accuracy correlate with human behavior; the GEE correctly adjusts for confounders; *maxROR* is a valid substantive fairness measure.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “introduce an LLM‑in‑the‑loop evaluator that approximates human assessment… validated with human experiments."; [section 4.2] GEE specification; Table 2 shows LLM reproduces human ordering of Marginal vs. Mondrian.
*   **Break condition:** LLMs may systematically differ from humans (e.g., over‑adoption, lack of skepticism) or show group‑specific bias; invalid responses may bias adoption estimates; correlation with human fairness judgments may fail for new tasks.

### Mechanism 3: Set Size Parity → Substantive Fairness; Coverage Parity → Harm
*   **Claim:** Minimizing set size gap across protected groups strongly correlates with improved substantive fairness (lower *maxROR*), whereas equalizing coverage often correlates with increased unfairness.
*   **Mechanism:** Across four tasks, empirical results show a V‑shaped relationship between *K* and set size disparity for label‑clustered CP; methods with smaller set size gaps (label‑clustered, Backward) yield lower *maxROR*. Plotting procedural gaps vs. *maxROR* reveals positive slope for set size gap and negative slope for coverage gap (Figure 4).
*   **Core assumption:** LLM‑in‑the‑loop accurately reflects human substantive fairness; observed correlations generalize beyond studied datasets and CP variants; set size parity is a leading indicator for downstream equity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “empirically show that equalized set sizes, rather than coverage, strongly correlate with improved substantive fairness."; [section 6.3] Figure 4 and regression analysis; [section 6.2] label‑clustered CP best *maxROR* on three datasets while maintaining utility.
*   **Break condition:** Correlation could reverse if decision‑makers interact with sets differently (e.g., singleton preference) or if task difficulty distributions interact with set size; coverage gaps reduced via extreme set inflation may shift trade‑offs.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Conformal Prediction fundamentals:** Split CP, calibration, coverage guarantees, score functions (APS, RAPS, SAPS). *Why needed:* Analysis hinges on how CP procedures compute thresholds and affect set size distribution. *Quick check:* Given a calibration set and score function, how do you compute the conformal threshold τ and construct a prediction set for a test point?
*   **Group fairness concepts:** Procedural fairness (fairness of the prediction process) vs. substantive fairness (equity of outcomes); group‑conditional coverage vs. equalized set size. *Why needed:* Shifts focus from procedural metrics to substantive outcomes. *Quick check:* What is the difference between equalized coverage and equalized set size, and which one correlates with lower *maxROR*?
*   **Statistical modeling for clustered outcomes:** Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with exchangeable working correlation; odds ratios and ratio of odds ratios (*maxROR*). *Why needed:* LLM‑in‑the‑loop generates correlated predictions per task; GEE adjusts for task‑level clustering and adoption. *Quick check:* Why is a GEE with exchangeable correlation appropriate when each task yields multiple predictions under different CP methods?
*   **Clustering for conditional coverage:** Using *k*-means on score quantiles to group labels with similar difficulty. *Why needed:* Label‑clustered CP relies on this to approximate conditional coverage while reducing set size disparity. *Quick check:* What score‑distribution features are used as embeddings for label clustering?
*   **LLM prompting and evaluation:** Designing prompts that present CP sets to an LLM; measuring adoption (whether the LLM’s answer lies inside the set). *Why needed:* Evaluator behavior must mimic the effect of prediction sets on human decision‑makers. *Quick check:* In the prompts, how is the coverage guarantee communicated, and why is adoption measured?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Base model -> Score function -> Calibration suite -> Clustering module -> LLM evaluator -> Analysis pipeline -> Evaluation orchestration.
*   **Critical path:**
    1. Split data into D<sub>calval</sub> (tuning), D<sub>cal</sub> (CP calibration), D<sub>test</sub> (evaluation).
    2. Tune score hyperparameters (T, λ, k<sub>reg</sub>) on D<sub>calval</sub> to minimize average set size.
    3. For each CP method, compute thresholds on D<sub>cal</sub> (cluster‑specific if needed).
    4. Generate prediction sets on D<sub>test</sub>; feed them to LLM evaluator to collect R<sub>jt</sub> and adoption.
    5. Fit logistic GEE: `logit(E[R_jt]) ~ treatment * group + difficulty + adoption`.
    6. Derive marginal probabilities p<sub>t,a</sub>, then odds ratios and *maxROR*.
    7. Compare *maxROR* across methods; plot procedural gaps vs. *maxROR*.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    - Human vs. LLM evaluation: LLMs are cheap and i.i.d. but may over‑adopt; adjusted by including adoption as covariate and validating against human data.
    - Clustering granularity: Choosing *K* controls bias–variance trade‑off; too small merges distinct label difficulties, too large yields noisy quantiles. V‑shaped gap curve suggests optimal middle ground (often *K=2*).
    - Score function selection: RAPS (with λ penalty) vs. SAPS (rank‑based) affect set size distributions; tuned per dataset.
    - CP method selection: Mondrian guarantees group‑conditional coverage but inflates set size gaps; label‑clustered CP prioritizes set size parity at the cost of not explicitly controlling coverage equality.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    - LLM produces many invalid labels (outside the label space) → check prompt clarity and whether the LLM respects class constraints.
    - Adoption rates near 0% or 100% for certain treatments/groups → the LLM is ignoring the set or following it blindly; may require prompt engineering or temperature adjustment.
    - Deviation from target coverage (1−α) especially for Mondrian on small groups → calibration instability; consider larger D<sub>cal</sub> or alternative clustering.
    - Unstable *maxROR* across random seeds or bootstrap → insufficient LLM samples (*M*) or test size; increase *M* or number of test points.
    - Extremely large set sizes for Backward CP when the size constraint is aggressive → adjust offset or target size.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1. **LLM vs. human validation:** Replicate Cresswell et al.’s comparison (Mondrian > Marginal in unfairness) using LLM evaluator on FACET, BiosBias, RA VDESS (Table 2).
    2. **Label‑clustered CP sensitivity:** Vary *K* on one dataset (e.g., RA VDESS); reproduce V‑shaped set size gap curve (Figure 5) and compute bound terms (Figure 6) to verify theoretical decomposition.
    3. **Procedural–substantive correlation:** For a fixed dataset, compute coverage gap and set size gap for all CP methods; regress *maxROR* on these gaps. Expect positive slope for set size gap and negative for coverage gap (Figure 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** What is the causal effect of decision-maker adoption rate on substantive fairness in conformal prediction systems?
    *   **Basis:** The authors identify that "explicitly controlling the adoption rate of the LLM evaluator… would allow for a rigorous isolation of the causal effects of set properties on substantive decision outcomes" as a future direction.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Current study only observes correlation; adoption is not experimentally manipulated.
    *   **What evidence:** Randomized controlled trials that systematically vary adoption (e.g., through instructions, incentives, or interface design) across CP methods and demographic groups, measuring the resulting change in substantive fairness metrics.
*   **Question:** How do different CP procedures interact with both procedural and substantive fairness notions in downstream decision-making pipelines, and can we derive general principles for designing equitable CP systems?
    *   **Basis:** "The interactions of CP with procedural and substantive notions of fairness in this broader context remain less well understood."
    *   **Why unresolved:** Lacks a unifying theoretical framework explaining why certain procedural choices (e.g., equalizing set size) yield better substantive fairness than others (e.g., equalizing coverage).
    *   **What evidence:** Formal theoretical analysis linking CP design parameters to fairness metrics, coupled with broad cross-domain validation to identify consistent patterns.
*   **Question:** Does the LLM-in-the-loop evaluator generalize as a reliable proxy for human fairness judgments across a wide range of tasks, protected groups, and CP methods beyond the limited settings already validated?
    *   **Basis:** Evaluator validated by comparing *maxROR* ordering only for Marginal vs. Mondrian on three datasets; behavior for other CP variants (e.g., Backward, Group-Clustered) and other modalities not directly compared to human decisions.
    *   **Why unresolved:** LLMs may exhibit different biases and heuristics than humans in unexplored tasks; prompt design may not capture human nuances.
    *   **What evidence:** Large-scale human subject experiments measuring *maxROR* across diverse tasks and CP methods, directly comparing to LLM outputs; systematic analysis of discrepancies to refine the evaluator.

## Limitations
*   Hyperparameter search procedure underspecified, hindering exact replication of score function settings.
*   LLM adoption rates vary; extreme values may distort *maxROR* and the correlation analysis.
*   Theoretical bound assumes clusters reflect score similarity; poor clustering inflates bound terms and may not reduce disparity.

## Confidence
*   **Theorem 4.1 (Set size disparity bound):** High – proof solid; depends on clustering quality and stable quantiles.
*   **LLM evaluator as human proxy:** Medium – human validation on three datasets shows similar rankings; LLM bias and limited modality coverage threaten generalizability.
*   **Set size parity → fairness correlation:** Medium – pattern holds across four tasks but may not generalize beyond studied models/scores.

## Next Checks
1.  **Cross-dataset robustness:** Apply label-clustered CP and LLM evaluator on ≥2 new datasets (e.g., medical images, multilingual text) to test set size–fairness generalization.
2.  **Human–LLM alignment:** Conduct human study on instances with extreme LLM adoption (<10% or >90%) to quantify evaluator bias and refine prompts.
