# A Monosemantic Attribution Framework for Stable Interpretability in Clinical Neuroscience Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17952
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17952
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: TEO-SAE reduces attribution instability (RIS/ROS) across in-distribution (ADNI) and out-of-distribution (BrainLAT) tests, achieving lowest RIS/ROS in binary and three-class tasks (e.g., ROS reduced from ~16.4 to ~12.3 in binary IID).

## Executive Summary
Polysemantic representations in clinical LLMs lead to unstable and variable attribution explanations for Alzheimer’s diagnosis. This paper introduces a monosemantic attribution framework combining sparse autoencoders (SAE) to disentangle layer activations, a transformer-based explanation optimizer (TEO) to integrate multiple attribution methods, and a UMAP linear constraint to enforce global coherence. On ADNI and BrainLAT datasets, TEO-SAE significantly lowers Relative Input Stability and Relative Output Stability metrics while revealing clinically coherent biomarkers (e.g., FAQ, AVLT tests) for Alzheimer’s progression.

## Method Summary
The authors fine-tune ModernBERT on ADNI clinical text for binary and three-class Alzheimer’s diagnosis. A sparse autoencoder (TopK variant) is trained on layer-22 activations to create a monosemantic feature space. Six attribution methods (Feature Ablation, Layer Activations, Layer Conductance, Layer Gradient SHAP, Layer Integrated Gradients, Gradient×Activation) are computed on either the original or SAE-augmented activations, yielding per-token attribution vectors averaged into a consensus target. A transformer explanation optimizer (TEO) is trained to map input token embeddings to an optimized attribution vector, minimizing a multi-objective loss that combines Relative Input Stability, Relative Output Stability, sparsity, and similarity to the consensus. An optional UMAP linear constraint (penalizing differences between first two UMAP components of the attribution tensor) is added to promote global structural coherence and restore sparsity. Final token attributions are obtained via backpropagation through the SAE encoder Jacobian and evaluated on ADNI (in-distribution) and BrainLAT (out-of-distribution) test sets.

## Key Results
- TEO-SAE achieved the lowest Relative Input Stability (RIS) and Relative Output Stability (ROS) values across all settings, e.g., ROS reduced from ~16.4 to ~12.3 in binary IID.
- The UMAP constraint (TEO-UMAP) restored higher sparsity while retaining stability improvements.
- Clinically, the framework identified coherent, class-specific biomarkers such as FAQ and AVLT tests, enhancing diagnostic interpretability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) transform polysemantic LLM layer activations into a more monosemantic latent feature space, which provides a cleaner substrate for attribution methods.
- **Mechanism:** An SAE is trained on activations from a specific LLM layer. Its encoder projects the high-dimensional, polysemantic activation vector **x** into a higher-dimensional, sparse feature vector **a** (F > d). The decoder reconstructs **x** as **Wa + b**. The learned dictionary matrix **W** contains feature directions; individual SAE features (columns of **W**) are encouraged to activate only for specific, coherent semantic concepts (monosemanticity) due to the sparsity constraint on **a**. This disentangles the overlapping representations (superposition) present in the original neuron activations.
- **Core assumption:** A sparser, over-complete representation (**a**) can effectively capture the salient factors of variation in the original activations (**x**) with each feature corresponding to a more interpretable concept.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract], [section 3.4], [section 3.5], and the neighbor paper *"Taming Polysemanticity in LLMs: Provable Feature Recovery via Sparse Autoencoders"*.
- **Break condition:** If the SAE fails to learn interpretable features (e.g., features remain polysemantic or not aligned with human-understandable concepts), downstream attribution improvements may not materialize. The paper notes a sparsity–stability trade-off where TEO-SAE reduces stability metrics but can also reduce sparseness.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A learning-based explanation optimizer (TEO) integrates multiple, variable attribution methods into a single, more stable explanation by explicitly optimizing for attributes like stability and sparsity.
- **Mechanism:** Six attribution methods are applied separately to the layer (either polysemantic or SAE-monosemantic space), producing attribution vectors φ⁽ᵏ⁾. A weighted average **φ̄** serves as a target. A transformer-based autoencoder (TEO) is trained to reconstruct an explanation **φ̂** from the original input, with a total loss function **L_total** that combines terms for Relative Input Stability (RIS), Relative Output Stability (ROS), Sparseness, and Similarity to **φ̄**. This optimizes the output **φ̂** to be simultaneously stable (low RIS/ROS), sparse, and aligned with the consensus of base methods.
- **Core assumption:** A differentiable optimizer can learn to produce attribution vectors that are superior (more stable/sparse) to any single base attribution method by leveraging their combined signals under a multi-objective loss.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract], [section 3.2] (cost function), [section 4.1] (Table 1), and neighbor papers *"Explainable Graph Neural Networks..."* and *"DiagnoLLM..."*.
- **Break condition:** If the optimization objective (**L_total**) is mis-specified (e.g., weights λ₁–λ₄ poorly tuned) or the model capacity of TEO is insufficient, the optimized explanations may overfit to the training distribution or fail to generalize (especially OOD). The paper shows TEO-UMAP makes a sparsity–stability trade-off.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A UMAP-based linear constraint imposes global geometric structure on the attribution manifold, promoting coherent, population-level patterns and restoring sparsity lost during SAE+TEO optimization.
- **Mechanism:** After TEO (or TEO-SAE) produces attributions, a feature-wise UMAP projection is applied to the normalized attribution tensor (samples × tokenizer features) to embed each tokenizer feature's attribution distribution into a 2D space. A penalty term λ₅∑(uᵢ₁ - uᵢ₂)² is added to the loss (or applied post-hoc) to enforce that the first and second UMAP components for each point are equal. This linear constraint forces the dominant low-dimensional manifold of the attribution space to align along a single dimension, creating a more globally coherent and linear structure across the cohort.
- **Core assumption:** Imposing a simple linear geometric constraint (equal UMAP components) on the high-dimensional attribution space will regularize it toward a more stable, population-level pattern without overly distorting the local (sample-specific) signal.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract], [section 3.3], [section 4.1 & Figure 2], and limited external anchoring (corpus shows little direct work combining UMAP with linear constraints; *"Mechanistic Interpretability of Fine-Tuned Vision Transformers..."* uses PCA).
- **Break condition:** If the linear constraint is too strong (λ₅ too high), it may force an artificially linear structure that discards meaningful, non-linear variations in the attribution space, potentially losing clinically relevant subgroups. The paper presents TEO-UMAP as a trade-off: it restores higher sparsity while retaining much stability.

## Foundational Learning
### Monosemanticity vs. Polysemanticity
Why needed: Standard LLM neurons are polysemantic—they fire for multiple unrelated concepts—making it impossible to assign a single, stable meaning to an attribution score. Monosemantic features (one feature ≈ one concept) are prerequisite for stable, interpretable attributions.  
Quick check: Can you explain why a neuron that activates for both "king" and "queen" (polysemantic) leads to unstable explanations compared to one that activates only for "royal title" (monosemantic)?

### Superposition
Why needed: Neural networks use high-dimensional spaces to represent more features than neurons (F > d), causing features to superpose (overlap) in the same neuron directions. SAEs aim to reverse this by learning an over-complete basis where features are less superimposed.  
Quick check: If a network has d=100 neurons but needs to represent F=200 features, what must necessarily happen to the neuron activations, and why does an SAE help?

### Attribution Stability Metrics (RIS/ROS)
Why needed: The paper's key outcome is reducing inter-method variability, quantified via Relative Input Stability (RIS) and Relative Output Stability (ROS). Understanding that these measure sensitivity to input/output perturbations is fundamental to evaluating the framework's success.  
Quick check: What would a high RIS score indicate about an attribution method's explanations for a given input?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Pretrained LLM (ModernBERT, layer 22) -> Sparse Autoencoder (optional) -> Attribution Engine (6 methods) -> Explanation Optimizer (TEO/DEO) -> UMAP Linear Constraint (optional) -> Projection to Input Tokens

### Critical Path
1. Fine-tune ModernBERT on ADNI (binary/three-class) using cross-entropy; keep best validation checkpoint.
2. Freeze LLM. Train SAE (TopK, 32× latent width) on layer-22 activations from ADNI training+validation.
3. For a test sample (ADNI or BrainLAT), get activation **x**.
4. Compute six attributions φ⁽ᵏ⁾ either on **x** or on SAE features **a** (via backward passes through SAE encoder).
5. Feed **x** (and optionally **a**) to pre-trained TEO to get optimized **φ̂**.
6. (Optional) Apply UMAP linear constraint regularization during TEO training or as a post-processing step on **φ̂**.
7. Project **φ̂** back to input tokens using chain rule (Equation 5) for clinical interpretation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- SAE improves stability but can reduce sparsity; TEO-UMAP aims to recover sparsity.
- TEO (transformer) outperforms DEO (diffusion) in stability-sparsity frontier.
- Explicit management of stability vs. sparsity via λ weights in L_total and UMAP constraint strength λ₅.
- IID vs. OOD: SAE and TEO trained only on IID (ADNI); paper claims robustness to OOD (BrainLAT) but performance drop possible.

### Failure Signatures
- High RIS/ROS values (>16 for binary IID): indicates highly unstable explanations; likely no SAE or poorly trained TEO/DEO.
- Non-linear PCA structure in Figure 2: if top PCA components do not show linear trend after UMAP constraint, geometric regularization failed.
- Loss of clinical biomarkers in top-attributed tokens: if top PCA-contributing tokens do not correspond to known AD markers (AVLT, FAQ, CDT), attribution is not clinically valid.
- SAE features not monosemantic: qualitative inspection (e.g., SAELens) shows mixed activations.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **SAE Architecture Selection:** Train TopK, JumpReLU, Gated-SAEs on ADNI activations. Evaluate monosemanticity (qualitative) and downstream stability (RIS/ROS). (Result: TopK performed best.)
2. **Optimizer Benchmark:** With fixed SAE (TopK), train TEO and DEO on attribution vectors from six base methods. Evaluate on ADNI test: Sparseness, RIS, ROS. (Result: TEO-SAE achieved best stability-sparsity trade-off.)
3. **UMAP Constraint Validation:** Run TEO-SAE with/without λ₅ penalty. Perform PCA on token-attribution vectors; visualize PC1 vs. PC2. Check linear alignment and compare Sparseness/RIS/ROS. (Result: UMAP constraint induces linear PCA structure and restores sparsity.)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does incorporating clinician-in-the-loop evaluation and patient feedback affect perceived interpretability, trustworthiness, and clinical utility? (Current evaluation uses automated metrics and external LLM, not real clinicians/patients.)
- To what extent does the framework generalize across different LLM architectures (e.g., decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder) and diverse medical diagnosis tasks beyond Alzheimer’s? (Study only uses ModernBERT and Alzheimer’s.)
- How do design choices within the SAE (e.g., TopK vs. JumpReLU, feature dimensionality F, sparsity strength) influence the stability-sparsity trade-off and clinical interpretability across tasks? (Paper compares variants but does not explore sensitivity.)
- Does the linear UMAP constraint optimally balance structural coherence and information preservation, or could alternative geometric constraints yield better results? (No alternative constraints are compared.)

## Limitations
- Monosemanticity of SAE features assumed but not directly quantified; core premise partially unverified.
- UMAP linear constraint is novel with weak external anchoring; may discard clinically relevant non-linear patterns.
- Hyperparameters and architecture details underspecified, hindering replication; evaluation limited to ADNI/BrainLAT, leaving generalizability open.

## Confidence
- **SAE improves stability via monosemanticity**: Medium (RIS/ROS reductions consistent, but direct evidence of monosemanticity missing).
- **TEO optimizer yields stable explanations**: Medium (strong empirical results, but complex multi-objective training may risk overfitting).
- **UMAP constraint restores sparsity and global coherence**: Low (PCA linearity observed, but constraint’s necessity and impact on clinical validity not sufficiently isolated).

## Next Checks
1. Quantify SAE feature monosemanticity using metrics like activation entropy, sparsity, and concept alignment via probing; correlate with stability gains.
2. Ablate the UMAP constraint (vary λ₅) to measure its isolated effect on stability, sparsity, and biomarker coherence.
3. Evaluate TEO-SAE on at least two external Alzheimer’s datasets (e.g., NACC, AIBL) to test OOD robustness and generalizability of identified biomarkers.
