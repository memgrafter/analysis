# TailNLG: A Multilingual Benchmark Addressing Verbalization of Long-Tail Entities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27768
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27768
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** Current LLMs exhibit a consistent, measurable bias against verbalizing long-tail (rare) entities, with standard overlap metrics often failing to capture this performance gap.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces TailNLG, a new multilingual (English, Italian, Spanish) benchmark built from Wikidata to systematically evaluate bias against long-tail entities in Data-to-Text generation. In a zero-shot evaluation of six instruction-tuned LLMs (Llama, Qwen, Gemma), the study reveals that models generate lower-quality, more uncertain text for rare entities. Crucially, common lexical overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) do not consistently detect this bias, whereas semantic similarity (BERTScore) and perplexity (PPL) do. The findings highlight a significant fairness issue in current LLM evaluation and underscore the need for more robust, bias-aware metrics.

## Method Summary
TailNLG is constructed by querying Wikidata to extract RDF triples for entities, classifying them as "head" or "long-tail" via a Pareto cutoff based on claim count and multilingual Wikipedia presence. For each entity, 10 data units (triple sets) are sampled, and human-authored verbalizations ("gold") are collected in three languages. An additional "silver" set is created via machine translation and automatic post-editing with quality estimation. The benchmark is used to evaluate six LLMs in a zero-shot setting, where models generate text given formatted triples. Performance is compared between long-tail and head entities using seven metrics (BERTScore, BLEU, chrF++, ROUGE, PPL) and statistical testing (Mann-Whitney U). A secondary analysis compares model scores on TailNLG's long-tail subset versus the established WebNLG benchmark to measure familiarity bias.

## Key Results
- LLMs show degraded performance on long-tail entities: **lower BERTScore** (semantic similarity) and **significantly higher PPL** (model uncertainty).
- Standard **overlap-based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) often fail to reflect this bias**, sometimes even showing opposite trends.
- Cross-lingual variation exists, with **English consistently outperforming Italian and Spanish**.
- Models achieve **higher scores on the WebNLG benchmark** than on TailNLG's long-tail entities, confirming a familiarity bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLMs exhibit degraded performance on long-tail entity verbalization due to insufficient exposure during pretraining.
- **Mechanism:** Rare entities appear infrequently in pretraining corpora, leading to weaker internal representations. This manifests as higher perplexity (model uncertainty) and lower semantic similarity scores.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract ("LLMs struggle with rare entities..."); Section 2 ("LLMs exhibit a marked decline... influenced by frequency"); corpus on long-tail knowledge tasks.
- **Break condition:** If models pretrained on frequency-balanced KG data show no performance gap between long-tail and head entities.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Standard overlap-based metrics fail to consistently detect bias against long-tail entities, while embedding-based and uncertainty metrics do.
- **Mechanism:** Lexical overlap can be inflated by verbose or diversely phrased outputs for head entities that are semantically off. BERTScore captures deeper alignment, and PPL measures uncertainty, both of which degrade for long-tail inputs.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract ("Overlap-based metrics do not consistently reflect this bias"); Section 5.1 (opposite pattern for overlap metrics, clear PPL insights).
- **Break condition:** If overlap metrics showed consistent, significant score drops for long-tail entities across models and languages, aligning with BERTScore/PPL.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Language-specific resource disparities cause cross-lingual variation in long-tail verbalization performance, with English consistently better.
- **Mechanism:** English-centric pretraining data and benchmarks lead to better internalized knowledge. For lower-resource languages, prevalence of long-tail entities and morphological complexity (e.g., gender agreement) exacerbate gaps.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract ("Performance varies by language, with English generally better"); Section 5.1 (higher PPL for Italian); Section 6 (richer morphologies); corpus on low-resource language challenges.
- **Break condition:** If models showed no significant performance difference between English and Italian/Spanish on long-tail entities, or morphology-rich languages outperformed English.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Long-tail distribution in knowledge graphs**
  *Why needed:* Entity popularity follows a power law; the Pareto cutoff operationalizes "long-tail" vs. "head".
  *Quick check:* How does defining long-tail by claim count and Wikipedia presence shape the benchmark's entity selection?
- **Concept: Zero-shot evaluation for LLMs**
  *Why needed:* Isolates biases innate to pretraining, without in-context learning or fine-tuning effects.
  *Quick check:* Why is zero-shot crucial for diagnosing entity popularity bias, and what might a few-shot setting obscure?
- **Concept: Evaluation metrics for text generation**
  *Why needed:* Contrasts semantic (BERTScore), lexical (BLEU/ROUGE), and uncertainty (PPL) metrics; key to interpreting bias detection.
  *Quick check:* Why might a model generate a lexically overlapping but semantically inaccurate description for a head entity, inflating BLEU?
- **Concept: Multilingual model fairness**
  *Why needed:* Performance disparities across languages reveal resource imbalances in pretraining.
  *Quick check:* How does morphological complexity (e.g., in Italian/Spanish) potentially interact with entity frequency to affect generation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Wikidata SPARQL querying → entity selection (Pareto cutoff) → triple extraction → human annotation (gold) → MT/APE (silver) → quality estimation (xCOMET) → post-editing (xTower) → benchmark dataset → LLM zero-shot inference (prompt template) → generation (3 samples) → metric computation (BERTScore, BLEU, chrF++, ROUGE, PPL) → statistical testing (Mann-Whitney U) → analysis (by type, language, dataset).
- **Critical path:** Entity selection (Pareto threshold) → triple extraction (parallel units) → verbalization (gold + silver) → model generation → metric calculation → significance testing.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Human gold vs. automatic silver labeling: gold ensures quality but limits scale; silver scales but may introduce translation bias.
  - Metric diversity: 7 metrics provide robustness but complicate interpretation; PPL is computationally heavier.
  - Multilinguality: Parallel units control structural complexity but may miss culturally specific phrasing.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High PPL with high BLEU: model uncertain but outputs lexically common phrases that superficially match.
  - Insignificant metric differences between long-tail and head: underpowered test or benchmark contamination.
  - Language performance inversion (e.g., Italian > English): likely data quality or tokenizer issue.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce bias: Run Llama-8B on TailNLG long-tail vs. head; confirm BERTScore ↓ and PPL ↑ for long-tail (p<0.05).
  2. Metric sensitivity check: Compute all metrics on a small set; verify BLEU/chrF do not consistently correlate with BERTScore/PPL trends.
  3. Cross-benchmark validation: Compare TailNLG long-tail scores with WebNLG for same model; expect significantly higher scores on WebNLG.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do current evaluation metrics reliably detect verbalization bias against long-tail entities across languages and models?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper finds embedding-based metrics (BERTScore) and PPL consistently show bias, while overlap metrics (BLEU, chrF++, ROUGE) often show opposite or no pattern. The authors state existing metrics "do not consistently capture these differences" and call for more reliable frameworks.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates inconsistency but does not investigate underlying reasons (e.g., whether overlap metrics are insensitive to semantic deviations, penalize verbosity differently, or misalign with human bias judgments).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-grained error analysis correlating metric scores with human annotations of bias (omissions, hallucinations, factual errors) across diverse model outputs for head and tail entities, in multiple languages.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific linguistic or socio-cultural factors drive the observed language-dependent performance differences in long-tail verbalization?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes higher English performance and suggests Italian/Spanish's richer morphologies may exacerbate gaps, but does not disentangle training data distribution, morphological simplicity, or other linguistic properties.
- **Why unresolved:** The study compares aggregate scores per language without controlling for or analyzing specific linguistic features (morphological agreement, word order) or correlating performance with language-specific data prevalence in pre-training corpora.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablation isolating linguistic variables (e.g., generating English-like syntax for Spanish/Italian, or vice versa) or analyzing model performance on tail entities with varying morphological/syntactic complexity within each language.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the inclusion of machine-translated ("silver") verbalizations in benchmarks like TailNLG systematically mask or alter the measured severity of long-tail bias?
- **Basis in paper:** Models achieve higher scores on silver data than gold. For significant differences, silver scores are higher specifically for head entities, hinting silver data may amplify the head–tail gap. The authors note this implication but do not explore its effect on bias measurement.
- **Why unresolved:** The study treats silver data as a quality variant but does not test whether the bias magnitude (head vs. tail score difference) differs between gold-only and silver-augmented sets, or whether silver translations introduce artifacts favoring models on frequent patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-evaluating model bias on a gold-only subset of TailNLG, comparing the head–tail performance delta to the full (gold+silver) set, and analyzing whether silver translations of tail entities are intrinsically easier/harder.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Are the identified biases primarily due to lack of memorized knowledge about long-tail entities during pre-training, or to deficiencies in the models' compositional generalization from structured data to text?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper frames the problem within LLMs struggling with "rare entities" and cites work linking performance drops to limited pre-training exposure. However, zero-shot verbalization requires both entity knowledge and generation ability. Increased PPL for tail entities could stem from either factor.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper reports aggregate performance without dissociating knowledge recall (accuracy of entity attributes) from generation quality (fluency, syntax). Without parsing outputs to separate factual accuracy from fluency, the root cause remains ambiguous.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Component-wise evaluation where model outputs for head and tail entities are parsed to extract factual claims, measuring factual accuracy separately from fluency metrics. Alternatively, a controlled study using synthetic triples with only tail entities but frequent relation types to isolate entity frequency from relation complexity.

## Limitations
- The exact SPARQL query and entity selection logic for extracting the 10 data units per entity from Wikidata are not provided, making exact reconstruction of the same data potentially impossible.
- The precise train/test splits for TailNLG and WebNLG are not defined; the paper evaluates on "the WebNLG test set" without specifying the exact subset or split used.
- Metric implementations can vary (BERTScore version, tokenization, PPL computation target), and the PPL calculation method (on input triple string vs. generated text) is ambiguous but critical for replication.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Frequency-based degradation):** Medium. Depends on confirming PPL methodology and that entity frequency aligns with claim count/Wikipedia presence.
- **Mechanism 2 (Metric reliability):** Medium. Requires full replication to confirm overlap metric patterns and PPL/BERTScore consistency.
- **Mechanism 3 (Cross-lingual variation):** Medium. Relies on metric consistency and assumes morphology/resource disparity as primary drivers.
