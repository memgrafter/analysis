# SurvHTE-Bench: A Benchmark for Heterogeneous Treatment Effect Estimation in Survival Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05483  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05483  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** No single HTE estimator universally dominates; survival meta-learners and Causal Survival Forests are robust under assumption violations and high censoring, while outcome imputation (e.g., Double-ML) excels in randomized settings with low censoring.

## Executive Summary
SurvHTE-Bench introduces the first standardized benchmark for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs) from right-censored survival data. It provides a modular evaluation framework combining 40 synthetic datasets with controlled variations in causal assumptions (ignorability, positivity, informative censoring) and survival dynamics, alongside semi-synthetic and real-world datasets with known ground truth. Evaluating 53 estimator variants reveals that method performance is highly context-dependent, with survival meta-learners and Causal Survival Forests showing superior robustness under challenging conditions, while outcome imputation methods dominate in ideal randomized experiments. The benchmark establishes that practitioners must carefully match estimator choice to dataset-specific causal and censoring characteristics.

## Method Summary
The framework centers on a suite of 53 HTE estimators grouped into three families: outcome imputation (42 variants), direct-survival CATE models (2), and survival meta-learners (9). Outcome imputation applies strategies (Margin, IPCW-T, Pseudo-observation) to convert censored times into continuous surrogates, then uses standard regression meta-learners. Direct models (Causal Survival Forests, SurvITE) ingest right-censored data directly. Survival meta-learners adapt S-, T-, and Matching-learners by replacing base learners with survival models (e.g., RSF, DeepSurv) and contrasting restricted mean survival time. The benchmark uses 52 datasets: 40 synthetic (crossing 8 causal configurations with 5 survival scenarios), 10 semi-synthetic (ACTG, MIMIC-IV), and 2 real-world (Twins with ground truth, ACTG 175). For each, 10 random splits are generated; hyperparameters are tuned on validation, and test CATE RMSE and ATE bias are computed. Rankings use Borda count and win-rates.

## Key Results
- No estimator universally dominates; best method depends on causal assumptions and censoring level.
- Under high censoring and assumption violations (e.g., unobserved confounding, informative censoring), S-Learner-Survival and Matching-Survival achieve top-3 win-rates of 67.5% on CATE RMSE, while Double-ML drops to 52.5%.
- In randomized, low-censoring settings, outcome imputation methods (Double-ML, Causal Forest) perform best.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Modular Synthetic Data Generation for Controlled Stress-Testing**  
If synthetic survival datasets can be generated with systematically varied causal assumptions and survival dynamics, then the robustness of HTE estimators to specific assumption violations and censoring regimes can be isolated and measured. The benchmark creates 40 datasets by crossing 8 causal configurations (e.g., RCT vs. observational, ignorability violations, positivity violations, informative censoring) with 5 survival scenarios (Cox, AFT, Poisson with low/medium/high censoring). Each dataset has known ground-truth CATEs, enabling direct calculation of CATE RMSE or ATE bias under each combination. The core assumption is that the set of variations adequately represents the diversity of challenges in real survival HTE problems. Evidence: The paper explicitly states "systematically varying causal assumptions and survival dynamics" and provides detailed generation formulas in Appendix A. Break condition: The modular design may not capture all real-world complexities (e.g., time-varying treatments, interference), limiting generalizability if violations occur outside designed configurations.

**Mechanism 2: Outcome Imputation to Convert Survival CATE to Regression CATE**  
If censored survival times can be replaced by statistically sound imputations that preserve the conditional expectation of the true event time, then standard regression-based CATE learners (e.g., Double-ML, Causal Forest) can be applied without modifying their core algorithms. Three imputation strategies—Margin, IPCW-T, and Pseudo-observation—are applied to right-censored data to produce fully observed continuous outcomes. These imputed times feed into outcome-imputation methods to estimate CATE. The core assumption is that imputed values are unbiased or low-variance estimates of missing event times, especially under ignorable censoring. Evidence: Section 2 describes the approach, and Appendix B mathematically defines the imputation methods. Break condition: Under informative censoring unaddressed by imputation (e.g., censoring depends on unobservables), imputed times become biased, causing dramatic failure even for perfectly specified regression learners.

**Mechanism 3: Survival Meta-Learners Directly Model Survival Functions**  
If the base regression model in meta-learners is replaced with a survival model that directly estimates the conditional survival function, the resulting estimator can better account for right-censoring and the time-to-event nature of the outcome compared to imputation-based approaches. Meta-learning frameworks (S-, T-, Matching) are adapted by training survival models (Random Survival Forests, DeepSurv, DeepHit) on treated and control groups to estimate RMST or survival probabilities; CATE is computed as a contrast of these quantities. The core assumption is that survival models accurately estimate conditional survival distributions and the chosen estimand is identifiable. Evidence: Section 2 outlines the adaptation, and Appendix D gives explicit algorithms. Break condition: If survival models are misspecified (e.g., proportional hazards violated with Cox-based RSF) or the outcome horizon is poorly chosen relative to censoring, estimated RMST can be highly biased.

## Foundational Learning
- **Right-censoring and survival estimands (e.g., RMST)**  
  Why needed: All methods must handle incomplete event times; understanding how RMST integrates the survival curve and how censoring affects estimation is necessary to interpret why some methods succeed or fail under different censoring rates.  
  Quick check: Given a Kaplan-Meier curve with high censoring after time 50, how would you approximate the RMST up to horizon 100, and what sources of uncertainty dominate?

- **Causal identification assumptions for HTE (ignorability, positivity, ignorable censoring)**  
  Why needed: The benchmark explicitly varies these assumptions; a practitioner must map estimator performance to which assumptions are likely violated in their target problem to choose an appropriate method.  
  Quick check: If treatment assignment depends strongly on an unmeasured variable U that also affects survival, which assumption fails and which estimators are theoretically robust?

- **Meta-learner frameworks (S-, T-, X-, DR-Learners)**  
  Why needed: Outcome-imputation methods rely on these frameworks; understanding their bias-variance trade-offs and how they interact with imputation errors is key to diagnosing performance.  
  Quick check: In an observational study with moderate treatment imbalance and correct propensity scores, would you expect S-Learner or T-Learner to have lower CATE variance? Why?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Data Generators → [Imputation Module] → Outcome-Imputation Learners / Direct-Survival CATE / Survival Meta-Learners → Prediction → Evaluation Engine

**Critical path:**  
Synthetic/semi-synthetic/real data are generated. Imputation (if used) transforms censored outcomes. Estimator families produce CATE predictions. The evaluation engine splits data, tunes hyperparameters on validation, and computes CATE RMSE/ATE bias on test. Direct and survival meta-learners skip imputation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Modularity vs. computational overhead:* Many combinations (53 variants) increase evaluation time but allow fair comparison.  
- *Synthetic control vs. realism:* Synthetic datasets offer ground truth and controlled violations but may not capture all real-world covariate complexities; semi-synthetic data bridges this gap but introduces modeling assumptions.  
- *Imputation vs. direct modeling:* Imputation enables reuse of off-the-shelf regression learners but can amplify bias under informative censoring; direct methods handle censoring natively but require specialized implementations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- CATE RMSE blowup from low to high censoring → likely imputation-based failure (check imputation MAE).  
- High variance in CATE estimates across repeats → unstable base models (e.g., T-Learner with sparse treated units).  
- Poor performance under OBS-NoPos for Causal Survival Forests → sensitivity to positivity violations.  
- Inconsistent rankings between synthetic and semi-synthetic → potential lack of transportability due to covariate shifts.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic RCT-50, Scenario A (low censoring, ignorable):** Expect outcome-imputation (Double-ML, Causal Forest) and survival meta-learners competitive. Validate low baseline RMSE and close rankings.  
2. **Synthetic OBS-UConf-InfC, Scenario D (unobserved confounding + informative censoring):** Expect S-Learner-Survival and Matching-Survival to dominate; check Double-ML and X-Learner degradation.  
3. **Real Twins dataset:** Use top methods from synthetic to predict CATE. Compare CATE RMSE across families; if survival meta-learners maintain top performance, validates robustness to unknown violations. Also inspect ATE bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1: How can causal survival benchmarks incorporate continuous degrees of assumption violations rather than binary present/absent conditions?**  
*Basis:* Binary synthetic datasets may not capture nuanced continuum of partial violations.  
*Why unresolved:* Real-world data often face partial assumption violations (e.g., unmeasured confounding with varying strength), but benchmark designs treat violations as on/off switches, limiting understanding of estimator sensitivity across realistic severity levels.  
*Evidence needed:* A framework for graded violations (e.g., Rosenbaum-type sensitivity parameters extended to survival); systematic evaluation of existing methods across violation strengths; guidelines for reporting robustness.

**Open Question 2: What modifications to survival CATE estimators can improve robustness to positivity violations?**  
*Basis:* Performance dropped sharply under OBS-NoPos; Causal Survival Forests showed vulnerability.  
*Why unresolved:* Positivity violations are common in observational studies with extreme covariate profiles, yet many estimators degrade significantly. Robust approaches (e.g., covariate trimming, Bayesian regularization) for survival outcomes remain underdeveloped and unevaluated.  
*Evidence needed:* New estimator variants that maintain accuracy under controlled positivity violations; theoretical analysis of trade-offs; benchmark comparisons showing improved stability over existing methods across violation severities.

**Open Question 3: How can causal survival analysis be extended to settings with time-varying treatments and longitudinal covariates?**  
*Basis:* Benchmark limited to static, binary treatments with fixed baseline covariates, explicitly excluding time-varying treatments and longitudinal structures.  
*Why unresolved:* Many high-stakes applications (e.g., chronic disease management) involve sequential treatment decisions and evolving measurements. Existing survival HTE methods do not directly generalize to dynamic regimes; no standardized benchmark exists.  
*Evidence needed:* Estimators that handle time-varying treatments and time-dependent confounding; synthetic/semi-synthetic data generators with known ground truth for longitudinal settings; comprehensive evaluations under censoring and missingness typical of panel data.

**Open Question 4: Beyond restricted mean survival time, which survival estimands should become standardized for HTE benchmarking and how should they be evaluated?**  
*Basis:* Extending benchmark to natively support wider array of task-specific estimands (e.g., conditional median survival, time-varying hazard ratios) would enrich scope.  
*Why unresolved:* Different clinical and policy questions require different survival metrics. Current coverage is narrow (RMST, limited survival probabilities). Without standardized protocols, comparisons across applications remain difficult and some estimands may be overlooked.  
*Evidence needed:* Systematic study of estimator behavior across multiple estimands under varied assumption violations; recommended estimand benchmarks for common domains; open-source benchmark modules with data generation pipelines tailored to each estimand.

## Limitations
- **Dataset diversity and realism:** 40 synthetic configurations may not capture complex real-world dynamics (e.g., high-dimensional nonlinear effects, time-varying treatments); semi-synthetic data reduces but does not eliminate simulation assumptions.
- **Imputation reliability:** Outcome-imputation methods assume ignorable censoring; violations (e.g., censoring depends on unobserved risk) can introduce bias not fully stress-tested.
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Exact random seeds, library versions, and split indices not disclosed; numeric results may shift across environments though trends are expected to hold.

## Confidence
- **Benchmark novelty and completeness:** High – Explicitly fills known gap; full code and data publicly released.
- **Empirical findings (no universal best method; context-dependent rankings):** Medium – 53 estimator variants evaluated across diverse settings provide strong evidence, but dataset coverage and external validity caution against overgeneralization.
- **Modular framework design (40 synthetic + semi-synthetic + real):** Medium – Structure is clear and reproducible, yet synthetic scenarios may not span full space of real-world challenges.

## Next Checks
1. **External real-world validation:** Apply top-5 methods from the benchmark to at least three additional survival HTE datasets (e.g., SEER cancer registry, multi-center ICU cohorts) with plausible ground truth to evaluate transportability of reported rankings.
2. **Sensitivity to informative censoring:** Create synthetic data where censoring explicitly depends on unobserved factors (outside the paper’s eight causal configs) and compare failure modes and performance gaps between imputation-based and direct-survival CATE estimators.
3. **Ablation on base survival model:** Within the existing benchmark, re-run experiments substituting base survival learners (e.g., replace Cox-based RSF with DeepSurv or a flexible neural survival model) to isolate impact of survival model misspecification on CATE accuracy for each meta-learner family.
