# Identifying and Characterising Response in Clinical Trials: Development and Validation of a Machine Learning Approach in Colorectal Cancer

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.00757
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00757
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A machine learning pipeline combining partly conditional modelling (PCM) with Virtual Twins and survLIME improved identification of dynamic treatment responders in synthetic data (AUC 0.597→0.685) and identified known genetic and metastatic biomarkers in a colorectal cancer trial.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles a key limitation in clinical trial analysis: conventional subgroup identification methods rely on static baseline covariates and ignore patients' time-varying measurements and dynamic treatment response. The authors develop and validate a novel machine learning framework to estimate individual, time-specific treatment effects for survival outcomes. The method first transforms longitudinal data using partly conditional modelling (PCM) to capture time-varying covariates. It then uses the Virtual Twins approach to estimate counterfactual outcomes and derive patient-specific treatment effects, which are interpreted via the explainable AI tool survLIME. In simulations, the PCM-integrated approach significantly improved responder identification. Applied to panitumumab trials in colorectal cancer, the model identified factors like KRAS/NRAS/BRAF mutations, specific metastasis sites (CNS, bone, skin), and ethnicity as important for treatment response, aligning with established biological knowledge.

## Method Summary
The core methodology integrates three components. First, **partly conditional modelling (PCM)** restructures longitudinal patient records: each observed time point becomes a separate row with the covariate values at that time and a residual survival time from that point onward. This allows survival models to learn from time-varying information. Second, a **survival model** (selected via nested cross-validation from candidates like DeepSurv, random survival forests, or Cox models) is trained on this PCM-transformed data. Third, **Virtual Twins** uses this final model to predict each patient's counterfactual survival function under the treatment they did not receive. The **treatment effect** at a specific time is defined as the log proportional change between the predicted survival time under the observed treatment and the predicted time under the counterfactual treatment. Finally, **survLIME** provides local explanations for these time-specific effects by fitting a simple, interpretable Cox model to perturbed samples around a particular patient-time instance, isolating which covariates drive the predicted response at that moment.

## Key Results
*   **Synthetic Simulations:** Incorporating PCM into the pipeline improved the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for identifying dynamic responders from 0.597 to 0.685, and for fixed responders from 0.732 to 0.773.
*   **Colorectal Cancer Application:** The method identified KRAS, BRAF, and NRAS genetic mutations, metastasis to the central nervous system, bone, and skin, and ethnicity as key factors influencing response to panitumumab, consistent with prior literature.
*   **Model Selection:** DeepSurv with two hidden layers was consistently selected as the best-performing survival model in nested cross-validation for both synthetic and clinical data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Partly Conditional Modelling (PCM) for Time-Varying Covariates
**Claim:** PCM restructures repeated time-varying measurements into a format suitable for survival modelling, capturing dynamic changes that static baseline models miss.
**Mechanism:** Each patient's longitudinal covariate history is split into multiple records at each observed time point. For each record, the survival time is recomputed as the residual time from that point to the event or censoring, and the censoring indicator is adjusted accordingly. This allows the model to learn how a covariate's value at a specific time influences the hazard from that time forward.
**Core Assumption:** The hazard at a given time depends only on covariates measured at that exact time (local Markov assumption), and the timing of measurements and censoring is independent of the underlying event process after conditioning on covariates.
**Break Condition:** Violations occur if measurement times are informative (e.g., sicker patients monitored more frequently) or if carry-forward imputation severely distorts true covariate trajectories.

### Mechanism 2: Virtual Twins for Individual Treatment Effect Estimation
**Claim:** Virtual Twins estimates a patient's counterfactual outcome (what would have happened under the other treatment) using a trained survival model, enabling calculation of a time-specific treatment effect.
**Mechanism:** For a given patient at a given time, the final survival model predicts the survival function under their *observed* treatment assignment. The model is then queried again with the same patient covariates but with the *unobserved* treatment assignment (i.e., swapping the treatment indicator). The treatment effect is defined as the log of the proportional change between the predicted survival times under the two scenarios.
**Core Assumption:** The survival model is sufficiently accurate and well-calibr in its predictions for both the observed and counterfactual treatment assignments, and treatment assignment is random (unconfounded).
**Break Condition:** Poor model calibration or extrapolation into covariate-treatment regions with little data leads to biased counterfactual predictions and invalid effect estimates.

### Mechanism 3: survLIME for Local Interpretation of Time-Specific Effects
**Claim:** survLIME approximates the complex, non-linear survival model locally around a patient-time point with a simple, interpretable Cox model to identify factors driving that patient's predicted treatment effect at that time.
**Mechanism:** For a selected patient at a specific time, binary (perturbed) versions of the patient's covariates are sampled, with the treatment indicator and the specific time held constant. The original black-box model predicts a treatment effect for each perturbed instance. A weighted linear Cox model (the surrogate) is fit to these synthetic samples, where weights decay with the distance from the original patient's covariate values. The resulting coefficients (log hazard ratios) indicate the local direction and magnitude of influence of each covariate on the treatment effect.
**Core Assumption:** The patient's predicted treatment effect is approximately linear in the covariate space within a small, local neighbourhood, and the sampling strategy adequately captures this neighbourhood without significant boundary effects.
**Break Condition:** The true relationship is highly non-linear even locally, or the sampling neighbourhood is poorly sized, causing the linear surrogate to be a poor fit. Averaging explanations across times may also mask important temporal shifts in drivers.

## Foundational Learning

*   **Concept: Partly Conditional Survival Models**
    *   **Why needed here:** Standard survival models (e.g., CoxPH) assume covariates are fixed at baseline. Clinical trials generate repeated measures (e.g., tumor scans, lab values), and ignoring this temporal information wastes data and misrepresents dynamic treatment response.
    *   **Quick check question:** How does converting a longitudinal patient record into multiple time-point rows change what a survival model can learn compared to using only baseline covariates?

*   **Concept: Counterfactual Prediction (Virtual Twins)**
    *   **Why needed here:** In a randomised trial, each patient only experiences one treatment. To estimate an individual's treatment effect, we need to predict their outcome under the *other* treatment they did not receive.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why is a highly accurate survival model a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for accurate individual treatment effect estimation via the Virtual Twins method?

*   **Concept: Local Surrogate Models (LIME) for Survival**
    *   **Why needed here:** The pipeline uses a complex, potentially deep learning survival model for prediction. To understand *why* a specific patient is predicted as a responder at a specific time, we need a human-interpretable explanation of the model's local logic.
    *   **Quick check question:** In survLIME, why are the treatment indicator and the specific time held constant during the sampling procedure? What would be the problem if they were allowed to vary?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Preprocessor -> Survival Model Trainer -> Treatment Effect Engine -> Characterisation Engine (survLIME) -> Validation & Reporting
**Critical Path:** Raw longitudinal data -> PCM-transformed dataset -> Best survival model selection (via nested CV) -> Final model training on full data -> Counterfactual prediction for all patients at all times -> Treatment effect score computation -> Patient-time sampling by responder tier -> survLIME explanation per sample -> Aggregation of factor importance.
**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   *Model Complexity vs. Interpretability:* Using DeepSurv may improve prediction accuracy but makes counterfactual predictions more unstable and survLIME approximations less faithful than a simpler Cox model.
*   *Temporal Resolution:* Finer-grained time intervals in PCM provide more data points but increase noise and computational cost; coarser intervals may miss dynamic transitions.
*   *SurvLIME Sampling Strategy:* Constraining treatment and time constant is essential for isolating *response* factors but limits the explanation to a very narrow context (specific time, specific treatment assignment).
**Failure Signatures:**
*   Low time-dependent concordance index (<0.65) in nested CV indicates an unreliable survival model, invalidating downstream effects.
*   Extreme variance in survLIME coefficients for the same patient across different time points or sampling seeds suggests unstable local approximations.
*   Identified "important" factors that are known strong confounders but were not adjusted for suggest the model is picking up bias.
*   In clinical application, if all identified factors are baseline-only (no time-varying), it may indicate PCM transformation was ineffective or the model ignored temporal signals.
**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  **PCM Verification:** On a synthetic dataset with a known time-limited covariate effect (e.g., hazard multiplier switches at t=5), confirm a model on PCM data detects the time-varying effect while a baseline-only model does not.
2.  **Model Selection Replication:** Re-run the nested CV on the provided synthetic data (fixed responder case) to confirm DeepSurv with 2 layers is selected. Stress-test by increasing irrelevant time-varying covariates (P=30) to see if selection holds.
3.  **Treatment Effect Recovery:** In the dynamic responder simulation (θ=0.9), compare the distribution of computed treatment effects for patients in the true responder region (X₁>0 & X₂<0) vs. others. A good separation, especially after their covariates change, is prerequisite for meaningful characterisation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can replacing the linear Cox surrogate model in survLIME with a non-linear survival tree improve the accuracy and stability of treatment response characterizations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Authors suggest: "Future work could address the inability to describe non-linear relationships... replacing the linear surrogate Cox model used in survLIME with a non-linear survival tree."
- **Why unresolved:** The linear surrogate cannot capture non-linear relationships, potentially missing or mischaracterizing subgroups.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop tree-based survLIME; compare with standard survLIME in simulations with known non-linear subgroup structures using identification AUC and explanation stability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What sample size is required to reliably identify dynamic responders using PCM+Virtual Twins, and how does it depend on dynamic responder prevalence and covariate count?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Simulations: AUC 0.547 (N=300) vs 0.685 (N=1000) for dynamic case; authors note >1000 patients typical in phase III trials.
- **Why unresolved:** Many trials have smaller N (e.g., phase II ~300); knowing threshold determines applicability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic simulation varying N (200–2000), dynamic responder prevalence (10–50%), and number of covariates (15–50); define N needed for AUC >0.7.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How robust is PCM to violations of independent measurement times/censoring, and what corrections can reduce bias?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Authors state: "Further bias can result if either measurement times or censoring are not independent, as required by partly conditional methods."
- **Why unresolved:** In practice, measurement frequency often depends on patient health, violating assumption and biasing results.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulate hazard-dependent observation times; compare standard PCM to methods like inverse probability weighting for observation times; measure bias in subgroup identification.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does modeling response as continuous rather than binary improve interpretability and predictive validity, and how should continuous responses be validated?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Authors propose future work to "consider response as continuous not binary."
- **Why unresolved:** Binary thresholds discard information and may not reflect response spectrum; continuous measures may better guide decisions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend Virtual Twins to predict continuous treatment effect curves; evaluate with time-dependent calibration/discrimination for continuous scores; clinician user studies on interpretability.

## Limitations
*   **Reproducibility Blockers:** Critical implementation details are unspecified, including DeepSurv hyperparameters (layer sizes, learning rate), survLIME sampling parameters, exact multiple imputation method, and the cutoff for excluding records near events, preventing exact replication.
*   **Simulation Ambiguity:** The computation of AUC for the dynamic responder simulation is unclear; it is unknown whether responder status was defined using baseline or time-updated covariate values, creating ambiguity in interpreting those results.
*   **Clinical Translation Risk:** The method's validity is entirely dependent on the accuracy of the final survival model. Without demonstrated high model calibration (e.g., c-index values), all downstream individual treatment effect estimates and explanations are suspect.

## Confidence
*   **Mechanism Claims (PCM, Virtual Twins, survLIME):** **Medium**. The description aligns with established literature, but confidence is reduced by lack of implementation specifics and potential deviation from standard practices.
*   **Simulation Results (AUC improvements):** **Medium**. The synthetic data generator is partially described, but key unknowns and ambiguous AUC calculation prevent independent verification. The reported improvements are plausible but not fully substantiated.
*   **Clinical Findings (KRAS, BRAF, etc.):** **Low**. While biologically plausible, this is an exploratory analysis on a single dataset with no external validation, adjustment for multiple testing, or demonstration of superiority over simpler subgroup analyses.
