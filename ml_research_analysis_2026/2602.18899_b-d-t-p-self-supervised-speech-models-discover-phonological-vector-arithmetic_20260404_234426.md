# [b]=[d]-[t]+[p]: Self-supervised Speech Models Discover Phonological Vector Arithmetic

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18899
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18899
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Self‑supervised speech models encode 19 phonological features as linearly separable directions; vector arithmetic analogies reach up to 94% success on TIMIT and 93% on VoxAngeles, and scaling these vectors yields strong correlations (Spearman \|ρ\| up to 0.9) with acoustic measurements.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether self‑supervised speech models (S3Ms) linearly encode phonological features. Using vector arithmetic analogies such as \[b\] ≈ \[p\] + (\[d\]−\[t\]), the authors test across 96 languages. WavLM achieves up to 94% success on TIMIT and 93% on VoxAngeles (unseen languages), far outperforming spectral baselines (0–19%). Moreover, scaling phonological vectors continuously modifies acoustic properties (e.g., formants, COG) with correlations up to \|ρ\|=0.9, enabling fine‑grained control in speech synthesis. The effects hold for both interpolation and extrapolation, confirming that phonological features are represented as scalable, continuous directions rather than binary distinctions.

## Method Summary
The study comprises two experiments. First, it tests the validity of phonological analogies in S3M latent spaces. Phone‑level vectors are extracted via feature slicing (averaging frames aligned to phone boundaries). Using PanPhon’s binary feature system, quadruplets \[p₁, p₂, p₃, p₄\] are constructed where the feature difference between p₁ and p₂ equals that between p₃ and p₄. The predicted vector p₂−p₁+p₃ should be closest (by cosine similarity) to p₄, defining a success. Success rates are computed per layer for TIMIT and VoxAngeles. Second, phonological vectors are derived as the mean difference between representations of phones with opposite feature values. These vectors are scaled by λ and added to a target phone’s representation; the modified sequence is resynthesized with a VoCoS vocoder. Acoustic measurements (formants, COG, HNR) are extracted from the resynthesized audio, and Spearman’s ρ is calculated between λ and the measurement changes.

## Key Results
- WavLM achieves up to 94% analogy success on TIMIT and 93% on VoxAngeles; spectral features (MFCC, MelSpec) perform near chance (0–19%).
- 19 phonological features are encoded as compositional linear directions; the optimal layer for WavLM is the final layer.
- Scaling phonological vectors correlates strongly with acoustic measurements (Spearman ρ up to \|0.9\|); effects are monotonic for both interpolation (\|λ\|≤1) and extrapolation (\|λ\|>1).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Linear encoding of phonological features**  
S3Ms are claimed to encode phonological features as linearly separable directions in their latent space. The mechanism constructs a phonological vector by subtracting the mean representation of phones with a feature value −1 from those with +1. This vector can then be added to one phone’s representation to approximate another phone that differs only on that feature, enabling analogies like \[b\] ≈ \[p\] + (\[d\]−\[t\]). The core assumption is that phonological features are compositional and linearly decomposable. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim, the quadruplet definition in section 3.1.1, and related work probing linearity, though direct replication is limited. Failure would manifest as success rates near baseline or non‑orthogonal vectors.

**Mechanism 2: Scalable acoustic control via vector magnitude**  
The magnitude (scale λ) of a phonological vector continuously influences the acoustic realization of the associated feature. By adding λ·v to a phone’s representation and resynthesizing with a vocoder, acoustic measurements (e.g., formants, COG) change monotonically with λ. This relies on the assumption that phonological features exist on a gradient and are scalable. Evidence includes strong Spearman correlations (\|ρ\| up to 0.9) reported in the abstract and section 4.1.2, and monotonic trends for both interpolation and extrapolation. A failure condition would be no correlation or distorted speech beyond moderate λ.

**Mechanism 3: Cross‑lingual generalization**  
Vectors derived from English (TIMIT) successfully predict analogous relations in VoxAngeles, which contains 95 non‑English languages. This suggests that phonological features, as defined by PanPhon, are universal and that S3Ms capture language‑agnostic regularities. Evidence: 93% success on VoxAngeles, with 68% of analogies involving phones unseen in training. The break condition would be significant performance drop on unseen phones or failure to generalize across languages.

## Foundational Learning

#### Self‑supervised learning (SSL) for speech
- **Why needed:** S3Ms are pretrained on raw audio without labels; understanding SSL objectives (e.g., masked prediction) explains why phonological structure emerges without explicit supervision.
- **Quick check:** What objective does WavLM use beyond contrastive loss? *Answer:* Speech denoising.

#### Phonological features & PanPhon
- **Why needed:** The paper maps phones to binary feature vectors (e.g., \[+voice, −nasal\]) using PanPhon, which provides the feature system to construct analogies.
- **Quick check:** How many features were actually tested? *Answer:* 19, after exclusions.

#### Vector arithmetic & linear representation hypothesis (LRH)
- **Why needed:** LRH posits that human‑interpretable features are linearly encoded, underpinning the analogy tests and vector scaling.
- **Quick check:** What is the difference between item‑based and offset‑based analogy tests? *Answer:* Item‑based predicts a single vector; offset‑based tests relational consistency.

#### Vocoder inversion
- **Why needed:** To translate modified S3M representations back to audio for acoustic evaluation. Without a reliable vocoder, scaling effects cannot be measured.
- **Quick check:** Why was Vocos chosen? *Answer:* Robustness to out‑of‑distribution inputs from aggressive representation modifications.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Raw waveform → S3M encoder (frame‑level representations) → Phone pooling (feature slicing) → phone vectors → PanPhon feature extraction → phonological vector *v* (mean difference per feature) → Modifier (add λ·*v* to target frames) → Vocoder (VoCoS) → resynthesized waveform → Acoustic analyzer (Praat/Parselmouth).

**Critical path:**  
1. Extract phone‑level representations from S3M using feature slicing.  
2. Build quadruplet sets from PanPhon feature differences.  
3. Compute analogy success rate via cosine similarity comparisons.  
4. For scaling, compute *v*ᵢ via mean subtraction.  
5. Modify representations with λ, resynthesize with vocoder.  
6. Correlate λ with acoustic measurements Δ via Spearman’s ρ.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Slicing method:** Feature slicing (contextual) vs. audio slicing (local); feature slicing better for S3Ms, audio slicing helps MFCCs.
- **Vector construction:** Averaged over many phones (robust, feature‑pure) vs. single minimal pair (noisy).
- **Model choice:** Different S3Ms show distinct layerwise peaks; WavLM last layer consistently best.
- **Vocoder:** Must handle out‑of‑distribution modified representations; Vocos chosen for robustness.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Anisotropic collapse: embeddings become too similar (cos≈1), reducing discrimination.
- Vocoder degradation: resynthesized audio acoustic measurements deviate from original even at λ=0.
- Insufficient phone samples: leads to noisy *v* estimates; drop phones with <50 occurrences.
- Acoustic measurement extraction errors: formant tracking failures; use Parselmouth defaults and manual inspection.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Analogy success rate test:** On TIMIT, compare S3Ms vs. MFCC/MelSpec; identify best layer (WavLM last layer ~92%).
2. **Cross‑lingual generalization:** Run same test on VoxAngales; WavLM achieves 93% despite unseen phones.
3. **Scaling correlation:** For each phonological feature, vary λ∈[−5,5], resynthesize, compute Spearman ρ between λ and acoustic measurement change; expect \|ρ\|>0.7 and monotonic trend.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Factors behind S3M layerwise differences:**  
   - *Basis:* Paper notes different models behave differently but cannot isolate causes.  
   - *Resolution:* Systematic ablation varying pretraining objectives, architecture, or data while probing phonological vector quality.

2. **Natural class vs. phonetic correlation:**  
   - *Basis:* Unclear whether extracted vectors capture abstract phonological categories or low‑level acoustic correlates.  
   - *Resolution:* Compare vectors derived from multiple competing phonological feature systems and test generalization to non‑canonical natural class members.

3. **Vocoder influence on scaling correlations:**  
   - *Basis:* Only one vocoder (VoCoS) evaluated; observed ρ may be vocoder‑dependent.  
   - *Resolution:* Repeat synthesis experiments with multiple vocoders of different architectures and training sets.

4. **Discrepancy between item‑based and offset‑based metrics:**  
   - *Basis:* Two evaluation metrics produce different layerwise trends; left for future work.  
   - *Resolution:* Analyze geometric meaning of each metric (proximity vs. relational consistency) and correlate with layer properties like anisotropy or effective receptive field.

5. **Temporal properties and layerwise peaks:**  
   - *Basis:* Speculation that vowels peak earlier (local cues) and consonants later (broad context); not directly tested.  
   - *Resolution:* Manipulate temporal context (receptive field, slicing method) and measure shifts in success rates for vowel vs. consonant analogies; also measure temporal window size per layer.

## Limitations
- **Vocoder training specifics:** Exact hyperparameters for VoCoS (batch size, learning rate, steps) are unreported, hindering precise replication of the inversion pipeline.
- **PanPhon filtering decisions:** Which specific phones/diphthongs were excluded from TIMIT/VoxAngeles and how plosive closures/releases were merged are not fully specified, affecting the exact phone inventory used for vector computation.
- **Random splits:** The VoxAngales train/test split is randomly selected without a fixed language list, making exact cross‑lingual replication impossible without rerunning the authors’ selection.

## Confidence
- Analogy success rates (TIMIT & VoxAngeles): **High**
- Scalable acoustic control: **High**
- Cross‑lingual generalization: **Medium**
