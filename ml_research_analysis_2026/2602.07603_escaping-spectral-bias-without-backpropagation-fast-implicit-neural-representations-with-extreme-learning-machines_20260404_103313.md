# Escaping Spectral Bias without Backpropagation: Fast Implicit Neural Representations with Extreme Learning Machines
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07603
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07603
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** ELM-INR reconstructs signals in seconds (vs. minutes/hours for backpropagation) and achieves approximately a 2× PSNR improvement over SIREN, FFN, GaussNet, and WIRE across image, multispectral, spatiotemporal, and geophysical data.

## Executive Summary
Implicit neural representations (INRs) are powerful for modeling continuous signals but suffer from slow iterative training and spectral bias, especially when the target signal contains non-uniform frequency content. This paper introduces ELM-INR, a framework that eliminates backpropagation by decomposing the domain into overlapping subdomains, fitting each with a single-layer Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) via closed-form least-squares, and blending local predictions with a partition of unity. To further enhance quality under capacity constraints, BEAM—an adaptive mesh refinement strategy—balances local spectral complexity (Spectral Barron norm) across subdomains. Experiments show ELM-INR is orders of magnitude faster than iterative baselines and yields roughly a 2× PSNR gain. BEAM provides additional improvements when per-subdomain capacity is limited.

## Method Summary
ELM-INR first partitions the input domain into overlapping subdomains, either uniformly or via BEAM’s adaptive merging based on a spectral complexity proxy. For each subdomain, a local ELM is trained: hidden weights are randomly initialized and frozen, while output weights are obtained by directly solving a linear least-squares problem on the subdomain’s coordinate-samples. The resulting local predictors are combined globally using a partition of unity—smooth window functions with local support that sum to one—ensuring continuity without needing joint training. Coordinates are encoded with random Fourier features. Training and inference are fully decoupled across subdomains, enabling massive speedups.

## Key Results
- **Speed:** Reconstruction time reduced from minutes/hours to seconds compared to backpropagation-based INRs.
- **Fidelity:** Average PSNR improvement of ~2× over SIREN, FFN, GaussNet, and WIRE across multiple data modalities.
- **BEAM:** Further quality gains in capacity-constrained settings by adaptively balancing local spectral complexity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Displacement of Backpropagation with Closed-Form Solutions
By freezing the hidden layer and solving for output weights via least-squares, each subdomain’s training becomes a convex linear problem, eliminating iterative optimization. The random hidden features provide a fixed basis; if well-conditioned, this yields an accurate local approximation.

### Smooth Blending via Partition of Unity
Independent local predictions are combined using smooth, overlapping window functions that sum to one. This partition of unity guarantees a continuous (or smooth) global output despite decoupled training.

### Adaptive Spectral Balancing with BEAM
Error analysis shows worst-case local error is proportional to the local Spectral Barron norm. BEAM estimates these norms from a fine grid and merges neighboring cells to equalize complexity, preventing high-frequency regions from dominating the global error and optimizing the use of fixed per-subdomain capacity.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Input Encoder:** Maps coordinates X to feature vector γ(X) (e.g., Random Fourier Features).
2. **Domain Decomposer:** Creates subdomains via uniform grid or BEAM’s adaptive partition.
3. **Local ELM Trainer:** For each subdomain, builds activation matrix H_i from frozen random hidden weights and solves α_i = (H_i^T H_i)^{-1} H_i^T y_i via least‑squares.
4. **PoU Blender:** Final prediction ŷ(X) = Σ_i ϕ_i(X) · (α_i^T σ(W_i·γ(X)+b_i)), with smooth windows ϕ_i.

### Critical Path
Decompose domain → train local ELMs in parallel (solve linear systems) → store output weights → at inference, for each query X, evaluate relevant local ELMs and blend with PoU weights.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Subdomain size vs. number: smaller patches reduce local spectral complexity but increase blending overhead.
- ELM width m: larger m improves local fit but increases solve cost and memory.
- BEAM threshold τ: lower τ forces more merges (larger patches), higher τ yields more smaller patches.
- PoU function: smoother windows improve blending continuity but may require larger support.

### Failure Signatures
- Numerical instability: ill-conditioned H_i^T H_i leads to errors or NaNs; remedy with ridge regularization.
- Visible seams: discontinuities at boundaries indicate inadequate PoU overlap or window smoothness.
- BEAM ineffective: partition similar to uniform grid; check spectral proxy sensitivity and atomic cell size.
- Over‑merging: BEAM produces very large patches in complex areas; adjust τ or atomic size.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline sanity check:** Run ELM-INR on a simple 1D signal (e.g., sin(x) + 0.5*sin(10x)) with uniform 4 patches, m=128, RFF. Verify closed‑form solve finishes instantly and PSNR > 50 dB. Check PoU blending smoothness.
2. **BEAM effect validation:** On a 2D image with a known high‑frequency region (e.g., text overlay), compare BEAM vs. uniform partitions at same total N and m=256. Visually inspect BEAM’s partition and quantify PSNR gain.
3. **Capacity constraint stress test:** Fix total computational budget (m·N constant). Sweep (N, m) pairs on a spectrally rich image. Expect BEAM to maintain higher PSNR than uniform as m decreases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can ELM-INR and BEAM scale to high‑dimensional neural field tasks like NeRF or dynamic scenes? The authors identify investigating such extensions as important future work.
- How can cross‑instance generalization be achieved while retaining fast closed‑form fitting? The current model is trained per instance and does not generalize across signals.
- What is a principled method for choosing the spectral Barron norm threshold τ in BEAM? The paper does not provide guidelines for τ selection.
- How do alternative activation functions (e.g., sinusoidal, Gabor) affect spectral properties and BEAM’s effectiveness? The study is limited to ReLU activations.

## Limitations
- Exact partition of unity construction (window function, boundary handling) is unspecified, potentially causing seam artifacts.
- ELM initialization parameters (random weight scale, bias range) are unreported, affecting conditioning and expressiveness.
- BEAM threshold τ selection strategy is not described, hindering reproducibility and optimal use.
- The discrete Fourier proxy for Spectral Barron norm may be inaccurate for small or non‑stationary patches, impairing BEAM’s adaptivity.
