# Qworld: Question-Specific Evaluation Criteria for LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23522
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23522
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: Qworld generates question-specific evaluation criteria via recursive expansion, achieving 89% coverage of expert-authored criteria and 79% novel criteria validated by experts, while revealing nuanced capability distinctions in frontier LLMs that standard rubrics miss.

## Executive Summary
Evaluating large language models (LLMs) on open-ended questions is hampered by static, one-size-fits-all rubrics that fail to capture question-specific nuances. Qworld addresses this by automatically generating tailored evaluation criteria through a recursive expansion tree (RET) that decomposes each question into scenarios, perspectives, and fine-grained binary criteria. Applied to health and reasoning benchmarks, Qworld produces criteria that human experts rate as more insightful and granular than prior methods. When used to score model responses, these criteria uncover hidden capability dimensions (e.g., long-term impact, equity), alter model rankings, and reduce score saturation, demonstrating that question-adaptive evaluation is both feasible and necessary for accurate LLM assessment.

## Method Summary
Qworld employs a Recursive Expansion Tree (RET) algorithm that starts with a question and alternates between horizontal expansion (adding sibling nodes for breadth) and hierarchical decomposition (breaking nodes into children for depth) across three levels: Scenarios → Perspectives → Binary Criteria. Each step is guided by the question’s inferred context (intent, audience, stakes). Generated criteria are weighted by importance (points) and aggregated to produce a final score. The method is benchmarked against expert-authored criteria on a HealthBench subset and used to re-evaluate 11 frontier LLMs on HealthBench and Humanity's Last Exam, comparing rankings and score distributions against official benchmark results.

## Key Results
- Qworld achieves 89% coverage of expert-authored criteria and generates 79% novel criteria validated by human experts.
- Human experts rate Qworld’s criteria higher in insight (0.83) and granularity (0.85) than prior automated methods.
- Applying Qworld criteria to 11 frontier LLMs changes model rankings and reduces score saturation by ~20% across models by revealing distinctions in dimensions like long-term impact, equity, and error handling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Recursive Expansion Enables Exhaustive Exploration
**Claim**: The recursive expansion tree systematically covers evaluation dimensions implied by a question, far beyond what single-pass generation can achieve.  
**Mechanism**: Starting from the question, the algorithm alternates between horizontal expansion (adding sibling nodes to increase breadth at the current level) and hierarchical decomposition (breaking each node into finer-grained children) across three levels: Scenarios → Perspectives → Criteria. This multiplies the space of possible criteria.  
**Core Assumption**: Many important evaluation criteria are not immediately obvious; they emerge only after repeatedly asking “what else?” and decomposing high-level concepts.  
**Evidence Anchors**:  
- Abstract: “generates question-specific evaluation criteria through a recursive expansion tree that decomposes each question into scenarios, perspectives, and fine-grained binary criteria via hierarchical and horizontal expansion.”  
- Section 3.2: “RET builds a three-level tree whose nodes correspond to Scenarios (ℓ=1), Perspectives (ℓ=2), and Criteria (ℓ=3).”  
- Corpus: Neighbor papers (e.g., TICK, RocketEval) use single‑pass prompting; Qworld’s recursive design yields substantially higher Coverage and Uniqueness (Table 3 left).  

### Mechanism 2: Question‑Specific Context Anchoring Avoids Generic Rubric Limitations
**Claim**: Grounding criteria in the specific context of each question captures nuance that static rubrics miss, leading to higher insight and granularity.  
**Mechanism**: The first step (“Scenario grounding”) infers the intent, audience, stakes, and implicit constraints of the question. These contextual signals steer subsequent perspective elicitation and criterion generation, ensuring every generated item is tailored to this particular instance.  
**Core Assumption**: Evaluation requirements are highly question‑dependent; even two questions within the same dataset may demand different quality dimensions (e.g., safety vs. pedagogy).  
**Evidence Anchors**:  
- Abstract: “Evaluating LLMs on open‑ended questions requires criteria that adapt to question‑specific context, but existing methods rely on static rubrics that miss these nuances.”  
- Section 1: “Task‑level criteria are also hard to write well: manual curation often omits subtle but important dimensions… and expert‑written question‑level criteria are expensive to produce at scale.”  
- Corpus: Qworld’s criteria achieve human‑rated Insight 0.83 and Granularity 0.85 (Table 1), far above prior methods, confirming context‑sensitivity.  

### Mechanism 3: Dual Expansion Strategy Balances Breadth and Depth
**Claim**: Combining horizontal expansion (breadth) with hierarchical decomposition (depth) produces criteria that are both comprehensive and precise, outperforming either strategy alone.  
**Mechanism**: Horizontal expansion (applied for *w* rounds at each level) identifies missing sibling concepts, ensuring diverse evaluation axes. Hierarchical expansion then decomposes each node into increasingly concrete sub‑aspects. The two operators interleave to cover many angles while sharpening granularity.  
**Core Assumption**: A one‑dimensional expansion (only breadth or only depth) cannot fully specify the rich evaluation space a question implies.  
**Evidence Anchors**:  
- Section 3.2: “RET grows the tree using two complementary expansion operators… Hierarchical expansion… Horizontal expansion…”  
- Section 4.4, Table 3 left: Ablation results. RET (both operators) Coverage=0.89, Uniqueness=0.79; Tree Decomposition (hierarchical only) Coverage=0.77; Self‑Reflection (horizontal only) Uniqueness=0.70.  
- Corpus: The superiority of the combined approach is clear in the neighbor comparison, where single‑pass methods fall short.  

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Recursive Tree Decomposition**  
  *Why needed*: It is the core algorithm; understanding the three‑level structure and expansion operators is essential to implement, debug, and extend Qworld.  
  *Quick Check*: How does the system decide when to stop expanding a node, and what role do the *w*₁, *w*₂ parameters play?  

- **Concept: Question‑Context Adaptation**  
  *Why needed*: Distinguishes Qworld from prior static‑rubric methods; explains why criteria vary across questions.  
  *Quick Check*: Why might two medically similar questions still require different evaluation perspectives?  

- **Concept: Dual Expansion (Breadth vs. Depth)**  
  *Why needed*: Governs the design trade‑offs; explains the ablation results and informs parameter tuning.  
  *Quick Check*: What would happen if you set horizontal expansion rounds to zero for the perspective level?  

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: Input → Question Parser → Scenario Analyzer (initial R⁰ₕ) → (for ℓ=1,2: Scenario/Perspective Expander → Decomposer) → Criteria Generator → Criteria Expander → Reviewer → Score Assigner → Negative Checker → Output JSON.  
- **Critical Path**: The deepest dependency chain runs from question → R⁰ₕ (scenarios) → horizontal expansion (scenarios) → hierarchical decomposition (perspectives) → horizontal expansion (perspectives) → hierarchical decomposition (criteria) → final consolidation.  
- **Design Tradeoffs**:  
  - *Expansion width* (*w*₁, *w*₂, *w*₃): Larger values increase coverage but raise cost and risk redundancy.  
  - *LLM backbone*: Stronger generators improve coverage and uniqueness but are slower.  
  - *Scoring weights*: The points system (positive 1‑10, negative –1‑10) must be calibrated to reflect importance; mis‑calibration skews final evaluation.  
- **Failure Signatures**:  
  - Stagnant Coverage/Uniqueness after many expansion rounds indicates redundancy.  
  - Low human‑rated Insight/Granularity suggests criteria remain too generic.  
  - Model rankings remain nearly identical to standard rubrics, implying criteria failed to capture distinguishing dimensions.  
- **First 3 Experiments**:  
  1. **Smoke Test**: Run Qworld on 5 sample questions with default parameters. Verify output includes 10‑15 scenarios, 20‑30 perspectives, and 30‑50 criteria, each with integer points and reasoning.  
  2. **Ablation Check**: Disable horizontal expansion (set *w*₁=*w*₂=0) and compare the number of unique criteria and Coverage against the full pipeline on 50 HealthBench questions; confirm a significant drop in Uniqueness.  
  3. **Robustness Probe**: Swap the judge model used for Coverage evaluation (e.g., use Qwen3‑30B instead of GPT‑4.1) on a fixed set of generated criteria; ensure Coverage varies by <0.05.  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How well does Qworld generalize to domains beyond medical and abstract reasoning, such as creative writing, coding, or social sciences?  
- **Basis in Paper**: Qworld is evaluated only on HealthBench (medical) and Humanity’s Last Exam (reasoning). The paper states that Qworld "customizes its taxonomy to the target dataset" but provides no evidence for other domains.  
- **Why Unresolved**: Without cross-domain validation, it is unclear if the recursive expansion tree consistently produces high-quality criteria for diverse question types or if it overfits to specific contexts like healthcare.  
- **What Evidence Would Resolve It**: Apply Qworld to a diverse suite of benchmarks (e.g., coding tasks, essay evaluation, ethics scenarios) and measure criteria quality via expert validation, coverage of human-authored rubrics, and impact on model rankings compared to existing benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What are the computational overhead and scalability limits of the recursive expansion tree, and how do they trade off with criteria quality?  
- **Basis in Paper**: The paper details the algorithm but does not report runtime, memory usage, or sensitivity to expansion parameters (*w*₁, *w*₂, *w*₃). Scaling tests only vary the generator model strength, not algorithmic complexity.  
- **Why Unresolved**: For practical deployment on large datasets or real-time evaluation, efficiency is critical. The recursive process may become prohibitively expensive for complex questions or high-volume benchmarks.  
- **What Evidence Would Resolve It**: Empirical benchmarks measuring time and resource consumption across varying question complexities, dataset sizes, and expansion depths, correlated with quality metrics like Coverage and Uniqueness to identify efficiency-quality Pareto frontiers.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How consistent and reliable are Qworld-generated criteria across different LLM generators, random seeds, or prompt variations?  
- **Basis in Paper**: The paper tests robustness only by varying the judger model, not the generator. It uses a single backbone (GPT-4.1) for all generation, except a small ablation with the GPT-4.1 family. No analysis of inter-run consistency or generator diversity is provided.  
- **Why Unresolved**: Criteria variability could undermine evaluation fairness—different runs might yield divergent criteria sets for the same question, affecting model scores and rankings.  
- **What Evidence Would Resolve It**: Generate criteria for a fixed question set using multiple generator models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Llama) and multiple seeds, then compute agreement metrics (e.g., Jaccard similarity, semantic entailment) between criteria sets; assess impact on downstream model evaluation stability.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do Qworld’s "novel" criteria (high Uniqueness) consistently align with human expert judgment on value and relevance, or do they introduce noise or spurious requirements?  
- **Basis in Paper**: The paper reports 79% Uniqueness and human ratings of Insight (0.83) and Granularity (0.85) for generated criteria, but human evaluation for Value is limited: "one evaluator rated 5 randomly chosen unique criteria per question across 5 questions" (extremely small sample). Also, Uniqueness is judged by an LLM-as-a-judge for semantic coverage.  
- **Why Unresolved**: High Uniqueness might capture irrelevant or trivial aspects not flagged by the small human sample. The paper shows value ratings remain high as criteria count increases (Figure 5), but this is based on a single evaluator and few questions.  
- **What Evidence Would Resolve It**: Conduct a large-scale human study where multiple experts rate the Value (importance) of a broad sample of Qworld’s unique criteria across many questions, checking for correlation with automated Uniqueness scores and downstream evaluation discriminability.

## Limitations
- **Data Accessibility**: Exact formats and access procedures for HealthBench (1K/5K splits) and Humanity's Last Exam are unspecified; repository may not include data loaders.
- **Judge Implementation**: Details for the GPT-4.1 judge used in Coverage/Uniqueness evaluation (prompts, parsing logic, scoring thresholds) are not fully documented, risking inconsistent replication.
- **Generalization Boundaries**: Break conditions for recursive expansion (e.g., ambiguous questions, excessive redundancy) are theoretical; empirical thresholds for *w*₁, *w*₂ across diverse domains are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Coverage & Uniqueness (89%/79%)**: Medium. Specific metrics are reported, but confidence is tempered by unknown judge robustness and data access barriers.
- **Human-Rated Insight/Granularity (0.83/0.85)**: High. Direct human evaluation provides strong evidence, though sample size and rater demographics are unspecified.
- **Ranking Changes & Saturation Reduction (~20%)**: Medium. Results depend on correct scoring implementation and faithful application of criteria to 11 models; potential normalization issues.
