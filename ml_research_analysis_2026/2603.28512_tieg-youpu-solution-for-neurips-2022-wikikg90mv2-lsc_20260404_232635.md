# TIEG-Youpu Solution for NeurIPS 2022 WikiKG90Mv2-LSC

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28512
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28512
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: Improved validation MRR@10 from 0.2342 to 0.2839 and achieved 0.2309 MRR on test-challenge set.

## Executive Summary
The solution addresses large-scale knowledge graph completion for WikiKG90Mv2, a dataset with over 91 million entities and 600 million triples. A retrieve‑then‑re‑rank pipeline was developed. The retrieval stage combines structural path‑based models (two PIE variants and 11 handcrafted path rules) with semantic similarity from MPNet embeddings, merged via a priority infilling ensemble that orders candidate lists by validation accuracy and collects up to 20,000 candidates per query. Re‑ranking uses neighbor‑enhanced entity representations (averaging MPNet vectors of first‑order neighbors) as initialization for KG embedding models (TransE, ComplEx, NOTE). A two‑step ensemble—greedy model selection followed by grid‑search weighting—produces the final prediction. On the validation set, MRR@10 increased from a baseline 0.2342 to 0.2839; the test‑challenge MRR@10 was 0.2309.

## Method Summary
Retrieval: Two PIE models (neighbor sample sizes 6 and 10) and 11 structural path rules (e.g., HT, RT‑HR‑RT) generate candidate tails from the training graph. Semantic retrieval uses pre‑trained MPNet embeddings with a FIASS index (product quantization; 64 centroids, 64‑bit codes) to find the 1,000 nearest neighbours per query.

Priority infilling: The four retrieval models are ranked by validation accuracy (|S_dev ∩ S_m| / |S_m|). For each query, candidate lists are merged in that order, adding unique entities until 20,000 candidates are collected.

Re‑ranking: Each entity’s structural embedding is the sum of MPNet vectors of its immediate training neighbors; this may replace or complement random or pure MPNet initialization. TransE, ComplEx, and NOTE are trained with three initialization types (random, MPNet, neighbor‑enhanced) on the candidate triples. A two‑step ensemble then selects a model subset via greedy forward selection on validation MRR@10 and optimises linear combination weights by grid search; the weighted scores yield the final ranking.

- Validation MRR@10: 0.2839 (baseline 0.2342)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Priority Infilling Ensemble**  
- **Claim:** The priority infilling ensemble improves candidate recall by merging structurally and semantically retrieved lists in order of validation accuracy, ensuring high‑quality entities are retained early.  
- **Mechanism:** For each query (h, r), candidate lists from multiple retrieval models are merged sequentially based on each model’s accuracy on the validation set (|S_dev ∩ S_m| / |S_m|). The process stops once a fixed candidate size (N=20,000) is reached, prioritizing higher‑accuracy models.  
- **Core assumption:** A model’s validation accuracy reliably predicts its ability to retrieve correct tails for unseen test queries.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “priority infilling ensemble”; Section 2.3 defines accuracy(m) and the merging process.  
- **Break condition:** If the validation set distribution diverges from test distribution, the model priority ordering may become misleading.

**Mechanism 2: Neighbor‑Enhanced Entity Representations**  
- **Claim:** Aggregating first‑order neighbours’ MPNet embeddings injects local graph structure into entity embeddings, improving re‑ranking quality.  
- **Mechanism:** Each entity eₓ’s structural embedding is e_structₓ = Σ_{e_t ∈ N(eₓ)} e_MPNet_t. This representation replaces or complements random/text embeddings during KG model training.  
- **Core assumption:** Entities connected by first‑order relations share semantic or functional similarity, so aggregated neighbour embeddings capture latent structural roles.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract refers to “neighbor enhanced entity representations”; Section 2.4 presents Equation (1) defining the aggregation.  
- **Break condition:** Noisy or extremely sparse neighbour sets may dilute the signal.

**Mechanism 3: Two‑Step Ensemble of Heterogeneous KG Embeddings**  
- **Claim:** A two‑step ensemble (greedy model selection, then grid search on weights) of diverse KG embedding models leverages complementary strengths across relation patterns.  
- **Mechanism:** First, a greedy filter selects a subset of models based on validation MRR@10. Second, grid search optimises linear combination weights for the selected models’ scores.  
- **Core assumption:** Different embedding architectures have non‑perfectly correlated errors and excel at different relation types.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes “ensemble based re‑ranking model” and “two step ensemble”; Section 2.4 describes the selection and weighting.  
- **Break condition:** If all candidate models fail similarly on certain relations, ensemble gains are limited.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Retrieval models (PIE variants, 11 structural path rules, MPNet k‑NN) → Priority Infilling Ensemble → Candidate set (20 000 entities per query) → Neighbor‑enhanced Encoder → KG Embedding Models (TransE, ComplEx, NOTE) → Two‑step Ensemble → Final ranking.

**Critical path:** The pipeline flows as follows: retrieval outputs → priority infilling → candidate set → neighbor‑enhanced embeddings computed → each KG model scores candidates → selected models’ scores weighted → final ranking.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Retrieval recall vs. speed: more models/longer lists improve recall but increase re‑ranking compute; priority infilling tries to maximise quality within fixed budget.
- Embedding dimensionality: higher dimensions capture more signal but increase memory/training time; NOTE uses 200‑dim with orthogonal constraints.
- Model diversity vs. overhead: training many KG model variants is costly but necessary for ensemble gains.

**Failure signatures:**
- Drop in validation MRR@10 after priority infilling: likely incorrect model accuracy estimates or non‑i.i.d. splits.
- Neighbor‑enhanced embeddings perform worse than random: indicates neighbour noise dominates signal (e.g., hub entities).
- Ensemble weights collapse to a single model: suggests models are highly correlated or one dominates all relation types.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Ablate priority infilling: Replace ordered merging with uniform union or random ordering while keeping candidate size 20 000; measure MRR@10 to verify ordering benefit.
2. Ablate neighbor‑enhanced initialization: For each KG model type (TransE, ComplEx, NOTE), train three variants with random, MPNet‑only, and neighbor‑enhanced initializations; compare validation MRR to isolate neighbour contribution.
3. Verify model complementarity: Compute pairwise correlation of validation prediction scores among the selected KG models; low correlation justifies ensemble weighting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does the priority infilling ensemble method compare to alternative ensemble techniques (e.g., weighted averaging, learning‑to‑rank) in terms of recall efficiency and robustness for large‑scale KG retrieval?  
*Basis:* The paper introduces priority infilling based on validation accuracy but does not benchmark against other strategies or analyse sensitivity to model priority.  
*Why unresolved:* No comparative experiments or theoretical justification; effectiveness may be dataset‑specific.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Systematic evaluation of multiple ensemble methods on WikiKG90Mv2 and diverse KGs, measuring recall@K, computational overhead, and stability.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent do neighbor‑enhanced entity representations improve re‑ranking performance across different KG embedding models, and what architectural factors mediate this effect?  
*Basis:* Neighbor aggregation is proposed, but Table 2 shows inconsistent gains (e.g., TransE‑3 underperforms TransE‑1) with no analysis of why models respond differently.  
*Why unresolved:* Lacks ablation isolating neighbour contribution per model; interactions with model‑specific scoring functions are unexplored.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Controlled experiments varying aggregation schemes for each model, tracking changes in validation MRR and embedding space properties.

**Open Question 3:** What intrinsic properties of the WikiKG90Mv2 dataset cause TransE to outperform more expressive models like ComplEx and NOTE, despite their theoretical advantages for complex relations?  
*Basis:* Table 2 reveals TransE achieves the highest MRR@10, contradicting expectations; the paper notes this surprise but offers no investigation.  
*Why unresolved:* No analysis of relation type distributions or entity degree skew that might favour TransE’s simplicity; potential dataset biases unexamined.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Statistical profiling of relational patterns, coupled with synthetic ablation where relation types are controlled.

**Open Question 4:** Can the retrieve‑then‑re‑rank pipeline’s computational cost be reduced for real‑time applications while preserving accuracy, and what are the primary bottlenecks?  
*Basis:* The solution prioritises accuracy but acknowledges efficiency challenges; no runtime or memory metrics are reported, and future work vaguely mentions improving retrieval efficiency.  
*Why unresolved:* Absence of empirical cost‑benefit analysis prevents understanding scalability; trade‑offs between candidate set size, model complexity, and speed are unquantified.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Comprehensive benchmarking of pipeline components on standardised hardware, identifying slowest stages, and testing optimisations like candidate pruning or model distillation.

## Limitations
- Incomplete methodological details (PIE training specifics, greedy model selection logic, grid search parameters, distributed handling of 91 M entities) hinder faithful reproduction.
- Core assumptions—validation accuracy as a proxy for retrieval quality, neighbour‑averaged MPNet embeddings capturing structural roles, and heterogeneity of KG model errors—lack independent validation and may fail under distribution shift or noisy neighbours.
- No comparative evaluation of alternative ensemble methods or analysis of why TransE outperformed more expressive models limits generalisability and understanding of dataset‑specific factors.
