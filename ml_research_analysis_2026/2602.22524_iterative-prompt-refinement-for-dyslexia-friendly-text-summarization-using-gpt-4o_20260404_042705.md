# Iterative Prompt Refinement for Dyslexia-Friendly Text Summarization Using GPT-4o

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.22524
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22524
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: The iterative GPT-4o pipeline produced dyslexia-friendly summaries (Flesch Reading Ease ≥90) for most news articles within four attempts, with an optimal trade‑off at two iterations.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the barrier of linguistic complexity for dyslexic readers by developing an iterative prompt refinement pipeline using GPT-4o to generate dyslexia‑friendly summaries. The pipeline starts with an explicit accessibility‑constrained prompt, then measures the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) of each generated summary; if FRE falls below 90, a corrective prompt referencing the score is issued to further simplify the text. Applied to 2,000 news articles, the method achieved the target FRE for most summaries within four iterations, with a composite score (balancing readability and BERTScore‑based semantic fidelity) averaging 0.55. Analysis shows that two iterations provide the optimal trade‑off, as additional iterations yield diminishing readability gains but erode semantic fidelity. The authors note the need for human‑subject studies to confirm real‑world accessibility benefits and for domain generalization beyond news articles.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates an iterative refinement approach on a random sample of 2,000 articles from the CNN/DailyMail dataset. Reference summaries following dyslexia‑friendly guidelines are generated automatically (since human references are unreleased) using a detailed prompt. The pipeline employs GPT‑4o with an initial prompt that instructs short sentences (≤15 words), common words, active voice, and avoidance of jargon. For each article, the system generates a summary, computes its FRE, and if below 90 (and fewer than four attempts have been made), issues a corrective prompt that includes the score and requests further simplification. This loop repeats up to four times. After termination, the final summary is evaluated with BERTScore against the reference, and a composite score (0.5×FRE/100 + 0.5×BERTScore F1) is computed. The analysis focuses on the distribution of attempts needed to reach FRE≥90, final composite scores, and the trade‑off between readability and fidelity across iterations.

## Key Results
- Achieved FRE ≥90 for most summaries within four attempts.
- Composite score (readability + semantic fidelity) averaged 0.55 (range 0.13–0.73).
- Two refinement iterations offer optimal balance; further iterations improve readability but degrade semantic fidelity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Iterative refinement reliably yields FRE ≥90 summaries within four attempts.  
**Mechanism**: Generate summary → compute FRE → if <90, append corrective prompt referencing the score and requesting simplification (shorten sentences, replace difficult words) → repeat.  
**Core assumption**: GPT‑4o can interpret numeric readability scores and follow corrective instructions without retraining.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract states the threshold was achieved for most summaries within four attempts; Section 3.3 details the generate‑score‑refine loop and corrective prompt format.  
**Break condition**: Loop exits when FRE ≥90 or after four iterations.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Explicit accessibility constraints and few‑shot examples in the prompt produce dyslexia‑friendly output from the first attempt, reducing iterations.  
**Mechanism**: Initial prompt includes “Use short sentences (under 15 words), common everyday words, and active voice. Avoid jargon and complex clauses.” An ablation adds few‑shot examples. These constraints embed dyslexia‑friendly principles directly.  
**Core assumption**: GPT‑4o can follow these constraints in a few‑shot setting without fine‑tuning.  
**Evidence anchors**: Contributions list “explicit accessibility constraints, few‑shot dyslexia‑friendly examples”; Section 3.3 gives the exact initial prompt and mentions the few‑shot condition.  
**Break condition**: If the initial summary already meets FRE ≥90, no further iteration occurs.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Two refinement iterations are optimal; additional iterations marginally improve readability but degrade semantic fidelity.  
**Mechanism**: Composite score (0.5×FRE/100 + 0.5×BERTScore) jointly optimizes readability and fidelity. Data show summaries needing three or four attempts have lower composite scores, indicating semantic drift outweighs readability gains beyond iteration two.  
**Core assumption**: The composite score is a valid proxy for overall dyslexia‑friendly quality, and the observed decline is due to over‑simplification.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract: “Analysis indicates two refinement iterations represent the optimal trade‑off before semantic fidelity declines.” Section 4.4 notes lower composite scores for 3–4 attempts; Section 5 reiterates two iterations as practical optimum.  
**Break condition**: A dynamic stopping criterion should halt when the composite score stops increasing (or after two iterations) to avoid diminishing returns.

## Foundational Learning
**Flesch Reading Ease**  
*Why needed*: Primary readability metric defining the dyslexia‑friendly target (≥90). Its calculation (sentence length, syllable count) drives the iterative loop.  
*Quick check*: How is FRE computed, and what textual features correspond to a high score (e.g., 90+)?  

**Prompt Engineering for Large Language Models**  
*Why needed*: The method relies on prompts that steer GPT‑4o to simplify text without retraining. Knowledge of zero/few‑shot prompting and instruction following is essential.  
*Quick check*: What specific phrases in the initial prompt enforce short sentences, common words, active voice, and avoidance of jargon? How could you modify the prompt to increase simplicity constraints?  

**Summarization Evaluation Metrics (BERTScore, ROUGE)**  
*Why needed*: Semantic fidelity is measured by BERTScore and ROUGE against human references, informing the composite score and trade‑off analysis.  
*Quick check*: How does BERTScore differ from ROUGE in assessing summary quality? What are the advantages and limitations of each in the dyslexia‑friendly context?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Loader -> Prompt Manager -> Generator -> Readability Scorer -> Iteration Controller  
Iteration Controller --(if FRE < 90 && attempts < 4)--> Prompt Manager  
Iteration Controller --(upon termination)--> Composite Scorer -> Metrics Calculator -> Output Store

**Critical path**
1. Load article and reference summary.  
2. Prompt Manager builds initial accessibility‑constrained prompt.  
3. Generator returns draft summary.  
4. Readability Scorer computes FRE.  
5. If FRE ≥ 90 or max attempts reached, exit loop; else Prompt Manager creates corrective prompt (includes current score and simplification directives), increment attempts, go to step 3.  
6. After exit, Composite Scorer computes final composite; optionally Metrics Calculator adds ROUGE/BLEU.  
7. Output Store persists final summary, scores, and iteration metadata.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Fixed iteration cap (4) vs. dynamic stopping based on composite trend: cap simplifies but may waste compute or stop before optimum.
- Composite weighting: equal (0.5/0.5) vs. favoring readability (e.g., 0.7/0.3) reflects priorities; choice affects optimal iteration count.
- Prompt prescriptiveness: more constraints reduce iterations but risk over‑constraining and unnatural text.
- Model selection: GPT‑4o offers strong instruction following but higher cost/latency; smaller models trade off reliability.
- Evaluation: automated metrics are cheap but may not capture dyslexic user experience; human evaluation remains essential.

**Failure signatures**
- FRE never reaches threshold after 4 iterations → source text too complex or prompt ineffective.
- Composite score declines after iteration 2 while FRE continues to improve → semantic drift; stop earlier.
- High variance in composite scores across articles → inconsistent pipeline behavior.
- Generated summary contains factual inconsistencies → check generator temperature or prompt clarity.
- Long tail of low FRE even after 4 iterations → may need more aggressive constraints (risk fidelity).

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Baseline validation**: Run pipeline with zero iterations (only initial prompt) on 100 articles; confirm average FRE well below 90, showing constraints alone are insufficient.
2. **Iteration distribution**: Run full pipeline (max 4) on same 100 articles; plot frequency of attempts needed to hit FRE≥90; verify bimodal pattern (peaks at 1 and 3).
3. **Composite score trend**: Compute composite at each iteration; plot average composite vs. iteration; check that composite peaks at iteration 2 and declines. Test alternative weightings (e.g., 0.6/0.4) to observe optimum shifts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Do automated readability improvements translate into measurable comprehension benefits for dyslexic readers? Human‑subject evaluation is an essential next step.
2. What specific linguistic characteristics of source articles determine whether a summary achieves FRE≥90 in one iteration versus requiring three or more? The bimodal pattern is noted but not analyzed.
3. Does the accessibility‑driven prompt engineering generalize to domains beyond news articles (e.g., educational, legal, clinical texts)? Current evaluation is limited to CNN/DailyMail.
4. Would a dynamic stopping criterion based on the composite score reduce semantic drift and improve overall quality compared to the fixed four‑iteration cap? The paper suggests this as future work.

## Limitations
- Reference summaries are not publicly released; automatically generated proxies may bias semantic fidelity metrics.
- No human evaluation with dyslexic participants; actual comprehension and reading experience impacts are unknown.
- Prompt details (exact wording, use of few‑shot examples) are underspecified and results are tied to GPT‑4o, limiting reproducibility and generalizability.
