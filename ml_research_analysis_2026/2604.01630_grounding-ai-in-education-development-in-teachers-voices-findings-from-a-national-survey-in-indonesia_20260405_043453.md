# Grounding AI-in-Education Development in Teachers' Voices: Findings from a National Survey in Indonesia

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01630
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01630
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** Indonesian K-12 teachers use AI mainly to reduce instructional preparation workload (particularly for assessment and lesson planning); adoption varies by school level, region, and experience, with key challenges of generic outputs (37%), infrastructure constraints (35.1%), and over-reliance concerns (33.1%).

## Executive Summary
This study surveyed 349 Indonesian K-12 teachers to examine AI adoption patterns, needs, and challenges. Results indicate that teachers primarily use AI to reduce instructional preparation workload, especially for assessment and lesson planning. Adoption is uneven, with elementary teachers and those in Eastern Indonesia using AI more frequently and valuing it more highly. Key challenges include generic AI outputs (37%), infrastructure constraints (35.1%), and concerns about over-reliance (33.1%). The findings highlight the need for context-aware, offline-capable AI systems that maintain teacher agency and align with local curricula.  
These insights are critical for developing tailored AI tools and shaping policies that support effective, equitable integration of AI in Indonesia’s diverse classrooms.

## Method Summary
The study employed a cross-sectional online survey of 349 in-service K-12 teachers across Indonesia, recruited via non-probability channels (education offices, teacher networks, social media) to capture diverse instructional domains and school levels. The Indonesian-language survey, developed by an expert panel, measured AI usage frequency and satisfaction across six teaching components, priority needs, perceived challenges, and demographics. Data were collected over four weeks in 2025 using a low-bandwidth platform.  
Analysis involved rescaling Likert responses to 0‑1, computing mean frequency and satisfaction for each component, generating subgroup deviation tables (by school level, region, experience, etc.), and applying Wilcoxon signed‑rank tests to compare usage versus satisfaction (p < 0.05).

## Key Results
- Adoption varies: elementary teachers use AI more consistently than senior high teachers; mid‑career teachers and those in Eastern Indonesia assign higher importance.
- Primary use is workload reduction: teachers mainly employ AI for assessment and lesson planning, with top needs being assessment item generation (72.2%) and lesson plan/syllabus design (69.6%).
- Major challenges: overly generic AI outputs (37%), infrastructure constraints (35.1%), and concerns about over‑reliance (33.1%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Workload Reduction Driver
- **Claim:** AI adoption is primarily driven by the need to reduce instructional preparation workload.
- **Mechanism:** AI tools act as productivity assistants for content creation (lesson plans, assessments, materials).
- **Core assumption:** Teachers in high‑workload, resource‑constrained settings prioritize efficiency over pedagogical novelty.
- **Evidence:** 72.2% need assessment item generation; 69.6% need lesson plan/syllabi design; similar findings in Brazilian and African contexts.
- **Break condition:** If AI use shifts to real‑time student interaction or adaptive pedagogy, this mechanism weakens.

### Mechanism 2: Infrastructure‑Need Paradox
- **Claim:** Uneven adoption across school levels and regions is partially caused by differential access to infrastructure and differing perceptions of AI’s value as a resource multiplier.
- **Mechanism:** Teachers in Eastern Indonesia (with poorer infrastructure) assign higher importance to AI, possibly viewing it as a critical resource multiplier, while senior high teachers engage less, perhaps due to curriculum rigidity or high‑stakes assessment concerns.
- **Core assumption:** Infrastructure constraints do not uniformly suppress AI interest; they may amplify perceived need where traditional resources are scarcest.
- **Evidence:** Infrastructure constraints are a top challenge (35.1%); teachers in Eastern Indonesia show higher AI use and importance despite lower infrastructure levels.
- **Break condition:** If controlled studies show school‑level/region effects disappear when accounting for school funding and connectivity, the mechanism reduces to pure access.

### Mechanism 3: Generic Outputs = Contextual Mismatch
- **Claim:** “Overly generic AI outputs” (37%) stem from misalignment between globally trained models and Indonesia’s specific pedagogical, linguistic, and cultural context.
- **Mechanism:** AI systems lack fine‑tuning on Indonesian curriculum standards, local examples, multilingual classroom dynamics (Bahasa Indonesia vs. regional languages), and socio‑cultural references, producing unusable output without significant modification.
- **Core assumption:** Generic outputs are a function of contextual mismatch, not just model capability.
- **Evidence:** Generic outputs are a top challenge (37%); conclusion calls for context‑aware, pedagogically grounded AI; similar complaints arise in Brazilian, Norwegian, and African contexts.
- **Break condition:** If localized fine‑tuning or RAG with Indonesian educational materials drastically reduces generic output complaints, the mechanism is confirmed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Survey methodology and non‑probability sampling biases:** Understanding these biases is crucial because the study’s findings rely on a convenience sample of 349 teachers, which may not represent the entire Indonesian teacher population. Quick check: How might self‑selection bias inflate estimates of AI adoption and need?
- **AI tool taxonomy for educational functions:** Mapping which AI tool serves which teaching component is essential for designing targeted systems that align with teachers’ needs. Quick check: Why might language teachers show the lowest AI usage given the prominence of LLMs and translation tools?
- **Contextual factors in EdTech adoption (infrastructure, policy, linguistic diversity):** Indonesia’s archipelagic nature, connectivity variation, and multilingualism actively shape AI utility; systems must account for these contextual nuances. Quick check: How would the top need for assessment item generation differ in a context with high‑stakes, standardized national exams versus more flexible local assessment?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
- **User Profiler:** Captures teacher attributes (school level, subject, region, experience) to adapt system behavior.
- **Task Engine:** Implements the 6 teaching components (CKD, Pedagogy, A&E, CD, TM, PP) as distinct workflows.
- **Tool Router:** Selects appropriate AI tool type (LLM, translation, image gen) per task, respecting the study’s taxonomy.
- **Context Injector:** Injects localization data (Indonesian curriculum, regional examples, Bahasa/regional language norms) to combat generic outputs.
- **Connectivity Adapter:** Manages online/offline modes, bandwidth‑aware model serving, and local caching for infrastructure‑constrained settings.
- **Interaction Guardrails:** Implements features to mitigate over‑reliance (e.g., mandatory teacher review steps, source citation, confidence flags).

**Critical Path:** Teacher profile + selected task → Context Injector (adds local curriculum/linguistic layer) → Tool Router (calls appropriate model) → Output post‑processor (adds citations, flags uncertainty) → Connectivity Adapter (delivers) → Interaction Guardrails enforce review.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Localization depth vs. Development cost:** Fully localized models for every region/grade are infeasible; trade‑off between fine‑tuned models for major regions/subjects vs. single base model with heavy RAG.
- **Cloud features vs. Offline reliability:** Advanced video generation requires cloud, but infrastructure constraints demand offline text/assessment tools; trade‑off is robust offline‑first core with optional cloud plugins.
- **Automation vs. Teacher Agency:** High automation reduces workload but risks over‑reliance; design for “human‑in‑the‑loop” by default (AI drafts, teacher edits) with efficiency metrics tracking prep time reduction.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Low adoption in senior high schools if system feels geared toward elementary activities.
- High dissatisfaction in urban private schools if system is only optimized for low‑bandwidth, elementary needs, ignoring advanced cloud‑based media tools.
- Persistent “generic output” complaints if Context Injector is weak or curriculum data is stale.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Assessment Item Generator Prototype:** Build a constrained LLM prompt pipeline that takes a topic (e.g., “Pancasila for 5th grade”) and outputs 5 multiple‑choice items with Indonesian‑specific distractors and references to Kurikulum Merdeka. Measure teacher‑rated usability vs. generic ChatGPT output.
2. **Bandwidth‑Switcher Test:** Simulate 2G/3G/offline conditions. Measure task completion rate and time for “generate lesson plan outline” using a quantized local LLM vs. a cloud API. Validate offline mode meets minimum utility threshold for basic text tasks.
3. **Regional Localization A/B Test:** For “teaching media” tasks (image generation), compare a baseline DALL‑E prompt with a version that appends “in a Javanese traditional classroom setting” or “using a Minangkabau rumah gadang example.” Measure perceived relevance and cultural appropriateness from teachers in those regions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific contextual factors (e.g., local curriculum standards, cultural norms, linguistic diversity) must AI tools incorporate to generate pedagogically relevant and non‑generic outputs for Indonesian classrooms, and how can these be operationalized in AI design?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states that “overly generic feedback” and “limited contextual alignment” hinder integration, calling for “AI systems that are more context aware and pedagogically grounded.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies generic outputs as a top challenge (37%) but does not explore which contextual dimensions are most critical or how to embed them computationally.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mixed‑methods studies with Indonesian teachers to define and prioritize contextual requirements, followed by technical prototyping and classroom testing of context‑enhanced AI outputs.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can AI systems be engineered to function reliably in Indonesia’s low‑bandwidth and offline environments, particularly in Eastern Indonesia and rural areas, without compromising core educational functionalities?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion notes “infrastructure constraints” (35.1%) as a major challenge and recommends “lightweight and offline tolerant designs suited to uneven connectivity.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper highlights infrastructure as a barrier but provides no technical specifications or evaluation of existing offline‑capable AI tools in Indonesian school contexts.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and field deployment of lightweight AI models or hybrid systems (e.g., on‑device processing, cached resources) with iterative feedback from teachers in connectivity‑limited regions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the underlying reasons for the uneven AI adoption patterns observed—such as higher use by elementary teachers and teachers in Eastern Indonesia, and lower use by senior high teachers and those with >15 years experience—and what targeted interventions could address these disparities?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 1 and Figure 1 show significant differences in AI use frequency by school level, region, and experience, but the paper does not investigate causal factors (e.g., training access, institutional support, perceived relevance).
- **Why unresolved:** The survey documents correlations but lacks qualitative insights into motivations, barriers, or socio‑institutional dynamics driving these patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Focus groups or in‑depth interviews with representative teachers from each subgroup to uncover barriers and incentives, complemented by policy analysis of school‑level support structures.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can AI tools be designed to mitigate teacher concerns about over‑reliance (33.1%) while still effectively supporting workload reduction in tasks like assessment and lesson planning, promoting critical human oversight?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states that concerns about over‑reliance “call for AI tools that support appropriate human–AI interaction when used to assist learning.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies over‑reliance as a top challenge but does not propose design principles or interaction paradigms that balance assistance with teacher agency.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design‑based research creating and testing AI interfaces with features like transparency (e.g., showing AI reasoning), adjustable autonomy, and reflection prompts, measuring effects on teacher dependency and pedagogical decision‑making.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What are the impacts of AI‑assisted instructional preparation (e.g., generating assessment items, lesson plans) on teaching efficiency, teacher professional development, and student learning outcomes in Indonesian K–12 education?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper details high demand for AI in these areas (72.2% for assessment, 69.6% for lesson plans) but does not examine consequences for teaching quality, time reallocation, or student performance.
- **Why unresolved:** The study focuses on adoption needs and challenges, not on measuring educational impacts; thus, benefits or drawbacks remain speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal mixed‑methods studies tracking classrooms using AI for these tasks, comparing metrics like teacher workload, instructional time, pedagogical innovation, and student achievement against control groups.

## Limitations
- Non‑probability convenience sample (349 teachers) raises self‑selection bias concerns, limiting generalizability to the broader Indonesian teacher population.
- Exact survey instrument details (items, Likert scales, attention checks) are unspecified, potentially compromising measurement reliability.
- Cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference about adoption drivers (e.g., infrastructure vs. perceived value).

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Workload Reduction Driver): High
- Mechanism 2 (Infrastructure‑Need Paradox): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (Generic Outputs = Contextual Mismatch): Medium
