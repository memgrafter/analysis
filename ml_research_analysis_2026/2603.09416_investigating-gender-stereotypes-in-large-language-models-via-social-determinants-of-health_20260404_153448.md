# Investigating Gender Stereotypes in Large Language Models via Social Determinants of Health

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09416
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09416
- **Reference Count:** 13
- **Primary Result:** All evaluated LLMs exhibited statistically significant gender stereotypes when predicting gender from neutralized Social Determinants of Health (SDoH) data, with smaller models and medically-adapted variants showing stronger bias.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces a novel probing framework to assess gender bias in Large Language Models (LLMs) within healthcare contexts. Instead of relying on textual cues, the authors task LLMs with predicting patient gender from structured, neutralized Social Determinants of Health (SDoH) data extracted from French clinical notes. This isolates stereotyping by removing linguistic gender markers. Findings reveal that all tested models, including medically-adapted ones, display significant and directional gender bias—measured via a modified Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)—based on stereotypical SDoH associations (e.g., certain occupations or marital statuses linked to a specific gender). Smaller models exhibited stronger bias, and medical fine-tuning amplified existing stereotypes. Human annotators showed similar patterns, indicating shared societal biases. The work demonstrates that evaluating bias through SDoH interactions offers a critical, context-specific complement to existing LLM fairness benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The study uses 958 anonymized French clinical notes with manually annotated SDoH (14 categories). These annotations are transformed into neutralized key-value pairs (e.g., gender-inclusive occupation terms) to eliminate linguistic cues. Nine instruction-tuned LLMs (six base, three medically-adapted) are prompted in French to predict gender on a 7-point Likert scale (1: definitely female, 4: neutral, 7: definitely male). The primary bias metric is a modified RMSE that captures both magnitude and directional deviation from the neutral point (4). Secondary analysis binarizes predictions and uses one-tailed Fisher’s exact tests to identify statistically significant associations between specific SDoH values (e.g., Occupation=Worker) and gendered outputs. Human annotators performed the same task on a 100-example subset for comparison. All decoding parameters were fixed, and each model was run three times for stability.

## Key Results
- All evaluated LLMs showed statistically significant gender stereotyping, with modified RMSE scores significantly above zero.
- Smaller models (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B) exhibited stronger bias than their larger counterparts.
- Medically-adapted models (e.g., Med42, OpenBioLLM) demonstrated higher bias than their base versions.
- Key SDoH driving bias included Occupation (e.g., "Worker"→male, "Employee"→female), Marital status (Married/In relationship), and Substance use (Tobacco/Alcohol linked to male).
- Human annotators displayed similar profession-based stereotype patterns, aligning with model behaviors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLMs infer gender from stereotypical associations between SDoH features and gender, even when linguistic cues are removed.
- **Mechanism:** Neutralized SDoH inputs activate latent representations where certain SDoH values (e.g., Occupation=Worker, MaritalStatus=Married) correlate with gendered token probabilities, steering the model’s output along the Likert scale away from neutral (4). This reflects training-data correlations where societal stereotypes link demographics to social roles.
- **Core assumption:** Stereotypes are encoded in model weights and can be probed by isolating SDoH from linguistic gender markers.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "embedded stereotypes can be probed using SDoH input and that LLMs rely on embedded stereotypes to make gendered decisions"
  - [section 3.2] Association analysis shows statistically significant odds ratios (e.g., Workers→Male, Employees→Female) across models.
  - [corpus] Neighbors focus on SDoH extraction, not bias probing; corpus evidence for stereotype mechanisms is indirect.
- **Break condition:** If neutralized SDoH inputs yielded predictions at the neutral mean (4) with no directional deviation, the mechanism would fail. Table 1 shows neutralization reduces but does not eliminate bias, supporting the mechanism.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Smaller LLMs exhibit stronger gender stereotypical bias from SDoH than larger counterparts.
- **Mechanism:** Reduced parameter counts constrain representational capacity, making smaller models more reliant on high-correlation heuristics (stereotypes) rather than nuanced integration of SDoH interactions. This manifests as higher modified RMSE scores (greater deviation from 4) and more consistent directional predictions.
- **Core assumption:** Model scale inversely correlates with reliance on simplistic stereotypical patterns in this probing task.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Smaller models showed stronger bias."
  - [section 3.1] Llama-3.1-8B (2.25) vs. Llama-3.3-70B (1.65); Qwen2.5-7B vs. 72B show similar size-bias trend.
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; neighboring papers examine SDoH extraction, not scale-bias relationships.
- **Break condition:** If larger models showed equal or greater bias, the scale-dependency hypothesis would be falsified. Results consistently show smaller models with higher scores.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Medical domain fine-tuning amplifies existing gender stereotypes compared to base models.
- **Mechanism:** Adaptation datasets (medical notes) contain skewed SDoH-gender distributions reflective of real-world societal biases (e.g., certain occupations gendered in clinical texts). Continued training reinforces these correlations, increasing the magnitude of stereotypical associations as measured by modified RMSE.
- **Core assumption:** Domain-specific fine-tuning on biased data exacerbates rather than mitigates latent stereotypes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Medically-adapted models demonstrated higher bias than their base versions."
  - [section 3.1] Med42 (from Llama3-70B) and OpenBioLLM show higher bias scores than their bases.
  - [corpus] Neighbors discuss SDoH extraction in medical contexts but do not address fine-tuning bias amplification; evidence is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If medical fine-tuning reduced bias scores relative to base models, this mechanism would not hold. Results show the opposite.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Social Determinants of Health (SDoH)**
  - Why needed: The entire probing framework uses structured SDoH data (e.g., Occupation, MaritalStatus) as input to isolate gender-stereotype associations. Understanding SDoH categories and their societal gendered associations is essential to interpret results.
  - Quick check: What are three SDoH categories from the paper that showed significant associations with gender predictions?
- **Concept: Probing via Regression on a Likert Scale**
  - Why needed: The task frames gender prediction as a 7-point regression problem, allowing quantification of bias direction (sign of error) and magnitude (RMSE). This design converts categorical stereotypes into a continuous bias metric.
  - Quick check: Why was a 7-point scale chosen instead of binary classification, and what does a prediction of "1" signify?
- **Concept: Data Neutralization for Causal Probing**
  - Why needed: To ensure predictions stem from SDoH associations and not from grammatical gender in French text, the authors neutralized values (e.g., "infirmier/infirmière"). This is a causal intervention to isolate the mechanism of interest.
  - Quick check: How was the Occupation SDoH neutralized, and what trade-off did the authors acknowledge?
- **Concept: Association Analysis via Fisher’s Exact Test**
  - Why needed: To identify which specific SDoH options (e.g., "Worker", "Married") drive bias, the paper binarizes predictions and tests for statistically significant enrichment with male/female predictions.
  - Quick check: What is the null hypothesis in the one-tailed Fisher’s exact test used for SDoH-profession associations?
- **Concept: Modified RMSE for Bias Magnitude**
  - Why needed: Standard RMSE measures overall error but does not preserve directional bias. The modified version weights larger deviations more heavily and retains the sign of the mean error to indicate bias direction.
  - Quick check: What does a positive modified RMSE score indicate about the model’s gender stereotyping?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Preprocessor -> Prompt Template -> Model Inference Engine -> Bias Scorer -> Association Analyzer
- **Critical path:** Raw clinical notes → Neutralized SDoH key-value string → French prompt injection → Model generation (fixed decoding) → Numeric response extraction (regex) → Modified RMSE computation / SDoH association heatmap generation.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Neutralization depth vs. information loss:** Over-neutralization (e.g., collapsing all occupations to "worker") may erase legitimate clinical variance; under-neutralization leaks linguistic cues. Paper uses INSEE-guideline inclusive forms as a compromise.
  - **Metric design:** Modified RMSE weights confident predictions (further from 4) more heavily; choice impacts sensitivity to extreme bias and comparability.
  - **Model selection:** Prioritized open-source, French-capable models for privacy and reproducibility; limits generalizability to proprietary models but ensures control over inference.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High variance across 3 runs → unstable decoding or prompt sensitivity.
  - Neutralized-input RMSE ≈ full-text RMSE (Table 1) → failed neutralization; model ignores SDoH, uses residual linguistic cues.
  - Association heatmaps with no significant odds ratios → model not leveraging SDoH, defaulting to neutral predictions (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B’s low bias).
  - >20% unparsable outputs → model does not follow `Valeur prédite : <X>` format; prompt compliance failure.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline bias sweep:** Run all 9 models on the full neutralized dataset (N=958) with fixed prompt/parameters; compute modified RMSE and prediction distributions. Validate stability (SD<0.01).
  2. **Input format ablation:** On 100 examples, compare 4 input formats (full text, filtered text, extracted SDoH, neutralized SDoH) to measure information loss from neutralization (replicating Table 1). Expect neutralized to show lowest bias if linguistic cues dominate.
  3. **SDoH association profiling:** For each model, binarize predictions, run one-tailed Fisher’s exact test for all 13 SDoH options and 6 Profession groups; generate heatmaps. Identify which SDoH (e.g., Occupation, MaritalStatus) show strong, significant associations across models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What bias thresholds are acceptable for LLMs in medical decision support? The authors observe persistent bias and ask for pragmatic trade-offs but do not establish clinically meaningful thresholds.
- **Open Question 2:** How do interactions between multiple SDoH factors influence gender prediction bias? The study analyzes SDoH in isolation via Fisher’s tests; compound effects of SDoH combinations remain unexplored.
- **Open Question 3:** To what extent are the findings generalizable to other languages and healthcare systems? The study uses a single French hospital dataset; social stereotypes and SDoH documentation vary culturally.
- **Open Question 4:** What is the trade-off between removing linguistic gender cues via SDoH neutralization and preserving clinically meaningful information? Neutralization may oversimplify SDoH and discard context relevant to real medical tasks.

## Limitations
- The original French clinical dataset is not publicly accessible due to ethics approvals, blocking direct replication.
- Neutralization methods for all SDoH fields, especially specific job titles, are under-specified; the balance between removing cues and retaining clinical information is unclear.
- The modified RMSE metric formula is not explicitly defined, hindering precise replication and cross-study comparison.
- Human annotation was performed by only nine individuals, limiting statistical power for human-model comparison.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** LLMs use stereotypical SDoH associations to predict gender when linguistic cues are removed. **Confidence:** Medium (associations evident but neutralization completeness uncertain).
- **Claim:** Smaller LLMs exhibit stronger gender stereotypical bias from SDoH than larger models. **Confidence:** High (consistent trend across multiple model families).
- **Claim:** Medical domain fine-tuning amplifies existing gender stereotypes compared to base models. **Confidence:** Medium (observed in two models; requires broader evidence for generalization).
