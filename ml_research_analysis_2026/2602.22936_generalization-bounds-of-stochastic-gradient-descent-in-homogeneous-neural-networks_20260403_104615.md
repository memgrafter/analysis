# Generalization Bounds of Stochastic Gradient Descent in Homogeneous Neural Networks
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22936
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22936
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** For homogeneous neural networks, SGD with raw step size Ω(1/√t) (e.g., for H=3) yields an effective step size Θ(1/t) on the unit sphere, leading to generalization bounds O((T/n)^{m/(m+1)}) and supporting multi-pass training.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the generalization properties of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in homogeneous neural networks. It reveals that homogeneity decouples weight direction from magnitude, enabling the effective step size on the unit sphere to be Θ(1/t) even with a raw step size as large as Ω(1/√t) (for H=3). Under bounded loss and Lipschitz/approximately smooth assumptions, the authors derive generalization bounds of the form O((T/n)^{m/(m+1)}) (with constants m derived from loss bounds) and support multi-pass training. The results also demonstrate an optimization separation: a stepsize of Ω(1/√t) achieves polynomial convergence under PL conditions, contrasting with the logarithmic convergence of O(1/t) stepsizes. This bridges the gap between theoretical generalization bounds (which typically require stepsize O(1/t)) and practical larger stepsizes like Θ(1/√t).

## Method Summary
Theoretical analysis centered on SGD for homogeneous models. Key steps: (1) Leveraging homogeneity to express weight updates as separate dynamics for direction (unit sphere) and norm; (2) Showing that with appropriate stepsize scaling (η_t ~ 1/√t) the effective stepsize on directions becomes Θ(1/t), which is crucial for algorithmic stability; (3) Proving a contraction in the weight norm (E‖w_t‖² = Θ(1/t)) under bounded loss and gradient smoothness approximations; (4) Bounding the stability metric Δ_t via a recurrence that exploits the small effective stepsize, yielding a generalization bound of order O((T/n)^{m/(m+1)}). The analysis accommodates multi-pass training and extends to non-Lipschitz homogeneous losses.

## Key Results
- Homogeneity decouples weight direction from magnitude, making the effective stepsize on the unit sphere Θ(1/t) even when the raw stepsize is Ω(1/√t) (for H=3).
- Generalization bound of O((T/n)^{m/(m+1)}) under bounded loss and Lipschitz/approx smooth assumptions, valid for multi-pass SGD.
- Under PL conditions, stepsize Ω(1/√t) yields polynomial convergence, while O(1/t) stepsizes yield only logarithmic convergence—demonstrating an optimization separation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Homogeneity decouples weight direction from magnitude, enabling the effective stepsize on the unit sphere to be Θ(1/t) even when the raw stepsize is Ω(1/√t) for H=3 neural networks.  
- **Mechanism:** For an H‑homogeneous model, the directional update uses effective stepsize ˜η_t = η_t · ‖w_t‖^{H‑2}. By ensuring the weight norm grows polynomially (e.g., ‖w_t‖² = Θ(1/t) under bounded loss), setting η_t = Ω(1/√t) yields ˜η_t = Θ(1/t). The small effective stepsize satisfies the algorithmic stability requirement despite aggressive raw stepsize.  
- **Core assumption:** The network is H‑homogeneous with H>2, the loss is H‑homogeneous and bounded, and the Bayesian optimal on the sphere is non‑zero to regulate weight decay.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “homogeneity decouples weight direction from magnitude, making the effective stepsize on the unit sphere Θ(1/t) even when the raw stepsize is Ω(1/√t) (for H=3).”  
  - [section 2] Lemma 1 establishes the formula ˜η_t = η_t ‖w_t‖^{H‑2}.  
  - [corpus] A relevant neighbor (arXiv:2406.08654) studies large stepsize GD but for non‑homogeneous two‑layer networks; our use of homogeneity is distinct and not covered there.  
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the network is not homogeneous (e.g., biased layers without scaling, non‑homogeneous activations like sigmoid). In that case, the effective stepsize reduces to η_t and the O(1/t) decay becomes necessary.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The weight norm obeys a contraction that bounds its growth while allowing it to compensate the large physical stepsize, thereby preserving a small effective stepsize.  
- **Mechanism:** From the norm iteration (Lemma 9) and homogeneity identity w_t⊤∇ℓ_t(w_t)=Hℓ_t(w_t), we get ℓ_t(w_t)=‖w_t‖^H ℓ_t(v_t). The norm update is ‖w_{t+1}‖² = ‖w_t‖² (1 + ˜η_t² ‖∇v_t ℓ_t(v_t)‖² - 2H˜η_t ℓ_t(v_t)). Taking expectation and using bounded loss ℓ_t(v_t)≥σ²/2 and Lipschitz/approx smooth gradients (‖∇ℓ‖≤L) yields E_{A,D}‖w_{t+1}‖² ≤ E_{A,D}‖w_t‖² (1 - (Hσ²/2)˜η_t). This forces E_{A,D}‖w_t‖² = Θ(1/t) for H=3 when ˜η_t = Θ(1/t). That in turn allows η_t = Ω(1/√t) while keeping ˜η_t in desired order.  
- **Core assumption:** Assumption 1 (loss bounded above and below on the sphere) and Assumption 2 (L‑Lipschitz and (γ,β)-approx smooth) plus the condition ˜η_t ≤ Hσ²/(2L) for the expectation bound.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.1] Assumptions 1 and 2.  
  - [appendix B.2] Lemma 12: E_{A,D}‖w_{t+1}‖² ≤ E_{A,D}‖w_t‖² (1 - (Hσ²/2)˜η_t).  
  - [section 3.4] Discussion linking Lemma 12 to weight norm evolution.  
- **Break condition:** If the lower bound on loss (ℓ_t(v_t) ≥ σ²/2) is violated (e.g., training loss collapses to zero) the contraction weakens and the norm may decay faster, making effective stepsize too small and slowing optimization. If gradients are unbounded (violating Lipschitz/approx smoothness) the norm may grow uncontrollably, breaking the delicate balance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The controlled effective stepsize yields algorithmic stability bounds of the form E_{A,D} L(v_T) - ˆL(v_T) ≤ O((T/n)^{m/(m+1)}) even with multi‑pass training and stepsizes Ω(1/√t), closing the theory‑practice gap.  
- **Mechanism:** The stability analysis tracks Δ_t = E_{A,D}[ ‖v_t - v_t^{(i)}‖² | E(i) ] for datasets differing in one sample. From the directional update with effective stepsize and the norm ratio bound ‖w_t‖/‖w_{t+1}‖ ≤ 1 + O(1/t) (Lemma 5), one obtains a recurrence Δ_{t+1} ≤ (1+β˜η_t)Δ_t + 2˜η_t L/n + 2˜η_t γ. With ˜η_t = Θ(1/t) (for H=3) this solves to Δ_T = O( T^{m/(m+1)} / n^{1/(m+1)} ). Plugging into the uniform stability‑to‑generalization conversion gives the stated bound. The order permits T up to o(n/γ̃^{1/m}), i.e., multiple epochs over the data.  
- **Core assumption:** Lipschitz and (γ,β)-approx smooth loss, H>2 homogeneity, stepsize condition E_{A,D}η_t = Ω(t^{(H‑4)/2}), and γ = o(T/n) for tightness.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “derive generalization bounds ... O((T/n)^(m/(m+1))) and support multi‑pass training.”  
  - [section 3.2] Theorem 1 presents the bound explicitly with constants m₁,m₂.  
  - [appendix B.2] Lemma 4 and the recurrence (11) lead to the final bound.  
- **Break condition:** The bound degrades if γ is not o(T/n), i.e., the non‑smoothness is too large relative to T and n. Also if η_t exceeds Ω(t^{(H‑4)/2}) then ˜η_t may become larger than O(1/t) and the recurrence yields a divergent Δ_T, destroying the polynomial guarantee.

## Foundational Learning
### H‑homogeneous neural networks
**Why needed:** Homogeneity is the prerequisite for decoupling weight direction and magnitude; it enables the effective stepsize formulation. Without homogeneity, the analysis collapses to the classical O(1/t) requirement.  
**Quick check:** "For an H‑homogeneous network Φ, what is Φ(c·w; X) equal to?" (Answer: c^H Φ(w; X))

### Effective stepsize on the unit sphere
**Why needed:** Stability depends on the dynamics of weight directions, not raw weights. The effective stepsize ˜η_t dictates how quickly the predictor changes in direction space; keeping it O(1/t) is the key to generalization.  
**Quick check:** "Given H=3, η_t = 1/√t, and ‖w_t‖² = √t, what is the order of ˜η_t?" (Answer: Θ(1/t))

### Algorithmic stability (uniform stability)
**Why needed:** It directly connects the sensitivity of the training algorithm to perturbations in the data with the expected generalization error. It is the main tool used to convert bounds on Δ_T into a generalization guarantee.  
**Quick check:** "If an algorithm is ϵ_T‑stable, what is the bound on the generalization gap L(w_T) - ˆL(w_T)?" (Answer: ≤ ϵ_T)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Normalized layers -> H unnormalized linear/ReLU layers -> Homogeneous loss -> SGD (no momentum) -> direction v_T

### Critical path
1. Initialize weights with small norm (‖w_0‖ = O(1)).
2. For t=0 to T‑1:
   a. Sample index i_t uniformly from {1,…,n}.
   b. Compute gradient g_t = ∇_w ℓ(w_t; X_{i_t}, Y_{i_t}).
   c. Update w_{t+1} = w_t - η_t g_t.
   d. (Optional) Update an exponential moving average of ‖w_t‖ to dynamically adjust η_t.
   e. If t % N_log == 0, log ‖w_t‖, loss, and maybe Δ_t.
3. Return final direction v_T = w_T / ‖w_T‖.

### Design tradeoffs
- Stepsize constant: Larger c speeds up initial progress but may cause effective stepsize spikes if the weight norm does not rise fast enough. A conservative c (e.g., 0.1) is safer.
- Choice of H: H=3 balances allowing Ω(1/√t) stepsize and manageable architecture depth. Increasing H permits even larger η_t but requires more unnormalized layers, increasing model size and possible optimization difficulty.
- Approximate smoothness vs efficiency: Smooth activations (e.g., (max{0,·})^u , u>1) set γ=0, improving theoretical bound but expanding computational cost and possibly empirical performance. ReLU is common (γ>0).
- Number of passes: Theory suggests T = o(n/γ̃^{1/m}). In practice, training often uses many more epochs; the bound becomes loose, yet stability often still holds empirically. One may stop early to stay within the theoretical regime.

### Failure signatures
- Weight norm collapse: ‖w_t‖ decays quickly (e.g., < 1e‑3) → effective stepsize becomes tiny → training stalls. Likely due to η_t too large relative to loss lower bound or violating H‑homogeneity.
- Weight norm explosion: ‖w_t‖ grows super‑polynomially → effective stepsize blows up → gradients destabilize, causing NaNs. Often from unbounded gradients or incorrect stepsize scaling.
- Increasing validation error: After a point, test error rises while training error continues falling. This can indicate that the effective stepsize is too large, harming stability. Reduce η_t constant or ensure norm is growing adequately.
- Homogeneity check: Evaluate Φ(c·w) vs c^H Φ(w) on random data; if deviation > tolerance, architecture may have broken homogeneity (e.g., bias terms). Fix by removing or properly scaling biases.

### First 3 experiments
1. Effective stepsize measurement: Train a 3‑homogeneous MLP on synthetic data with fixed random seed. Record t, η_t, and ‖w_t‖ at each iteration. Compute ˜η_t = η_t · ‖w_t‖^{H‑2}. Plot η_t and ˜η_t vs t on log‑log; confirm η_t ∝ 1/√t and ˜η_t ∝ 1/t.
2. Generalization vs T/n scaling: For various n (e.g., 500, 1000, 2000) and various T (multiple of n), compute training and test errors. Plot generalization gap (test – train) vs T/n on log‑log; fit slope. Compare with predicted exponent m/(m+1) (use estimated constants from loss smoothness). Also compare to η_t = 1/t baseline.
3. Homogeneity ablation: Replace the first normalized layers with unnormalized ones, or add bias parameters in the last layer. Train with same η_t = 1/√t. Observe changes in weight norm dynamics, training stability, and generalization gap. This demonstrates the necessity of the homogeneity assumption.

## Limitations
- **Homogeneity requirement**: Excludes common architectures (biases, BatchNorm, skip connections).
- **Loss assumptions**: Boundedness and a positive lower bound σ² on the sphere may fail in over‑parametrized settings.
- **Multi‑pass regime**: The bound is guaranteed only for T = o(n/γ̃^{1/m}); beyond that it becomes vacuous.

## Confidence
- **Homogeneity‑enabled effective stepsize**: Medium. Derivation is rigorous but hinges on strict homogeneity; real‑world architectures often violate these conditions.
- **Generalization bound O((T/n)^{m/(m+1)})**: Medium‑High. Stability analysis is sound under bounded loss and a positive lower bound σ²; those assumptions may fail when training loss collapses to zero.
- **Optimization separation under PL conditions**: Low‑Medium. Shows polynomial convergence for larger stepsizes but PL is rarely verified; no empirical validation.
