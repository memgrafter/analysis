# No Memorization, No Detection: Output Distribution-Based Contamination Detection in Small Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03203  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03203  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CDD fails to detect contamination in small models fine‑tuned with low‑capacity adapters (e.g., LoRA rank 8) unless memorization (output collapse) occurs; probability‑based methods succeed more broadly.

## Executive Summary
Data contamination—when evaluation examples appear in training data—threatens the validity of language model benchmarks. This paper evaluates **CDD** (Contamination Detection via output Distribution), a method that detects memorization by measuring the peakedness of a model’s output distribution through the token‑level edit distance between greedy and temperature‑sampled outputs. Using Pythia models (70M–410M parameters) fine‑tuned on GSM8K, HumanEval, and MATH, with controlled contamination (repeating 100 evaluation examples 0, 1, 5, or 10 times), they find CDD performs at chance (~0.50 accuracy) when fine‑tuning uses low‑capacity LoRA adapters (rank 8), even with high contamination and low training loss, because the output distribution does not collapse. CDD only exceeds 90% accuracy once fine‑tuning crosses a memorization threshold determined by model size, adapter rank, and training epochs. In contrast, probability‑based detectors (perplexity and Min‑k% Prob) consistently outperform CDD, including at minimal contamination (c=1) where CDD remains at chance. The study thus demonstrates CDD’s reliance on strong memorization and its unreliability for small models with parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning.

## Method Summary
The authors compare CDD against probability‑based baselines (perplexity, Min‑k% Prob) and an oracle n‑gram overlap detector. Experiments use **Pythia** (70M, 160M, 410M) and three benchmarks: **GSM8K** (math word problems), **HumanEval** (code generation), and **MATH** (competition math). Contamination is introduced by repeating 100 held‑out evaluation examples within the training set, with repetition count *c* ∈ {0,1,5,10}. Fine‑tuning approaches include **LoRA** (rank 8 or 256, targeting QKV projections) and **full fine‑tuning**, each run for 3 or 20 epochs. CDD computes a **peakedness** score: for each prompt it generates one greedy output (temperature 0) and *n*=50 temperature‑sampled outputs (*t*=0.8). The score is the fraction of samples whose token‑level edit distance from the greedy output is ≤ α·*l* (α=0.05), where *l* is the greedy output length. Classification thresholds are chosen via the Youden index on a held‑out validation slice. Performance is measured by accuracy, AUROC, and AUPRC. Training loss is recorded to monitor learning progress.

## Key Results
- CDD accuracy is at chance (~0.50) when fine‑tuning uses low‑capacity LoRA (rank 8), even with heavy contamination (c=10) and low training loss; it only exceeds 90% once fine‑tuning crosses a memorization threshold (e.g., LoRA rank 256, full fine‑tuning, or longer training) where the model reproduces contaminated answers verbatim.  
- Probability‑based detection (perplexity and Min‑k% Prob) consistently outperforms CDD in all conditions where any method works; they succeed even at minimal contamination (c=1) where CDD remains at chance.  
- Output distribution collapse—a sharp concentration of the model’s output distribution—is necessary for CDD to work. Without collapse, peakedness scores for both contaminated and clean prompts cluster near zero, yielding random classification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CDD can detect contamination only when fine‑tuning causes the model's output distribution to collapse onto the contaminated answers (verbatim memorization).  
- **Mechanism:** CDD measures peakedness as the fraction of temperature‑sampled outputs that are edit‑distance close to the greedy output. A high peakedness indicates a highly concentrated distribution, which occurs when the model has memorized the exact training sequence and reproduces it regardless of sampling randomness. Low peakedness indicates a diffuse distribution with diverse outputs.  
- **Core assumption:** Memorized contaminated examples will be reproduced consistently across samples; non‑memorized contaminated examples yield diverse outputs.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states CDD fails because the output distribution does not collapse. Section 4.1 explicitly says CDD requires output distribution collapse. Figure 3 shows that when CDD works, contaminated examples have high peakedness; when it fails, both contaminated and clean examples have peakedness near zero.  
- **Break condition:** If fine‑tuning does not produce memorization (e.g., low‑capacity adapters, insufficient epochs), peakedness remains low and CDD accuracy stays at chance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Whether output distribution collapse occurs depends on crossing a memorization threshold determined by the absolute number of trainable parameters (model size × fine‑tuning method) and training steps.  
- **Mechanism:** Memorizing exact training sequences requires sufficient parameter capacity. Low‑rank LoRA adapters provide very few trainable parameters (< 0.2% for *r*=8), limiting the model’s ability to store exact examples; the model may still learn general patterns (loss decreases) but cannot collapse the distribution. Increasing capacity (higher rank, full fine‑tuning, larger base model) or training longer can push the system past the threshold, enabling memorization and thus distribution collapse.  
- **Core assumption:** The number of trainable parameters is the primary bottleneck for memorization in fine‑tuning; training duration can trade off against capacity.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes CDD only succeeds once fine‑tuning crosses a memorization threshold—dependent on model size, adapter rank, and epochs. Section 4.3 shows CDD accuracy transitions sharply from chance to >90% as fine‑tuning capacity crosses a threshold; LoRA *r*=8 at 20 epochs reaches 0.920, comparable to LoRA *r*=256 at 3 epochs. Figure 2 illustrates two regimes: loss 1.0–3.0 (learning without memorization, CDD at chance) and loss below ~0.5 (memorization, CDD rises). The transition is abrupt.  
- **Break condition:** If the total trainable parameter budget (rank × layers × dimensions) is below the memorization threshold for the given training steps and contamination level, memorization will not occur and CDD will fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Probability‑based detection methods (perplexity and Min‑k% Prob) can detect contamination even when output distribution collapse does not occur, because they directly measure shifts in the model’s internal probability distribution.  
- **Mechanism:** Perplexity computes the average negative log‑likelihood of the prompt; Min‑k% Prob averages the probabilities of the *k*% least likely tokens. Training on contaminated data increases the likelihood of those prompts, lowering perplexity and raising the minimum token probabilities. These metrics respond to any reduction in surprise, including cases where the model has learned to generalize from the data without memorizing exact outputs. Thus they are sensitive to weaker forms of contamination that CDD misses.  
- **Core assumption:** Fine‑tuning on a text sequence makes that sequence more probable under the model, even if it does not cause deterministic reproduction. This probability shift is detectable via token‑level log‑probabilities.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states probability‑based approaches consistently outperform CDD, including at low contamination levels (*c*=1) where CDD remains at chance. Section 4.2 (Table 2) shows perplexity already reaches 0.75 at *c*=1 and 1.00 at *c*=5 while CDD is at chance; probability‑based methods detect contamination at lower levels and in conditions where CDD fails. Section 4.4 discusses the gap between learning and memorization, noting that it is a blind spot for CDD but not for probability‑based methods. Section 5 reiterates that these methods evaluate the internal probability distribution over prompt tokens, which shifts even without memorization.  
- **Break condition:** If the model has not seen the contaminated data during training, or if access to output probabilities is denied (black‑box only), these methods cannot be applied or will not show a signal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Output distribution collapse**  
  *Why needed:* It is the central condition for CDD’s success; without it, peakedness scores are uninformative.  
  *Quick check:* What is output distribution collapse, and how does it manifest in CDD’s peakedness scores?
- **Trainable parameter budget**  
  *Why needed:* It determines whether memorization (and thus collapse) can occur during fine‑tuning.  
  *Quick check:* How does LoRA rank affect the number of trainable parameters, and why does a higher rank increase the chance of memorization?
- **Probability‑based contamination signals**  
  *Why needed:* They provide an alternative detection pathway that works without memorization, highlighting CDD’s limitation.  
  *Quick check:* Why do perplexity and Min‑k% Prob detect contamination even when the model does not reproduce memorized outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Pythia models (70M/160M/410M) → Datasets (GSM8K, HumanEval, MATH) → Fine‑tuning (LoRA r=8/256, full) → Contamination injection (repeat selected examples) → Detection methods (CDD peakedness, perplexity, Min‑k% Prob, n‑gram overlap) → Metrics (accuracy, AUROC, AUPRC).

**Critical path (reproducing CDD’s failure with low‑capacity fine‑tuning):**
1. Load a Pythia model and prepare a dataset (e.g., GSM8K) with a chosen contamination level *c* (number of times selected evaluation examples are repeated in the training set).
2. Fine‑tune using the specified method (LoRA rank 8/256 or full) for the designated number of epochs (3 or 20) with learning rate 2e‑4, batch 8, warmup 0.1, targeting QKV projections.
3. For each evaluation prompt, generate one greedy output (temperature 0) and 50 temperature‑sampled outputs (*t*=0.8), truncating to 100 tokens.
4. Compute CDD peakedness: fraction of sampled outputs whose token‑level edit distance from the greedy output is ≤ α·*l* (α=0.05), where *l* is the greedy output length.
5. Determine a classification threshold ξ by maximizing the Youden index on a held‑out validation slice.
6. Evaluate detection accuracy, AUROC, and AUPRC. Also compute baseline metrics: perplexity (negative log‑likelihood of the prompt) and Min‑k% Prob (average probability of the lowest‑probability *k*% tokens, with *k*=10).
7. Compare performance across conditions to identify memorization thresholds and method reliability.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Black‑box vs. white‑box: CDD needs only sampled text (works with APIs); probability‑based methods require logit access.
- Efficiency vs. memorization: LoRA is parameter‑efficient but may not collapse; full FT gives strong signals but is costly.
- Contamination level: Higher repetition increases memorization likelihood but may be unrealistic; *c*=1 is the most realistic scenario where CDD often fails.
- Hyperparameters: CDD’s α, temperature, and number of samples have limited impact; the dominant factor is capacity.

**Failure signatures:**
- Peakedness scores clustered near 0 for both contaminated and clean prompts.
- CDD accuracy ≈ 0.5 despite low training loss (< 2) and high baseline (perplexity/Min‑k%) accuracy.
- No visible separation in peakedness histograms (Figure 3a).
- Training loss in the “learning without memorization” regime (1.0–3.0) with no abrupt drop to near‑zero.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. *Capacity contrast:* Run GSM8K (*c*=10) on Pythia‑410M with LoRA *r*=8 vs. full FT (3 epochs) and compare CDD accuracy. Expect chance vs. >0.95.
2. *Memorization threshold:* Vary LoRA rank (8 vs. 256) and epochs (3 vs. 20) for a fixed model and *c*=10; identify condition where CDD transitions to high accuracy.
3. *Probability superiority:* For LoRA *r*=8 (3 epochs) across all contamination levels (0–10), compute perplexity and Min‑k% Prob alongside CDD; verify that the probability‑based methods exceed chance at *c*=1 while CDD does not.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** For small models fine‑tuned with parameter‑efficient methods like LoRA (rank ≤ 256), what specific fine‑tuning regime characteristics (e.g., trainable parameter count, training epochs, data repetition) determine whether output distribution collapse (memorization) occurs?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: "CDD’s effectiveness depends critically on whether fine‑tuning produces verbatim memorization" and identify a "memorization threshold" dependent on model size, adapter rank, and epochs. They show CDD fails with LoRA r=8 but succeeds with r=256 or full fine‑tuning.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the existence of a threshold but does not provide a predictive formula or theory for precisely where it lies across different model architectures, datasets, or fine‑tuning configurations. The interaction effects are shown empirically but not mechanistically explained.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study varying trainable parameters (e.g., LoRA rank, full FT %) and training duration across multiple model families (not just Pythia) to derive a generalizable condition (e.g., minimum % of parameters tuned × epochs) that predicts output collapse. Ablation studies isolating capacity vs. training signal strength would help.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do probability‑based detection methods (perplexity, Min‑k% Prob) succeed in the "learning without memorization" regime where CDD fails, and what does this imply about the nature of contamination signals in small models?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note: "Probability‑based methods… outperform CDD in all conditions where any method exceeds chance" and explain that these methods detect shifts in the internal probability distribution even without output collapse. CDD requires external behavioral collapse (verbatim reproduction).
- **Why unresolved:** While the paper contrasts the signals (probability distribution shift vs. output collapse), it does not deeply analyze *why* fine‑tuning on contaminated data consistently shifts token probabilities for small models even when it doesn't force deterministic output generation. Is it due to residual gradient updates affecting early layers? The precise relationship between training dynamics, model capacity, and the type of internal representation change is not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of logit or hidden‑state distributions on contaminated vs. clean prompts across fine‑tuning steps for models below the memorization threshold. Probing whether probability shifts correlate with specific layers or attention heads. Comparing gradient norms or weight updates for LoRA vs. full fine‑tuning to see if capacity limits affect which parameters learn the contamination signal.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can alternative sample‑based distribution tests (e.g., MMD, classifier two‑sample tests) detect contamination in the "learning without memorization" regime identified in this study, or do they share CDD's reliance on output collapse?
- **Basis in paper:** In the Discussion, the authors mention: "Alternative two‑sample tests operate differently… However, applying these tests to variable‑length text generations requires nontrivial design choices… Whether such approaches can detect contamination in the ‘learning without memorization’ regime we identify remains open." They specifically name MMD and classifier tests.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates CDD (a specific peakedness test) and probability‑based methods. It hypothesizes that other sample‑based tests might be sensitive to broader distributional shifts but does not implement or test them. The feasibility of applying these tests to text (representation choices, reference distributions) is noted as a challenge but not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement MMD and classifier two‑sample tests on the same experimental setup (Pythia models, same contamination conditions). Use a fixed representation (e.g., average last‑hidden‑state embeddings of generated sequences) and a reference distribution (e.g., generations from a model trained without contamination). Test whether these methods achieve above‑chance accuracy in LoRA r=8 conditions where CDD fails and probability methods succeed.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent are the paper's findings (CDD's failure on small models with low‑rank adapters) specific to the Pythia model family and the GSM8K/HumanEval/MATH datasets, versus being generalizable to other small model architectures (e.g., OPT, TinyLlama) and domains (e.g., summarization, dialogue)?
- **Basis in paper:** The study is limited to Pythia‑70M/160M/410M and three specific benchmarks (math, code, math). The Limitations section states: "Our study is limited to the Pythia family… Our conclusions about sample‑based detection are specific to CDD’s peakedness statistic." The variation in CDD accuracy across datasets (e.g., MATH vs. GSM8K) is noted but not deeply analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear if the memorization threshold and CDD’s failure mode are universal properties of small models generally, or if they depend on Pythia's specific pre‑training data (the Pile) or architecture details. The domain variation (CDD works poorly on MATH even with full FT) suggests dataset characteristics (answer length, structure) matter, but the causal factors are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the experimental design with at least one other small model family (e.g., OPT‑125M, TinyLlama‑160M) and one additional domain (e.g., a narrative QA dataset). Compare the memorization threshold positions and the relative performance of CDD vs. probability methods. Conduct more detailed analysis of dataset properties (e.g., average solution length, token repetition, lexical diversity) to model their effect on output collapse.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the mechanistic relationship between training loss reduction and the emergence of output distribution collapse (memorization) in parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning? Can loss trajectories predict the memorization threshold?
- **Basis in paper:** Figure 2 shows "learning without memorization" (loss ~1.0‑3.0, CDD chance) vs. "memorization" (loss < ~0.5, CDD high). The paper notes: "Loss reduction reflects learning, but not necessarily memorization." However, loss is not a perfect predictor—some conditions with very low loss still show chance CDD.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes that low loss is necessary but not sufficient for collapse. The precise loss value at which collapse occurs seems to vary with configuration. Why does LoRA r=8 at 20 epochs reach low loss (comparable to other conditions) yet still sometimes not collapse? The interplay between optimization dynamics (e.g., convergence speed, weight magnitudes) and collapse is not measured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Track loss and a direct memorization metric (e.g., exact match of generated outputs with training sequences) throughout training for all configurations. Analyze gradient norms, weight update magnitudes, and the condition number of the optimized low‑rank matrices. Determine if collapse correlates with a specific optimization milestone (e.g., reaching a certain training loss *on contaminated examples only*), or with saturation of adapter capacity.

## Limitations
- The study focuses on small Pythia models (≤ 410M) and three specific benchmarks; generalization to larger models or other domains remains unclear.
- The exact memorization threshold for output collapse may vary with fine‑tuning hyperparameters and data characteristics; the paper does not provide a universal formula.
- Reproduction requires precise data splits and contamination injection scripts, which are not included in the paper, and the full experimental grid demands substantial compute resources (~8× NVIDIA A100 GPUs).

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (CDD requires output distribution collapse): **High**  
- Mechanism 2 (Memorization threshold depends on trainable parameters and epochs): **Medium**  
- Mechanism 3 (Probability‑based methods detect contamination without memorization): **High**
