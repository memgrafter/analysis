# SDFP: Speculative Decoding with FIT-Pruned Models for Training-Free and Plug-and-Play LLM Acceleration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05499  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05499  
- **Reference count**: 25  
- **Primary result**: A training-free method that constructs a lightweight draft model via Fisher Information Trace–based layer pruning, achieving 1.32×–1.5× decoding speedup with 0.95–0.98 token acceptance rates across LLaMA-2 models and tasks.

## Executive Summary
SDFP (Speculative Decoding with FIT-Pruned Models) tackles the high deployment cost of separate draft models in speculative decoding by automatically creating a draft from the target LLM itself. It prunes entire transformer layers identified as low-sensitivity via Fisher Information Trace (FIT) scores computed on a general corpus (WikiText2). This training-free, plug-and-play approach preserves architectural compatibility, enabling standard speculative verification without KV cache recomputation. Across LLaMA-2 models and diverse tasks (CNN/DM, GSM8K, TinyStories), SDFP matches the speedup and acceptance of prior optimized drafts while requiring zero additional training or hyperparameter tuning.

## Method Summary
SDFP consists of two phases. First, it computes per-layer FIT scores: for each transformer layer, it accumulates squared gradient norms of the layer’s parameters over a small calibration set (WikiText2) via a single forward-backward pass. The FIT score approximates the expected KL divergence if that layer were altered; layers with lower scores contribute less to output distribution changes. Second, it ranks layers by ascending FIT and prunes a predefined ratio of attention and FFN layers (e.g., 50% attention, 35% FFN for LLaMA-2-13B). The resulting subnetwork—structurally identical to the target except for fewer layers—serves as the draft model. During inference, the draft generates *k* speculative tokens, and the target model verifies them in parallel using the standard accept-reject rule, reusing the draft’s KV cache for efficiency.

## Key Results
- **Speedup**: Achieves 1.32×–1.5× end-to-end decoding speedup over autoregressive baseline.
- **Acceptance rate**: Maintains high token acceptance α = 0.95–0.98 across CNN/DM, GSM8K, and TinyStories.
- **Zero overhead**: Requires no draft model training, optimization, or task-specific calibration, eliminating deployment costs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Pruning layers with the lowest FIT scores yields a draft whose output distribution stays close to the target’s, enabling high acceptance during verification.
- **Mechanism**: FIT approximates the Fisher information trace; a low score indicates that removing the layer causes a small increase in KL divergence. Thus, the pruned draft’s token probabilities remain similar to the target’s, leading to high acceptance probabilities α ≈ 1.
- **Evidence**: The paper reports acceptance rates of 0.95–0.98 when pruning based on FIT computed on WikiText2 and applying to diverse tasks. Section III-A justifies FIT as a fast, training-free layer sensitivity metric.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Constructing the draft by removing entire layers from the same pretrained model guarantees architectural compatibility for efficient KV cache reuse.
- **Mechanism**: Because the draft is a structured subnetwork of the target, all retained layers have identical input/output dimensions and attention/FFN structures. This allows the draft’s KV caches to be passed directly to the target during verification without reshaping or recomputation.
- **Evidence**: Section III-C explicitly states that structured pruning from the same LLM ensures compatibility. No runtime errors or extra KV-copy overhead are reported.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: One-shot FIT computation on a small general dataset eliminates deployment overhead, making SDFP instantly usable.
- **Mechanism**: FIT requires only a single forward-backward pass per minibatch over a few hundred samples; it avoids second-order derivatives, Bayesian optimization, or fine-tuning. The pruning decision is fixed thereafter.
- **Evidence**: Table II shows SDFP has no training cost or optimization latency, while baselines like SWIFT require minutes and SELF-SD requires thousands of BO iterations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Speculative Decoding**: A draft model proposes tokens in parallel; the target verifies them sequentially using the draft’s probabilities. Needed because SDFP instantiates this loop; acceptance rule ensures lossless output distribution.
  - *Quick check*: If the draft assigns higher probability to a token than the target, the token is accepted with probability `min(1, p_target/q_draft)`. Why does this preserve the target’s distribution?
- **Fisher Information & Empirical Approximation**: Fisher information measures parameter sensitivity; its trace approximates KL divergence under small perturbations. Needed because FIT uses gradient norms to estimate layer importance without second-order calculus.
  - *Quick check*: In `D_KL ≈ ½ δθᵀ I(θ) δθ`, what does a larger diagonal entry in `I(θ)` imply about the corresponding parameter’s importance?
- **Structured Pruning (Layer Removal)**: Removing entire transformer blocks instead of individual weights. Needed because it preserves dimensional consistency across layers, enabling KV cache compatibility.
  - *Quick check*: After removing an attention block, why must the subsequent feed-forward network’s input dimension match the retained layers’ output dimension?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: FIT Score Computer -> Layer Ranker & Pruner -> Speculative Decoder
- **Critical path**: Load calibration data (WikiText2) -> Forward/backward passes to accumulate layer gradient norms -> Compute FIT per layer -> Rank and prune lowest-scoring layers -> Save pruned weights as draft -> During inference: draft.generate(k tokens) -> target.verify(draft tokens) -> apply accept-reject logic.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Pruning ratio: Higher ratio yields faster draft but risks lower acceptance; fixed heuristics used (e.g., 50% attention, 35% FFN for 13B).
  - Calibration dataset: WikiText2 is general-purpose; task-specific data could improve acceptance but violates plug-and-play goal.
  - Speculation depth *k*: Larger *k* increases potential speedup but may degrade acceptance if draft quality drops over longer horizons.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low acceptance rate (α < 0.9) → FIT scores misranked; check calibration data representativeness and gradient accumulation stability.
  - No speedup → draft not sufficiently faster; profile draft vs. target latency; adjust pruning ratio.
  - Runtime errors during verification → architectural mismatch; validate pruned model’s layer dimensions and KV cache shapes.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **FIT sanity check**: Compute FIT on 100 WikiText2 samples for LLaMA-2-7B; verify middle layers have higher scores than early/late (as in Fig. 3).
  2. **Single-layer ablation**: Prune only the lowest-FIT attention layer; measure acceptance on 100 test samples. Expect α > 0.9; a drop to ~0.5 suggests miscalibrated FIT.
  3. **KV cache compatibility test**: Run one speculative step with pruned draft and target; confirm no shape mismatches in KV tensors during verification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Does FIT-based layer sensitivity generalize across domains without task-specific calibration? FIT scores computed on WikiText2 are applied unchanged to CNN/DM, GSM8K, and TinyStories. No analysis of ranking stability across datasets is provided.
- **Open Question 2**: Can the pruning ratio be automatically derived from the FIT score distribution instead of using fixed heuristics? Ratios are static and borrowed from prior Bayesian pruning work; optimal trade-off likely varies with model scale and task.
- **Open Question 3**: Does SDFP transfer to other transformer architectures beyond LLaMA-2 (e.g., GPT-style, Mixture-of-Experts)? All experiments are on LLaMA-2; architectural differences could affect FIT’s validity.
- **Open Question 4**: Does the pruned draft exactly preserve the target’s output distribution? High acceptance does not guarantee absence of systematic bias over long generations; no empirical distributional equivalence test (perplexity, KL) is performed.

## Limitations
- **FIT generalization risk**: Using a single general corpus (WikiText2) may not capture task-specific layer sensitivities; domain shift could lead to misranking and low acceptance.
- **Underspecified hyperparameters**: Pruning ratios for LLaMA-2-7B/70B and exact speculation depth *k* are not provided, requiring heuristic adoption.
- **Architectural constraints**: Assumes homogeneous transformer blocks; heterogeneous designs (e.g., varying FFN sizes) may break dimensional compatibility after pruning.
