# High Fidelity Textual User Representation over Heterogeneous Sources via Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07333
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07333
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Pointwise string reward yields +3.5% relative offline pClick AUC (0.769 vs 0.743) and online CTR lift +1.48% on LinkedIn Job Search.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of generating unified, interpretable user representations from heterogeneous textual sources (profiles, activity logs) for large language model (LLM)-based job recommendation. Traditional embedding methods are opaque and incompatible across sources. The authors propose a reinforcement learning (RL) framework to fine‑tune a 1.7B parameter LLM policy that outputs concise textual summaries of users. The reward signal is derived from implicit engagement data (clicks, applies) via a frozen 30B LLM evaluator, and explicit length and format penalties are added to ensure brevity and single‑paragraph structure.

In offline evaluation, the pointwise string‑based reward model improves pClick AUC by 3.5% relative to a baseline (0.769 vs 0.743). More importantly, online A/B testing on LinkedIn Job Search shows statistically significant business lifts: click‑through rate +1.48%, job applications +1.2%, and session success rate +0.8%. The deployed system produces summaries for 25 million members daily using 16 H100 GPUs, demonstrating that the approach is both effective and production‑ready.

## Method Summary
The system ingests heterogeneous user data (profile, experience, skills, search history) and job interaction logs, formatting them into a context string of up to 5000 tokens. A 1.7B parameter LLM actor generates a user summary given this context. A separate, frozen 30B LLM acts as a reward model, scoring the summary's ability to predict future job engagement via either pointwise (binary apply prediction) or listwise (ranking of recent interactions) tasks. The actor is optimized with group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO), a variant of PPO that uses a group‑relative baseline and asymmetric clipping (ε_low=0.2, ε_high=0.28). KL regularization (β=0.001) prevents deviation from the reference policy. Additional rewards include a quadratic length penalty for outputs exceeding 150 tokens (λ=10⁻⁵) and a -1 penalty for multi‑paragraph outputs. Training uses a global batch size of 768, micro‑batch 32, learning rate 3e‑7, for 8 epochs on ~200k training examples. The trained actor is deployed with vLLM on 16 H100 GPUs, generating summaries for 25 million members each day.

## Key Results
- Offline: Pointwise string reward improves relative pClick AUC by 3.5% (0.769 vs 0.743 baseline).
- Online A/B test on LinkedIn Job Search: CTR +1.48%, Job Applications +1.2%, Session Success Rate +0.8%.
- Scalable production: Summaries generated for 25 million members daily using 16 H100 GPUs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The RL policy learns to generate user summaries that predict future job engagement because the reward signal is directly derived from implicit engagement signals (clicks, applies).
- **Mechanism:** The policy (1.7B LLM) is optimized via group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO) to maximize a reward computed by a frozen 30B LLM evaluator. The evaluator scores a summary's ability to predict a specific user action (pointwise) or rank jobs (listwise) based on the summary alone, using engagement‑derived labels.
- **Core assumption:** A summary that enables accurate prediction of future job interactions on unseen postings is a high‑fidelity representation of the user's job‑seeking intent.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "leverages implicit user engagement signals (e.g., clicks, applies) as the primary reward to distill salient information."
  - [section 3.2] "Our approach relies on the intuition that an effective user representation ... should be sufficient to reliably predict the user’s future job‑related actions."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows related work (e.g., "Learning Unified User Quantized Tokenizers...") also uses engagement signals, but direct corpus evidence for *this* causal mechanism is weak; the paper's own experimental validation (Tables 6, 8) serves as primary evidence.
- **Break condition:** If the engagement labels are sparse, biased, or not causally related to long‑term user satisfaction (e.g., clickbait jobs), the policy may optimize for short‑term proxies that do not capture true user intent.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The RL framework produces sufficiently concise summaries for latency‑sensitive production by integrating explicit length penalties and formatting constraints into the reward.
- **Mechanism:** A quadratic length penalty `f_length(|o|) = -(|o| - 150)^2` for |o|>150 is added to the total reward `R_total = R + λ*f_length`. An additional format penalty (reward of -1 for multi‑paragraph outputs) enforces a single‑paragraph structure.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a token budget (e.g., ~150 tokens) that preserves predictive signal while meeting online latency constraints; deviations beyond this budget yield diminishing or negative returns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3.3] "We augment the base reward with a length penalty... The total reward is defined as `R_total = R + λ·f_length(|o|)`... we set λ=10^{-5} in our experiments." and "This approach effectively mitigates length explosion."
  - [figure 2 & 3] Graphs show output token length stabilizing around 150 tokens with the length loss, versus rapid growth without constraints.
  - [corpus] Corpus lacks direct support; this is a novel implementation detail from the paper.
- **Break condition:** If the λ coefficient is poorly tuned (too low), verbosity returns; if too high, the policy may become overly terse and lose salient information, harming predictive accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Assumption:** The RL optimization process, by repeatedly maximizing engagement‑based rewards over diverse contexts, induces emergent capabilities like latent profile imputation and structured output generation (e.g., ranked lists) without explicit supervision.
- **Mechanism:** The pre‑trained LLM actor's priors are steered by the reward signal to perform "in‑the‑loop" feature selection and synthesis. For sparse inputs, it leverages its internal knowledge to infer plausible missing attributes (imputation). For discriminative tasks, it discovers that generating a ranked list improves its reward (though this is later penalized).
- **Core assumption:** The base LLM possesses sufficient world knowledge and reasoning capacity; the RL process acts as a sculptor, aligning its outputs to the engagement objective, which surfaces useful reasoning patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1.2] "latent profile imputation: when provided with sparse user inputs... the model leverages its pre‑trained priors to infer missing contextual attributes." Table 4 demonstrates this.
  - [section 4.1.1] "emergent behavior... the actor generates an explicit ranked list of potential job matches, despite receiving no explicit instruction." Table 3 shows an example.
  - [corpus] Related work (e.g., "Rec‑R1") also notes emergent reasoning in RL for RecSys, but this specific emergence is paper‑specific.
- **Break condition:** If the reward signal is too noisy or the RL updates are unstable, the policy may converge to a degenerate solution (e.g., generic templates) instead of discovering useful emergent structures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning from Human/Preference Feedback (RLHF/RLAIF)**
  - *Why needed here:* The core method uses a frozen LLM as a reward model (RLAIF) to avoid costly human labeling. Understanding policy gradient methods (GRPO), KL regularization, and reward shaping (length penalty) is essential to grasp the optimization loop.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is a group‑relative baseline (GRPO) used instead of a learned critic (PPO) in this system?

- **Concept: Token‑level vs. Sequence‑level Reward Optimization**
  - *Why needed here:* The reward is defined at the sequence level (whole summary's predictive accuracy), but policy gradients are applied token‑by‑token. Understanding advantage estimation and how length penalties affect token‑level gradients is key to preventing verbosity hacking.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the quadratic length penalty `f_length` affect the gradient for a token generated at position *t* when the final length exceeds 150?

- **Concept: Emergent Behaviors in Fine‑tuned LLMs**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper observes unexpected capabilities (ranked list generation, profile imputation) arising from RL optimization. This requires understanding how gradient‑based steering of a pre‑trained model can activate latent skills.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the trade‑off between encouraging beneficial emergent structures (like imputation) and controlling for undesired ones (like ranked lists)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
- Data Ingestion & Sampling: Pulls heterogeneous user data (profile, activity) and engagement logs. Implements temporal split and downsampling (Section 3.4).
- Actor (1.7B LLM): Generates the textual summary `o` given a formatted context `q`. Initialized from a pre‑trained model.
- Reward Model (30B LLM): Frozen evaluator. Takes `(q, o)` and outputs a scalar reward `R` via pointwise (predict apply) or listwise (rank jobs) task (Section 3.3).
- RL Optimizer (GRPO + DAPO/Dr. GRPO): Computes group‑relative advantages from sampled outputs, applies clipped policy update, KL penalty, and asymmetric clipping (Section 3.1, 3.5).
- Constraint Module: Applies length penalty and format penalty to the computed reward before the policy update (Section 3.3.3, 3.3.4).
- Deployment Pipeline: Schedules inference for 25M members daily using vLLM on 16x H100, persists output to KV‑store (Section 6).

**Critical path:** Daily member sample → For each member: (a) Actor generates 4 summaries; (b) Reward model scores all; (c) GRPO computes advantages & updates Actor. Loop over batches → Deploy updated Actor.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Reward model size vs. throughput:* A larger reward model (30B) provides better semantic judgment but doubles GPU requirements (8/16 GPUs dedicated). A smaller, fine‑tuned model would be faster but require per‑task SFT.
- *Pointwise vs. Listwise reward:* Pointwise (binary apply prediction) aligns better with the downstream predictor's objective (AUC gain). Listwise offers richer supervision but may be less congruent and required no format penalty.
- *Length penalty vs. Information retention:* Aggressive length penalties ensure latency but risk over‑truncation. The λ=10⁻⁵ and threshold (150) are empirical.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Reward hacking (verbosity):* Output length trends upward during training. Check length penalty λ and format penalty efficacy.
- *Low coverage/accuracy:* Summaries omit key sources. Inspect LLM‑as‑judge scores (Table 5). Likely cause: reward signal not diverse enough or λ too high.
- *High resource use:* Training does not fit on 16 H100. Check batch size, micro‑batch size, and use of vLLM for reward inference.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Ablation on reward formulation:** Train with pointwise string, pointwise logprob, and listwise rewards only. Compare validation AUC (Table 6) and LLM judge scores (Table 5) to confirm pointwise string reward efficacy and logprob hacking.
2. **Length constraint sensitivity:** Vary λ in {0, 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴} and plot average output length vs. validation reward. Identify the point where length stabilizes without reward collapse.
3. **Qualitative pattern check:** Manually inspect 100 generated summaries from the pointwise model. Verify: (a) summaries are single‑paragraph; (b) they cite multiple input sources; (c) they avoid generating explicit ranked job lists. Use synthetic data with redacted entities to prevent attribution bias (as in Tables 2‑4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can explicit ranked job preferences from users be integrated into the RL framework to improve representation fidelity, and does this outperform emergent ranking behaviors?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.1.1 states that emergent ranking "suggests new research directions, such as explicitly eliciting ranked job preferences from users."
- **Why unresolved:** The current system suppresses explicit rankings via format penalties and relies on unobservable emergent behavior, which may not align with actual user preferences.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect explicit rankings via user interactions, add them as reward signals, and compare summary quality and downstream metrics (e.g., CTR) in A/B tests against baseline.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can reward model design be improved to prevent exploitation of spurious correlations (e.g., verbosity, activity overemphasis) while maintaining RL training stability?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper identifies reward hacking in logprob‑based rewards and uses heuristic length/format penalties (Sections 3.3.3, 4). Underlying vulnerabilities remain.
- **Why unresolved:** Simple penalties may not address all hacking vectors; more robust reward learning techniques are needed for reliable optimization.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace the frozen LLM reward with methods like adversarial reward models or ensemble approaches, and evaluate on factuality, conciseness, and downstream AUC, monitoring for unexpected policy behaviors.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do textual representation quality and downstream performance differ between cold‑start and warm‑start users, and can the RL policy be specialized for sparse data?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.1.2 describes latent profile imputation for sparse inputs, but no evaluation isolates cold‑start performance.
- **Why unresolved:** Aggregate results mask heterogeneity; cold‑start users may receive lower‑quality summaries, affecting fairness and overall metrics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Segment users by activity level, measure summary accuracy (LLM‑judge) and business metrics per segment, and experiment with cold‑start‑specific data augmentation or policy initialization.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Should length constraints be dynamic (e.g., based on user activity or job category) rather than fixed, to optimize the trade‑off between conciseness and information retention?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] A fixed quadratic penalty targets ~150 tokens (Section 3.3.3), but optimal length may vary across contexts.
- **Why unresolved:** One‑size‑fits‑all constraint might discard useful details for complex users or include noise for simple cases, harming predictive power.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Vary length targets across user segments, measure downstream performance and human‑rated conciseness, and build a model to predict optimal length per user based on input characteristics.

## Limitations
- Proprietary LinkedIn dataset limits reproducibility and generalizability of the findings.
- Reward model reliance on a frozen 30B LLM introduces risk of reward hacking and high computational cost.
- Emergent capabilities (profile imputation, ranking) lack causal ablation; offline validation uses a frozen predictor, so transfer to joint optimization is uncertain.
