# RELATE: A Reinforcement Learning-Enhanced LLM Framework for Advertising Text Generation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11780
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11780
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: End-to-end RL framework unifies text generation and online performance optimization, achieving +9.19% relative CTCVR lift in online A/B test.

## Executive Summary
RELATE addresses the misalignment between traditional two-stage advertising text generation systems and online business goals by integrating text generation and performance optimization into a single end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework. It introduces a multi-dimensional reward that combines conversion-oriented metrics (CTCVR), quality constraints, and diversity incentives, with token-level credit assignment for fine-grained control. Offline evaluations on industrial data showed improved compliance (93.98% vs 82.70%) and diversity (0.82 vs 0.72). Most importantly, online A/B testing on a production advertising platform demonstrated a statistically significant 9.19% relative improvement in click-through conversion rate (CTCVR) compared to the baseline.

## Method Summary
RELATE formulates ad text generation as a constrained optimization problem: maximize CTCVR while satisfying quality and diversity constraints. The base policy is a Qwen3-8B large language model fine-tuned via supervised learning. A CTCVR predictor—a Shared-Bottom multi-task model using embeddings—estimates conversion performance from generated text. Additional reward components include deterministic quality rules (length, format, blacklist) and a diversity penalty based on high-frequency n-grams. The system employs Generalized Reward-based Policy Optimization (GRPO) with group-relative advantage estimation: for each prompt, G=5 completions are sampled; sentence-level rewards are normalized within the batch to compute advantages; token-level rewards (for risk and diversity) are added per token. A KL divergence penalty relative to a frozen reference model preserves language quality. Training uses 400k Baidu search ad samples on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs with a global batch size of 1440.

## Key Results
- Online A/B test: +9.19% relative CTCVR lift versus production baseline.
- Offline compliance: 93.98% (vs 82.70% for Qwen-SFT).
- Offline diversity score: 0.82 (vs 0.72).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Unifying conversion, quality, and diversity objectives into a single RL reward function resolves the misalignment between offline language modeling objectives and online business goals in two-stage systems.
- **Mechanism:** The framework defines a scalar reward `reward(x, y) = f(ctcvr(x,y), quality(y), diversity(y))` that is optimized directly via policy gradient. This collapses the generate-then-align pipeline into a single end-to-end process where business metrics (CTCVR) guide token predictions alongside hard constraints (compliance, length) and soft exploration incentives (diversity).
- **Core assumption:** A properly shaped multi-dimensional reward can be backpropagated through an LLM policy without destructive interference between objectives, provided credit assignment respects the natural granularity of each signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "unifies text generation and online performance optimization into an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework... integrates conversion-oriented (CTCVR), quality, and diversity objectives as multi-dimensional rewards."
  - [section 3.2] Formalizes the constrained optimization problem (Eq. 1) and the unified RL objective (Eq. 3), explicitly stating that constraints are softened and incorporated into the generation stage.
  - [section 4.2] Table 1 shows RELATE (full rewards) outperforms Qwen-SFT (which lacks integrated RL rewards) on all metrics: Compliance Rate (93.98% vs. 82.70%), Diversity (0.82 vs. 0.72), and ΔCTCVR (+9.19%).
- **Break condition:** If the reward weighting causes catastrophic forgetting of base language modeling (e.g., severe fluency drop) or if one objective (e.g., diversity) dominates to the point of degrading CTCVR, the unified optimization fails. The ablation in Table 3/Figure 3 shows trade-offs even with token-level credit, implying sensitivity to reward scaling.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Token-level credit assignment for diversity and risk-control signals prevents mode collapse and improves compliance by applying penalties precisely at the token causing the violation, rather than as a coarse sentence-level score.
- **Mechanism:** Certain constraints (e.g., blacklisted words, high-frequency n-grams) have natural token-level triggers. The framework computes `r_token_t = -λ1 I(T_t ∈ V_black) - λ2 I(T_t ∈ G_high)` per token. These local rewards are combined with sentence-level advantages (from CTCVR, semantic quality) to form the final per-token advantage `A_i,t = A_sentence_i + A_token_i,t`. This allows each gradient update to directly penalize the specific token choices that cause constraint violations.
- **Core assumption:** Token-level rewards are sufficiently informative and frequent to shape behavior locally, and their combination with a global sentence-level advantage does not create conflicting gradient signals that destabilize training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.4.1] Explicitly categorizes rewards (token vs. sentence-level) and defines the token-level reward formula (Eq. 5). States: "This design enables credit to be assigned at the computation stage, directly affecting the tokens that trigger the constraints."
  - [section 4.4] Ablation study (Model 4 vs. RELATE) isolates the effect of credit assignment granularity. Model 4 uses sentence-level rewards only; RELATE adds token-level assignment for diversity/risk. Table 3 shows RELATE achieves higher Diversity (0.965 vs. 0.923) and Semantic Quality (0.939 vs. 0.935) at step 150, with comparable CTCVR and Structural Quality. Figure 3 shows more stable reward curves for diversity and semantic quality under token-level assignment.
  - [section 4.3] Case studies show RELATE avoids generic templates while maintaining relevance, suggesting token-level penalties effectively suppress high-frequency patterns without harming overall coherence.
- **Break condition:** If token-level penalties are too aggressive (λ coefficients too high), the model may become overly conservative, losing fluency or diversity. If the set of high-frequency n-grams (G_high) is poorly defined or static, it may not adapt to emerging patterns, reducing effectiveness over time.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using GRPO with group-relative advantage estimation provides a stable and sample-efficient RL algorithm for LLMs by normalizing rewards within a batch of sampled responses for the same prompt, mitigating reward sparsity and variance.
- **Mechanism:** For each input x, G candidate sequences {y1...yG} are sampled. The sentence-level advantage is `A_sentence_i = (r_i - mean(r_1..r_G)) / std(r_1..r_G)`. This intra-batch normalization makes the advantage signal relative rather than absolute, which is more robust when absolute reward values are noisy or sparse (common with predicted CTCVR). The KL penalty `-β D_KL(π_θ || π_ref)` prevents excessive deviation from the reference model, maintaining text quality.
- **Core assumption:** Relative advantages within a batch are a valid proxy for the true policy gradient, and a sufficiently large batch size (G=5) provides a stable baseline for normalization. The KL penalty strength (β) is well-tuned to balance exploration and language quality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.4.2] Presents the GRPO loss (Eq. 6) and the group-relative advantage formula (Eq. 8). States: "Using intra-batch relative advantage... This formulation allows... synergistic optimization."
  - [section 4.1] Training details specify G=5 candidates per prompt and a global batch size of 1440, providing the sample diversity needed for reliable relative advantage estimation.
  - [section 4.2] Online A/B test result (+9.19% ΔCTCVR) demonstrates that the training procedure leads to a policy that improves a real-world, sparse, delayed-feedback metric (conversion), implying the RL algorithm successfully navigated the exploration-exploitation trade-off.
- **Break condition:** If batch size G is too small (e.g., G=2), the mean/std estimates become noisy, leading to high-variance gradients and unstable training. If the KL penalty β is too low, the model diverges from the reference and generates incoherent or non-compliant text; if too high, learning is stifled and rewards don't improve.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning for Language Models (PPO/GRPO)**
  - **Why needed here:** RELATE uses RL to optimize non-differentiable business metrics (CTCVR). An engineer must understand policy gradients, advantage estimation, and the role of KL regularization to prevent language collapse.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is a reference model and KL penalty necessary when fine-tuning an LLM with RL, and what happens if it's omitted?
- **Concept: Multi-Objective Reward design**
  - **Why needed here:** The system must balance competing goals: conversion lift, compliance, diversity, and base quality. An engineer needs to know how to formulate, weight, and combine such rewards without one dominating.
  - **Quick check question:** If adding a new reward component (e.g., semantic diversity) causes CTCVR to drop in ablation, what are the potential causes and how would you diagnose them?
- **Concept: Token-level vs. Sentence-level Credit Assignment**
  - **Why needed here:** RELATE's key innovation is applying penalties (for blacklisted words, high-frequency n‑grams) at the token level. An engineer must grasp why some rewards naturally decompose to tokens and the pitfalls of forcing decomposition for others.
  - **Quick check question:** For a "relevance" reward that compares the whole ad text to the query, why is it problematic to try to assign credit to individual tokens, and what alternative exists?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Base LLM:** Qwen3-8B, initialized from pre-trained weights and optionally SFT-finetuned. Generates ad text token-by-token.
  2. **Reward Models/Calculators:**
     - CTCVR Predictor: A Shared-Bottom multi-task model (CTR + CTCVR) using Qwen3-Embeddings to score generated text.
     - Quality Rules: Deterministic functions for length, format, and a risk-control module (blacklist lookup).
     - Diversity Calculator: n-gram frequency counter over a batch to identify high-frequency patterns (G_high) and compute penalties.
  3. **RL Trainer (GRPO):** Takes (prompt, sampled responses, per-token and per-sentence rewards), computes group-relative advantages, and updates the LLM policy via the clipped objective with KL penalty.
  4. **Reference Model:** Frozen copy of the policy (at start of current RL step) for KL divergence calculation.
- **Critical path:** Input (query + ad info) -> LLM samples G responses -> Reward calculators produce token-level (for risk/diversity) and sentence-level scores (for CTCVR, semantic quality) -> Group computation normalizes sentence-level rewards per prompt -> Token-level and sentence-level advantages are summed per token -> GRPO loss computed and policy updated.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Reward complexity vs. training stability:** More reward components increase the risk of conflicting gradients. Token-level assignment helps but does not eliminate trade-offs (seen in ablation).
  - **Group size (G) vs. compute/memory:** Larger G gives more reliable advantage estimates but increases memory for sampling and reward calculation. The paper uses G=5.
  - **KL penalty strength (β):** Must be tuned. Too high -> no learning; too low -> language quality collapse. Typically found via small-scale validation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Compliance drops:** Token-level risk penalties (λ1) are too weak or blacklist (V_black) is incomplete. Monitor Compliance Rate metric.
  - **Mode collapse / diversity loss:** Token-level diversity penalty (λ2) is too weak, or the high-frequency n-gram set (G_high) is not updated frequently enough. Monitor Diversity score and human evaluation for repetitive templates.
  - **Fluency degradation:** KL penalty β is too low, or reward scale for sentence-level quality is too high relative to token-level constraints. Monitor perplexity on a held-out set or human evaluation for "bad" samples.
  - **No CTCVR improvement:** CTCVR predictor is misaligned with true online performance, reward scaling is off, or the model is stuck in a poor local optimum due to inadequate exploration (G too small).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation study (as in Table 2/3):** Train models with incremental reward additions and with/without token-level credit. Validate that token-level assignment specifically improves Diversity and Semantic Quality without harming others catastrophically.
  2. **Human evaluation (GSB):** As in Section 4.3. Compare RELATE vs. SFT baseline on semantic correctness and naturalness. This validates that online metric gains do not come at the cost of perceived quality.
  3. **Online A/B test (small scale):** Before full deployment, run a limited traffic test (e.g., 1% of traffic) for 1-2 weeks to measure initial ΔCTR/ΔCTCVR and monitor compliance/complaint rates closely. This is the ultimate validator but comes last due to product risk.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How to integrate semantic diversity rewards to improve text variety beyond n-gram penalties?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work: "explore richer reward designs, such as semantic diversity rewards, and more fine-grained credit assignment strategies."
- **Why unresolved:** Current diversity reward only suppresses frequent n‑grams, missing deeper semantic or stylistic variations that could mitigate fatigue.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace n‑gram penalty with an embedding‑based diversity reward; evaluate on diversity score, CTCVR, and compliance in offline and online tests.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How to assign token‑level credit for sentence‑level rewards to enhance RL efficiency?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work: "more fine‑grained credit assignment strategies."
- **Why unresolved:** Sentence‑level rewards are broadcast uniformly across all tokens, leading to misattribution and slower convergence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a token attribution method (e.g., gradient‑based or attention rollout) to decompose sentence rewards; compare convergence speed and final metrics against current approach.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How to automatically balance multi‑reward conflicts (CTCVR, quality, diversity) to avoid performance trade‑offs?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Ablation results (Table 3, Figure 3) show that adding reward components slows improvement in other metrics, indicating inherent goal conflicts.
- **Why unresolved:** Reward weights are manually fixed; no adaptive mechanism exists to optimize trade‑offs between competing objectives.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct systematic weight sweeps to characterize Pareto frontiers; or incorporate constrained/ multi‑objective RL that dynamically adjusts weights while satisfying constraints.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter opacity and sensitivity**: The paper does not disclose critical hyperparameters such as reward weights (for CTCVR, quality, diversity), token-level penalty coefficients (λ₁, λ₂), KL penalty strength (β), or GRPO clipping parameter (ε). This hinders exact replication and leaves the sensitivity of performance to these settings unknown. Break conditions in Mechanisms 1–3 indicate that mis‑tuning can cause catastrophic forgetting, fluency loss, or unstable training.
- **Domain-specific training data**: The model was trained exclusively on 400k Baidu search advertising samples. Its effectiveness on other ad platforms (e.g., e‑commerce, social media), languages, or cultural contexts remains unproven, limiting claims of generalizability.
- **Diversity limited to surface patterns**: Diversity is encouraged only by penalizing high‑frequency n‑grams. This addresses superficial repetition but does not capture semantic or stylistic diversity, which are important for long‑term user engagement. The open questions explicitly call for richer semantic diversity rewards.
- **Sentence‑level reward broadcasting**: Credits for CTCVR and semantic quality are applied uniformly across all tokens, rather than attributing to specific token contributions. This can lead to misattribution of responsibility and slower convergence, as noted in the future work direction on fine‑grained credit assignment.
- **CTCVR predictor dependency**: The RL policy depends on a predicted CTCVR score from a Shared‑Bottom model. If this predictor is miscalibrated or biased (e.g., due to log‑level noise), the optimization may diverge from the true business objective. The paper does not analyze predictor robustness.
- **GRPO group size considerations**: Group‑relative advantage estimation uses G=5 sampled completions per prompt. While this is typical, the paper does not explore whether a larger G would yield more stable gradients. Break condition for Mechanism 3 warns that too small G leads to high variance, so the chosen G may be borderline.

## Confidence
- **Unified RL framework: Medium** – The end‑to‑end formulation achieved a significant online lift (+9.19% CTCVR), demonstrating practical effectiveness. However, the approach integrates multiple complex components (multi‑dimensional reward, GRPO, KL regularization) whose interactions are not fully elucidated. Undisclosed hyperparameters and the sensitivity indicated by break conditions reduce confidence in robustness and generalizability beyond the reported setting.
- **Token-level credit assignment: Medium** – Ablation studies show that token-level assignment for risk and diversity improves offline metrics (e.g., Diversity 0.965 vs 0.923) and stabilizes training. Yet the technique is limited to constraints with clear token‑level triggers; its applicability to other objectives (e.g., semantic relevance) is unknown. The break condition highlights risk of over‑penalization if λ coefficients are mis‑tuned.
- **GRPO stability: Medium** – The algorithm is designed to stabilize RL for LLMs, and the training converged to a policy that improved online metrics. Nonetheless, key hyperparameters (β, ε, group size G) are not fully disclosed, and the paper does not analyze their impact. The break condition notes that too small G or inappropriate β can cause instability, leaving some uncertainty.
- **Overall CTCVR improvement: High** – The reported +9.19% relative lift is statistically significant and derived from a large‑scale online A/B test on real production traffic. This constitutes strong empirical evidence that the system delivers measurable business value under the tested conditions.
