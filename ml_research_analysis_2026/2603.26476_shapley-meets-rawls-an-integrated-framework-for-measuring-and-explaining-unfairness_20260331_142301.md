# Shapley meets Rawls: an integrated framework for measuring and explaining unfairness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.26476  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26476  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Proposes a two-stage Shapley/Efficient-Symmetric-Linear (ESL) framework that both quantifies group fairness violations and explains their sources via feature contributions, with asymptotic inference for fast statistical testing.

## Executive Summary
This paper unifies fairness quantification and explanation using Shapley values. It defines group fairness criteria (independence, separation, sufficiency) through a first-stage Group-Shapley decomposition of a classification metric (e.g., TPR) across sensitive groups. A second Shapley layer allocates each group's metric share to individual features, pinpointing drivers of disparity. Extending to the ESL family yields linear-time computation (e.g., Equal Surplus) and robustness via majority voting across allocation rules. Applied to the UCI Census Income dataset (gender as sensitive attribute), the method detects significant unfairness in equal opportunity: men contributed 64.95% and women 35.05% of the TPR gain, with Age, Hours/Week, and Marital Status as key opposing drivers. Asymptotic tests match bootstrap accuracy but run in minutes instead of hours; fairness mitigation eliminates the gap.

## Method Summary
The framework first defines a characteristic function *v(m)* as a fairness metric's deviation from a random-classifier baseline. Group-Shapley values over sensitive groups are computed; Theorem 3.1 proves that equality of these values is equivalent to satisfaction of independence, separation, or sufficiency (depending on *m*). Second, each group's Group-Shapley allocation is recursively decomposed over features via Shapley, yielding feature contributions that sum to the group disparity. To reduce computational cost, the authors generalize to ESL values—any allocation rule satisfying efficiency, symmetry, and linearity—where Equal Surplus runs in O(N) vs Shapley's O(2^N). Asymptotic normality is derived for hypothesis testing, avoiding bootstrapping. Robustness is achieved by running multiple ESL values and using majority voting to declare a feature’s cross-group difference significant.

## Key Results
- On UCI Census (gender sensitive), equal opportunity is violated: overall TPR is 1.61× baseline; men contribute 64.95% and women 35.05% of the gain (ESL/Shapley).
- Feature attribution identifies Age, Hours/Week, and Marital Status as primary drivers, with opposite signs across groups (e.g., Age increases men’s TPR but decreases women’s).
- Asymptotic inference matches bootstrap confidence intervals but reduces runtime from >1 hour to ~8 minutes; after EqualizedOdds post-processing, the group gap disappears.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Group-Shapley fairness equivalence
The Group-Shapley value over sensitive groups is defined using *v(m)* as the metric deviation from baseline. Theorem 3.1 proves that independence, separation, and sufficiency hold if and only if Group-Shapley values are equal across groups. This links fairness directly to Shapley's axioms (efficiency, symmetry, dummy, additivity).  
**Evidence**: [PAGE 11-12] (definitions and theorem); [PAGE 6-8] (fairness criteria).

### Mechanism 2: Two-stage feature attribution
After computing group allocation φ_g, a second Shapley over features yields per-feature contributions C_k^g. By Shapley's additivity, the group gap Δφ equals the sum of cross-group feature contribution differences ΔC. This isolates which features drive the disparity.  
**Evidence**: [PAGE 12-13] (Proposition 3.1); [PAGE 13] (Figure 1); [PAGE 20] (Table 2, Census feature contributions).

### Mechanism 3: ESL extension for efficiency and robustness
The framework generalizes to any Efficient-Symmetric-Linear (ESL) value (characterized by coefficients *b_s*). Theorem 4.1 shows equality of group ESL values is equivalent to fairness. Equal Surplus (ES) runs in O(N) vs Shapley's O(2^N). Robustness: compute contributions for several ESL values (Shapley, ES, Solidarity, Consensus, LSP) and declare a feature “unfair” if a majority (e.g., 3/5) indicate a significant cross-group difference.  
**Evidence**: [PAGE 14-15] (ESL definitions); [PAGE 15] (Theorem 4.1); [PAGE 20-21] (runtime and significance consistency tables).

## Foundational Learning
- **Shapley value**: Why needed: Provides axiomatic allocation of a metric’s value to players (groups or features) based on marginal contributions. Quick check: Why does Shapley satisfy efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity? How do these axioms translate into fairness explanations?
- **Group fairness criteria**: Why needed: These are the target notions (independence, separation, sufficiency); understanding their probabilistic definitions guides metric selection (e.g., TPR for separation). Quick check: What distinguishes separation (equalized odds) from sufficiency (conditional use accuracy)? Which corresponds to equal opportunity?
- **Asymptotic inference**: Why needed: Enables fast hypothesis testing for fairness violations without expensive bootstrapping. Quick check: Under the null hypothesis of fairness, what is the asymptotic variance of the difference in group TPR contributions, and how is it estimated from data?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data module → Model module → Metric module → ESL module → Inference module → Mitigation module (optional)

**Critical path**: ESL module’s feature-stage Shapley is the bottleneck; use Equal Surplus for high-dimensional features. Inference module (asymptotic test) is O(N) per ESL value, far faster than bootstrap.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Shapley vs Equal Surplus**: Shapley captures all interactions but is exponential; ES is linear but assumes equal surplus distribution, potentially underweighting interactions.
- **Asymptotic vs Bootstrap**: Asymptotic test is fast but requires large per-group positive counts; bootstrap is reliable for small groups but slow.
- **Majority voting**: Increases robustness but uses a fixed 3/5 threshold; doubling compute for five ESL values remains efficient.

**Failure signatures**:
- Small n⁺_g: asymptotic Z-test inaccurate, wide confidence intervals.
- High feature correlation: Shapley values unstable; consider ES or feature clustering.
- Divergent ESL contributions: If ESL values disagree on sign/direction of a feature’s contribution, majority voting may yield ambiguous conclusions.
- Multi-level sensitive attribute: binary-group formulas require non-trivial extension (Theorem A.1).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Replicate Census results**: Train the soft voting classifier on UCI Census Income (gender sensitive). Verify Table 1 (group-ESL values) and Table 2 (feature contributions). Test H₀: φ_ESL^men = φ_ESL^women; expect rejection at α=0.05.
2. **Compare asymptotic vs bootstrap CIs**: For each feature, compute asymptotic 95% CI (Proposition B.2) and stratified bootstrap CI. Check coverage and width similarity; asymptotic should be orders of magnitude faster.
3. **Assess fairness mitigation**: Apply EqualizedOdds post-processing (AIF360). Re-run two-stage analysis. Expect group-ESL gap to vanish and feature contribution differences to become non-significant.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions. However, based on the limitations and the discussion of the framework's scope, the following questions naturally arise:
- How to extend the Group-Shapley fairness equivalence to multi-level sensitive attributes? (The paper notes this is non-trivial.)
- What are the power and size properties of the asymptotic test for small per-group counts, and how does it compare to bootstrap in such regimes?
- How stable are feature contributions under increasing feature correlation, and do alternative ESL values (e.g., Equal Surplus) mitigate instability?
- Does the majority voting approach (e.g., 3/5 threshold) optimally balance robustness and computational cost, or could adaptive thresholds improve reliability?

These questions align with the paper's own acknowledgment of limitations and suggest avenues for future research.

## Limitations
- Post-hoc evaluation for a fixed classifier ignores potential training biases.
- Assumes a binary sensitive attribute; multi-level extension is complex.
- Asymptotic inference relies on large per-group counts and may fail for small groups.
- Shapley’s exponential cost persists for high-dimensional features; Equal Surplus may underweight interactions.
- Majority voting robustness depends on an arbitrary 3/5 threshold.

## Confidence
- **Group-Shapley fairness equivalence**: High (theoretical proof under assumptions).
- **Two-stage feature attribution**: Medium (empirical validation on one dataset; sensitive to model instability and Shapley variance).
- **ESL efficiency and robustness**: Low (computational gains clear, but robustness hinges on arbitrary voting rule and limited ESL set).
