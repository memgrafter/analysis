# A Very Big Video Reasoning Suite

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20159
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20159
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Fine-tuning Wan-2.2-I2V-A14B on the VBVR-Dataset yields an 84.6% relative gain on the VBVR-Bench (0.371 → 0.685), though still below human performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Very Big Video Reasoning (VBVR) suite to address the scarcity of large-scale, systematic video reasoning data. The suite includes the VBVR-Dataset—1,007,500 video clips across 200 tasks, approximately 1000× larger than prior benchmarks—and VBVR-Bench, a rule-based evaluation framework with high human agreement (Spearman ρ > 0.9). Fine-tuning the Wan-2.2-I2V-A14B model on this dataset significantly improves reasoning performance, demonstrates emergent generalization across domains, and reveals interpretable capability trade-offs via correlation analysis. The work establishes a new foundation for scaling video reasoning research.

## Method Summary
The VBVR-Dataset provides 1,000,000 training videos from 100 tasks and 7,500 test videos spanning 150 tasks (50 in-domain, 50 out-of-domain). Each sample consists of a first-frame image, a text prompt, and a ground-truth solution video. The VBVR-Bench uses rule-based automatic scoring across five cognitive categories (Abstraction, Knowledge, Perception, Spatiality, Transformation) aligned with human judgment. For reproduction, the base model Wan-2.2-I2V-A14B is fine-tuned via LoRA applied to DiT backbone modules (`q`, `k`, `v`, `o`, `ffn.0`, `ffn.2`) with rank 32, using AdamW (LR=1e-4) for one epoch. No architectural changes are made; only training data scale varies.

## Key Results
- The VBVR-Dataset is ~1000× larger than previous video reasoning benchmarks.
- VBVR-Wan2.2 achieves a VBVR-Bench score of 0.685, an 84.6% improvement over the base model’s 0.371, though human performance remains at 0.974.
- Scaling training data from 0 to 500K samples improves in-domain (0.412→0.760) and out-of-domain (0.329→0.610) performance, with a persistent ~15% ID-OOD gap.
- Correlation analysis shows a positive Knowledge–Spatiality link (ρ=0.461) and a negative Knowledge–Perception link (ρ=-0.757) across models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The dramatic performance gain from data scaling suggests that video reasoning abilities emerge with sufficient diverse training examples. The rule-based evaluation ensures reliable, interpretable metrics that correlate highly with human judgment, enabling consistent measurement of subtle reasoning skills. The positive Knowledge–Spatiality and negative Knowledge–Perception correlations indicate that models develop specialized, trade-off-bound capabilities rather than uniform mastery, which aligns with the dataset’s design separating cognitive categories.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Large-scale synthetic video generation**  
   *Why needed*: Systematic video reasoning requires diverse, controlled scenarios that real-world datasets lack.  
   *Quick check*: Verify dataset contains ≥1M clips with uniform specs (24 fps, 512x512).

2. **Rule-based evaluation with human alignment**  
   *Why needed*: Learned metrics often fail on complex reasoning; rules provide transparency and consistency.  
   *Quick check*: Confirm Spearman ρ > 0.9 between automatic scores and human annotations on a sample.

3. **Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) for large video models**  
   *Why needed*: Full fine-tuning of 14B models is prohibitively costly; LoRA offers a scalable alternative.  
   *Quick check*: Ensure LoRA adapters are correctly applied to DiT modules (`q,k,v,o,ffn`).

4. **Cognitive category decomposition**  
   *Why needed*: Isolating reasoning skills (Abstraction, Knowledge, etc.) enables targeted analysis.  
   *Quick check*: Each task is labeled with at least one primary cognitive category; rubrics score per category.

5. **In-domain vs. out-of-domain generalization testing**  
   *Why needed*: Measures robustness beyond task-specific memorization.  
   *Quick check*: Test set includes 50 overlapping (ID) and 50 novel (OOD) tasks relative to training tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input (first_frame.png, prompt.txt) → DiT backbone (Wan-2.2-I2V-A14B, with LoRA on q,k,v,o,ffn.0,ffn.2) → Decoder → Output video frames → Rule-based scorer (VBVR-Bench) → Category scores → Overall VBVR score.

**Critical path**: Data loading → LoRA forward pass → Loss computation → Backward pass → Checkpoint → Evaluation on VBVR-Bench.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- LoRA reduces memory but may limit adaptation depth compared to full fine-tuning.  
- Rule-based scoring ensures interpretability but may not capture all nuanced human judgments.  
- Synthetic dataset offers scale and control but may not fully replicate real-world complexity.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Training loss decreases but evaluation scores stagnate → LoRA misconfiguration or data leakage.  
- Base model score on VBVR-Bench deviates significantly from 0.371 → evaluation script mismatch.  
- OOD scores near ID scores → test/train split contamination.

**First three experiments**:  
1. Run official VBVR-Bench on unmodified Wan-2.2-I2V-A14B; confirm overall score ~0.371.  
2. Audit 100 random training samples for video integrity (playable, correct resolution/framerate) and prompt validity.  
3. Fine-tune on 10K samples; verify loss decreases and evaluate on a held-out subset to confirm learning signal.

## Limitations
- Training hardware and hyperparameters (GPU count, batch size, gradient accumulation) are unreported, hindering exact replication.  
- Task split definitions (which 50 tasks are ID vs. OOD) are unspecified, preventing precise generalization gap verification.  
- Preprocessing details (frame rate/resolution uniformity, temporal padding) are not fully documented.

## Confidence
- **Dataset scale (1000× larger)**: High  
- **Human-aligned scoring (Spearman ρ > 0.9)**: Medium  
- **Fine-tuning gains (0.371 → 0.685)**: Low  
- **Generalization (ID-OOD gap ~15%)**: Low  
- **Correlation findings (Knowledge–Spatiality ρ=0.461, Knowledge–Perception ρ=-0.757)**: Medium
