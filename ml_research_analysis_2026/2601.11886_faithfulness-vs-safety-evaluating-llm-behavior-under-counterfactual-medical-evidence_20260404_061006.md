# Faithfulness vs. Safety: Evaluating LLM Behavior Under Counterfactual Medical Evidence

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.11886
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11886
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMs overwhelmingly accept counterfactual medical evidence (including toxic substances) as true, showing high evidence adherence and low uncertainty, with minimal improvement from scaling, medical fine-tuning, or skeptical prompting.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) behave when presented with counterfactual medical evidence, exposing a fundamental tension between faithfulness to provided context and safety constraints. The authors create MedCounterFact, a dataset derived from MedEvidence by substituting real medical interventions with four types of counterfactual stimuli: nonce words, medical terms for other conditions, non-medical objects, and toxic substances. They evaluate nine frontier LLMs across multiple prompts and response formats, measuring Evidence Adherence (EA), Uncertain rates, and analyzing reasoning traces for awareness of implausibility. Results show that models almost always accept even dangerous or absurd evidence as true, maintaining high EA and low uncertainty, and rarely acknowledge implausibility. Neither model scaling, medical fine-tuning, nor skeptical prompting significantly mitigates the problem, indicating a critical vulnerability for medical LLM deployment.

## Method Summary
The study uses MedCounterFact, containing 809 clinical comparison question-evidence pairs. Each instance includes a question and evidence where the true intervention is replaced by a counterfactual (one of four types). The ground truth answer (Higher, Lower, No Difference) is inherited from the original valid evidence. The authors perform zero-shot evaluation of nine LLMs using four prompt variants (No Evidence, With Evidence, Skeptical, Expert) and two output formats (multiple-choice and free-form). Primary metrics are Evidence Adherence (the proportion of model answers matching the original ground truth) and Uncertain rate (responses labeled Uncertain). They also classify reasoning traces via an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5) for implausibility awareness (Explicit, Implicit, No). Additionally, they conduct representation analysis on Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, training a linear probe to distinguish intervention terms from non-interventions and tracking how counterfactual terms shift into the intervention space across mentions.

## Key Results
- Models exhibit very high Evidence Adherence to counterfactual evidence (often >80%), comparable to performance on valid evidence, demonstrating unconditional acceptance of the provided context.
- Adding any evidence (including implausible or toxic) drastically reduces expressed uncertainty; Uncertain rates remain low even when evidence is contradictory or dangerous, and pre-generation probability distributions already concentrate on a single answer.
- Explicit recognition of implausibility is rare (<20% overall); none of the tested models refused to answer, and skeptical or expert prompting yields only marginal improvements, while scaling and medical fine-tuning fail to mitigate the issue.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Contextual override of parametric knowledge
- **Claim:** Models overwhelmingly accept counterfactual evidence at face value, overriding their parametric knowledge and built‑in safety constraints.
- **Mechanism:** When provided with evidence (even fabricated or toxic), the model’s internal representations rapidly shift: counterfactual intervention terms merge into the “intervention” region of the embedding space within a few mentions, suppressing earlier activations of the true meaning. This causes the model to reason as if the false evidence were genuine.
- **Core assumption:** The training objective heavily weights adherence to contextual information, which can dominate prior knowledge when context is coherent and formal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “...existing models overwhelmingly accept such ‘evidence’ at face value even when it is dangerous or implausible, and provide confident and uncaveated answers. ... models overemphasize the former [faithfulness].”
  - [Section 4] “models rarely refuse, express uncertainty, or fall back to parametric priors; instead, transient safety signals are quickly overridden, yielding confident yet unsafe reasoning.”
  - [corpus] Neighboring work on context‑faithfulness (e.g., *FaithEval*, arXiv:2410.03727) discusses similar risks but does not directly examine parametric override; corpus support for this specific mechanism is limited.
- **Break condition:** If future models systematically detect and reject implausible evidence, or if safety guardrails successfully intervene before reasoning, this mechanism would no longer dominate.

### Mechanism 2: Evidence collapses uncertainty
- **Claim:** The presence of any evidence (including counterfactual) drastically reduces the model’s expressed uncertainty, making it confident even in absurd scenarios.
- **Mechanism:** Introducing evidence sharply concentrates the model’s pre‑generation probability distribution onto a single answer label (often the one matching the original ground‑truth) and almost eliminates the “Uncertain” token probability. This happens before any token is emitted, indicating a fundamental shift in the model’s internal certainty.
- **Core assumption:** Models treat the provided evidence as definitive and do not maintain an uncertainty buffer for potential flaws.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Uncertain rates remain low with evidence, and explicit recognition of implausibility is rare (<20%).”
  - [Section 5] “Introducing evidence sharply concentrates probability mass on a single answer label and almost eliminates Uncertain, even before any token is generated.”
  - [corpus] Limited direct corpus evidence; studies on medical hallucination (e.g., *CARES*, arXiv:2503.05777) note low uncertainty expression but focus on different failure modes.
- **Break condition:** If adding evidence (especially adversarial) increases “Uncertain” rates substantially, the collapse effect would be mitigated.

### Mechanism 3: Standard mitigations fail
- **Claim:** Increasing model scale, applying medical fine‑tuning, and using skeptical or expert prompts produce only marginal improvements in detecting counterfactual evidence.
- **Mechanism:** The bias toward contextual fidelity is robust across architectures and prompting strategies. Even purpose‑built medical models (e.g., HuatuoGPT‑o1) and chain‑of‑thought variants do not significantly raise “Uncertain” rates or implausibility awareness.
- **Core assumption:** The underlying training objective (next‑token prediction) and the nature of in‑context learning cannot be easily redirected toward safety‑first behavior without fundamental architectural changes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Neither model scaling nor medical fine‑tuning mitigates the issue; skeptical prompting yields only marginal improvements.”
  - [Section 4] “None of the models, regardless of size or training paradigm, were sensitive to the counterfactuals, with average increases in Uncertain rates all below 0.13.” (Figure 4)
  - [corpus] Neighboring work on counterfactual safety (e.g., *Enhancing RL Safety with Counterfactual LLM Reasoning*, arXiv:2409.10188) suggests alternative post‑training methods, implying current LLM safety is insufficient.
- **Break condition:** If a new technique (e.g., targeted reinforcement learning, retrieval validation) yields a substantial drop in Evidence Adherence to counterfactuals or a rise in uncertainty, this mechanism would be overcome.

## Foundational Learning
### In‑context learning vs. parametric knowledge
- **Why needed here:** The paper’s setup creates a conflict between the model’s pre‑trained medical knowledge and the presented evidence. Understanding this conflict explains why models suppress valid priors.
- **Quick check question:** In the experiments, which source dominates the model’s final answer when evidence is present: its internal knowledge or the provided context?

### Uncertainty calibration in LLMs
- **Why needed here:** The low “Uncertain” rates reveal a failure to recognize when evidence is insufficient or dangerous, a key safety gap.
- **Quick check question:** What happens to the “Uncertain” label frequency when evidence (even false) is added compared to the no‑evidence condition?

### Safety guardrails in medical AI
- **Why needed here:** The models’ refusal to reject toxic interventions shows that existing safety mechanisms are ineffective in the presence of coherent context.
- **Quick check question:** Did any model refuse to answer questions involving toxic substances like mustard gas or heroin when presented with supporting “evidence”?

### Evidence‑based medical QA
- **Why needed here:** The task (synthesizing RCTs) mirrors real‑world systematic reviews; failures here have high‑stakes implications.
- **Quick check question:** What type of question does MedCounterFact use, and what does it ask the model to do with the evidence?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input parser → Retriever (optional) → Evidence formatter → LLM core → Safety filter → Uncertainty estimator

### Critical path
User query → Input parser → (Retriever) → Evidence formatter → LLM core → [Safety filter → Uncertainty estimator] → Final answer

### Design tradeoffs
- Faithfulness vs. safety: Strict adherence improves consistency but risks accepting dangerous misinformation.
- Medical fine-tuning vs. general training: Medical tuning does not improve safety, suggesting knowledge alone is insufficient.
- Constrained output (multiple‑choice) vs. free‑form: Free‑form yields fewer explicit uncertainties, heightening risk.

### Failure signatures
- High Evidence Adherence (EA) to counterfactual evidence (>0.8 in many cases).
- Low “Uncertain” rates despite absurd interventions.
- Reasoning traces lacking any mention of implausibility (“No” awareness in >70% of toxic cases).
- Linear probe shows counterfactual terms quickly enter the intervention embedding space after ~6 mentions.
- No model refusals across the entire suite.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Run MedCounterFact (with original and counterfactual interventions) on the target model to establish EA and Uncertain benchmarks. Use both multiple‑choice and free‑form prompts.
2. **Representation drift analysis:** Train a linear probe (logistic regression) to distinguish medical interventions from non‑medical objects, then extract hidden states for a counterfactual term (e.g., “toaster”) across evidence mentions to see if it drifts into the “intervention” cluster (replicating Section 6).
3. **Prompt‑variant stress test:** Compare baseline Evd, Skept+Evd, and Expert+Evd prompts. If improvements are marginal (<0.1 increase in Uncertain or Implausibility‑Awareness rates), conclude that prompting alone is inadequate and investigate architectural changes (e.g., external fact‑checkers, reinforcement learning with safety rewards).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: How can we formally define and operationalize a boundary between evidence faithfulness and safety constraints in medical LLMs?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state that "where to draw the boundary between faithfulness and safety is unclear" and that "future work on identifying stable fallback boundaries between parametric knowledge and contextual evidence is therefore essential."
- **Why unresolved:** Current models show no such boundary; they overwhelmingly accept counterfactual evidence even when it is dangerous or implausible.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of new training paradigms, fine-tuning objectives, or architectural mechanisms that explicitly balance adherence to provided context with the invocation of safety guardrails, evaluated on counterfactual benchmarks like MedCounterFact.

### Open Question 2: What specific interventions (e.g., prompting strategies, model architectures, training data) can effectively cause LLMs to prioritize safety over blind adherence to implausible contextual evidence?
- **Basis in paper:** The results show that "skeptical prompting yields only marginal improvements" and that "neither model scaling nor medical fine-tuning mitigates the issue."
- **Why unresolved:** Existing methods (skeptical/expert prompting, scale, medical fine-tuning) fail to significantly increase uncertainty or refusal rates for toxic or nonsensical interventions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic ablation studies testing novel prompting techniques (e.g., explicit uncertainty induction, verification chains), safety‑focused fine‑tuning, or modular architecture modifications, measuring safety failure rates on MedCounterFact.

### Open Question 3: Does the rapid distributional shift of counterfactual terms into the "intervention space" (overriding parametric knowledge) generalize across all counterfactual stimulus types (NONCE, MEDICAL, NON-MEDICAL, TOXIC) and model families?
- **Basis in paper:** The representation analysis is a single‑case study on the term "toaster" (NON‑MEDICAL). The paper notes this as a mechanism but does not generalize.
- **Why unresolved:** The linear probe analysis was conducted only for one NON‑MEDICAL term on one model (Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct); it is unknown if similar rapid shifts occur for TOXIC or MEDICAL terms or across different model architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the linear probe and cosine similarity analysis across a diverse set of counterfactual terms from all four categories and a wider range of models to compare shift dynamics and rates.

### Open Question 4: How reliable are LLM‑based evaluators (e.g., Claude Sonnet 4.5) for assessing safety failure phenomena like implausibility awareness, and how do their biases affect reported trends?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors acknowledge reliance on Claude for classification and note that this "risks propagating the judge model’s own limitations," though they validated with a small human sample (n=70).
- **Why unresolved:** The validity of core metrics (Implausibility Awareness, answer extraction) hinges on the judge model's impartiality; its own safety tuning may systematically under‑ or over‑detection of unsafe reasoning patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a large‑scale, multi‑annotator human evaluation of model reasoning traces for implausibility awareness, and compare judgments against multiple diverse LLM‑as‑judge models to quantify and correct for judge bias.

## Limitations
- Dataset construction may have subtle biases (e.g., replacement process) that affect generalizability.
- Proprietary model opacity (Gemini, GPT‑5‑mini) limits reproducibility and internal analysis.
- Implausibility awareness assessment relies on a single LLM judge (Claude Sonnet) with limited human validation, risking systematic bias.
