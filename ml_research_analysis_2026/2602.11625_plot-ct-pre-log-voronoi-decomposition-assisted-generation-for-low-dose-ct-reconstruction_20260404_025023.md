# PLOT-CT: Pre-log Voronoi Decomposition Assisted Generation for Low-dose CT Reconstruction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11625
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11625
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** Achieves 42.19 PSNR, 0.9721 SSIM, and 6.38e-5 MSE at 1e4 incident photons, representing a 2.36 dB PSNR gain over traditional methods; consistently outperforms six baselines across AAPM and CHAOS datasets at doses 1e4, 5e3, and 1e3 photons.

## Executive Summary
PLOT-CT introduces a novel framework for low-dose CT reconstruction that operates directly on raw pre-log sinograms to avoid noise amplification from logarithmic transformation. The method partitions the sinogram into three intensity-based clusters—high, low, and background—using K-means–driven Voronoi decomposition, which locally stabilizes gradient magnitudes. Within each cluster, a diffusion transformer iteratively denoises the latent representation; a subsequent transformer block with multi-head transposed attention and a gated feed‑forward network refines features across clusters. The refined clusters are fused, converted to post‑log, and reconstructed via filtered back projection. Extensive evaluation on AAPM and CHAOS datasets demonstrates state‑of‑the‑art performance, with up to 2.36 dB PSNR improvement at 1e4 photons and superior visual quality with fewer artifacts.

## Method Summary
PLOT-CT processes a low-dose pre‑log sinogram through four main stages. First, the **Pre‑log Sinogram Decomposition Block (PSDB)** applies K‑means clustering (k=3) to separate the sinogram into three intensity‑based clusters, creating distinct latent spaces. The clusters are concatenated to form a multi‑channel latent vector. Second, a **Diffusion Block** employs a Markov chain: a forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to the latent, while a reverse process trains a transformer‑based network to predict and remove the noise, denoising the representation over T steps. Third, a **Transformer Block** with Multi‑head Transposed Attention (MTA) and Gated Feed‑Forward Network (GFN) models long‑range dependencies across the three clusters, further refining features. Fourth, the refined clusters are fused by element‑wise summation, transformed to the post‑log domain via a logarithm, and reconstructed into a CT image using filtered back projection. Training follows a two‑stage Adam optimization: the transformer is first pre‑trained on clean clusters, then the diffusion model is trained with the pre‑trained transformer providing conditioning.

## Key Results
- At 1e4 incident photons, PLOT‑CT achieves 42.19 PSNR, 0.9721 SSIM, and 6.38e‑5 MSE on the AAPM dataset, a 2.36 dB improvement over traditional methods.
- It consistently outperforms six baseline methods (Pix2Pix, NCSN++, CaGAN, Re‑UNet, WiTUnet, SDCNN) across multiple noise levels (1e4, 5e3, 1e3 photons) on both AAPM and CHAOS datasets.
- Visual results show reduced artifacts and better preservation of fine structures compared to competitors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PLOT‑CT’s superiority arises from three interconnected mechanisms.

1. **Gradient stabilization via Voronoi decomposition**  
   K‑means clustering groups pixels with similar attenuation values into three regions. Within each cluster, the true line integral \(P\) varies little, so the exponential factor \(e^{-P}\) in the gradient (derived from the Beer–Lambert law) is approximately constant. This yields locally uniform gradient scales, preventing noise amplification during reconstruction. The paper mathematically shows that with local averaging \(P_k\), the gradient becomes \(\Delta I \approx I e^{-P_k} \Delta P\), confirming the stabilization.

2. **Diffusion‑based denoising in latent space**  
   After clustering, each latent representation is denoised by a diffusion model. The forward process incrementally adds Gaussian noise; the reverse process trains a transformer to estimate the added noise and recover the clean feature. This additive noise model fits well with the pre‑log data’s statistics and allows iterative refinement without destroying structural details.

3. **Transformer refinement with MTA and GFN**  
   A dedicated Transformer block—comprising Multi‑head Transposed Attention (MTA) and a Gated Feed‑Forward Network (GFN)—models long‑range dependencies between the three latent clusters. MTA captures global interactions, while GFN emphasizes informative features through a gating mechanism. This cross‑cluster integration enhances feature discriminability and ensures a coherent final pre‑log sinogram.

## Foundational Learning
Understanding PLOT‑CT requires familiarity with four core concepts:

- **Pre‑log projection domain**  
  *Why needed:* The method operates on raw photon counts before logarithmic conversion. Knowing the Poisson noise model and its amplification after the log step explains why early processing is beneficial.  
  *Quick check:* What is the primary statistical characteristic of noise in pre‑log CT measurements and why does it become approximately additive Gaussian after taking the logarithm?

- **Voronoi decomposition / K‑means clustering**  
  *Why needed:* The core novelty is the Voronoi‑based partition of the sinogram. Clustering creates locally homogeneous gradient environments.  
  *Quick check:* How does K‑means grouping of intensities address the exponential gradient scaling problem derived from the Beer–Lambert law?

- **Diffusion models**  
  *Why needed:* The denoising engine within each latent space is a diffusion process. Understanding the forward and reverse processes is crucial for the training objective.  
  *Quick check:* What are the two main stochastic processes in a diffusion model and how do they interact during training?

- **Transformer attention (MTA)**  
  *Why needed:* The Transformer block refines features across clusters; its attention mechanism is adapted for the spatial structure of sinograms.  
  *Quick check:* What modification does multi‑head transposed attention introduce compared to standard transformer attention, and why is it suitable for this task?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
PSDB -> Concatenation (3‑channel latent) -> Diffusion Block (transformer‑based denoising) -> Transformer Block (MTA + GFN) -> Fusion (summation) -> Log Transform -> FBP

**Critical path:**  
1. Input low‑dose pre‑log sinogram.  
2. Decompose into three clusters via PSDB.  
3. Concatenate clusters to form multi‑channel latent.  
4. Perform T‑step diffusion: at each step, predict noise with transformer, update latent.  
5. Apply Transformer Block for cross‑cluster refinement.  
6. Fuse refined clusters by summation.  
7. Convert to post‑log domain via logarithm.  
8. Reconstruct image with filtered back projection.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Number of clusters:* 3 balances quantization error and model capacity; more clusters risk over‑fitting, fewer leave gradient imbalance.  
- *Diffusion steps T:* More steps improve denoising but increase inference time; paper does not disclose exact value.  
- *Latent dimension:* Higher dimension preserves details but demands more memory and data.  
- *Transformer depth:* Deeper networks capture complex dependencies but may cause training instability or over‑fitting.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Poor clustering:* Overlapping intensity distributions lead to fragmented clusters and residual gradient variance.  
- *Diffusion collapse:* Over‑smoothed results, loss of fine textures, PSNR drop at low doses.  
- *Transformer overfitting:* Performance degrades on unseen noise levels or datasets despite good training metrics.  
- *Numerical instability:* Exploding gradients early in training; may require gradient clipping.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Main evaluation** on AAPM Challenge dataset at photon levels 1e4, 5e3, 1e3, comparing PSNR/SSIM/MSE against six baselines.  
2. **Generalization test** on CHAOS CT dataset with the same noise levels to assess cross‑dataset robustness.  
3. **Ablation study** varying the number of clusters (1,2,3,4) and removing either Diffusion Block or PSDB to quantify each component’s contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Optimal number of clusters:** The paper selects three clusters via ablation, but the theoretical justification for why *k* = 3 is universally optimal across noise levels, anatomies, and scanner geometries remains unclear. The proof in Section IV shows an optimal *k* exists but does not derive its value as a function of data statistics or physical parameters. Without an analytical characterization, the generalizability of *k* = 3 to ultra‑low doses (<1e3 photons) or spectral CT is uncertain.

2. **Necessity of the Transformer component:** The method combines diffusion and a Transformer block, but it is unknown whether the Transformer is indispensable. Could a simpler architecture (e.g., CNN residual block) or a different generative model (GAN, flow) achieve comparable performance when applied to the same decomposed latent spaces? The paper lacks an ablation replacing the Transformer while keeping the PSDB and diffusion, leaving the unique contribution of the Transformer unclear.

3. **Impact of cluster boundaries:** K‑means clustering is purely intensity‑based and may produce spatially fragmented clusters, especially at tissue interfaces. The fusion step (simple summation) might not reconcile features at cluster boundaries, potentially causing blurring or new artifacts. The paper does not analyze reconstruction quality specifically at these boundaries or compare with spatially regularized clustering.

4. **Comparison with hybrid domain methods:** The introduction criticizes post‑log and image‑domain approaches, claiming pre‑log processing is superior. However, recent hybrid methods (citations [8,23‑25]) combine pre‑log and post‑log domains. The experimental evaluation does not include these hybrids, so the claimed advantage of pure pre‑log processing remains untested against the latest hybrid frameworks.

5. **Extreme low‑dose and spectral CT:** The lowest dose tested is 1e3 photons/ray; clinical ultra‑low‑dose often targets <1e3. At such extreme doses, the pre‑log statistics become highly non‑Gaussian and sparse, possibly violating assumptions of the diffusion model and K‑means. Moreover, spectral CT adds energy bins, making the data 3‑D; the current 2‑D sinogram decomposition may not extend naturally. The paper does not address these scenarios.

## Limitations
- **Omitted implementation details** (latent encoder architecture, diffusion hyperparameters, FBP parameters) hinder exact reproduction.
- **Clustering instability** due to K‑means initialization may lead to variable partitions across sinograms, affecting reproducibility.
- **Lack of direct empirical validation** for gradient stabilization claim and insufficient analysis of overfitting risks in diffusion and transformer stages.

## Confidence
- Gradient stabilization via Voronoi decomposition: **Medium** (theoretical derivation sound but lacks quantitative validation).
- Diffusion‑based denoising: **High** (well‑established method and strongly supported by results).
- Transformer refinement: **Medium** (attention mechanisms promising but architectural details under‑specified).

## Next Checks
