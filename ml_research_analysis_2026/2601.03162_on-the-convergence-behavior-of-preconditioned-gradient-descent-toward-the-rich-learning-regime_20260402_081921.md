# On the Convergence Behavior of Preconditioned Gradient Descent Toward the Rich Learning Regime

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03162
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03162
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Preconditioned gradient descent (PGD) equalizes convergence across frequency modes by conditioning the NTK matrix, reducing spectral bias and compressing grokking delays, but PGD alone yields lower final test accuracy; switching to first-order optimizers like AdamW after PGD restores generalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies preconditioned gradient descent (PGD) methods—such as Gauss-Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt—to address spectral bias and grokking in neural networks. Theoretically, under the lazy (NTK) regime, PGD equalizes convergence rates across frequency modes by conditioning the NTK matrix, leading to uniform error decay. Empirically, on tasks including 1D regression, PINNs, modular arithmetic, and MNIST, PGD accelerates uniform mode convergence and compresses the grokking delay (the gap between training loss minimization and test accuracy improvement). However, PGD alone often results in lower final test accuracy. The authors show that transitioning to a first-order optimizer like AdamW after a PGD phase restores generalization, suggesting PGD efficiently explores the NTK subspace but may not effectively transition to the rich, feature-learning regime.

## Method Summary
The method employs a two-phase training strategy. In the first phase, preconditioned gradient descent (PGD) is used with a preconditioner derived from the network’s Jacobian (e.g., Levenberg-Marquardt: \(M = \mu I + J^T J\); Gauss-Newton: approximates \(J^T J\) pseudoinverse). This preconditioner rescales gradients to equalize convergence across all frequency modes under the NTK linearization. The PGD phase runs for a fixed number of iterations or until an NTK-regime indicator triggers. In the second phase, training switches to a standard first-order optimizer (e.g., AdamW) to further improve generalization by enabling feature learning beyond the NTK subspace. The approach requires computing Jacobian-vector products, solving linear systems (via direct inversion or iterative methods like conjugate gradient), and managing a damping parameter \(\mu\).

## Key Results
- PGD theoretically equalizes convergence rates across frequency modes by conditioning the NTK matrix, mitigating spectral bias in the lazy regime.
- Empirically, PGD accelerates uniform mode convergence and compresses the grokking delay on tasks like modular addition and MNIST.
- PGD alone often yields lower final test accuracy; switching to AdamW after PGD restores or improves generalization, indicating PGD’s strength is in rapid NTK-subspace exploration but not in escaping to the rich regime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Equalizing Convergence Rates
**Claim**: PGD equalizes convergence rates across frequency modes by conditioning the NTK matrix, reducing spectral bias in the lazy regime.  
**Mechanism**: In the NTK regime, the network’s Jacobian \(J\) and kernel \(K = JJ^T\) are approximately constant. PGD uses a preconditioner (e.g., Levenberg-Marquardt: \(M = \mu I + J^T J\)) that yields error dynamics \(\partial \hat{e}_i/\partial t = -\lambda_i/(\mu+\lambda_i) \hat{e}_i\) for mode \(i\), or effectively uniform decay for Gauss-Newton above a threshold \(\varepsilon\). This rescales the effective learning rate by \(1/\lambda_i\), counteracting the spectral bias that favors low-frequency modes.  
**Core assumption**: The network operates in the lazy (NTK) regime where the linearization holds and the kernel is approximately constant.  
**Break condition**: The preconditioning relies on the fixed NTK approximation; once features evolve significantly (rich regime), the preconditioner based on the current \(J\) may no longer equalize modes and the guarantee fails.

### Mechanism 2: Compressing Grokking Delay
**Claim**: PGD compresses the grokking delay by enabling uniform exploration of the NTK subspace, leading to earlier alignment of training and test performance.  
**Mechanism**: Grokking arises because standard optimizers exhibit spectral bias: low-frequency modes (abundant in training data) converge quickly, while high-frequency modes (often critical for test accuracy) converge slowly. PGD accelerates convergence of all modes, so the point where high-frequency, test-relevant modes are learned occurs earlier, reducing the gap between memorization (train loss near zero) and generalization.  
**Core assumption**: The grokking delay is caused by the need to first explore the linear NTK subspace before transitioning to a feature-rich regime that generalizes; test error is dominated by high-frequency modes initially neglected due to spectral bias.  
**Break condition**: If test generalization depends on factors beyond spectral bias (e.g., weight decay, dataset size), PGD may not fully eliminate the delay. Effectiveness diminishes once dynamics leave the NTK regime.

### Mechanism 3: Switching to First-Order Optimizers Restores Generalization
**Claim**: Starting with PGD and then switching to a first-order optimizer like AdamW restores final generalization that PGD alone cannot achieve, because first-order methods better navigate the rich, feature-learning regime.  
**Mechanism**: PGD efficiently finds a solution within the NTK subspace by rapidly reducing error across all modes, but this subspace may not contain the globally generalizing solution. First-order optimizers, while slower in the NTK regime, can explore beyond the linearization into parameter directions that change the NTK itself (feature learning). Switching after the lazy regime is largely exhausted inherits PGD’s speed and lets AdamW escape the NTK subspace for better generalization.  
**Core assumption**: Transition from lazy to rich regime is necessary for high generalization on certain tasks; PGD tends to stay near the NTK solution because its preconditioner assumes a fixed feature map.  
**Break condition**: Benefit depends on the task having a rich regime that generalizes better; if the solution is already in the NTK subspace, switching may not help or could degrade. Switching point must be chosen appropriately.

## Foundational Learning
- **Spectral bias**: The tendency of wide neural networks to fit low-frequency components of the target function faster than high-frequency ones. *Why needed*: It is the core problem PGD aims to solve. *Quick check*: When training a wide neural network on a target function with high-frequency components, which frequencies converge first?
- **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime**: A lazy training regime where the network’s Jacobian remains approximately constant, leading to linearized dynamics governed by the NTK. *Why needed*: PGD’s theoretical analysis assumes this regime; its benefits are confined there. *Quick check*: How can you tell if a network is in the NTK regime during training?
- **Grokking transition**: A phenomenon where test accuracy improves long after training loss has reached zero, often seen in algorithmic tasks. *Why needed*: The paper links PGD’s acceleration to compression of the grokking delay. *Quick check*: Does test accuracy improve long after training loss has reached zero?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**:  
Network (differentiable architecture requiring Jacobian-vector products) → Loss (MSE or cross-entropy) → Preconditioner (\(M = \mu I + J^T J\) for least-squares; \(J^T H J\) for cross-entropy via GGN) → Solver (direct inversion or iterative conjugate gradient, matrix-free) → Damping schedule (constant or decaying \(\mu\)) → Switch triggers (fixed iterations or NTK change indicator).

**Critical Path**:
1. Initialize network.
2. For each PGD iteration:
   a. Forward pass to get predictions \(\hat{y}\).
   b. Compute loss and residual \(r = \hat{y} - y\) (MSE) or logits.
   c. Compute Jacobian \(J = \nabla_\theta f\) via autograd (jvp/vjp).
   d. Build preconditioner \(M = \mu I + J^T J\) (or GGN) and solve \(M \delta\theta = J^T r\) using CG or SMW.
   e. Update parameters: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \delta\theta\).
3. Monitor training loss or NTK spectrum to decide when PGD phase ends.
4. Switch to first-order optimizer (e.g., AdamW) and continue training.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- \(\mu\) vs. stability: Small \(\mu\) strengthens preconditioning but risks ill-conditioning; large \(\mu\) makes PGD similar to GD. Damping schedules help.
- Computational cost: Jacobian computation \(O(N \cdot p)\) per batch; iterative solvers reduce memory; SMW shifts cost to \((JJ^T)^{-1}\) (\(O(n^3)\)) but \(n \ll p\).
- Batch size: Larger batches give better \(J\) approximation but increase memory; full batch may be needed for accurate GN.
- CG tolerance: Loose tolerance reduces cost but degrades spectral equalization; trade-off depends on hardware.
- Switching point: Fixed iterations may be suboptimal; adaptive switching based on validation loss or NTK constancy could improve speed/accuracy trade-off.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Loss spikes/divergence: Step size too large, \(\mu\) too small, or inaccurate CG solution. Reduce \(\eta\), increase \(\mu\), tighten CG tolerance.
- No reduction in spectral bias: Preconditioner ineffective (e.g., \(\mu\) too large, wrong matrix). Check eigenvalues of \(J^T J\).
- Poor final accuracy with pure PGD: Test accuracy plateaus low; consider switching to first-order.
- High memory usage: Using full \(J^T J\) for large \(p\); switch to matrix-free CG or approximations (e.g., K-FAC).

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. **Frequency mode equalization**: Train a 2‑layer MLP on a 1‑D target with multiple harmonics (e.g., \(u(x)=\sum_{k=1}^3 k\sin((2k+1)\pi x - k)\)). Compute FFT of residuals each epoch for SGD, LM, GN. Expect uniform decay for PGD methods per Lemmas 3.2/3.3.
2. **Grokking compression on modular arithmetic**: Train a shallow MLP on addition modulo \(p\) with output scaling \(\alpha>1\). Compare training/test accuracy curves for SGD vs LM across \(\alpha\). Observe reduced gap (Figures 5,6). Track error in highest frequency mode along training/test subspaces.
3. **Switching strategy on MNIST**: Train a 2‑layer MLP on MNIST with LM for first \(T\) iterations (e.g., 2000), then switch to AdamW (lr \(10^{-3}\), weight decay 0.1). Compare final test accuracy with pure LM and pure AdamW. Expect switching to recover high accuracy (Figure 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What are the convergence properties of preconditioned gradient descent (e.g., Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt) in the rich, feature-learning regime beyond the NTK approximation?  
  *Basis*: The authors state that further work on convergence in the rich regime is of particular interest; their analysis relies on the NTK regime.  
  *Why unresolved*: The NTK linearization breaks down when features evolve significantly; without a non-linear theory, effectiveness in the rich regime is unclear.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: A theoretical convergence analysis without NTK assumptions, or extensive experiments tracking NTK condition number and feature alignment across training to map transitions.

- **Open Question 2**: How does the relative size of the training and test datasets influence the grokking phenomenon?  
  *Basis*: The authors note their approach does not address train/test dataset sizes as a likely contributor to grokking.  
  *Why unresolved*: Experiments used fixed splits (e.g., 90/10 for modular arithmetic); scaling with data quantity and PGD’s consistency across data regimes are unknown.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: Systematic experiments varying training/test sample sizes, measuring grokking metrics (time to generalization, final accuracy) under SGD and PGD; theoretical models incorporating dataset size.

- **Open Question 3**: Why do higher-order optimizers (e.g., Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt) often achieve lower final test accuracy despite reducing the grokking delay?  
  *Basis*: The paper shows PGD accelerates uniform mode convergence and compresses delay, but final test accuracy is lower than first-order methods; switching to AdamW later restores accuracy. The authors hypothesize PGD may remain near the lazy regime but do not explain why this leads to poorer generalization.  
  *Why unresolved*: The mechanism—whether PGD converges to a wide minimum within the NTK subspace, overfits due to lack of implicit regularization, or fails to escape the lazy manifold—is unidentified.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: Comparing solutions found by PGD vs. first-order methods (weight norm, margin, Jacobian norm, CKA with random features); theoretical generalization bounds tied to the preconditioner’s bias.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies on the NTK regime assumption, which may break during training as features evolve.
- Experiments are limited to small networks; scalability to large models (e.g., GPT-2) is unknown.
- Jacobian computation is computationally costly; efficient approximations or matrix-free methods are not thoroughly validated for PGD.
- No theory for optimal switching timing; using a fixed iteration count may be suboptimal across tasks and architectures.

## Confidence
- **C1 (Spectral equalization)**: High under NTK assumptions, but Low when features evolve.
- **C2 (Grokking compression)**: Medium (supported by small-model experiments only).
- **C3 (Switch to AdamW)**: Medium (empirical benefit, but switch timing heuristic).
