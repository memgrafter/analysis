# Membership Inference Attacks from Causal Principles

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02819
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02819
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reformulates MIA evaluation as a causal inference problem, providing unbiased estimators that correct for interference and distribution shift, validated on CIFAR-10 and DP-SGD.

## Executive Summary
Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) are used to assess whether a model has memorized specific training data points. However, standard evaluation methods suffer from systematic biases: in the one-run setting (a single model trained on all data), interference between data points invalidates classical metrics; in the zero-run setting (post-hoc evaluation on a fixed model), distribution shift between member and non-member data confounds the attack. This paper addresses these issues by reformulating MIA evaluation as a causal inference problem, defining memorization as the average treatment effect (ATE) of including a data point in training. The authors derive causal analogs of standard metrics (e.g., ATE, AUC) and propose practical estimators for different evaluation regimes, supported by non-asymptotic consistency guarantees. Experiments demonstrate that the proposed causal estimators remove distribution shift bias, align zero-run evaluations with multi-run baselines, and respect differential privacy bounds where naive methods fail.

## Method Summary
The core methodological contribution is a causal framework that treats training set membership as a binary treatment and the MIA score as an outcome. The target estimand is the ATE of membership on the attack score. For the multi-run regime (multiple models trained on random subsets), classical metrics like mean score difference or AUC coincide with their causal counterparts due to randomized treatment assignment. For the one-run regime, interference arises because all scores come from a single model trained on the entire dataset; the authors invoke learning-theoretic stability concepts (α-error stability, β-uniform training stability) to bound the influence of any single point and derive consistent estimators. For the zero-run regime, confounding from distribution shift is addressed via inverse probability weighting (IPW) or doubly robust methods, using a learned propensity score to rebalance member and non-member distributions. Theoretical identification and consistency results are provided for each setting, and the estimators are shown to be practically applicable.

## Key Results
- Causal estimators for zero-run MIAs eliminate distribution shift bias, producing results that align with multi-run baselines (e.g., on CIFAR-10, naive zero-run AUC 0.75 → corrected AUC 0.66, matching the IID baseline of 0.65).
- Corrected evaluations respect differential privacy bounds, whereas naive zero-run methods violate them, demonstrating improved reliability in privacy auditing.
- The framework yields non-asymptotic consistency guarantees for one-run evaluations under algorithmic stability conditions, bridging the gap between practical one-run attacks and sound statistical inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Causal reformulation and multi-run equivalence.**  
The paper defines memorization as the ATE of training membership on the MIA score, using potential outcomes \(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)\). In the multi-run (RCT) setting, random assignment of membership ensures that observed score differences directly estimate the ATE, making classical metrics (mean difference, AUC) valid causal estimands. This provides an unbiased baseline when interference is absent.

**Mechanism 2: One-run consistency via stability.**  
In the one-run setting, all scores depend on a single model trained on the entire dataset, creating complete interference: including/excluding any point affects all outcomes. The authors show that if the learning algorithm satisfies α-error stability and β-uniform training stability with respect to the attack loss, the variance of the ATE estimator can be bounded, yielding non-asymptotic consistency. Stability essentially limits how much a single data point can change the model’s loss on others, controlling the interference.

**Mechanism 3: Zero-run confounding adjustment with IPW.**  
Zero-run evaluation uses a fixed model trained on member data \(D_T\) and evaluates on non-member data \(D_0\). Since membership is not randomly assigned (e.g., due to temporal shift), \(D_T\) and \(D_0\) differ systematically, confounding the MIA. Under the assumption of domain overlap (i.e., \(D_0\) covers the support of \(D_T\)), the ATE is identified by reweighting the non-member distribution via the propensity score \(\pi(x)=P(A=1|X=x)\). IPW and doubly robust (AIPW) estimators then correct the bias, recovering the true memorization effect.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Assumptions & Auxiliary Models → Regime-specific Estimators → Causal Estimands (estimates)

**Critical path:**
1. Identify the evaluation regime (multi-run, one-run, or zero-run).
2. For multi-run: directly compute causal metrics (they equal classical ones).
3. For one-run: verify stability conditions of the learning algorithm; if satisfied, use the same estimator as multi-run; if violated, results are unreliable.
4. For zero-run:
   a. Check domain overlap between member and non-member data (e.g., via visualization or density estimation).
   b. Learn a propensity score model \(\hat{\pi}\) on held-out data (cross-fitting recommended).
   c. Apply IPW or doubly robust (AIPW) estimator; monitor weight distribution for extreme values.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Bias-variance in zero-run: IPW has high variance if \(\hat{\pi}\) is extreme; doubly robust (AIPW) reduces sensitivity but requires correct specification of either \(\hat{\pi}\) or the outcome model \(\hat{\mu}_0\).
- Computational cost vs bias: Multi-run is gold standard but expensive; one-run is cheaper but needs stability proof; zero-run is cheapest but requires building and validating \(\hat{\pi}\).
- Generality vs tightness: Stability bounds may be loose; tighter problem-specific bounds would improve sample complexity but require more analysis.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Zero-run:** Extreme IPW weights (e.g., >1000), corrected ROC curve still differs from IID baseline, high variance across seeds for corrected estimator.
- **One-run:** Classical metric significantly differs from multi-run baseline on controlled non-shifted data, indicating uncorrected interference bias.
- **All regimes:** Violation of core assumptions: overlap fails if member and non-member supports are disjoint; stability fails if training is unstable (e.g., deep nets without regularization); SUTVA fails if interference present beyond what stability can handle.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate synthetic linear regression (Section G.1):** Run multi-run, one-run, and zero-run regimes on Ridge and DP-SGD. Verify: (a) multi-run ≈ one-run when interference is small, (b) zero-run naive AUC is inflated and corrected AUC matches multi-run, (c) DP-SGD naive zero-run violates DP bound while corrected does not.
2. **Test stability conditions:** For various models (logistic regression, ResNet, transformer), empirically estimate the influence of a single point on loss (e.g., via leave-one-out) to check α, β scaling with n. For underparametrized models, expect τ̂ATE ≈ 0 per Theorem B.1.
3. **Propensity score sensitivity in zero-run:** On a CIFAR-10-like shift, vary the member/non-member mixture ratio (e.g., 50/50 to 99/1). Observe: (a) naive bias increases, (b) IPW variance explodes as overlap diminishes, (c) doubly robust may still work if outcome model is correctly specified.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we estimate heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) for memorization to assess differential privacy risks across subgroups in one-run and zero-run MIA evaluations?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper states: "it is also crucial to study how different subgroups or subpopulations may be differently affected by memorization... In causal inference, this corresponds to estimating heterogeneous treatment effects... Our framework provides a foundation for developing principled, subgroup-level membership risk estimates, even in challenging one-run and zero-run regimes."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on population-level ATE and ROC metrics; extending to HTE requires additional assumptions and estimation strategies, especially under interference and confounding.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of HTE estimators compatible with the causal MIA framework, along with theoretical guarantees (e.g., consistency, robustness) and empirical validation on real data showing subgroup disparities.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the causal MIA framework be extended to other privacy attacks (e.g., attribute inference, dataset inference), and how do interference and confounding manifest in those settings?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes: "the causal perspective we advocate can be extended to other classes of privacy attacks beyond MIAs. For instance, attribute inference attacks... also exhibit evaluation biases that could be mitigated by counterfactual reasoning."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only treats MIAs; the structure of other attacks may differ (e.g., outcome is continuous, interference patterns differ). A systematic causal reformulation for those attacks is lacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formulation of causal estimands for those attacks, identification strategies under appropriate assumptions, and practical estimators with validation experiments.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** For one-run MIA evaluation, can we obtain consistency guarantees for MIA scoring functions beyond the loss-based attack (e.g., likelihood-based, shadow-model scores) under the existing stability framework or new assumptions?
- **Basis in paper:** Remark 5.5 indicates: "Consistency results in this section are specific to the loss-based MIA... Extending Theorem 5.4 to other MIAs would require assuming stability of the confidence score function s_MIA, rather than stability of the loss itself."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper proves consistency only for loss-based attacks; other common MIAs (e.g., using likelihood ratios) are not covered. The stability of those s_MIA functions is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Prove that popular MIA scoring functions satisfy suitable stability conditions or propose modified assumptions that hold for those functions, with empirical support showing that corrected zero-run evaluations yield valid results for these attacks.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** In zero-run MIA evaluation, how can we reliably estimate causal metrics when the overlap assumption (Assumption 6.1) fails or propensity scores are extreme (near 0 or 1), leading to high variance in IPW and related estimators?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6 discusses that severe distribution shift leads to extreme propensity scores, making IPW unstable. It mentions alternative estimators (G-Formula, AIPW) but notes they still require Assumptions 6.1 and 6.3 (bounded propensity scores).
- **Why unresolved:** Theoretical guarantees require bounded propensity scores away from 0 and 1. In practice, with LLMs where members are old data and non-members are recent data, some topics may appear only in one set, causing complete separation. The paper does not provide a solution for such cases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop robust causal estimation methods less sensitive to extreme weights (e.g., trimming, doubly robust with stronger outcome modeling, Bayesian approaches), and demonstrate effectiveness under simulated and real-world severe distribution shifts where overlap is poor.

## Limitations
- Core assumptions (SUTVA, domain overlap, algorithmic stability) may be violated in practice, especially for large LLMs with strong interference and distribution shift.
- Stability bounds are theoretical and may be too loose for practical sample sizes; empirical verification is challenging.
- Zero-run IPW depends on propensity scores; in high-dimensional data, overlap is often weak, leading to high variance and sensitivity to model misspecification.
- Experiments are limited to small models/datasets; scalability to large generative models remains unproven.
- The interaction between MIA scoring function choice and the causal correction is not fully explored.

## Confidence
- Causal ATE definition: **High** (theoretically sound)
- One-run consistency via stability: **Medium** (depends on unverified stability for deep models)
- Zero-run confounding adjustment (identification): **Medium** (identification solid, but propensity score practical challenges)
- Experimental validation (DP bounds, CIFAR-10): **High** (clear on tested setups)
- General alignment claim (causal MIA corrects biases): **Medium** (needs more validation across datasets/models)
