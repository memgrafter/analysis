# Evaluating LLMs for Police Decision-Making: A Framework Based on Police Action Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03553
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03553
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: LLMs significantly underperform expert references on police-specific metrics, with only moderate alignment between LLM judges and human experts.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces the Police Action Scenarios (PAS) framework to address the lack of a rigorous, domain-specific evaluation method for LLMs in policing. PAS uses scenarios derived from over 8,000 Korean police manuals to create expert-validated reference answers and identifies five core metrics for assessing LLM responses: Logical Correctness, Completeness, Factuality, Logical Efficiency, and Logical Robustness. Evaluation of GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini reveals a substantial performance gap compared to expert references, and a moderate correlation (ρ=0.315–0.560) between LLM-based scoring and human expert judgment. The findings underscore the necessity of an "expert-in-the-loop" approach for safe LLM integration in high-stakes police operations.

## Method Summary
The PAS framework is a five-stage pipeline. First, policing scenarios are defined as `(Situation, Action)` pairs extracted and validated from a curated corpus of 8,348 Korean police manual entries. Second, expert police officers construct reference answers for these scenarios. Third, commercial LLMs generate zero-shot responses to the scenarios. Fourth, a two-stage statistical process isolates core metrics: stepwise regression identifies candidate metrics that predict expert overall quality scores, and Spearman correlation filters for metrics positively aligned with expert judgment. Finally, the framework evaluates LLM performance using these metrics, combining scalable LLM-as-a-judge scoring with mandatory expert validation stages to ensure domain fidelity.

## Key Results
- Five core metrics were derived that predict police expert judgments of response quality: Logical Correctness, Completeness, Factuality, Logical Efficiency, and Logical Robustness.
- All evaluated LLMs (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) significantly underperformed expert reference answers, with an average gap of approximately -1.1 points on a 5-point scale.
- The correlation between LLM-as-a-judge and human police专家 scores was moderate (ρ=0.315–0.560), indicating LLM judges miss critical domain-specific errors and require expert oversight.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Scenario-based task design grounds LLM evaluation in realistic police operational contexts, revealing limitations missed by generic benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** By defining police tasks through `(Situation, Action)` pairs derived from manuals, the framework forces models to demonstrate procedural and legal reasoning within authentic constraints, not just factual recall. The scenario acts as a controlled "mental rehearsal" context officers use, making abstract knowledge actionable.
- **Core assumption:** Police work quality is situational and procedural; an LLM's value depends on output alignment with domain-specific decision protocols, not just general correctness.
- **Evidence anchors:** The PAS framework's first step is "Police Action Scenarios... to create expert-validated reference answers." The objective is to assess reasoning in "realistic policing contexts."
- **Break condition:** If the `(Situation, Action)` decomposition fails to capture critical nuances of a specific police sub-domain (e.g., cybercrime incident command), the evaluation's ecological validity collapses.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A two-stage statistical filtering process (regression + correlation with experts) isolates metrics that are both predictive of overall quality *and* aligned with professional police judgment, creating a valid evaluation rubric.
- **Mechanism:** Stepwise multiple regression identifies candidate metrics that significantly predict human expert overall quality scores. A subsequent Spearman correlation check ensures retained metrics are directionally aligned with expert rankings, filtering out metrics that are statistically predictive but domain-irrelevant or misleading.
- **Core assumption:** Expert judgment of a "good" police response is the gold standard; metrics must both correlate with this judgment and contribute unique predictive power beyond other metrics.
- **Evidence anchors:** The analysis yielded five core metrics. Table 3 and surrounding text detail the two-stage analysis (β coefficients from regression, ρ correlations) leading to the final set.
- **Break condition:** If the expert sample is unrepresentative or the scoring rubric is inconsistent, the final metric set may reflect rater idiosyncrasies rather than stable domain requirements.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Integrating LLM-as-a-judge with mandatory human expert validation ("expert-in-the-loop") mitigates the moderate alignment (ρ=0.315–0.560) between automated and expert scoring, preventing silent failures in high-stakes domains.
- **Mechanism:** The framework uses LLM judges for scalable initial scoring but relies on a final validation stage where police experts directly assess a subset of responses and the metric definitions themselves. The moderate correlation found in the study justifies this hybrid approach; the LLM judge provides consistency, while the expert ensures domain fidelity.
- **Core assumption:** In specialized, high-risk domains, the risk of an LLM judge systematically missing critical errors (e.g., unlawful arrest procedures) outweighs the cost of human validation; perfect automation is unsafe.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper explicitly states that the "moderate correlation... reveals the limitations of LLM-as-a-judge" and that "PAS incorporates domain expert validation at key stages."
- **Break condition:** If expert validation is skipped due to cost or if the LLM judge's bias systematically diverges from experts in a non-random way (e.g., over-penalizing minor factual errors while missing major procedural flaws), the safety net fails.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Scenario-Based Evaluation vs. Generic QA
- **Why needed here:** The paper's core innovation is moving beyond accuracy-on-legal-articles to situational judgment. Understanding this distinction is key to grasping *why* standard LLM benchmarks are insufficient for policing.
- **Quick check:** What is a concrete example of a police question that would score well on a multiple-choice legal knowledge test but fail the PAS's "Logical Robustness" or "Completeness" metrics?

### Concept: Metric Derivation via Predictive Validity
- **Why needed here:** The framework doesn't assume standard NLP metrics are suitable. It empirically derives metrics by testing which ones predict *expert-defined* overall quality. This statistical approach (regression + correlation) is central to RQ1's answer.
- **Quick check:** Why is a metric like "Readability" (which scored high for LLMs) not a core metric, even though it might correlate somewhat with human preference?

### Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge Calibration and Limits
- **Why needed here:** The study's finding of only moderate expert-judge alignment is a critical result. It informs the proposed "expert-in-the-loop" design and cautions against blind automation.
- **Quick check:** If an LLM judge gives a high score to a police response that a human expert would reject, what specific types of errors is the LLM judge likely *missing* based on the paper's results?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: S (Scenario Definition) -> R (Expert Reference Answers) -> G (LLM Response Generation) -> M (Core Metric Application & Scoring via LLM-judge + Experts) -> P (Performance Interpretation). The M stage contains the two-stage statistical filter (regression, correlation).

Critical path: The sequence is strictly linear for dataset/benchmark construction: you must have scenarios (S) and references (R) before you can generate (G) and evaluate (M). The expert validation steps are embedded in R (building references) and M (correlation check).

Design tradeoffs:
- **Manual Expert Effort vs. Evaluation Fidelity:** Building R and validating M requires significant police expert time. This is traded for domain validity.
- **Metric Specificity vs. Generalizability:** The five core metrics are derived from one scenario ("Police Readiness through Operational Reasoning"). Using them for other scenarios may require re-running the metric derivation process, sacrificing immediate reuse for contextual accuracy.
- **LLM-Judge Scalability vs. Safety:** Using an LLM judge for M enables scaling to hundreds of responses but introduces the risk of misalignment, necessitating costly expert spot-checks.

Failure signatures:
- **Low correlation (ρ < 0.3) between LLM-judge and experts on core metrics** during validation, indicating the judge is not capturing domain-critical flaws.
- **Reference answer scores are not significantly higher than LLM scores** on the core metrics, suggesting the scenario or reference construction failed to establish a meaningful performance gap.
- **High variance in expert scores for the same reference response** during R construction, indicating unclear or subjective ground truth.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Replicate Metric Selection:** Using the provided dataset and prompt templates, run the two-stage analysis (stepwise regression + Spearman correlation) on a held-out set of LLM responses to verify the five core metrics are stable. Check if "Logical Explanation" consistently fails the correlation filter.
2. **Cross-Scenario Generalization Test:** Apply the *derived* five-metric set (from Readiness scenarios) to a new PAS scenario type (e.g., "Post-Incident Report Writing"). Does the LLM-as-judge/expert correlation hold? Do the same metrics predict overall quality?
3. **LLM-Judge Variant Sensitivity:** For the core metrics, compare scores from different LLM-as-a-judge models (e.g., GPT-4 vs. Claude) on the same LLM responses. Do the relative model rankings and the LLM-judge/expert correlation change significantly? This tests robustness of the automated component.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the PAS framework be effectively adapted and validated for police operations in jurisdictions with different legal systems and procedural norms beyond the Korean context used in this study?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion states the framework's Reference Responses (R) component is designed for "local police experts build the Reference Responses aligned with their own jurisdiction’s laws," and notes "broader applicability may be limited" since evaluation was based on Korean manuals. Future work calls for expanding with "jurisdictional diversity."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates PAS's structure but only implements and validates it within a single national (Korean) policing context. The process for translating the framework—especially metric adaptation and reference answer construction—to fundamentally different legal traditions (e.g., common law vs. civil law) or policing models is not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A replication study applying the full PAS pipeline in a distinct jurisdiction (e.g., U.S., U.K., or a country with a significantly different legal code). This would require new expert-validated reference datasets, a re-running of the metric selection two-stage analysis to see if the same five core metrics emerge as significant predictors, and a report on challenges in cross-cultural scenario definition.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific architectural modifications or specialized training methodologies (beyond prompt engineering) are most effective for improving LLM performance on the five identified core police-specific metrics (Logical Correctness, Completeness, Factuality, Logical Efficiency, Logical Robustness)?
- **Basis in paper:** The results show commercial LLMs significantly underperform reference answers, particularly in "factual accuracy and procedural reasoning" and "specialized subjects." The framework identifies the gap but does not investigate *how* to close it, focusing instead on evaluation.
- **Why unresolved:** The study is diagnostic, not interventional. It establishes that a performance gap exists on police-specific criteria but does not test solutions like supervised fine-tuning on police manuals, retrieval-augmented generation with verified procedural data, or curriculum learning for logical reasoning chains. The root causes of the failure modes are hypothesized but not isolated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments comparing baseline LLMs against variants fine-tuned on the PAS dataset or augmented with a procedural knowledge base. Performance would be measured specifically on the five core metrics to determine which modification yields the largest gain, ideally with analysis of error type reduction.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent can the "LLM-as-a-judge" component of PAS be refined or replaced to achieve higher agreement with human police experts, and what is the minimal necessary level of expert-in-the-loop oversight for safe deployment in high-stakes policing tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper finds "moderate correlations (ρ=0.315–0.560) between LLM-as-a-judge and human experts" and concludes this "highlights the need for expert-in-the-loop validation." The Discussion section is titled "Implications of Moderate Correlation: The Need for an Expert-in-the-Loop Framework."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework currently uses LLM-as-a-judge as a scalable first pass but acknowledges its limitations in a high-stakes domain. The optimal hybrid model—the precise ratio of automated to expert judgment, the design of expert review stages, and the potential for training a domain-specific judge model—remains an open design problem.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A study varying the expert review sampling rate (e.g., 0%, 10%, 50%, 100%) and measuring the impact on (a) correlation with full-expert judgments, (b) cost, and (c) the system's ability to detect unsafe LLM outputs. Additionally, training and evaluating a specialized judge model fine-tuned on the expert-rated PAS data could test if domain-specific judges bridge the agreement gap.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the set of five core evaluation metrics identified in the Korean police context be generalized as universally primary for policing evaluation, or are they context-dependent, requiring re-derivation for different operational scenarios (e.g., patrol vs. investigation vs. cybercrime)?
- **Basis in paper:** The metric selection process was applied to responses from the "Police Readiness through Operational Reasoning" scenario. Table 6 shows performance varies significantly by work domain, suggesting metric importance might shift by domain.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes these metrics as strongly predictive of *overall quality* in their specific scenario and dataset. However, it does not test whether these same metrics are the top predictors for other distinct policing scenarios. A metric crucial for reasoning about use-of-force might differ from one for evidence chain documentation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying the identical two-stage metric selection pipeline to multiple, distinct PAS scenario types (e.g., "Immediate Incident Response," "Investigative Interview Planning"). Comparing the final sets of significant metrics across scenarios would reveal if the five metrics are stable or if a dynamic, scenario-specific metric set is required.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the primary bottleneck causing LLMs to fail on police manual-based tasks: a deficiency in memorized procedural knowledge, an inability to perform multi-step legal-procedural reasoning, or a lack of situational awareness regarding operational constraints and risks?
- **Basis in paper:** The results show largest gaps in "factuality" and "logical" metrics. The Discussion states LLMs lack "expertise in police work" and "specificity and factuality in police-related contexts," failing to deliver "manual-based procedures, practical knowledge, or warnings about legal risks." Figure 3 shows an LLM response that is coherent but misses critical procedural warnings.
- **Why unresolved:** The evaluation metrics are composite scores that conflate knowledge recall, reasoning chain validity, and risk awareness. The paper identifies *that* LLMs fail but cannot disaggregate *why*. This is critical for targeted improvement: should efforts focus on knowledge retrieval, chain-of-thought prompting, or training on risk-annotated cases?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fine-grained error analysis of LLM failures, manually coded by police experts into categories: (1) factual incorrectness (wrong law/statute), (2) procedural omission (missing a mandatory step), (3) logical flaw (invalid inference from facts to action), (4) risk blindness (failure to note potential legal/physical danger). The distribution of error types would pinpoint the dominant failure mode.

## Limitations
- **Domain Specificity:** The framework and reference answers are built exclusively from Korean police manuals. Its validity for other legal/jurisdictional contexts is unknown without rebuilding the corpus and reference set.
- **LLM-Judge Reliability:** The moderate expert-judge correlation (ρ=0.315–0.560) limits fully automated evaluation, confirming LLM judges miss critical domain-specific errors without expert oversight.
- **Generalization of Metrics:** The five core metrics were derived from one scenario type. Their predictive validity for other police decision-making scenarios is unproven and may require re-derivation.
- **Corpus Construction Opacity:** The exact process for curating the 8,348 manual entries from PDFs is not detailed, hindering exact replication and assessment of potential selection biases.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Scenario-Based Grounding):** Medium. The logic is sound and supported by the paper's design, but ecological validity is constrained by the single-scenario derivation and Korean context.
- **Mechanism 2 (Metric Derivation):** High. The two-stage statistical filtering is a clear, reproducible method for identifying metrics aligned with expert judgment, as shown in their results.
- **Mechanism 3 (Expert-in-the-Loop):** High. The necessity is strongly evidenced by the reported low-to-moderate LLM-judge/expert alignment. The proposed hybrid mitigation is logical.
- **Overall Claim (LLMs Underperform):** Medium-High. The result is robust *for the specific Korean PAS scenarios tested*, but generalizability to broader policing tasks or other LLMs is uncertain.
