# Multifidelity Surrogate Modeling of Depressurized Loss of Forced Cooling in High-temperature Gas Reactors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14143
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14143
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Multifidelity Gaussian process surrogates, trained on low- and high-fidelity CFD data with inputs selected via prior sensitivity analysis, achieve high accuracy (R² > 0.98) in predicting HTGR safety metrics while neural network variants offer comparable speed at much lower training cost.

## Executive Summary
This study develops multifidelity machine learning surrogates to predict key safety metrics—time to onset of natural circulation (ONC) and post-ONC temperature—during a depressurized loss of forced cooling transient in a high-temperature gas reactor. By combining low-, medium-, and high-fidelity (mesh-coarsened) CFD simulations, the work compares several multifidelity architectures (Gaussian processes and neural networks) under equal computational budgets. The findings establish that using only the most influential input parameters, identified through prior sensitivity analysis, significantly improves predictive accuracy. A low- and high-fidelity pairing consistently outperforms configurations involving medium-fidelity data or three fidelities, and the multifidelity Gaussian process provides the most robust performance, though neural networks achieve similar accuracy with far lower training times when dominant inputs are used.

## Method Summary
The authors generate 1000 CFD samples per fidelity level (low: ~17.5k mesh, medium: ~35k, high: ~70k) using Ansys Fluent, with eight input parameters and two scalar outputs. Data splits strictly prevent leakage across fidelities; for example, a total cost budget of 1800 (where low=1, medium=2, high=4) might allocate 1000 low-fidelity and 200 high-fidelity samples, leaving 800 high-fidelity samples for testing. Multifidelity models implemented include a two-stage co-kriging Gaussian process (MF-GP) and several neural network architectures (Delta, Flag, Intermediate, etc.) that integrate low- and high-fidelity data via residual learning, fidelity flags, or shared trunks. Hyperparameters are first tuned on analytical benchmarks, then fixed for application to the reactor dataset. Models are evaluated on held-out high-fidelity test sets using RMSE and R², with experiments varying fidelity pairings (e.g., LF+HF vs. LF+MF), number of fidelities (two vs. three), and input subsets (all vs. dominant vs. non-dominant parameters identified from prior global sensitivity analysis).

## Key Results
- The multifidelity Gaussian process achieved the best overall robustness, with R² = 0.9796 (RMSE = 102.35 s) for time to ONC and R² = 1.0 (RMSE = 1.08 K) for temperature after ONC when using the low+high fidelity pairing at a fixed budget.
- Neural network approaches (e.g., MFNN-Delta, MFNN-Flag) matched this accuracy when trained on dominant inputs only, but their training time was two orders of magnitude lower (~5–10 s vs. ~400 s for MF-GP).
- Models trained solely on the 1–2 dominant inputs (from prior sensitivity analysis) consistently outperformed those using all eight inputs; using only non-dominant inputs led to failure (near-zero or negative R²).
- The low+high fidelity combination was superior to low+medium or medium+high for time to ONC, and two-fidelity configurations generally matched or exceeded three-fidelity models at equal computational cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dominant Input Selection Mitigates Noise
- **Claim:** Multifidelity surrogate performance is strongly determined by the informativeness of selected input parameters; training on a subset of dominant inputs—identified via prior global sensitivity analysis—consistently yields higher accuracy than using the full input set.
- **Mechanism:** Weakly informative (non-dominant) inputs introduce noise that degrades the learned input-output mapping, especially when high-fidelity data is limited. The surrogates learn correlational patterns; non-dominant parameters contribute spurious correlations that overfit the training data.
- **Core assumption:** The sensitivity analysis from prior work (using 500 high-fidelity samples) correctly identifies parameters with negligible influence, and this structural property holds for the expanded 1000-sample dataset.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract explicitly states that models using dominant inputs outperformed those using the full set. Section 2.1.2 details that heated section temperature is dominant for time to ONC, while temperature and heat transfer coefficient dominate temperature after ONC, with minimal impact from other parameters.
- **Break condition:** If the underlying physics or parameter ranges change such that non-dominant parameters become influential, or if the fidelity levels themselves alter the sensitivity structure.

### Mechanism 2: Optimal Fidelity Pairing is Problem-Specific
- **Claim:** The optimal fidelity pairing (LF+HF) and the marginal value of adding a third fidelity (MF) depend on the correlation structure between fidelity outputs; here, the LF+HF combination provides the strongest performance boost because the LF captures dominant trends and HF provides corrections, while the MF offers less complementary information.
- **Core assumption:** The mesh-coarsening scheme creates fidelity levels where the LF–MF–HF correlation is non-linear; a direct LF→HF correction is more effective than a hierarchical LF→MF→HF at fixed computational cost.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports LF+HF outperformed LF+MF and MF+HF, particularly for time to ONC. Section 3.3 (Tables 10, A.13, A.14) shows LF+HF (2F) often best; three-fidelity models underperform two-fidelity peers. Section 4 reiterates the superiority of LF+HF.
- **Break condition:** If a different coarsening strategy or fidelity definition (e.g., different turbulence models, not just mesh) creates a more informative MF level that bridges LF and HF more effectively.

### Mechanism 3: Trade-off Between Robustness and Training Efficiency
- **Claim:** The choice of multifidelity architecture involves a trade-off between predictive robustness (with limited or noisy information) and computational training efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** The multifidelity Gaussian process (MF-GP) provides the most robust performance across input subsets and fidelity configurations due to its probabilistic kernel-based treatment of correlations. However, its non-parametric nature leads to cubic scaling with training data size, resulting in high runtimes. Neural network methods achieve comparable accuracy when dominant inputs are available but degrade more with non-dominant inputs due to their reliance on learning direct mappings from potentially uninformative features.
- **Core assumption:** The observed runtime differences are primarily due to algorithmic scaling properties (GP O(n³) vs. NN O(n)) and not implementation details or hardware.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes MF-GP’s robust performance and the substantially lower training times of neural nets (5–10 s vs. ~400 s). Section 3.3 shows that for non-dominant inputs, only MF-GP retains positive R². Figure 8 illustrates MF-GP runtime scaling with budget and input dimensionality, while NN runtimes remain low.
- **Break condition:** If neural networks are scaled up significantly (more layers/parameters), their training time could approach GP’s; if sparse/approximate GP implementations are used, the runtime gap could close.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multifidelity Surrogate Modeling**
  - **Why needed here:** The core problem is replacing expensive CFD simulations (high-fidelity) with cheap-to-evaluate models. Multifidelity methods explicitly combine data from models of varying cost/accuracy (low-, medium-, high-fidelity) to learn a high-fidelity surrogate more efficiently than using high-fidelity data alone.
  - **Quick check question:** If low- and high-fidelity outputs are completely uncorrelated, what is the theoretical value of a multifidelity model compared to a surrogate trained only on high-fidelity data?

- **Concept: Sensitivity Analysis in Model Reduction**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper leverages prior sensitivity analysis to focus modeling efforts on the 1–2 most influential parameters for each output. This reduces the dimensionality of the learning problem, critical when high-fidelity data is costly, and prevents the "curse of dimensionality" from degrading surrogate accuracy.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might including a parameter with near-zero Sobol index still harm a neural network surrogate's performance, even if its *average* effect is negligible?

- **Concept: Causal vs. Correlational Learning in Surrogates**
  - **Why needed here:** Surrogate models learn correlational relationships from simulation data. The finding that non-dominant inputs lead to failure implies the surrogates are not robustly learning the *causal* drivers of the output (which sensitivity analysis identified). A model using all inputs may be overfitting to spurious correlations present in the specific CFD dataset.
  - **Quick check question:** If a parameter has zero global sensitivity but interacts non-linearly with a dominant parameter, could it become locally important in certain regions of the input space? How would this affect a global surrogate?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Generation Engine (CFD simulations with three mesh resolutions) -> Pre-processing (normalization, train/test splitting, fidelity-paired dataset creation) -> Model Zoo (MF-GP, MFNN-Delta, MFNN-Flag, MFNN-Intermediate, MFNN-Two/ThreeStep, GPmimic) -> Hyperparameter Tuner (grid search on benchmarks, then fixed for ONC) -> Evaluation Harness (computes RMSE/R² for fidelity pairings, fidelity levels, input subsets, budgets) -> Scaling Analyzer (measures training time vs. budget and dimensionality).
- **Critical path:**
  1. Reproduce CFD data generation (Section 2.1) to verify mesh independence and time-step consistency; this is the irreversible upfront cost.
  2. Perform/obtain prior sensitivity analysis (Section 2.1.2) to define input subsets; without this, the dominant-input experiment is meaningless.
  3. Choose a baseline multifidelity method based on computational constraints: MF-GP for best accuracy; MFNN-Delta/Flag for speed when dominant inputs are known.
  4. Follow the cost-matching protocol (Table 4) for fair comparison; do not simply compare models with different numbers of high-fidelity samples.
  5. Always evaluate on a large, held-out high-fidelity test set (800 samples here) to get reliable metrics.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Accuracy vs. Training Time: MF-GP is robust but slow (minutes/hours); neural nets are fast (seconds) but sensitive to input relevance.
  - Fidelity Levels vs. Budget: Adding a third fidelity (MF) did not justify its cost; a two-fidelity LF+HF strategy was optimal. Investing more budget into more LF/HF samples was better than adding MF.
  - Input Subset vs. Generality: Training on dominant inputs maximizes predictive accuracy for the specific output; training on all inputs is more general but noisier. Non-dominant inputs lead to expected failure—a validation check.
  - Method Stability: MFNN-Two/ThreeStep performed poorly everywhere; GPmimic showed some random seed sensitivity. MF-GP, MFNN-Delta, MFNN-Flag, MFNN-Intermediate were reliable.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Non-dominant inputs yield near-zero or negative R² (expected sanity check).
  - Three-fidelity model underperforms two-fidelity at equal cost (MF data is redundant or poorly correlated).
  - Large performance gap between dominant-input and all-inputs models (excessive noise from non-informative variables).
  - Neural network method fails on non-dominant inputs while MF-GP still works (highlights GP’s robustness to uninformative features).
  - Instability across random seeds (e.g., GPmimic); check variance in repeated runs.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Benchmark Validation:** Replicate Table 7. Train all two-fidelity methods on the Forrester, Booth, and Borehole benchmarks with hyperparameters from Table 5. Confirm MF-GP achieves lowest RMSE; this validates implementation correctness before costly ONC runs.
  2. **Fidelity Pairing Baseline (All Inputs, Fixed Budget):** For total cost=1800, train LF+HF, LF+MF, and MF+HF versions of MF-GP and MFNN-Delta. Replicate the pattern in Tables 10 & A.13–A.14: LF+HF should be best for time to ONC; all pairings should be good for temperature after ONC.
  3. **Dominant Input Sanity Check:** Train MF-GP and one fast NN (e.g., MFNN-Flag) on the *dominant input set* vs. the *non-dominant input set* for time to ONC at budget=1800. Expect near-perfect R² (>0.97) for dominant inputs and R²<0 or ~0.7 for non-dominant (MF-GP only). This tests the sensitivity-analysis-driven input selection hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How can multifidelity surrogates be extended to predict full transient field quantities (e.g., spatial temperature fields over time) rather than scalar outputs?** The current models predict only integrated scalar metrics. Predicting spatiotemporal fields requires handling high-dimensional outputs and temporal dynamics, which may need new architectures. (Basis: explicit in conclusions; unresolved because it requires new methodological development; evidence: a working multifidelity surrogate for time-evolving 3D fields.)
- **How does the sensitivity structure of input parameters fundamentally affect the learned correlations between fidelity levels?** The paper shows empirical performance differences but does not analyze whether dominant inputs improve cross-fidelity correlation strength or reduce the complexity of the inter-fidelity mapping. (Basis: explicit in introduction; unresolved due to lack of controlled experiments; evidence: systematic variation of input sensitivity in benchmarks.)
- **To what extent do the observed advantages of LF+HF pairing generalize beyond mesh coarsening to other fidelity definitions (e.g., different turbulence models, including experimental data)?** The findings are specific to mesh-based fidelities; other fidelity hierarchies (e.g., different physics) might yield different optimal pairings. (Basis: inferred from discussion; unresolved because the study uses only mesh coarsening; evidence: a follow-on study with different fidelity sources.)
- **What is the computational cost–accuracy Pareto frontier for multifidelity surrogates in large-scale HTGR safety analysis, and how does it scale with input dimensionality and budget?** The study evaluates fixed budgets (300–1800) and up to 8 inputs; the nonlinear scaling for larger problems remains unclear. (Basis: inferred from scaling analysis; unresolved due to limited scope; evidence: extensive scaling experiments with higher dimensions and larger budgets.)
- **Can interpretable multifidelity models (e.g., based on symbolic regression or Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks) maintain competitive accuracy while providing physical insights?** The paper uses black-box models; interpretable alternatives could improve transparency for safety analysis. (Basis: explicit in conclusions; unresolved because not implemented; evidence: a KAN-based multifidelity surrogate with comparable accuracy and extracted symbolic formulas.)

## Limitations
- Key neural network training details (activation functions, optimizer, batch size, learning rate schedule, exact fidelity weighting for Flag method) are unspecified, requiring assumptions for reproduction.
- MF-GP kernel specifics (Matern smoothness parameter ν, optimization routine) and hardware for timing measurements are not reported, affecting runtime comparisons.
- The cost-per-simulation ratios (LF = 1, MF = 2, HF = 4) may not generalize to other computing environments.
- The study relies on a single prior sensitivity analysis; if the identified dominant inputs shift on the full 1000-sample HF dataset, the input-selection experiments could be invalid.
- Potential data leakage across fidelity splits (e.g., overlapping samples) is not explicitly ruled out.

## Confidence
- **Dominant-input selection improves accuracy:** Medium. Internal evidence is strong but rests on one sensitivity analysis; generalizability is uncertain.
- **LF+HF pairing is optimal and third fidelity provides marginal value:** High. Consistent across outputs, budgets, and backed by cost-matched experiments.
- **MF-GP robustness vs. NN training-time efficiency trade-off:** High. The observed runtime gap and accuracy patterns are clear, though exact numbers are implementation- and hardware-dependent.
- **Overall reproducibility of reported metrics:** Medium due to unspecified details and potential variability in data splits and hardware.
