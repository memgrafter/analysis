# Does Unification Come at a Cost? Uni-SafeBench: A Safety Benchmark for Unified Multimodal Large Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.00547
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00547
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Uni-SafeBench reveals that unifying understanding and generation in multimodal models degrades safety; open-source UMLMs score <70% on Both-Safe vs. GPT-4o's 94.61%.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Uni-SafeBench, the first comprehensive safety benchmark for Unified Multimodal Large Models (UMLMs) that jointly evaluates understanding and generation across six safety categories and seven multimodal tasks. Accompanying this is Uni-Judger, an automatic evaluator that separates contextual and intrinsic safety to enable fine-grained analysis. Evaluations on a variety of models demonstrate that the architectural unification process significantly harms the safety alignment inherited from the language model backbone, with open-source UMLMs scoring below 70% on the holistic Both-Safe metric, while closed-source models like GPT-4o achieve 94.61%. The study thus quantifies a trade-off between versatile multimodal capability and robust safety.

## Method Summary
Uni-SafeBench covers six safety categories (e.g., toxicity, bias, misinformation) and seven multimodal tasks, including both understanding-oriented (e.g., visual question answering) and generation-oriented (e.g., image captioning, editing) scenarios. Uni-Judger operates in two stages: first it extracts contextual elements from the input and the model's response, then it judges the intrinsic safety of the output independent of context. This decoupling allows identification of whether failures arise from misleading context or from the model's own unsafe tendencies.

## Key Results
- Architectural unification significantly degrades the inherent safety alignment of the underlying LLM backbone.
- Open-source UMLMs achieve Both-Safe scores below 70%, while closed-source unified models like GPT-4o achieve 94.61%.
- The trade-off between versatile capability and robust safety is quantitatively established.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
While the paper does not pinpoint a single underlying mechanism, the evaluation results and the design of Uni-Judger suggest that the safety degradation in UMLMs may arise from two interacting factors: (1) the model's reliance on contextual cues in multimodal inputs, which can override intrinsic safety constraints, and (2) interference during multimodal fine-tuning that overwrites safety-aligned parameters in the LLM backbone. The separation of contextual and intrinsic safety in Uni-Judger reveals that failures are often due to the model's inability to maintain safety when faced with misleading imagery or combined text-image attacks, rather than a complete loss of safety knowledge. This is consistent with the observed modality asymmetry and the open question about preservation of LLM backbone safety.

## Foundational Learning
The work builds on foundational concepts in AI safety, including the classification of harms into categories such as toxicity, bias, and misinformation. It also leverages the paradigm of large language model alignment (e.g., instruction tuning, RLHF) and extends it to multimodal domains. The necessity of these concepts lies in adapting text-centric safety frameworks to the more complex multimodal setting, where interactions between modalities can introduce new risks. Quick checks include evaluating whether the model's responses remain safe across diverse input types and whether the automatic evaluator correlates with human judgments (though this remains unevaluated).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: The Uni-SafeBench system comprises (a) a dataset of multimodal inputs spanning six safety categories and seven tasks, and (b) the Uni-Judger evaluator, which operates in two stages: contextual element extraction and intrinsic safety judgment.
- **Critical path**: The evaluation workflow: (1) collect model responses to benchmark inputs, (2) feed each input-response pair to Uni-Judger, (3) compute safety scores (e.g., Both-Safe) by aggregating judgments across categories and tasks.
- **Design tradeoffs**: The authors prioritize scalability by using an automatic evaluator instead of human labeling, but this introduces potential biases and limits capture of subtle harms. They also trade off between breadth (covering many tasks) and depth (fine-grained analysis via contextual vs. intrinsic separation).
- **Failure signatures**: Key failure patterns include: (1) lower Both-Safe scores on visual-side attacks (Image-Unsafe VQA) compared to dual-unsafe cases, indicating visual risk detection is weaker; (2) open-source UMLMs consistently scoring below 70% on Both-Safe, suggesting unification causes catastrophic forgetting of safety; (3) higher variance in generation-oriented tasks.
- **First experiments**: Initial experiments involved evaluating a suite of UMLMs (e.g., JanusFlow, GPT-4o) on the full benchmark to establish baseline safety-utility trade-offs and identify the degradation effect of unification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What alignment strategies can preserve LLM backbone safety while integrating multimodal generation in UMLMs without degrading inherent safety?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state that unification "significantly degrades the inherent safety of the underlying LLM" and identify "balancing this trade-off as a pivotal direction for the community."
- **Why unresolved**: Open-source UMLMs show large safety drops (Both-Safe <70%) compared to specialized models, indicating current unification methods (e.g., shared decoding, multimodal fine-tuning) actively harm safety alignment. No existing methods reconcile versatility with robust safety.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments comparing unification architectures (e.g., decoupled vs. shared encoders) with safety-preserving training objectives (e.g., multimodal DPO, constrained generation). Ablation studies on data mixtures (understanding vs. generation) to isolate factors causing "catastrophic forgetting" of safety.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can safety evaluation frameworks detect subtle, context-dependent harms (e.g., sarcasm, cultural nuances, symbolic cues) in multimodal model outputs?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors acknowledge that "Uni-Judge may not fully capture subtle or implicit harms (e.g., sarcasm, symbolic cues, or culturally specific content)."
- **Why unresolved**: Uni-Judger relies on explicit intent extraction and coarse safety categories, missing nuanced social or cultural risks that depend on shared context. Human evaluation shows lower agreement on tasks like malicious editing where harms are implicit.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Construction of a dataset with fine-grained annotations for implicit harms across cultures. Development of evaluators with social reasoning capabilities (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting for cultural context). Correlation studies between evaluator performance and human judgments on edge-case samples.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why do UMLMs exhibit modality asymmetry in safety, performing worse on visual-side attacks (Image-Unsafe VQA) than text-side attacks (Dual-Unsafe VQA), and how can visual safety detection be improved?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Results show lower Both-Safe scores for Image-Unsafe VQA vs. Dual-Unsafe VQA across models (e.g., JanusFlow: 0.193 vs. 0.505), suggesting textual safety mechanisms dominate and visual risks are undertrained.
- **Why unresolved**: Safety training and benchmarks are historically text-centric; visual risk perception in unified architectures may suffer from competing objectives (perception vs. generation) or insufficient multimodal adversarial data.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analysis of attention/activation maps in UMLMs when processing unsafe images vs. text. Training studies isolating visual safety tuning (e.g., adversarial images) while freezing text modules. Benchmark expansions with diverse visual attack vectors (e.g., subtle edits, symbolic imagery).

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What proprietary techniques enable closed-source UMLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini) to achieve superior safety-utility trade-offs, and can these be adapted to open-source models?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Closed-source models achieve Both-Safe >87% with high utility, while open-source UMLMs cluster below 70% (Table 5). Authors speculate "decoupled safety guardrails or scale" but lack details.
- **Why unresolved**: Closed models' training data, objectives, and inference-time safeguards are undisclosed. Open-source unification research prioritizes capability over safety, leaving a gap in known best practices for alignment.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic API probing of closed models to infer safety behaviors (e.g., refusal patterns, editing capabilities). Comparative scaling laws for safety metrics in UMLMs. Open-source replication attempts using hypothesized techniques (e.g., separate safety modules, reinforcement learning from multimodal feedback).

## Limitations
- The benchmark covers six safety categories and seven tasks; it may not be comprehensive for all multimodal safety risks.
- Uni-Judger's automatic evaluations are not validated against human judgments; its reliability and potential biases remain unknown.
- Comparisons between open‑source UMLMs and specialized models likely confound architectural unification with factors like model scale and training data.

## Confidence
- Construction of Uni‑SafeBench and Uni‑Judger: **High**
- Architectural unification degrades inherent safety alignment: **Medium**
- Open‑source UMLMs underperform specialized models: **Medium**
- Specific scores (e.g., GPT‑4o at 94.61%) lack variance estimates: **Medium**
- Existence of a capability‑safety trade‑off: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Human validation of Uni‑Judger: Recruit experts to rate model outputs across safety categories and compare with automatic scores to quantify accuracy and bias.
2. Controlled model comparison: Evaluate a diverse set of UMLMs and specialized models while matching parameter counts and training data distributions to isolate the effect of unification.
3. Ablation study: Starting from a common backbone, train separate unimodal heads versus a unified multimodal head and directly measure safety metric differences.
