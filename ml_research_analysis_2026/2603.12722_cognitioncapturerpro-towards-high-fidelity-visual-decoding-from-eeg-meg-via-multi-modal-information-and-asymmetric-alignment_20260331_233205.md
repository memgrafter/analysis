# CognitionCapturerPro: Towards High-Fidelity Visual Decoding from EEG/MEG via Multi-modal Information and Asymmetric Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12722  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12722  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** On THINGS-EEG, zero‑shot retrieval reaches Top‑1 61.2% and Top‑5 90.8%, a 25.9%/10.6% relative improvement over the original CognitionCapturer and surpasses prior SOTA ATS by 1.0% Top‑1 and 4.1% Top‑5.

## Executive Summary
CognitionCapturerPro addresses two fundamental challenges in EEG‑based visual decoding: fidelity loss (partial information loss during neural encoding) and representational shift (the brain’s semantic associations deviating from visual content). The framework fuses EEG with multiple auxiliary modalities—images, text, depth, and edges—through a collaborative pipeline. Key components include Uncertainty‑Weighted Masking (UM) to simulate perceptual uncertainty, a Fusion Encoder that integrates modality‑specific and shared features, Shared‑Trunk & Heads Alignment (STH‑Align) that maps all embeddings into a unified CLIP image space, and an SDXL‑Turbo diffusion model conditioned on the aligned embeddings for high‑fidelity reconstruction. Evaluations on THINGS‑EEG and THINGS‑MEG demonstrate substantial gains: retrieval Top‑1 61.2% on EEG (31.8% on MEG) and Top‑5 90.8% (64.6%), along with improved reconstruction metrics (PixCorr 0.163, SSIM 0.398, CLIP 0.830). Ablations confirm the effectiveness of each module, particularly the fusion and alignment stages.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with parallel modality expert encoders extracting private embeddings from EEG and four auxiliary modalities. The image branch first passes through the Uncertainty‑Weighted Masking module, which adaptively blurs the image periphery based on alignment confidence estimated from a memory bank of similarity scores. The five embeddings (four auxiliaries plus EEG) are then fed into a Fusion Encoder that uses learned modality tokens and cross‑modal self‑attention to produce a fused vector `z_fus` that combines complementary information. Next, a Shared‑Trunk & Heads Alignment (STH‑Align) module maps any modality embedding (including `z_fus`) into a common image‑aligned space: a shared MLP trunk processes the concatenated embeddings, after which modality‑specific projection heads are trained with multiple losses (MSE, cosine, L2) against frozen CLIP image embeddings. Finally, the aligned embedding conditions an SDXL‑Turbo diffusion model via three IP‑Adapters (image, depth, edge branches) to generate the reconstructed image. The training objective combines a contrastive retrieval loss (SCM‑Loss) with reconstruction losses, and includes modality dropout for robustness.

## Key Results
- **THINGS‑EEG retrieval:** Top‑1 61.2% and Top‑5 90.8%, achieving a 25.9% and 10.6% relative improvement over the original CognitionCapturer and surpassing prior SOTA ATS by 1.0% (Top‑1) and 4.1% (Top‑5).
- **THINGS‑MEG retrieval:** Top‑1 31.8% and Top‑5 64.6%.
- **Reconstruction quality:** Pixel Correlation 0.163, SSIM 0.398, CLIP score 0.830.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Uncertainty‑Weighted Masking (UM) Mitigates Fidelity Loss
- **Claim:** UM mitigates fidelity loss by simulating the observer’s selective attention and perceptual uncertainty during neural encoding.  
- **Mechanism:** It applies a spatially variant foveated blur to input images, where the blur radius σ for each sample is dynamically adjusted based on the model’s confidence in the current EEG‑image alignment. Samples with low alignment confidence (“hard”) receive less blur, forcing the model to focus on central details; samples with high confidence (“easy”) receive more blur, preventing overfitting to noisy peripheral features.  
- **Core assumption:** The misalignment between a specific EEG segment and its paired image can be quantified by their embedding similarity, and this similarity score is a valid proxy for the perceptual fidelity of that neural representation.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - **[abstract]** mentions UM as addressing fidelity loss by “dynamically quantifies the brain‑image fidelity relationship.”  
  - **[section III.B]** details the fovea‑inspired blurring (Eq. 1‑2) and adaptive σ selection via a confidence interval from a memory bank (Eq. 3‑4).  
  - **[corpus]** Neighbor papers like UMind and DynaMind explore uncertainty and temporal dynamics, providing contextual support but no direct citation validates the specific UM mechanism.  
- **Break condition:** If the similarity score in the memory bank becomes a poor indicator of true alignment (e.g., due to model collapse), the adaptive blurring would degrade instead of improve performance.

### Mechanism 2: Shared‑Trunk & Heads Alignment (STH‑Align) Reduces Representational Shift
- **Claim:** STH‑Align reduces representational shift by forcing all modality embeddings into a unified, image‑like space, suppressing non‑visual semantics and aligning neural representations with CLIP’s semantics.  
- **Mechanism:** A shared MLP trunk processes concatenated embeddings from all modalities (EEG + fused), producing a common representation. Modality‑specific projection heads then map this shared vector back to individual modality spaces, trained with MSE, cosine, and L2 regularization losses against frozen CLIP image embeddings.  
- **Core assumption:** A shared high‑level representation can be learned that is simultaneously compatible with the CLIP image space and robust to the noise and bias of individual input modalities, especially EEG.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - **[abstract]** describes STH‑Align as a “simplified alignment module” that maps embeddings into a “unified image space.”  
  - **[section III.E]** explicitly defines the architecture (Eq. 10‑11) and multi‑term loss (Eq. 12).  
  - **[table VI]** ablation shows removing the alignment module causes significant performance drop in reconstruction metrics (e.g., CLIP ↓ from 0.833 to 0.483).  
- **Break condition:** If modality dropout during training causes the shared trunk to learn an overly generic or collapsed representation that cannot reconstruct fine‑grained details.

### Mechanism 3: Fusion Encoder Integrates Complementary Information
- **Claim:** The Fusion Encoder with modality expert encoders and cross‑modal attention integrates complementary information (semantics from text/image, structure from depth/edge) to produce a fused embedding more robust to both fidelity loss and representational shift than any single modality.  
- **Mechanism:** Parallel modality expert encoders extract private embeddings. A two‑layer Transformer with learned modality tokens fuses these via self‑attention. Modality masking during training forces robustness to missing cues.  
- **Core assumption:** The private embeddings retain modality‑specific information (e.g., edges from a depth map) that is complementary to the EEG’s noisy signal. Cross‑attention can effectively synthesize these into a single vector that inherits the strengths of each.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - **[abstract]** states the fusion encoder “integrates modality‑specific and shared information.”  
  - **[section III.D]** describes the tokenization, cross‑modal interaction (Eq. 8‑9), and aggregation.  
  - **[tables I, II]** the “Fusion” modality (F) consistently achieves the highest Top‑1/Top‑5 retrieval scores on both EEG and MEG datasets, outperforming any single modality input (I, T, D, E).  
- **Break condition:** If the cross‑attention fails to properly weight modality contributions (e.g., over‑suppressing depth/edge cues), the fusion could become dominated by the noisiest modality (EEG) or the most generic (text).

## Foundational Learning

### Contrastive Learning with noisy/misaligned pairs
- **Why needed here:** The training objective uses an InfoNCE‑style retrieval task where the positive pair (EEG, its paired image) is inherently imperfect due to fidelity loss and representational shift. The SCM‑Loss adapts the standard contrastive denominator to handle the “one‑to‑many” mapping in the dataset, preventing the model from pushing away valid but visually different positives.  
- **Quick check question:** Given a batch with multiple images of the same semantic category, how does SCM‑Loss modify the standard contrastive denominator to prevent the model from pushing away valid but visually different positives?

### Multi‑modal fusion and information bottleneck
- **Why needed here:** The framework must compress information from four auxiliary modalities plus EEG into a single vector for retrieval/generation. Understanding the trade‑off between private information preservation and shared feature extraction in the Fusion Encoder is key to predicting performance.  
- **Quick check question:** What is the purpose of the learnable modality positional encoding `P` added to the raw projections `H_raw` before the Transformer (Eq. 8)?

### Uncertainty modeling in representation learning
- **Why needed here:** The UM module operationalizes uncertainty as a dynamic difficulty modulator. This connects to broader ideas of curriculum learning and noise‑robust training, where the model’s own confidence shapes its learning signal.  
- **Quick check question:** How does the memory bank `S` with EMA smoothing (Eq. 3) stabilize the uncertainty estimation compared to using the instantaneous batch similarity?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Raw EEG/MEG and auxiliary modalities (image, text, depth, edge) → Modality Expert Encoders → (Image branch goes through Uncertainty‑Weighted Masking) → Fusion Encoder (cross‑modal attention) → STH‑Align (shared trunk + heads) → SDXL‑Turbo via IP‑Adapters → Reconstructed image.

### Critical path
The data flows from modality experts → (optional UM for image) → fusion → STH‑Align → generation.

### Design tradeoffs
- **UM vs. Straight‑Through:** Adaptive blur adds complexity but aims for better generalization; a simpler baseline (fixed blur or none) is easier but may overfit.  
- **STH‑Align vs. Diffusion Prior:** The paper trades the higher capacity of a diffusion‑based prior for a simpler, faster MLP‑based trunk, reducing parameters and inference cost but potentially limiting expressiveness.  
- **Fusion vs. Single Modality:** Fusing four modalities improves robustness but increases memory/computation; the text modality contributes little, suggesting its cost may not be justified.

### Failure signatures
- **UM mis‑calibration:** If σ adjustments are too aggressive (large `c`), overly distorted images for “easy” samples harm semantic learning. Monitor σ distribution per epoch.  
- **STH‑Align collapse:** If the shared trunk output `f` loses all modality information (check via linear probes), the heads cannot reconstruct. Modality dropout rate is critical.  
- **Fusion degradation:** If removing one modality (especially image or depth) causes a large performance drop, the fusion is not robust; Modality Masking training must be effective.

### First 3 experiments
1. **UM Ablation:** Train with UM disabled (σ fixed to 0). Expect a drop in Top‑1/Top‑5 (~5‑7% based on Table IV progression), confirming its role in combating fidelity loss.  
2. **Encoder Selection Test:** Swap the CogCap Encoder for a standard architecture like EEGNet or a shallow CNN. Expect a significant performance drop (EEGNet: 31.0% Top‑1 vs. 61.2%), verifying the custom encoder’s importance.  
3. **Modality Combination Ablation:** Train STH‑Align and generator using only the image branch (no depth/edge/IP‑Adapters). Expect a drop in SSIM and structural consistency (e.g., “coffee cup” handle distortion as in Fig. 3/4), demonstrating the structural role of non‑image modalities.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper highlights several implicit open questions through its core assumptions and break conditions:
1. For Uncertainty‑Weighted Masking (UM): Is the embedding similarity from the memory bank a reliable indicator of true perceptual fidelity, or does it risk circular validation?
2. For Shared‑Trunk & Heads Alignment (STH‑Align): Can a simple MLP trunk capture fine‑grained details as effectively as a diffusion prior, especially under high neural noise?
3. For the Fusion Encoder: Does cross‑modal attention properly weight modality contributions, and is the text modality truly redundant as suggested by ablations?

## Limitations
- **UM validity assumption:** The core proxy that embedding similarity equals perceptual fidelity is untested and risks circular validation if the memory bank similarity is derived from the same alignment model.  
- **STH‑Align expressivity trade‑off:** Replacing a diffusion prior with an MLP trunk may limit representational power for fine‑grained detail reconstruction, especially under high noise.  
- **Modality utility ambiguity:** Text modality contributes minimally in ablations yet is included; its value may be marginal or redundant, adding computational cost.

## Confidence

- **UM Mechanism (Fidelity Mitigation):** Medium  
- **STH‑Align Mechanism (Shift Reduction):** Medium  
- **Fusion Encoder Complementarity:** High

## Next Checks
1. **UM Confidence Proxy Stress Test:** Train with a controlled oracle that knows true alignment difficulty (e.g., via synthetic noise levels). Compare performance against the proposed similarity‑based UM. A significant gap would weaken the core UM assumption.  
2. **STH‑Align vs. Diffusion Prior Controlled Comparison:** Re‑implement the original CogCap’s diffusion‑based alignment module. Match training data/compute and directly compare retrieval & reconstruction metrics to CognitionCapturerPro’s MLP trunk. This isolates the architectural trade‑off.  
3. **Modality Contribution Diagnostic:** During fusion training, log the attention weights or gradient norms for each modality token. Correlate these with per‑sample performance. Expectation: Depth/Edge weights should consistently correlate with SSIM; Text weight should be near‑zero or uncorrelated.
