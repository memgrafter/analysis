# Evaluating Prompting Strategies for Chart Question Answering with Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22288
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22288
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought (FS-CoT) yields highest accuracy (78.2% on GPT-4o) but lowest Exact Match (57.9% avg); Few-Shot gives best EM (64.7% avg) with competitive accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates four prompting strategies—Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought, and Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought—for chart question answering using structured tabular data from the ChartQA benchmark. The study isolates prompt design as the variable, testing across 1,200 samples and three GPT models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o). Results show that Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought consistently achieves the highest accuracy, peaking at 78.2% on GPT-4o, particularly for reasoning-intensive questions. However, it suffers from poor output formatting, yielding the lowest Exact Match at 57.9% average. In contrast, Few-Shot prompting offers the best Exact Match (64.7% average) while maintaining competitive accuracy, providing a balance between efficiency and performance. Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought provides modest gains over plain Zero-Shot at minimal cost, while Zero-Shot only performs adequately on simpler queries with the most capable models. A persistent gap of 10–20 points between Accuracy and Exact Match highlights a fundamental challenge: models often reason correctly but fail to adhere to strict output formats.

## Method Summary
The study draws 1,200 samples from the ChartQA benchmark, equally split between human-authored and synthetic questions, covering four reasoning categories: Arithmetic, Comparative, Boolean, and Direct Retrieval. Each chart is serialized into a plain-text table (CSV-like). Four prompting strategies are constructed: (1) Zero-Shot: instruction only; (2) Few-Shot: instruction plus three exemplars from the same reasoning category; (3) Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought (ZS-CoT): instruction with “Let’s think step by step.” and a blank “Reasoning:” line; (4) Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought (FS-CoT): exemplars that include concise reasoning traces ending with “Answer:”, plus the same reasoning prompt for the query. Experiments are conducted via OpenAI API using GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o with deterministic decoding (temperature=0, top-p=1). A fixed pool of 50 exemplars (balanced across categories) is used for all few-shot conditions and is disjoint from the evaluation set. Model responses are lightly normalized (lowercasing, punctuation trimming, number word conversion) and evaluated against gold answers using two metrics: Accuracy (semantic correctness, tolerant to minor formatting) and Exact Match (strict string equality). Category-level breakdowns are also reported.

## Key Results
- FS-CoT achieved the highest accuracy (up to 78.2% on GPT-4o) but the lowest Exact Match (average 57.9%).
- Few-Shot prompting provided the best Exact Match (average 64.7%) with near-top accuracy.
- A consistent 10–20 point gap between Accuracy and Exact Match was observed across all strategies and models, indicating systemic challenges with output formatting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We infer mechanisms from the observed performance patterns:
- FS-CoT’s accuracy advantage stems from explicit reasoning traces that break down complex tasks, reducing arithmetic and comparative errors. The step-by-step structure guides the model through intermediate computations, akin to human problem-solving.
- Few-Shot’s superior Exact Match likely results from exemplars demonstrating the exact output format (e.g., “Answer: <value>”), which trains the model via in-context learning to produce tightly constrained responses. The absence of extended reasoning in Few-Shot avoids extraneous text that might break strict matching.
- Zero-Shot CoT’s modest gains over Zero-Shot suggest that adding a “think step by step” prompt alone can stimulate some reasoning, but without concrete examples the model’s internalized knowledge is insufficient for optimal performance.
- The persistent Accuracy-Exact Match gap reveals that while models can arrive at correct semantic answers, they often fail to isolate the final answer cleanly—producing additional commentary, misplacing the “Answer:” anchor, or including units/formatting variations. This points to a calibration issue: models are not sufficiently trained (via prompts) to treat output format as a hard constraint.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**: Technique that elicits intermediate reasoning steps from LLMs. *Why needed*: Improves performance on tasks requiring arithmetic or multi-step logic by decomposing problems. *Quick check*: Can you generate a step-by-step solution for “What is 15% of 80?” using a simple prompt?
- **Few-Shot Learning**: Providing a small set of exemplars in the prompt to demonstrate the task. *Why needed*: Shows the model the desired input-output mapping and output format. *Quick check*: Given three question-answer pairs, can you construct a prompt that teaches the model to respond with only the numeric answer?
- **Zero-Shot Learning**: Asking the model to perform a task without examples. *Why needed*: Tests the model’s inherent knowledge and ability to follow instructions; cost-effective. *Quick check*: Does your model follow the instruction “Answer only with the number” without any examples?
- **Structured Data Serialization**: Representing charts/tables as plain text (e.g., CSV). *Why needed*: Provides LLMs with a linearized format they can process. *Quick check*: Can you convert a bar chart’s data into a CSV string that preserves all values?
- **Evaluation Metrics (Accuracy vs Exact Match)**: Accuracy measures semantic correctness, tolerant of minor formatting; Exact Match requires string identity. *Why needed*: Separates reasoning correctness from output compliance. *Quick check*: If gold answer is “42”, is “The answer is 42.” semantically correct but not exact match?
- **ChartQA Benchmark**: A dataset of chart questions with human and synthetic splits. *Why needed*: Standard benchmark for evaluating chart understanding and reasoning. *Quick check*: Are you familiar with its four reasoning categories and data distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding
The experimental architecture comprises these components:
ChartQA dataset → Prompt generator → LLM API (GPT-3.5/4/4o) → Response collector → Normalizer → Evaluator → Metrics.

Critical path: Prepare evaluation set (disjoint from exemplars) → For each prompt strategy and model, generate prompts → Call API with fixed decoding → Normalize outputs → Compute Accuracy and Exact Match → Aggregate results.

Design tradeoffs:
- Few-shot improves accuracy but increases token count and cost; also sensitivity to exemplar selection.
- Chain-of-Thought enhances reasoning but may introduce extra text that harms Exact Match; requires careful instruction to delimit final answer.
- Deterministic decoding (temperature=0) ensures reproducibility but may reduce diversity and could miss valid alternative phrasings.

Failure signatures:
- High Accuracy but low Exact Match indicates the model includes extraneous content or misplaces the answer anchor.
- Arithmetic errors concentrated in Zero-Shot and Zero-Shot CoT, especially for Arithmetic-category questions.
- Model performance gaps between human and synthetic questions may reveal dataset biases.

First three experiments to onboard:
1. **Baseline replication**: Run all four strategies on GPT-4o with the full 1,200-sample eval set. Verify that FS-CoT reaches ~78% accuracy and ~58% EM.
2. **Format enforcement test**: Modify the prompt to explicitly state “Respond only with the final answer after ‘Answer:’. No additional text.” and measure impact on EM across strategies.
3. **Few-shot exemplar variation**: Replace the fixed 50-exemplar pool with a new random sample of balanced exemplars (same size) and re-run FS-CoT to assess robustness to exemplar choice.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What specific factors cause the persistent 5–10 point gap between semantic Accuracy and Exact Match in structured chart QA, leading to correct reasoning but incorrect formatting? The paper measures the gap but does not analyze root causes.
- **Open Question 2**: How do different tabular serialization formats (e.g., JSON, row-wise key-value, columnar) interact with prompting strategies to affect accuracy and EM in chart QA? The study uses a single CSV-like format; interactions are unexplored.
- **Open Question 3**: To what extent do the observed prompting strategy hierarchies (FS-CoT > FSP > ZS-CoT > ZSP) generalize beyond the ChartQA benchmark to other structured data reasoning tasks or multimodal chart understanding? Findings may be specific to ChartQA’s distribution and text-based input.

## Limitations
- The exact composition of the 50 exemplars and the rule-based classifier for reasoning categories are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- Results are tied to a specific prompt template and CSV-like serialization; alternative representations might alter the relative performance of strategies.
- The systematic Accuracy-Exact Match gap indicates that current prompting strategies fail to enforce strict output formatting, limiting real-world deployability without post‑processing.

## Confidence
- FS‑CoT achieves highest accuracy: High (consistent across three LLMs and 1,200 samples)
- Few‑Shot yields best Exact Match: Medium (exemplar choice could shift EM)
- Zero‑Shot CoT provides modest gains: Medium (gain magnitude small, prompt‑sensitive)
- Zero‑Shot works only on simple queries: Medium (category assignment depends on unreported classifier rules)
- Accuracy‑Exact Match gap is systemic: High (observed across all strategies and models)

## Next Checks

- Vary input serialization: Convert charts to JSON or markdown tables, then re‑evaluate all four prompting strategies to test robustness to representation.
- Cross‑dataset generalization: Apply top strategies (FS‑CoT, Few-Shot) to a different chart QA benchmark (e.g., PlotQA) to assess transferability beyond ChartQA.
