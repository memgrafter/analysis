# Blind to the Human Touch: Overlap Bias in LLM-Based Summary Evaluation
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.07673
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07673
*   **Reference Count:** 0
*   **Primary Result:** LLM judges systematically prefer machine-generated summaries over human-written ones, with the bias intensifying as lexical n-gram overlap with the human reference decreases.

## Executive Summary
This study reveals a critical flaw in using large language models (LLMs) as judges for summarization quality. When presented with a human-written and a machine-generated summary, nine different LLM judges (1B–12B parameters) consistently exhibited a preference for the AI-generated summary, especially when the generated summary had low lexical similarity (measured by averaged ROUGE and BLEU scores) to the human reference. This "overlap bias" persisted across model families and was unaffected by each judge's inherent position bias (preference for first or last presented option). The findings indicate that LLM-as-judge frameworks are not semantically neutral and can be swayed by stylistic cues inherent to machine text, demanding more robust evaluation protocols beyond simple pairwise comparison.

## Method Summary
The researchers selected 6,744article-summary pairs from filtered test sets of WikiSum and CNN/DailyMail, ensuring human reference summaries were 95–105 words. Nine different LLMs served as *generators* to produce summaries via a fixed prompt. Nine different LLMs served as *evaluators* in a pairwise setup: each judge compared a human reference against a generated summary in both presentation orders using a specific prompt. A choice was deemed "consistent" if the same summary was selected in both orders; otherwise, it was classified as a "tie" with a subtype indicating order dependence. For each generated summary, an "overlap score" was computed as the average of its ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, BLEU-1, and BLEU-4 scores against the human reference. Results were binned by this score, and the proportion of evaluations falling into "Human (GT)", "Generated", "Tied-chose-first", or "Tied-chose-last" categories was plotted to analyze trends.

## Key Results
*   **Inverse Overlap Preference:** For all but one evaluator model, the proportion of judgments preferring the *generated* summary increased monotonically as the n-gram overlap between the generated summary and the human reference decreased.
*   **Position-Overlap Interaction:** Position bias (ties) was stronger when overlap was high. Larger models (more parameters) tended to exhibit a last-position bias, while smaller models showed a first-position bias.
*   **Cross-Model Robustness:** The preference for generated summaries at low overlap was consistent across diverse model architectures (Gemma, Llama, Phi, Mistral, Qwen, GPT) and scales, regardless of the judge's specific position bias direction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** LLM judges exhibit a bias preferring machine-generated summaries over human-written ones, intensifying as n-gram overlap with the human reference decreases.
*   **Mechanism:** The bias is driven by stylistic or distributional cues learned during training that are inherent to LLM-generated text. When lexical similarity is low, these cues become more salient relative to content matching, leading judges to favor the AI summary.
*   **Core Assumption:** LLM-generated text contains consistent, detectable "AI-ness" markers that judge LLMs have learned to subconsciously associate with higher quality.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] "LLM judges increasingly prefer summaries generated by other LLMs... as the similarities... decrease." [section 3] Pattern visible in Figure 4 where the proportion of 'generated' choices is larger towards the left (lower overlap).
*   **Break Condition:** Bias diminishes as overlap increases but persists even at minimal overlap; no precise threshold for consistent human preference was found.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Position bias is stronger when the generated summary has higher overlap with the human reference. Model size correlates with direction (larger models prefer last).
*   **Mechanism:** When summaries are lexically similar (high overlap), judges struggle to discriminate based on content and rely more on positional heuristics. Model size may correlate with training or architectural factors influencing this heuristic reliance.
*   **Core Assumption:** LLMs use presentation order as a shortcut when semantic distinctions are less pronounced.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] "Position bias is more prominent when the generated summaries are more like human-written summaries... models with more parameters tend to prefer the last-presented summaries." [section 3] Larger proportion of 'tied' summaries towards the right of each subfigure in Figure 4.
*   **Break Condition:** Position bias becomes less detectable at low overlap (left side of Figure 4), but the pattern changes rather than fully vanishes.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** The overlap bias is robust across all tested LLM architectures and parameter scales (1B–12B), persisting regardless of a model's position bias.
*   **Mechanism:** The preference may be an emergent property of standard LLM training (next-token prediction on web data), creating a shared susceptibility to "AI-ness" cues that overrides semantic evaluation.
*   **Core Assumption:** The bias stems from common training objectives and exposure to synthetic text distributions.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] "This pattern extends to all but one model tested, and exists regardless of the models' own position biases." [section 3] Similar preference patterns observed even when judges evaluate summaries from smaller models with opposite position biases.
*   **Break Condition:** The bias did not break for any tested model except one unspecified Gemma-3 variant, suggesting no clear mitigation within the tested range.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** n-gram Overlap Metrics (ROUGE, BLEU)
    *   **Why needed here:** Quantify lexical similarity between generated and human summaries; the core hypothesis is that judge bias varies as a function of these scores.
    *   **Quick check:** If ROUGE-1 is high but BLEU-4 is low, what does that suggest about the summary's similarity to the reference?
*   **Concept:** Position Bias in LLM Evaluation
    *   **Why needed here:** A known confounder in pairwise LLM judgments; the paper controls for it and examines its interaction with overlap bias.
    *   **Quick check:** How would you design an experiment to disentangle position bias from content preference when two summaries are presented?
*   **Concept:** LLM-as-a-Judge Framework
    *   **Why needed here:** The overall paradigm being critiqued; understanding its typical setup (pairwise comparison, zero-shot prompting) is essential.
    *   **Quick check:** What are two inherent advantages of LLM judges over traditional metrics like ROUGE, and what are two new vulnerabilities this paper reveals?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data -> Generation -> Similarity Scoring -> Judgment -> Analysis
*   **Critical Path:**
    1.  Filter datasets for human summary length (95–105 words).
    2.  Generate summaries for all articles using all generator models (temperature=0.7).
    3.  Compute average n-gram overlap score (ROUGE-1/2, BLEU-1/4) for each generated summary vs. human reference.
    4.  For each (evaluator, generator, article) tuple, run pairwise judgment with human vs. generated in both orders.
    5.  Categorize choices: Consistent "GT"/"Generated" or "Tied" with order subtype.
    6.  Aggregate by overlap bins and plot trends (Figures 4, 5).
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   Length control reduces confounder but limits generalizability to other lengths.
    *   Averaging four overlap metrics provides a robust proxy but may obscure metric-specific effects.
    *   Single human reference limits diversity; multiple references could change overlap distributions.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   High "tied" rate indicates strong, uncontrolled position bias.
    *   Non-monotonic "generated" preference trend across overlap bins contradicts main pattern.
    *   Specific model (e.g., Gemma-3-1B-it) not following trend may indicate data/prompt issues.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  Replicate Figure 4 for a single evaluator-generator pair (e.g., Gemma-3-4B-it judging Phi-4-mini outputs) to confirm the inverse correlation.
    2.  Isolate position bias: For a fixed overlap bin (e.g., 0.3–0.35), compute fraction of "tied-chose-first" vs. "tied-chose-last" per evaluator; verify larger models show more "tied-chose-last."
    3.  Stress test with adversarial rephrasing: Use the "rephrase" prompt (Figure 6) to generate summaries with artificially high overlap; check if preference for generated summaries drops as expected.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** Does the correlation between n-gram overlap and LLM preference for generated summaries hold when multiple diverse human-written summaries are available as references?
    *   **Basis:** Single human reference per article due to dataset constraints.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** A single reference may not capture variability of acceptable human summaries.
    *   **What Evidence:** Experiments with datasets containing multiple human summaries per article.
*   **Open Question 2:** What is the underlying mechanism causing position bias to intensify at higher overlap levels?
    *   **Basis:** Paper reports the interaction but does not explain it.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** Interaction is correlational; speculative whether models rely on heuristics when content is similar.
    *   **What Evidence:** Controlled experiments varying overlap and position while probing model decisions (e.g., attention analysis).
*   **Open Question 3:** Would semantic similarity metrics alter the correlation pattern compared to n-gram metrics?
    *   **Basis:** Authors note ROUGE/BLEU are a "crude approximation" and call for investigating "many more potential metrics."
    *   **Why Unresolved:** LLMs may judge based on semantics; bias pattern might change with deeper similarity measures.
    *   **What Evidence:** Recompute similarity scores using diverse semantic metrics and compare resulting preference curves.
*   **Open Question 4:** Can practical techniques mitigate overlap bias in LLM-as-judge summarization evaluation?
    *   **Basis:** Conclusion states need for "techniques beyond a simple comparison," but no mitigations are tested.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** No solution evaluation; deployment requires actionable methods.
    *   **What Evidence:** Comparative experiments applying mitigations (multi-reference averaging, chain-of-thought, calibration) and measuring bias reduction.

## Limitations
*   Exact prompt templates (Figures 1, 2, 6) are presented as images without raw text, risking prompt drift in replication.
*   Dataset filtering details beyond word count (e.g., quality filters, original sample sizes) are unspecified.
*   Implementation specifics for ROUGE/BLEU (tokenization, averaging) may affect overlap binning and trends.
*   Using a single human reference per article limits overlap variability and robustness of the observed bias pattern.

## Confidence
*   **Primary overlap bias (preference for low-overlap AI summaries): High**
    *   *Evidence:* Consistent across 8/9 models in controlled pairwise design; monotonic trend in Figure 4.
    *   *Caveat:* One model (Gemma-3-1B-it) shows weak/no bias; exact cause unknown.
*   **Position bias modulation by overlap & model size: Medium**
    *   *Evidence:* Trend visible in Figure 4 rightward shift in "tied" choices; size-direction correlation reported.
    *   *Caveat:* No formal interaction model; directionality for "larger prefers last" holds for 3/4 large models.
*   **Robustness across architectures/scales: Medium-High**
    *   *Evidence:* Pattern holds across Gemma, Llama, Phi, Mistral, Qwen, GPT families.
    *   *Caveat:* All are instruction-tuned, decoder-only models; may not generalize to other types.

2.  **Controlled overlap decoupling:** Generate summary pairs with identical semantics (e.g., via back-translation) but systematically varied lexical overlap. Test if bias persists when content is held constant. *Success:* Bias remains significant even when semantic similarity is maximized via embedding-based filtering.
