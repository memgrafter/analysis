# IBCircuit: Towards Holistic Circuit Discovery with Information Bottleneck

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2602.22581  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22581  
- Reference count: 27  
- Primary result: IBCircuit discovers circuits that are more faithful (higher logit difference/greater probability), more minimal (fewer components), and with lower output divergence than baselines, while scaling to large models without manual corruption engineering.

## Executive Summary

Circuit discovery aims to identify minimal subgraphs within language models responsible for specific tasks. Existing methods are non‑holistic and require manual design of corrupted activations. IBCircuit introduces an end‑to‑end Information Bottleneck framework that optimizes circuits holistically by injecting learnable Gaussian noise into model components (nodes/edges) and minimizing a dual objective: preserving output fidelity (via KL divergence between original and noisy model outputs) while compressing irrelevant information (via a variational upper bound on mutual information). Applied to IOI and Greater-Than tasks in GPT‑2 small and XL, IBCircuit identifies more faithful and minimal circuits than ACDC and Attribution Patching, and scales to 1.5B parameters without task‑specific engineering.

## Method Summary

IBCircuit treats each model component (attention head or edge) as a candidate for the circuit. For each component, a learnable scalar λ (sigmoid of ω) is introduced. During a forward pass on a batch, the component’s activation h is replaced by a noisy version: compute batch mean μ and variance σ² from h, sample ε ∼ N(μ,σ²), and output activation = λ·h + (1‑λ)·ε. The full model and the noisy circuit produce logits. The loss combines output fidelity and compression: L = D_KL(p_full || p_circuit) + β·D_MI, where D_MI is a variational upper bound on the mutual information between full activations and the circuit, approximated using λ, μ, σ, and batch size n (Equation 9). ω is optimized with Adam (learning rate 0.05 for nodes, 0.1 for edges, β = 1 for nodes). After training, components are ranked by λ and a top‑k set (k = floor(p·total)) is selected as the circuit. Evaluation uses activation patching: on corrupted inputs, non‑selected components are replaced with their corrupted activations, and task metrics (logit difference or greater probability) and KL divergence are measured.

## Key Results

- IBCircuit yields circuits with higher task fidelity (logit difference or greater probability) than ACDC and Attribution Patching while using fewer components.  
- The resulting circuits have lower KL divergence to the full model, indicating more faithful approximations.  
- The method scales to GPT‑2 XL (1.5B) and remains effective under high sparsity, without requiring task‑specific corruption engineering.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The Information Bottleneck principle drives IBCircuit: by learnably attenuating each component’s influence through λ and adding noise, the model is forced to rely only on those components that are critical for preserving the output distribution. The KL term ensures output fidelity, while the MI upper bound penalizes reliance on many components, naturally inducing sparsity. Joint optimization over all components (holistic) avoids the pitfalls of greedy or manually corrupted methods, leading to more faithful and minimal circuits.

## Foundational Learning

- **Information Bottleneck (IB)**: Framework for extracting a compressed representation Z of X that maximizes relevance to Y (I(Y;Z)) while minimizing redundancy (I(X;Z)). *Why needed*: Provides theoretical foundation for balancing fidelity and compression. *Quick check*: How does the IB objective trade off between preserving Y and compressing X?  
- **Mutual Information (MI)**: Quantifies the amount of information shared between two random variables. *Why needed*: The compression term in the IB objective is the mutual information between the full model and the circuit, which IBCircuit approximates. *Quick check*: What is the relationship between MI and KL divergence?  
- **Variational Upper Bound on MI**: IBCircuit uses a bound based on sample statistics to make MI tractable for continuous activations. *Why needed*: Enables differentiation through the MI term during training. *Quick check*: What are the components of the bound (Eq.9) and why is it an upper bound?  
- **KL Divergence**: Measures the difference between two probability distributions; here used to compare logits of full and noisy models. *Why needed*: Captures output fidelity; the first term in the loss. *Quick check*: How is KL divergence computed for model outputs (softmax distributions)?  
- **Activation Patching**: Technique for evaluating component importance by replacing activations from a clean run with those from a corrupted run. *Why needed*: Used for evaluation after circuit extraction; baseline methods like ACDC rely on it. *Quick check*: In activation patching, what does a large drop in performance indicate about the patched component?  
- **Circuit Sparsity**: The property of using a small number of components; a key goal. *Why needed*: IBCircuit explicitly induces sparsity via the λ parameters and thresholding. *Quick check*: How does thresholding λ relate to the number of selected components?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
The IBCircuit extends a standard Transformer by inserting a noise injection module after each target component (attention head or edge). The overall flow:  
Input tokens -> Transformer layers (each containing Multi‑Head Attention and MLP) -> Logits.  
For each component c, its activation h is processed: λ_c = sigmoid(ω_c); compute batch μ_c, σ_c from h; sample ε ∼ N(μ_c, σ_c²); output activation = λ_c·h + (1‑λ_c)·ε. The circuit is defined by thresholding λ_c to retain the top‑k components. Evaluation employs activation patching: on corrupted inputs, non‑selected components are replaced with their corrupted activations.

**Critical Path**  
The core pipeline is:  
(1) Initialize ω_c.  
(2) Forward pass with noise.  
(3) Compute loss = KL(full_logits, circuit_logits) + β·D_MI.  
(4) Backpropagate and update ω_c.  
(5) Repeat for many epochs.  
(6) Sort λ_c and pick top‑k.  
(7) Evaluate via activation patching on clean and corrupted data.

**Design Tradeoffs**  

- *Sparsity vs fidelity*: Increasing β promotes sparsity but may degrade task performance.  
- *Node vs edge granularity*: Edges offer finer‑grained circuits but increase the number of λ parameters dramatically, raising memory and compute costs.  
- *Noise statistics*: Using batch μ,σ introduces stochasticity that may affect training stability; additional ε may help.  
- *Thresholding*: Fixed k vs λ threshold leads to different circuit sizes; the choice influences the sparsity‑fidelity curve.  
- *Hyperparameters*: Learning rates (0.05 vs 0.1) and β require tuning; edge experiments may need warm‑up steps.

**Failure Signatures**  

- All λ stay near 1 → MI loss weight β too low or MI term not contributing.  
- λ collapse to 0 → β too high or training instability.  
- D_MI becomes negative or NaN → bound miscomputed; ensure A > 0, add ε to σ, clamp variances.  
- KL divergence remains high despite training → learning rate too low or full model not accurate enough.  
- Out‑of‑memory on edges → reduce batch size, use gradient checkpointing, or switch to node‑level.
