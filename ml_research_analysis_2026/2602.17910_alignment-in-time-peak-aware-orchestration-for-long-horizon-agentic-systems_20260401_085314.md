# Alignment in Time: Peak-Aware Orchestration for Long-Horizon Agentic Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17910
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17910
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: APEMO improves peak-end weighted quality by +0.0791 and reuse probability by +0.0609 over a peak-end baseline in strict 8-turn evaluations, with modest coordination cost increases.

## Executive Summary
APEMO is a runtime scheduling layer that enhances long-horizon AI agent workflows by reallocating fixed computational budgets toward temporally critical segments (peaks and endings) based on real-time behavioral frustration proxies. It operates without model weight changes, instead dynamically adjusting per-turn reasoning precision under a global compute cap. Evaluations on small models show significant gains in trajectory-level robustness metrics—peak-end weighted quality, reuse probability, and reduced average frustration—particularly in multi-agent flows. The approach operationalizes the psychological peak-end rule and demonstrates that targeted temporal control is an effective, cost-aware alignment mechanism for extended agentic systems.

## Method Summary
APEMO functions as an orthogonal overlay on existing agentic topologies (e.g., planner-executor-critic). At each turn, a behavioral analyzer computes a scalar frustration score `S_f(t)` from signals like repetition similarity and context drift. When a negative peak is detected—indicating trajectory instability—a precision repair is triggered: the system increases reasoning effort (e.g., more sampling, longer outputs) for the current and final turns while reducing precision on flagged low-impact turns, all while enforcing a fixed global compute budget `C_max`. The optimization explicitly weights worst-turn and end-turn quality to maximize a composite metric `Q`, inspired by the peak-end rule. No model fine-tuning occurs; improvements come purely from compute reallocation guided by real-time trajectory monitoring.

## Key Results
- Peak-end weighted quality (`Q`) improved by +0.0791 over a peak-end baseline and +0.1874 over an affect-aware baseline in 8-turn evaluations.
- Reuse probability (`R`) increased by +0.0609 and +0.1161 against the same baselines.
- Average frustration (`F`) decreased, with robust endpoint stabilization under perturbation recovery.
- In multi-agent planner-executor-critic flows, APEMO increased quality by +0.1782 over a plain flow baseline, with only modest coordination cost increases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Behavioral Proxy-Driven Peak Detection**
APEMO infers trajectory instability via low-cost behavioral proxies (e.g., repetition similarity, context drift) aggregated into a frustration score `S_f(t)`. A negative peak is flagged when `S_f(t)` crosses a threshold, triggering targeted compute reallocation to the peak and final turns. The core assumption is that observable output patterns reliably correlate with underlying instability that could lead to failures. Evidence includes the internal control loop design and ablation against baselines, though human validation of proxy fidelity is pending. If proxies fail to align with human-perceived frustration or downstream failure, repair timing misaligns.

**Mechanism 2: Peak-End Weighted Optimization**
The system optimizes a metric `Q` that explicitly weights the worst turn and the final two turns, operationalizing the psychological peak-end rule. This assumes that user reuse decisions depend disproportionately on these segments rather than average performance. Evidence is theoretical (citing HCI literature) and internal (improved `Q` and `R` scores), but direct human-subject linkage remains untested. If reuse decisions correlate more with mean consistency or transparency, the weighting mis-specifies the target.

**Mechanism 3: Fixed-Budget Temporal Reallocation**
APEMO acts as a scheduler that shifts compute from temporally low-impact turns (identified via a salience model) to high-weight peak/end turns, without increasing total budget. This zero-sum reallocation assumes sufficient variance in per-turn marginal utility. Evidence includes the constrained optimization formulation and cost-benefit analyses showing quality gains often outpace coordination overhead, especially for longer horizons (`T=8` vs. `T=2`). If coordination overhead is underestimated or all turns are equally critical, net gains diminish or reverse.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
1. Per-Turn Behavioral Analyzer: Aggregates outputs and context into frustration score `S_f(t)`.
2. Temporal Significance Model: Estimates turn importance based on position and frustration level.
3. Budget & Precision Controller: Manages global compute ledger, adjusts precision (e.g., sampling, token limits) per turn.
4. (Optional) Repair Module: Executes extra compute (e.g., additional reasoning passes) on flagged turns.

**Critical path:**
Output y_t & context → Behavioral Analyzer computes S_f(t) → Check if S_f(t) < threshold AND (peak OR end phase) → YES → Significance Model marks turn t as high-weight → Budget Controller allocates extra precision (borrowing from low-weight turns) → Repair Module executes.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Horizon length: Benefits amortize over longer trajectories (stronger for `T=8` vs. `T=2`).
- Baseline strength: Gains diminish against sophisticated temporal baselines.
- Proxy fidelity: Simpler proxies reduce overhead but risk false triggers.

**Failure signatures:**
- No improvement in `Q` or `R` on short horizons or vs. strong temporal baselines.
- Coordination cost increase approaches or exceeds quality gain.
- `S_f(t)` shows no correlation with human frustration labels.
- Improved endpoints but degraded mid-trajectory consistency.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Proxy validation: Correlate `S_f(t)` with human frustration labels; target `r > 0.6`.
2. Horizon sensitivity: Vary `T` from 2 to 20; expect superlinear `ΔQ` growth beyond `T≈6`.
3. Orthogonality sanity check: Overlay APEMO on `plan_execute_reflect`; expect positive but smaller `ΔQ` than vs. `plan_execute`.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does peak-aware orchestration improve human trust and reuse intention? (No human-subject validation yet.)
2. Can learned temporal schedulers outperform fixed-threshold APEMO? (Fixed thresholds may not generalize across tasks.)
3. How do model scale and context length affect the cost-benefit trade-off? (Only small models and short horizons tested.)
4. What causes high variance in trap-recovery rebound across model families? (Source of variability unknown.)

## Limitations
- Behavioral proxy specifications (exact `S_f(t)` formulation and thresholds) are underspecified.
- Repair mechanism details (e.g., precision adjustment method) are conceptually described but not implemented concretely.
- The peak-end rule's applicability to AI agent reuse decisions lacks direct human-subject validation.

## Confidence
- Empirical gains in reported benchmarks: High (numerically precise, controlled comparisons).
- Mechanism causality (proxy-driven reallocation): Medium (coherent logic but proxy validity inferential).
- Generalizability across domains/horizons: Low (limited to small models, `T=8`, synthetic flows).
