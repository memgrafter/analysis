# NCL-UoR at SemEval-2026 Task 5: Embedding-Based Methods, Fine-Tuning, and LLMs for Word Sense Plausibility Rating

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08256  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08256  
- **Reference count:** 23  
- **Primary result:** Structured prompting with GPT-4o (prompt P2) achieves ρ=0.731 and accuracy=0.794 on the SemEval-2026 Task 5 test set, substantially outperforming fine-tuned models and embedding-based methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles word sense plausibility rating for ambiguous homonyms in narratives. The authors compare three families of approaches: embedding-based regression, fine-tuned transformer models with LoRA, and LLM prompting. The best system employs GPT-4o with a structured prompt that decomposes evaluation across narrative components (precontext, target sentence, ending) and applies explicit decision rules for rating calibration. This approach yields a Spearman correlation of 0.731 and accuracy of 0.794 on the test set, far surpassing fine-tuned models (best ρ=0.527) and embedding methods (best ρ=0.133). The findings demonstrate that prompt design and reasoning structure are more critical than model scale for this task.

## Method Summary
The task involves predicting a continuous plausibility score between 1 and 5 for a candidate sense of a homonym within a five-sentence narrative. Three parallel architectures are implemented: (1) **Embedding branch** – extracts handcrafted features (similarities, lexical, structural) from Sentence‑BERT embeddings of the story parts and the sense description, then regresses with Ridge or XGBoost. (2) **Fine‑tuning branch** – applies LoRA to pre‑trained transformers (ELECTRA, DeBERTa) and trains with a combination of MSE, pairwise RankNet loss (optimizing for rank correlation), and an uncertainty‑aware loss to down‑weight samples with high annotator disagreement. (3) **LLM prompting branch** – compares two prompting strategies: P1 (few‑shot) and P2 (structured). P2 adds component‑wise evaluation and deterministic decision rules (e.g., “if ending contradicts, rate 1–2”). The structured prompt with GPT‑4o produces the best performance.

## Key Results
- Structured prompting (P2) with GPT‑4o: ρ=0.731, accuracy=0.794 (test).  
- Best fine‑tuned model (DeBERTa‑large): ρ=0.527 (test); dev‑to‑test drop indicates poor generalization.  
- Embedding‑based methods (Ridge, XGBoost): ρ<0.18, failing to capture narrative reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Structured Prompting Calibrates Reasoning
The structured prompt (P2) breaks the narrative into precontext, target, and ending, then applies explicit decision rules (e.g., contradictory ending forces rating 1–2). This enforces a reasoning process aligned with annotation guidelines, anchoring judgments to the disambiguating ending and reducing reliance on memorized few‑shot examples. Evidence: P2 outperforms P1 and all fine‑tuned models; the prompt design is the key differentiator.  
**Break condition:** If the ending is absent or ambiguously supports multiple senses, the rigid rules may misalign with graded human judgments.

### Mechanism 2: Fine‑Tuning Struggles with Compositional Narrative Reasoning
LoRA fine‑tuning with auxiliary losses (pairwise ranking, uncertainty‑aware) improves over basic regression but still lags behind LLM prompting. The model cannot generalize the compositional reasoning needed to integrate evidence across narrative parts. The auxiliary losses help align training with evaluation metrics and handle noise, yet the task’s context‑specific patterns are too varied to be captured by weight updates alone. Evidence: Large dev‑to‑test performance drops (e.g., ELECTRA‑large: 0.644 → 0.527) indicate overfitting and poor generalization.  
**Break condition:** Test stories that are lexically or structurally distant from training will degrade fine‑tuned performance more severely than prompting.

### Mechanism 3: Embedding Methods Lack Narrative Dynamics
Embedding approaches compute static similarity between story parts and sense descriptions, treating the narrative as a bag‑of‑context. This misses sequential, causal, and contradictory relationships crucial for plausibility judgments. Evidence: All embedding variants yield ρ<0.18.  
**Break condition:** Any task requiring structural or temporal flow analysis will likely fail with such features.

## Foundational Learning
### Graded Word Sense Assignment
The task reframes Word Sense Disambiguation as regression on a 1–5 plausibility scale, recognizing that senses exist on a continuum and human judgments involve degrees of support.  
**Quick check:** If a story’s ending strongly supports sense A but the precontext strongly supports sense B, what does a “moderately plausible” rating (3) for sense B imply about the human judgment process?

### Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (LoRA)
LoRA adapts large pre‑trained models (e.g., DeBERTa) with minimal added parameters, avoiding catastrophic overfitting on the small training set (2,280 samples) while learning task‑specific patterns.  
**Quick check:** Why might using a higher LoRA rank `r` (e.g., 12 vs. 8) risk overfitting in this dataset, even with early stopping?

### Structured Prompting with Decision Rules
The top system relies on a meticulously designed prompt that decomposes the task and imposes hard constraints, turning prompt engineering into program design that guides the LLM through a fixed reasoning skeleton.  
**Quick check:** How does the rule “if evidence is mixed or unclear, choose the lower plausible rating” encode a conservative bias, and why might this be particularly suited to the annotation guidelines?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input (homonym, candidate sense, precontext, target sentence, ending) feeds three parallel branches:  
- Embedding: Story+Sense → Sentence‑BERT embeddings → Feature extraction (similarity, lexical, structural) → Regressor (Ridge/XGBoost) → Rating.  
- Fine‑tuning: Story+Sense → Transformer (ELECTRA/DeBERTa) with LoRA adapters → Pooling (mean/[CLS]/attention) → Regression head → Rating.  
- LLM prompting: Story+Sense → Structured prompt (P1 or P2) → LLM (GPT/Llama/Ministral) → Parsed integer rating.  

**Critical path:** For the top system, the decisive flow is: Structured prompt (P2) → GPT‑4o → Parsed rating. The prompt’s component breakdown and decision rules are the active differentiators, not the model itself (as shown by GPT‑4o outperforming GPT‑5.2 with the same prompt).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Prompt complexity vs consistency: P2’s rules improve calibration but add rigidity; may fail on uncovered edge cases.  
- Model scale vs reasoning structure: GPT‑4o outperforms larger GPT‑5.2, suggesting a Capability‑Prompt Fit—smaller, instruction‑tuned models may follow structured rules more reliably.  
- Fine‑tuning efficiency vs adaptability: LoRA is parameter‑efficient but locks knowledge; prompting offers dynamic, interpretable reasoning but incurs API cost/latency.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Embedding branch: Near‑zero ρ (<0.2) across all test sets.  
- Fine‑tuning branch: Significant dev→test drop (e.g., ELECTRA‑large: 0.644 → 0.527), indicating overfitting.  
- LLM prompting (P1): Predictions cluster at integer values, lacking fine‑grained calibration.  
- LLM prompting (P2, GPT‑4o): Slight over‑correction for high‑plausibility (MAE higher for ratings 4.5–5.0), reflecting conservative bias.  
- Universal: High MAE for mid‑range ratings and samples with high annotator disagreement (σ≥1.0).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Ablate each decision rule in P2 (e.g., remove “if ending contradicts…” rule) to measure its isolated contribution to ρ=0.731.  
2. Compare pooling strategies (mean, [CLS], attention) for DeBERTa‑large on the dev set with MSE+RankNet+Uncertainty loss, mirroring the paper’s mean pooling choice.  
3. Apply the identical P2 prompt to other LLMs (Claude 3.5, Llama 3.2‑70B) to test cross‑model portability of structured prompting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Ensemble methods:** Can combining fine‑tuned models and LLM predictors improve performance? The paper did not explore ensembles, leaving potential synergies unknown.  
- **Prompt conflict resolution:** How to improve structured prompts to better handle conflicts between narrative components (e.g., precontext vs. ending)? Current rules are basic; advanced conflict‑handling could reduce errors in mid‑range ratings.  
- **Cross‑model prompt generalizability:** Does the structured prompt work equally across diverse LLM architectures without per‑model tuning? Performance differences between GPT‑4o and GPT‑5.2 suggest possible model‑specific adaptation needs.  
- **Multilingual and longer contexts:** Does the approach generalize to other languages or longer narratives? Evaluation was limited to five‑sentence English stories; extension may require prompt modifications.

## Limitations
- Prompt fidelity: Exact whitespace/formatting may affect GPT‑4o; reproduction requires an identical string.  
- Generalizability: P2’s gains may be tied to GPT‑4o’s instruction‑following; other LLMs might not adhere to the rules.  
- Data size: Small training set (2,280) limits fine‑tuning; more data could close the performance gap.  
- Test distribution: Dev‑to‑test drop for fine‑tuned models suggests possible domain shift.  
- Cost: API dependence on GPT‑4o hinders scalability and reproducibility.

## Confidence
- **Structured prompting (Mechanism 1):** High – clear performance leap and internal validation.  
- **Fine‑tuning limits (Mechanism 2):** Medium – dev‑to‑test drop indicates generalization issues, but causal attribution to compositional reasoning is interpretive.  
- **Embedding inadequacy (Mechanism 3):** High – consistently very low ρ (<0.18) strongly supports the claim.
