# Prioritize the Process, Not Just the Outcome: Rewarding Latent Thought Trajectories Improves Reasoning in Looped Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10520
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10520
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** RLTT, a reward aggregation framework for looped LMs, yields large gains on math reasoning (+14.4% to +34.3%) and promotes shorter, more token‑efficient responses.

## Executive Summary
Looped Language Models (LoopLMs) perform multi‑step latent reasoning before generating each token, but standard reinforcement learning methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) only reward the final latent state. This misaligns the learning signal with the iterative computation. The authors propose Reward Latent Thought Trajectories (RLTT), which distributes reinforcement learning reward across all latent thought distributions by aggregating next‑token distributions from every internal loop in the policy gradient. RLTT provides dense credit assignment without external verifiers and with negligible overhead. Evaluated on Ouro‑2.6B‑Thinking, RLTT substantially outperforms GRPO on a suite of mathematical reasoning benchmarks and induces more token‑efficient outputs, demonstrating the effectiveness of trajectory‑level credit assignment for LoopLMs.

## Method Summary
RLTT modifies the standard policy gradient used in GRPO by summing, for each generated token, the log‑probabilities from every latent loop (t = 1 … T_max), weighted by a coefficient ω_t. This loss term directly depends on the full latent thought trajectory, forcing all intermediate reasoning steps to move toward directions indicated by the final outcome. The total objective combines this policy gradient with a KL penalty against a frozen reference policy, computed only on the terminal‑loop distribution. During training, for each batch, the algorithm samples rollouts, records per‑loop log‑probabilities for every token, computes group‑normalized advantages from binary correctness rewards, and updates the model. The approach requires capturing intermediate logits, increasing memory linearly with the number of loops, but yields a richer gradient signal and encourages the latent reasoning to converge quickly, leading to shorter generated responses at inference.

## Key Results
- **Large accuracy gains:** +14.4% on MATH‑500, +16.6% on AIME24, +10.0% on BeyondAIME, and +34.3% on GSM8K; average improvements of +18.8% on math benchmarks and +6.6% on non‑math benchmarks (ARC‑C, MMLU‑ST, GPQA, MBPP).
- **Token‑efficient outputs:** RLTT reduces response length by approximately 10% and lowers entropy, indicating faster latent convergence.
- **Improved gradient signal:** Gradient signal‑to‑noise ratio (GSNR) is significantly higher on the hardest math tasks (AIME24, BeyondAIME) compared to GRPO, while remaining comparable on easier ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### **Mechanism 1: Trajectory‑Level Credit Assignment**
- **Claim:** By distributing reinforcement learning reward across all latent thought distributions instead of only the final loop, RLTT aligns the learning signal with the LoopLM’s multi‑step internal computation, resolving the credit assignment bottleneck inherent in standard objectives like GRPO.
- **Mechanism:** The policy gradient is altered to sum, for each generated token, the log‑probabilities from every loop t = 1..T_max, weighted by ω_t. This makes the loss a direct function of the entire latent thought trajectory P^(1)_θ → … → P^(T_max)_θ, forcing all intermediate reasoning steps to move toward the high‑advantage directions indicated by the final outcome.
- **Core assumption:** The intermediate latent thought distributions, although not exposed during standard inference, are meaningful predictors of eventual success and can be shaped by the terminal reward.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: “RLTT distributes reward across the full latent reasoning trajectory by aggregating next‑token distributions from all internal loops in the policy gradient.”  
  [section 3.2]: Equation (4) formalizes the gradient sum over loops; Figure 1 illustrates the credit assignment difference.  
  [corpus]: Neighboring works on process‑based rewards (e.g., “Stop Rewarding Hallucinated Steps…”, “Process‑based Self‑Rewarding Language Models”) converge on the insight that dense, step‑level supervision improves reasoning.
- **Break condition:** If early latent distributions are highly stochastic or only weakly correlated with the final outcome, the extra gradient terms could introduce noise without improving learning.

### **Mechanism 2: Token‑Efficient Reasoning**
- **Claim:** RLTT produces shorter, more token‑efficient responses because it encourages rapid convergence of the latent reasoning process, reducing the need for lengthy explicit token generation at inference time.
- **Mechanism:** By aligning every latent distribution with the advantage estimate, RLTT creates pressure to stabilize on the correct reasoning pattern within the latent loops themselves. Consequently, the model does not rely on extended chain‑of‑thought token sequences to correct or refine its answer, so the generated output becomes more concise.
- **Core assumption:** There is a substitutability between latent computation (loops) and explicit token output; the optimal policy minimizes total computation (latent + token steps) for a given reward.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: “RLTT induces shorter, more token‑efficient responses.”  
  [section 5.1.2]: Figure 3 shows RLTT‑trained models steadily reducing response length; Table 1 reports a 10% reduction in training time.  
  [section 6.1]: Theorem A.5 proves that under diminishing returns and a higher per‑token uncertainty cost for RLTT, the optimal decoding length L*_RLTT ≤ L*_GRPO.
- **Break condition:** For tasks that intrinsically require long, explicit reasoning traces (e.g., formal proofs), forcing early latent convergence could degrade accuracy despite improved efficiency.

### **Mechanism 3: Richer Gradient Signal**
- **Claim:** RLTT improves gradient signal‑to‑noise ratio (GSNR), especially on the hardest benchmarks, because distributing credit across the latent thought trajectory yields a denser and more informative learning signal per rollout.
- **Mechanism:** Each token’s gradient contribution is expanded from a single terminal‑loop term to a weighted sum over all loops. This increases the number of gradient components that directly reflect the final advantage, mitigating the credit assignment problem over long latent sequences.
- **Core assumption:** In LoopLMs, the terminal‑only gradient suffers from a “bottleneck” where the reward must back‑propagate through many latent refinements, amplifying noise; exposing the full trajectory increases gradient consistency.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: “improves gradient signal on challenging tasks.”  
  [section 5.1.3] & [appendix A.7]: GSNR measurements show RLTT significantly outperforms GRPO on AIME24 and BeyondAIME (hardest), while GSM8K shows a decrease attributed to training saturation.  
  [corpus]: Neighbors focusing on step‑level process rewards (e.g., “Reinforcing Chain‑of‑Thought Reasoning with Self‑Evolving Rubrics”) similarly report richer gradient information.
- **Break condition:** If the per‑loop distributions are highly correlated (i.e., loops do not diversify the gradient), the additional terms may become redundant and not enhance signal quality.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Looped Language Model Architecture**  
*Why needed:* RLTT is designed specifically for LoopLMs, which perform multiple latent iterations per token. Engineers must understand how the forward pass reuses transformer blocks, how to extract per‑loop hidden states h_j^(t), and how to map them to token distributions via the LM head.  
*Quick check:* During token generation, where in the code would you insert hooks to capture the logits from each intermediate loop before the final sampling distribution is used?

**Concept 2: Policy Gradient Methods with KL Regularization**  
*Why needed:* RLTT modifies the standard REINFORCE‑style gradient (used in GRPO) and adds a KL penalty against a frozen reference policy. Implementers need to correctly combine the two loss terms and compute advantages (group‑normalized).  
*Quick check:* In the combined objective J_RLTT(θ) = J_RLTT_PG(θ) + β D_KL(π_θ ∥ π_ref), why is the KL term computed only on the terminal‑loop distribution P^(T_max)_θ, while the PG term uses the summed trajectory?

**Concept 3: Memory‑Throughput Tradeoffs in RL Training**  
*Why needed:* Storing per‑loop log‑probabilities for every token increases memory linearly with T_max, forcing a reduction in tokens per GPU (ppo_max_token_len_per_gpu). Engineers must balance memory constraints with training efficiency (e.g., using more gradient accumulation).  
*Quick check:* If the original GRPO configuration allows 16K tokens per GPU but RLTT requires keeping T_max=4 sets of logits per token, what is the minimum new token limit, and how might you compensate to maintain the same effective batch size?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Looped LM policy network (θ) → Frozen reference policy (θ_ref) → Loop‑weighting module → Reward function (binary correctness) → Advantage estimator (group‑normalized) → Loss (J_RLTT = J_RLTT_PG + β D_KL) → Optimizer (AdamW with 8‑bit, gradient clip 0.1)

**Critical path:**
1. For each prompt batch, sample g rollouts y_i ∼ π_θ(·|x) using the model’s autoregressive sampling, which internally runs T_max loops per token.
2. For every generated token position j and every loop t, record the log‑probability ℓ_{i,j,t} = log P^(t)_θ(y_{i,j} | x, y_{i,<j}).
3. Compute advantages Â_{i} from the binary rewards r_i (group‑normalized across rollouts).
4. Compute the RLTT PG loss: - (1/(g·L)) Σ_{i,j,t} ω_t * ℓ_{i,j,t} * Â_{i}.
5. For each token, compute the KL divergence between the terminal‑loop distribution P^(T_max)_θ and P^(T_max)_θ_ref, average over batch.
6. Sum the two losses, scale KL by β, back‑propagate, and update θ.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Memory vs. sequence length:* storing per‑loop logits multiplies memory by T_max, forcing a lower ppo_max_token_len_per_gpu (Table 1 uses 8192 for RLTT vs larger for GRPO). Compensate with more gradient accumulation steps or fewer concurrent samples.
- *Loop weighting:* The paper finds uniform, progressive(α=1), and exit‑probability strategies yield similar performance (Table 3); exit‑probability leverages Ouro’s learned halting signal but requires that signal’s availability. For general LoopLMs without early exit, uniform weighting is a simple fallback.
- *KL coefficient β:* Too high prevents adaptation; too low risks collapse. Paper uses β=1e‑3; a smaller T_max may allow larger β due to lower variance.

**Failure signatures:**
- *No response‑length reduction:* RLTT should shorten outputs (Fig 3). If lengths remain constant, the trajectory‑level signal may be mis‑implemented (e.g., only terminal loop used).
- *No reward improvement:* The gap between RLTT and GRPO in Figure 2 should emerge early. If absent, check if per‑loop logits are correctly captured and weighted.
- *Out‑of‑memory crashes:* Verify that ppo_max_token_len_per_gpu is reduced appropriately relative to T_max; if using uniform weighting, memory scales linearly with T_max.
- *GSNR not improving on hard tasks:* Compute validation GSNR as in Appendix A.7; a drop on easy tasks (like GSM8K) may be saturation, but on hard tasks a failure to improve could indicate poor gradient aggregation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Core replication:** Train Ouro‑2.6B‑Thinking on the MATH train split with RLTT and GRPO under identical hyperparameters (Table 6). Evaluate on MATH‑500 deterministic to confirm RLTT’s +14% gain.
2. **Weighting ablation:** Run RLTT with uniform, progressive (α=1), and exit‑probability weighting; expect small performance differences (<1‑2% across benchmarks) to confirm robustness (Table 3).
3. **Token‑efficiency validation:** During training, log average response length and entropy every few steps. RLTT should show decreasing length and entropy (Figures 3 & 4) while GRPO remains flat.

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to Ouro‑2.6B‑Thinking; generalizability to other LoopLMs unclear.
- Benefits primarily on math; may degrade on tasks needing long explicit reasoning.
- Memory overhead scales with loop count; optimal weighting task‑dependent.
- Relies on binary rewards; performance with learned/sparse rewards unknown.

## Confidence
- **Accuracy improvement:** High on evaluated math benchmarks, Low for broader generalization.
- **Token‑efficient reasoning:** Medium; length reduction observed but link to latent convergence not fully proven.
- **Richer gradient signal:** Medium; GSNR gains on hard math shown, but underlying mechanism and transfer uncertain.
