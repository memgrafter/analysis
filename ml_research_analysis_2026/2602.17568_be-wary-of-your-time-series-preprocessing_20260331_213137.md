# Be Wary of Your Time Series Preprocessing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17568
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17568
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: Normalization choice critically affects Transformer expressivity for time series, with instance scaling preserving per-channel dynamics and global scaling amplifying high-variance channels; no single normalizer dominates across tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how different normalization techniques impact the expressivity of Transformer models for time series analysis. Through theoretical analysis, the authors derive expressivity bounds showing that instance-based normalization (per-sample scaling) preserves individual channel characteristics, while global normalization (using dataset-wide statistics) makes model sensitivity dominated by high-variance channels. Empirical validation on classification (UEA archive) and forecasting (ETT-Small) datasets confirms that the optimal normalizer is task-dependent: instance scaling excels on JapaneseVowels classification (98.11% vs. 97.84% for global Standard), whereas global Standard yields lower MAE on ETT-Small forecasting (e.g., 0.7617 vs. 1.0023 for instance). The findings underscore normalization as a crucial, often overlooked, design choice in time series Transformers.

## Method Summary
The study combines theoretical expressivity analysis with empirical comparisons. Theoretically, it introduces an (ϵ,σ,γ)-expressiveness metric to measure a model’s ability to distinguish between nearby inputs, deriving closed-form bounds for Standard and Min-Max scaling under instance and global schemes. Empirically, it evaluates multiple Transformer architectures (Vanilla Transformer, PatchTST, Autoformer, TimesNet) on multivariate time series classification (UEA datasets) and forecasting (ETT-Small), testing five normalization conditions: instance Standard/Min-Max, global Standard/Min-Max, and no normalization. Performance is measured via classification accuracy and forecasting MAE over multiple runs, with consistent training protocols (Adam, LR 1e-3, 100 epochs, early stopping).

## Key Results
- Instance-based normalization preserves per-channel dynamics by making model sensitivity inversely proportional to each channel’s variance, preventing dominance by any single channel.
- Global standardization amplifies the influence of high-variance channels, suppressing information from lower-variance ones, as bound γ scales inversely with global variance.
- No normalizer universally outperforms; task-specific effects observed: instance scaling best for JapaneseVowels classification, global Standard best for ETT-Small forecasting, and sometimes no normalization is optimal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Instance-based normalization preserves per-channel dynamics by making model sensitivity dependent on each channel’s individual variance.
- **Mechanism:** The expressivity bound γ for instance-based Standard scaling is proportional to `√(∑ 1/v_i²)`. This means the model’s ability to distinguish similar inputs is inversely related to the precision (variance) of each channel separately, preventing any single channel from dominating the representation.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s Lipschitz constants (C₁, C₂) and attention structure allow the input sensitivity to decompose additively across channels after normalization.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "instance scaling preserves per‑channel dynamics."
  - [section 4.2] Theorem 1 bound: `γ ∝ √(∑ 1/v_i²) ϵ/σ`.
  - [corpus] Weak; no direct anchor found in neighboring papers.
- **Break condition:** When a task requires comparing relative magnitudes across channels (e.g., anomaly detection across sensors), instance scaling may hinder cross-channel alignment.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Global standardization amplifies the influence of high-variance channels, suppressing information from lower-variance channels.
- **Mechanism:** The expressivity bound for global Standard scaling simplifies to `γ ∝ (√d / v) ϵ/σ`, where v is the global variance. If one channel has disproportionately high variance, v becomes large, γ becomes small, and the model becomes less sensitive to variations in other channels.
- **Core assumption:** Global variance v aggregates all channel scales; dominance by one channel is not mitigated by the normalization step.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2] "global scaling makes model sensitivity dominated by high‑variance channels."
  - [section 4.2] Theorem 1 global bound: `γ = ϵ√d / (σ v)`.
  - [corpus] Adjacent paper *On the Role of Reversible Instance Normalization* discusses spatial/temporal distribution shifts, supporting the idea that global statistics can obscure local patterns.
- **Break condition:** When no single channel dominates the global variance, or when cross-channel scale consistency is semantically meaningful (e.g., multivariate signals with physically comparable units).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The expressivity framework links preprocessing directly to the model’s capacity to separate semantically similar inputs, making normalization a tunable lever on representation quality.
- **Mechanism:** Expressivity `E_ϵ[f]` measures the probability that representations of ϵ‑similar inputs differ by more than σ. Normalization rescales inputs, effectively altering the neighborhood size ϵ in the original feature space, which then propagates through the model’s Lipschitz constant to bound `∥f(X)−f(˜X)∥`.
- **Core assumption:** The Transformer’s self‑attention and feed‑forward layers are Lipschitz continuous; thus input perturbations transform predictably through the network.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] Definition 1: `(ϵ,σ,γ)`‑expressive model.
  - [section 4.2] Proof structure: `∥f(X)−f(˜X)∥ ≤ (normalization factor) × (model factor) × ϵ`.
  - [corpus] *A Comparative Study on How Data Normalization Affects Zero‑Shot Generalization in Time Series Foundation Models* explicitly studies normalization’s role in generalization, aligning with the expressivity‑performance link.
- **Break condition:** If the model’s internal nonlinearities (e.g., softmax saturation) or optimization dynamics violate the Lipschitz assumptions, the derived bounds may not predict empirical expressivity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Expressivity Metric `(ϵ,σ,γ)`‑expressive
  - **Why needed here:** Quantifies how well a model preserves or exaggerates differences between similar inputs; normalization directly influences this by rescaling input neighborhoods.
  - **Quick check question:** If you halve all channel variances via instance Standard scaling, what happens to the bound γ and why?
- **Concept:** Translation Invariance of Self‑Attention
  - **Why needed here:** Explains why mean‑centering (in Standard scaling) does not affect attention outputs; only the variance (scale) matters for the bounds.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does subtracting a per‑channel constant not change the dot‑product attention matrix?
- **Concept:** Lipschitz Continuity of Network Components
  - **Why needed here:** The expressivity bounds rely on composing Lipschitz constants of attention, FFN, and layer‑norm; normalization changes the input Lipschitz constant.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the norm of `W_FFN` (C₂ in the proofs) affect the overall sensitivity?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Time Series -> Normalization -> Tokenization -> Transformer Encoder -> Pooling -> Task Head
- **Critical path:** Normalization occurs at the very front; its scaling factors directly multiply the input to the first Transformer layer, affecting all subsequent gradient flows and attention logits.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Instance vs. Global:** Instance scaling treats channels independently—good for tasks where per-channel morphology matters (e.g., vowel classification). Global scaling enforces cross-channel scale comparability—good for forecasting where absolute magnitude relationships matter (as in ETT‑Small results).
  - **None vs. Any:** Omitting normalization may work if data are already well‑scaled (e.g., UWaveGestureLibrary) or if model’s positional embeddings/attentions are scale‑invariant enough; but risks unstable training on heterogeneous‑scale data.
  - **Task‑dependency:** Classification often benefits from instance scaling (preserves local shape), forecasting from global scaling (preserves long‑range amplitude trends).
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High MAE in forecasting with instance scaling** (as seen on ETT‑Small) suggests global trends are under‑represented.
  - **Low classification accuracy with global scaling** on datasets like PEMS‑SF or Handwriting indicates loss of channel‑specific patterns.
  - **Training instability** (exploding/vanishing gradients) without normalization on high‑variance data.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate key results:** Run Transformer on JapaneseVowels (classification) and ETT‑Small (forecasting) with instance Standard, global Standard, and None. Verify that instance wins on JapaneseVowels and global wins on ETT‑Small.
  2. **Channel dominance test:** Synthetically create a dataset where one channel has 10× the variance of others; compare instance vs. global Standard scaling. Expect global to suppress minor channels, hurting performance if minor channels are label‑informative.
  3. **Ablation on pre‑normalized data:** Take a dataset already normalized (e.g., UWaveGestureLibrary) and test all methods; expect minimal differences, confirming that normalization matters mostly when raw scales vary.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Do the expressivity bounds for single‑layer, 1‑Lipschitz Transformers hold for deep architectures with activations like GELU?  
  *Basis in paper:* Assumes single layer and 1‑Lipschitz activations for tractability.  
  *Why unresolved:* Practical time series Transformers are deep and use non‑Lipschitz activations; bounds may not apply.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Extend the theory to multi‑layer settings with common activations and validate empirically.

- **Open Question 2:** Can the framework theoretically analyze Quantile and Robust normalizations, which were only tested empirically?  
  *Basis in paper:* Theory covers only Standard and Min‑Max scaling; other normalizations lack analysis.  
  *Why unresolved:* Without theory, reasons for their occasional superiority remain unexplained.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Derive expressivity bounds for these methods and correlate with performance.

- **Open Question 3:** How does tokenization (patch size/stride) interact with normalization to affect expressivity?  
  *Basis in paper:* Normalization studied in isolation; tokenization is a known factor.  
  *Why unresolved:* Token granularity may change the impact of scaling, but this is unknown.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Empirical study varying patch sizes across normalization methods; extend theory to include tokenization.

- **Open Question 4:** How sensitive is model expressivity to errors in estimated normalization statistics?  
  *Basis in paper:* Assumes true statistics known; in practice they are estimated from finite data.  
  *Why unresolved:* Estimation error could degrade performance, possibly explaining benefits of no normalization.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Analyze perturbed bounds with inaccurate statistics; test performance with varying sample sizes for estimation.

## Limitations
- Dataset splits and preprocessing details for UEA/ETT‑Small not fully specified; reproduction assumptions may cause variance.
- Theory assumes perfect Lipschitz continuity and translation‑invariant self‑attention; real Transformers with softmax and layer norms may violate.
- Empirical validation limited to two tasks (classification, forecasting) and few Transformer variants; poorly tested on other architectures or tasks (e.g., anomaly detection).
- Expressivity bound is worst‑case; correlation with actual performance not proven.

## Confidence
- Global scaling dominance: **High** (theorem proof, solid math).
- Instance scaling preserves per‑channel dynamics: **High** (bound depends on per‑channel variance).
- No universal optimal normalizer; task‑dependency: **Medium** (only one dataset per task tested).
