# Legal interpretation and AI: from expert systems to argumentation and LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05392

## Executive Summary
The paper surveys the evolution of artificial intelligence in legal interpretation, focusing on three paradigms: expert systems (1970s–1990s), argumentation frameworks, and large language models (LLMs). Expert systems encoded interpretations into knowledge bases, shifting interpretive authority to engineers but suffering from inflexibility. Argumentation frameworks treat interpretations as defeasible arguments grounded in legal canons (e.g., ordinary meaning, purpose) and evaluate them using formal semantics. LLMs can generate interpretive outputs such as paraphrases, ambiguity detection, and canon-based arguments, yet they exhibit high hallucination rates (17–34% on legal queries) and inconsistent reasoning, failing tasks like statutory reasoning and principle extraction. Consequently, the authors recommend LLMs only as brainstorming aids under strict human oversight. Future work should explore hybrid systems that combine LLMs with logical controls to mitigate these issues.

## Method Summary
The paper is a narrative literature review of AI & Law research on legal interpretation. It synthesizes historical accounts of expert systems, theoretical models of argumentation-based interpretation, and recent empirical evaluations of LLMs. No original experiments or reproduction details are provided; the analysis relies on previously reported findings and conceptual discussions.

## Key Results
- Expert systems encoded interpretations into knowledge bases, transferring interpretive power to engineers but lacking adaptability.
- Argumentation frameworks model interpretations as defeasible arguments using legal canons (ordinary meaning, purpose), evaluated via formal semantics.
- LLMs show hallucination (17–34%) and inconsistent reasoning, leading to a recommendation for restricted use as brainstorming aids; hybrid systems with logical controls are suggested for future development.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The surveyed paradigms each reflect a different balance between structure and flexibility. Expert systems provide rigorous, deterministic reasoning by hard-coding legal rules, but their brittleness arises from the impossibility of capturing all nuances in a static knowledge base. Argumentation frameworks introduce defeasibility, allowing conflicting interpretations to be weighed according to established canons; this mirrors the adversarial nature of legal reasoning and supports transparent, revisable conclusions. LLMs leverage massive pretraining to generate plausible interpretations, yet their statistical nature leads to hallucinations because they lack grounding in a formal logical model and often ignore legal constraints. The proposed hybrid approach aims to harness LLMs' language fluency while imposing logical checks (e.g., canon enforcement, knowledge base lookup) to ensure outputs remain faithful to legal norms and reduce spurious content.

## Foundational Learning
Three core concepts underpin the discussed AI & Law research, with additional supporting ideas:

1. **Legal Interpretation Canons**
   - *Why needed*: Provide a structured set of principles (e.g., ordinary meaning, purposive approach) that guide reasoning and resolve ambiguities.
   - *Quick check*: Apply a canon (e.g., "ejusdem generis") to a statutory list and verify if the resulting interpretation aligns with established case law examples.

2. **Defeasible Reasoning**
   - *Why needed*: Allows legal arguments to be accepted provisionally while remaining open to rebuttal, reflecting the non-monotonic character of law.
   - *Quick check*: Construct an argument and a counter-argument; confirm that the system can retract the conclusion when the counter-argument is introduced.

3. **Knowledge Base Engineering**
   - *Why needed*: Encodes legal rules and relationships in a machine-readable form, enabling inference and retrieval.
   - *Quick check*: Query a simple KB (e.g., "Is a bicycle a vehicle?") and check if the answer matches the encoded legal definition.

4. **Large Language Models (LLMs)**
   - *Why needed*: Offer flexible natural language generation and understanding without explicit rule coding.
   - *Quick check*: Prompt the model to paraphrase a statutory sentence and evaluate grammatical correctness and preservation of meaning.

5. **Hallucination Mitigation**
   - *Why needed*: Reduces the risk of false or unsupported statements, which is critical in high-stakes legal contexts.
   - *Quick check*: Measure the frequency of factual claims that cannot be verified against a trusted source during model output analysis.

6. **Hybrid AI Architectures**
   - *Why needed*: Combines the strengths of LLMs (fluency, coverage) with symbolic components (precision, verifiability) to achieve reliable legal reasoning.
   - *Quick check*: Implement a pipeline where LLM-generated arguments are filtered by a rule-based module; evaluate the reduction in hallucination rate compared to a standalone LLM.

## Architecture Onboarding
A hybrid system for legal interpretation could adopt the following architecture:

*Component map:*
User query → Interpretation planner → LLM generator → Canon & consistency checker → Knowledge base validator → Final output

*Critical path:*
1. User submits a legal query (e.g., interpret a statute).
2. Planner decomposes the query into sub-tasks (e.g., identify relevant canons, retrieve definitions).
3. LLM generates a draft interpretation considering the sub-tasks.
4. Canon & consistency checker verifies that the draft respects applicable canons (e.g., ordinary meaning, legislative history) and flags contradictions.
5. Knowledge base validator cross-references factual claims and rule citations with an authoritative legal KB.
6. The system revises the draft based on feedback or returns the validated output.

*Design tradeoffs:*
- **Accuracy vs. flexibility**: Tight logical controls improve reliability but may constrain the LLM's creative generation.
- **Complexity vs. maintainability**: More intricate pipelines increase engineering overhead and may obscure failure modes.
- **Latency vs. thoroughness**: Multi-step validation introduces delays, which could affect user experience in time-sensitive settings.

*Failure signatures:*
- LLM produces a statement unsupported by any canon or KB (hallucination that passes filters).
- Canon checker erroneously rejects a valid interpretation due to overly rigid rule encoding.
- Knowledge base lookup fails (e.g., missing rule), causing the system to stall or output incomplete analysis.

*First experiments:*
1. **Canon enforcement test** – Run the hybrid system on a suite of statutory ambiguity cases; compare the proportion of canon-compliant arguments against a baseline LLM.
2. **Hallucination reduction benchmark** – Measure hallucination rates (e.g., via expert annotation) on a legal QA dataset with and without the logical control layer.
3. **Interpretation quality evaluation** – Have legal experts rank the persuasiveness and correctness of outputs from the hybrid system versus pure LLM and human-written benchmarks.
