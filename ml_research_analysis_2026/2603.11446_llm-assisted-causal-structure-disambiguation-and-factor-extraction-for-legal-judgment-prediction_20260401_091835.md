# LLM-Assisted Causal Structure Disambiguation and Factor Extraction for Legal Judgment Prediction
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11446
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11446
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Proposes an LLM-assisted causal inference framework that achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on multiple legal judgment prediction benchmarks, notably 89.31% on CAIL (vs. 87.31% best baseline) and strong low-resource gains (61.17% avg with 1% data).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses two core challenges in Legal Judgment Prediction (LJP): noisy extraction of legal factors from unstructured case text and uncertainty in causal structure learning. The authors propose a novel LLM-assisted framework with three integrated modules. First, a coarse-to-fine hybrid extraction uses statistical sampling (YAKE) and LLM-based semantic reasoning with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to purify legal factor identification. Second, it employs an LLM to probabilistically disambiguate uncertain causal edges from a Partial Ancestral Graph (PAG) produced by the GFCI algorithm, using legal knowledge and judicial constraints. Third, causal strengths estimated from the disambiguated graphs constrain the attention mechanism of a pre-trained language model (ALBERT) for prediction. The approach yields significant accuracy improvements on several Chinese legal datasets, particularly in distinguishing confusing charges and low-resource settings, demonstrating the value of integrating structured causal reasoning with LLM capabilities for LJP.

## Method Summary
The framework consists of three main stages. (1) **Factor Extraction:** Candidate legal elements are first sampled via YAKE and uniform frequency sampling to avoid bias toward generic terms. Each candidate is then processed by an LLM prompted with a structured Chain-of-Thought and augmented by a legal lexicon (RAG) to filter out narrative noise and map terms to standardized legal elements. (2) **Causal Disambiguation:** The GFCI algorithm learns a PAG from the extracted factors, but contains ambiguous edges (`◦ − ◦`) due to Markov equivalence. For each ambiguous edge, an LLM is prompted with node semantics and relevant legal provisions to output a probabilistic prior over possible directions. This prior, combined with judicial logic constraints (e.g., no charge-to-fact edges) and temporal constraints, guides the sampling of plausible directed graphs. (3) **Causal-Aware Prediction:** From the ensemble of sampled graphs, Average Treatment Effects (ATEs) for each factor on each charge are estimated via Propensity Score Matching (PSM) and aggregated using BIC weights. These causal strengths are then used to supervise the attention weights of an ALBERT model via an auxiliary loss, forcing it to focus on causally relevant tokens during final judgment prediction.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on LEVEN, QA, CAIL, LEDGAR, and Overruling datasets.
- On CAIL, reaches 89.31% accuracy, surpassing the best baseline (87.31%) by 2 percentage points.
- Demonstrates substantial gains in low-resource scenarios, with an average accuracy of 61.17% using only 1% of training data, significantly outperforming baselines.
- Ablation studies confirm the contribution of all three core modules (hybrid extraction, LLM-guided disambiguation, attention constraint), with the attention mechanism alone contributing approximately 2-3 percentage points.
- Random-edge control experiments validate that performance improvements stem from LLM-driven semantic logic in edge disambiguation, not merely increased graph density.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hybrid Factor Extraction Purifies Legal Elements
- **Claim:** A coarse-to-fine hybrid extraction combining statistical sampling (YAKE) with LLM semantic reasoning and RAG purifies legal factor extraction by filtering noise while preserving long-tail evidence.
- **Mechanism:** (1) YAKE computes word saliency scores, and uniform sampling in word-frequency space creates a candidate set `Kcand` to avoid high-frequency generic word bias. (2) For each candidate, RAG retrieves semantically similar terms from a legal lexicon (e.g., THUOCL) to provide domain context. (3) An LLM, prompted with a structured Chain-of-Thought (semantic parsing → element-relevance assessment → multi-dimensional POS/entity filtering → validation), maps candidates to standard legal elements and filters out non-constituent noise (e.g., person names, dates, template phrases).
- **Core assumption:** LLMs, when guided by legal-domain RAG context and a constrained CoT inference chain, can reliably distinguish substantive legal elements from narrative noise in unstructured case text.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "coarse-to-fine hybrid extraction combining statistical sampling (YAKE) with LLM semantic reasoning and RAG to purify legal elements."
  - [section 3.1] Details the uniform sampling from `Kcand` (Eq. 2) and the quadruple prompt `Φ = (R,G,C,O)` with its four-step CoT and POS/entity filtering rules (Eq. 4).
- **Break condition:** If the LLM's semantic parsing or element-mapping fails due to ambiguous legal terminology or insufficient RAG coverage, noise (e.g., template phrases) remains in `Krefine`, propagating errors into causal graph construction.

### Mechanism 2: LLM-Guided Disambiguation Resolves Causal Uncertainty
- **Claim:** LLM-assisted probabilistic disambiguation mitigates structural uncertainty from Markov equivalence in GFCI's PAG output by assigning directional priors to ambiguous edges, producing legally plausible candidate graphs.
- **Mechanism:** (1) GFCI generates a PAG with partially directed edges `u◦ − ◦v` representing Markov equivalence. (2) For each ambiguous edge, an LLM, prompted with node semantics and relevant legal provisions/cases, outputs a probabilistic prior over `{u→v, v→u, u↔v}`. (3) This prior (with a small `ϵ` for exploration) guides ternary sampling of edge direction. (4) Judicial logic constraints (e.g., no `Y→X` where Y is charge) and temporal constraints (cause precedes effect) prune invalid edges.
- **Core assumption:** An LLM, when provided with case-specific legal context, can act as a "soft legal knowledge base" to estimate the relative plausibility of causal directions, effectively breaking Markov equivalence where statistical tests are underpowered.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "LLM-guided probabilistic disambiguation of ambiguous causal edges from GFCI output, using legal knowledge and constraints."
  - [section 3.2] Defines `Eamb` (Eq. 3), the structured prompt `Φ(u,v,K)` with role anchoring and knowledge injection, and the sampling strategy for `◦ − ◦` edges using `P_llm[e]` (Alg. 1, lines 6-8, 19-22).
- **Break condition:** If the LLM's causal reasoning is inconsistent with judicial logic (e.g., suggesting `charge→fact` edges) or if legal context in prompts is insufficient, the sampled graphs may retain legally invalid structures despite constraint filtering.

### Mechanism 3: Causal-Aware Attention Enhances Predictive Focus
- **Claim:** Causal-aware prediction via attention constrained by generated causal graphs integrates structural causal knowledge into PLMs, enhancing discriminative power, especially for confusing charges.
- **Mechanism:** (1) Multiple causal graphs `G_q` are sampled from the disambiguated PAG and weighted by BIC fit. (2) For each factor node `T_j` and charge node `Y_i`, the overall causal strength `ψ_{T_j,Y_i}` is computed as the BIC-weighted sum of ATEs (via PSM) across graphs (Eqs. 7-9). (3) During ALBERT encoding, the normalized causal strength `g_i` for tokens belonging to legal factors is used as a target for attention weights `a_i` via an auxiliary loss `L_causal` (Eqs. 10-13), aligning model focus with exogenous causal estimates.
- **Core assumption:** Attention weights in a PLM can be effectively aligned with exogenously estimated causal strengths from perturbed graph ensembles, forcing the model to focus on causally relevant tokens for judgment prediction.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "causal-aware prediction via attention constrained by generated graphs."
  - [section 3.5] Explicitly defines the attention mechanism (`a_i` in Eq. 10), the sum `v_d` (Eq. 11), and the auxiliary loss `L_causal` (Eqs. 12-13).
  - [section 4.3] Ablation shows removing this constraint ("without the attention mechanism") drops accuracy significantly (e.g., -3.76 pp in EE&MPF), supporting its contribution.
- **Break condition:** If the estimated causal strengths `ψ` are noisy (e.g., from poor graph sampling or PSM bias), constraining attention to them may misguide the PLM, harming performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Structural Causal Models (SCMs) and do-calculus**
  - **Why needed here:** The framework frames judgment prediction as a causal inference problem where legal factors cause charges. Understanding SCMs (nodes as variables, edges as causal mechanisms) and intervention (`do(T=1)`) is essential to grasp why they move beyond correlation and use PSM for ATE estimation.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between observing `X` and `Y` and intervening on `X` when estimating the effect of a legal factor on a charge?
- **Concept: Markov Equivalence and Partial Ancestral Graphs (PAGs)**
  - **Why needed here:** GFCI's PAG output contains indistinguishable edge directions (`◦ − ◦`) under observational data alone, representing Markov equivalence. Understanding why conditional independences constrain but do not fully orient graphs explains the need for LLM disambiguation.
  - **Quick check question:** Why do two different causal graphs (e.g., `A→B` vs `B→A`) produce the same conditional independence test results on observational data?
- **Concept: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**
  - **Why needed here:** Factor extraction relies on RAG to ground LLM reasoning in a legal lexicon and a structured CoT to enforce logical filtering steps, reducing hallucination when identifying legal elements from noisy text.
  - **Quick check question:** How does injecting external legal definitions via RAG and enforcing a multi-step CoT help reduce an LLM's hallucination when identifying legal elements?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw case text `D` -> Factor Extraction Module (YAKE sampling -> LLM+RAG+CoT -> `Krefine`) -> Causal Discovery Module (GFCI on `Krefine` -> `PAG`) -> Disambiguation & Sampling Module (LLM priors on `PAG` -> constraint filtering -> sample `{G_1,...,G_Q}`) -> Strength Estimation Module (PSM on each `G_q` -> ATE -> BIC-weighted aggregate -> `ψ_{T_j,Y_i}`) -> Prediction Module (ALBERT encoding -> attention constrained by `g_i` (from `ψ`) -> judgment prediction)
- **Critical path:** Data flows linearly through all modules. Purity of extracted factors (`Krefine`) is foundational; errors cascade. Disambiguation quality directly impacts graph ensemble validity. Strength estimation can average some noise via multi-graph weighting. The attention constraint finalizes integration into the PLM.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - **LLM Cost vs. Accuracy:** Using LLMs for extraction and disambiguation is computationally expensive but improves factor purity and resolves PAG uncertainty.
  - **Graph Sparsity vs. Coverage:** Aggressive constraint filtering may remove valid edges (false negatives), while lenient sampling introduces noise; `ϵ` calibration and BIC-weighting balance this.
  - **Attention Alignment vs. Model Flexibility:** Forcing attention via `L_causal` may over-constrain the PLM if `ψ` estimates are poor; the loss weight `λ` must be tuned.
- **Failure signatures:** 
  1. **Factor extraction failure:** `Krefine` still contains high noise (e.g., person names). Symptom: Ablation removing LLM refinement drops accuracy (observed ~2 pp loss).
  2. **High PAG uncertainty:** Many `◦ − ◦` edges remain after disambiguation. Symptom: Random-edge control experiment shows no gain over random sampling, indicating LLM priors failed to provide meaningful disambiguation.
  3. **Attention misalignment:** `L_causal` loss fails to converge or harms cross-entropy. Symptom: Ablating the attention constraint drops performance significantly (e.g., -2.88 pp on F&E dataset).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Main results (RQ1):** Compare full model against 12 baselines (BiLSTM, BERT variants, Legal-RoBERTa, CASAM, etc.) on 5 datasets at varying training proportions. Expected: SOTA or near-SOTA accuracy, especially in low-resource (1% data: 61.17% avg).
  2. **Ablation studies:** Remove (a) LLM in factor extraction, (b) knowledge augmentation in disambiguation prompt, (c) attention constraint. Expected: Each removal causes a noticeable accuracy drop (~1-3 pp), confirming modules' contributions.
  3. **Random-edge control (RQ4):** Replace LLM-guided edge selection with random edge addition while matching edge count. Expected: LLM-edge strategy significantly outperforms random (Figure 2), proving gains come from semantic logic, not graph density.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can the correctness of LLM-disambiguated causal edges be evaluated without extensive expert annotation, and what metrics best capture legal plausibility?
- Do the proposed causal assumptions (e.g., legal factors as exogenous causes of charges) and temporal constraints generalize beyond Chinese civil law to other legal systems (e.g., common-law precedent reasoning)?
- What is the sensitivity of the attention mechanism to noise or bias in the estimated causal strengths (ψ), and how can the framework be made robust to imperfect graph ensembles?
- Can the hybrid factor extraction and disambiguation steps be optimized for scalability to large legal corpora without prohibitive LLM inference costs?

## Limitations
- **LLM Output Validation:** Extraction and disambiguation rely on LLM reasoning; no human expert evaluation of factor purity or causal direction correctness.
- **Dataset Scope:** All evaluations on Chinese civil law datasets; generalizability to common-law or other legal systems is unknown.
- **Scalability:** LLM calls for candidate sampling and edge disambiguation may be computationally prohibitive for large-scale deployment.

## Confidence
- **Hybrid extraction (coarse-to-fine with YAKE + LLM+RAG):** Medium. Ablations confirm contribution, but no gold-standard legal factor annotations for direct validation.
- **LLM-guided disambiguation (PAG edge orientation):** Medium. Random-edge controls rule out density effects, but absence of expert-annotated causal graphs limits certainty.
- **Causal-aware attention (constraining PLM with ψ):** High. Consistently large ablation losses (~3 pp) across datasets strongly support its role, though sensitivity to noisy ψ estimates remains unassessed.

## Next Checks
1. **Expert Annotation Study:** Engage legal professionals to label extracted factors and causal directions on a held-out subset; compute inter-annotator agreement with LLM outputs.
2. **Cross-Jurisdiction Test:** Replicate experiments on a Western common-law dataset (e.g., US federal court opinions) to evaluate transferability of factor definitions and causal assumptions.
3. **Robustness Perturbation:** Systematically inject noise into estimated causal strengths (ψ) and measure degradation in prediction accuracy to quantify the attention mechanism’s sensitivity.
