# Adaptive Protein Tokenization
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06418
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06418
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: APT matches or exceeds local tokenizers on reconstruction (RMSD 0.90 Å, TMscore 0.94) and sets new generative benchmarks (designability 0.871, scRMSD 1.35 Å), with applications in zero-shot protein shrinking and affinity maturation.

## Executive Summary
APT introduces Adaptive Protein Tokenization (APT), a global tokenization scheme for protein structures that overcomes limitations of local neighborhood tokenizers. By using nested dropout within a diffusion autoencoder, APT learns a coarse-to-fine hierarchy of tokens where any prefix yields a valid global representation. This design mitigates error accumulation during generation, scales efficiently to large proteins by compressing simple folds, and enables inference-time control over information content (e.g., entropy-based token dropping, beam search). Empirical results on CAMEO and CATH demonstrate APT’s effectiveness: it matches or surpasses local tokenizers in reconstruction (RMSD 0.90 Å, TMscore 0.94), achieves state-of-the-art generative performance (designability 0.871, scRMSD 1.35 Å), and even with 16 tokens yields superior CATH classification via probing. The work opens new avenues for controllable protein design.

## Method Summary
APT comprises three stages. First, a transformer encoder maps Cα coordinates to a continuous latent sequence `c`. Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) discretizes `c` into a token sequence `ˆc` using a fixed codebook defined by scalar quantization levels, avoiding codebook collapse. Second, a diffusion transformer decoder, conditioned on `ˆc` and a predicted protein size (from a linear head on early `c`), reconstructs the coordinates through a flow-matching objective. During training, nested dropout randomly truncates the token sequence at a random cutoff, forcing the encoder to place essential global information in early tokens. Third, a separate GPT-style autoregressive model is trained on the discrete token distributions to generate new `ˆc` sequences. At inference, after sampling tokens autoregressively, one may drop high-entropy tail tokens to improve designability and refine generations via classifier annealing, which modulates the diffusion sampling with a learned reward model.

## Key Results
- **Reconstruction**: APT achieves RMSD 0.90 Å and TMscore 0.94 on CAMEO, on par with local tokenizers; using only 128 tokens it maintains 1.79 Å RMSD, demonstrating effective compression.
- **Generative performance**: With entropy sampling and classifier annealing, APT reaches designability 0.871 and scRMSD 1.35 Å, setting new benchmarks in protein design.
- **Probing efficiency**: MLP probes on the first 16 APT tokens outperform mean-pooled DPLM2/ESM3 embeddings on CATH top-1 classification, indicating that even a small number of tokens capture rich semantic information.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
APT’s advantages arise from three interconnected mechanisms. First, global tokenization mitigates error accumulation: because tokens form a coarse-to-fine hierarchy and any prefix is a valid global representation, dropping high-entropy tail tokens removes likely error-prone details without collapsing the fold. This is supported by the designability jump from 0.48 (fixed 16 tokens) to over 80% with classifier annealing (Section 4.2). Second, the method scales sublinearly with protein size; simple repetitive structures like tropomyosin are encoded in far fewer tokens than residues, and reconstruction remains accurate with truncated token budgets (e.g., 128 tokens yield 1.79 Å RMSD vs. full 0.90 Å), reducing computational burden. Third, nested dropout enforces a hierarchical latent layout, enabling inference-time adaptability: entropy cutoffs trade local detail for designability, and beam search over token prefixes allows alignment with external rewards for tasks like zero‑shot shrinking and affinity maturation (Section 4.4). Potential break conditions include uniform error distribution across tokens (invalidating tail dropout) or non‑hierarchical semantic content (e.g., critical motifs in late tokens), but empirical evidence supports the assumptions.

- **Protein structure representation**: Inputs are Cα coordinates; knowledge of SE(3) invariance and losses (RMSD, TMscore) is needed. *Quick check*: Why mean‑center and randomly rotate structures during training, and how does this relate to equivariance?
- **Autoregressive decoding with latent conditions**: A GPT‑style model samples tokens that condition the diffusion decoder, bridging discrete planning and continuous synthesis. *Quick check*: What role does size prediction (from the first token) play, and why train it with cross‑entropy?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Transformer encoder (Cα → latent c) → FSQ (c → discrete tokens ˆc) → Diffusion transformer decoder (conditioned on ˆc and predicted size) → reconstructed coordinates. Separate GPT‑style autoregressive model generates ˆc. Size predictor (linear head on early c) → length.

### Critical Path
1. Train diffusion autoencoder with nested dropout: for each protein, sample cutoff `k ~ U(1, min(L, k_max))` and drop tokens beyond `k`.
2. Jointly train size predictor (λ_size ≈ 0.01) to cross‑entropy predict length from first few latent tokens.
3. Freeze the tokenizer (encoder + FSQ) and train an autoregressive transformer on discrete token sequences.
4. Inference: (a) Autoregressively sample `ˆc`; (b) optionally drop tail tokens based on entropy threshold; (c) decode with classifier annealing: `vθ(xt,t) = vθ(xt,t|∅) + (1−t^α)(vθ(xt,t|ˆc)−vθ(xt,t|∅))`.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Requires Float32 training for stable ambient diffusion; mixed‑precision risks instability.
- Variable‑length proteins need sequence packing to avoid noisy gradient updates from padding.
- Encoder and decoder capacities must be balanced; scaling encoder alone harms performance (Fig 7c).
- Absolute positional encodings in the encoder are essential for convergence; RoPE is used elsewhere.
- Larger FSQ codebook (e.g., 4 k tokens) improves reconstruction but increases compute.

### Failure Signatures
- Synthetic data bias causes excessive α‑helix generation (Appendix D, Fig 8).
- Reconstruction RMSD diverges from flow loss after ~50 k steps (Fig 7a), suggesting schedule issues.
- Detached size loss (trained separately) fails completely (Appendix I.1).
- Cross‑attention conditioning underperforms in‑context conditioning.
- Local tokenizers repeat token patterns; APT’s stochastic nested dropout mitigates this.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Reconstruction with varying token counts**: Encode held‑out proteins, decode with full tail vs. budgets (128, 64, 32, 16). Compute RMSD/TMscore. Expected: full tail TMscore >0.94, 128 tokens >0.88 TMscore.
2. **Designability with tail dropout & classifier annealing**: Generate 1000 samples; decode with 16 tokens, apply entropy‑based cutoff, vary annealing α ∈ {∞, 1.0, 0.8, 0.3}. Report designability (scRMSD<2 Å) and collapse. Best designability ~0.871 at α=0.8.
3. **CATH classification via probing**: Extract first `K` tokens (K=16,32,...,128) from APT tokenizer, train Linear‑Swish‑Linear probe on CATH classes. Compare top‑1 accuracy to mean‑pooled DPLM2/ESM3. APT outperforms with only 16 tokens.

## Limitations
- Hierarchical information layout may fail for proteins with dispersed motifs or symmetric domains, limiting utility for local tasks like motif scaffolding.
- Record generative performance heavily depends on entropy sampling and classifier annealing, introducing hyperparameter sensitivity and potential overfitting; computational cost is also high due to Float32 training and packing requirements.
- Evaluation metrics (designability, scRMSD) rely on PyRosetta energy and May‑gen sampling, which carry known biases (e.g., α‑helical overproduction); synthetic data generation may further skew results.

## Confidence
- Reconstruction accuracy (RMSD 0.90 Å, TMscore 0.94): **High**
- Generative benchmarks (designability 0.871, scRMSD 1.35 Å): **Medium**
- Scaling to large proteins (sublinear token compression): **Medium**
- Inference‑time adaptability (zero‑shot shrinking, affinity maturation): **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Per‑token error distribution analysis**: For a diverse protein set, reconstruct with incremental token prefixes and compute local RMSD per token position to directly verify error concentration in later tokens.
2. **Ablation of adaptive mechanisms**: Train APT variants without nested dropout and without classifier annealing; evaluate designability and scRMSD on fixed budgets (e.g., 32 tokens) to quantify each component’s contribution and sensitivity to α.
3. **External dataset replication**: Apply the full APT pipeline to an independent benchmark (e.g., CASP14 targets or antibody‑antigen complexes). Report reconstruction vs. token count, designability with entropy sampling, and CATH probing accuracy to rule out dataset overfitting.
