# Knowledge Localization in Mixture-of-Experts LLMs Using Cross-Lingual Inconsistency

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17102  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17102  
- **Reference count:** 29  
- **Primary result:** XICI identifies sparse expert sets (~20 experts) whose ablation degrades correct answers at rates up to 48.0% (vs. ~3% random), demonstrating effective knowledge localization.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces XICI (Cross-Lingual Inconsistency), a method for localizing knowledge in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models. By contrasting router logits from correct versus incorrect responses to the same factual query in multiple languages, XICI isolates experts crucial for retrieving specific facts. The method applies a Mann-Whitney U test on normalized router weight distributions, supplemented by a magnitude threshold, to select a sparse set of experts. Evaluations on GLM-4.5-Air and Qwen3-30B-A3B yield ablation success rates around 40–48%, far exceeding random baselines (~3%), confirming that the identified experts are causally involved in knowledge retrieval.

## Method Summary
XICI proceeds in three stages: data collection, expert identification, and causal validation. First, for each factual question, the model is queried in multiple languages; responses are automatically rated for correctness, and router logits are captured for every token. Languages are split into correct and incorrect groups. Second, preprocessing excludes surface layers and generalist experts, then subtracts per-language mean activations to obtain Δ values. For each expert in each retained layer, a one-tailed Mann-Whitney U test compares Δ distributions between correct and incorrect languages; experts with significant (p≤0.05) higher medians and exceeding a magnitude threshold τ are selected, capped at 25 per question. Third, the identified experts are deactivated by setting their router logits to the minimum among all experts, and the model is re-evaluated on all language variants. Success is measured by the drop in correct answers (Ablation Success Rate) and any spurious gains, with Rate Difference (RD) indicating targeted disruption.

## Key Results
- On GLM-4.5-Air (≈6000 experts), deactivating ~20 experts per question yields an ablation success rate of 48.0% and RD of 44.1%.
- On Qwen3-30B-A3B (≈6000 experts), the same process achieves 35.5% ASR and 32.0% RD.
- Random expert baselines produce ASR near 3%, demonstrating XICI’s precision.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Cross-lingual inconsistency in factual recall provides a natural contrastive signal to isolate experts important for specific knowledge.
- **Mechanism:** By querying the same factual question in multiple languages, the model produces some correct and some incorrect responses. Routing data (expert weights) from successful vs. failed forward passes are contrasted. Experts consistently more active in successes than failures are candidates for knowledge storage/retrieval.
- **Core assumption:** Knowledge parameterization is largely language-agnostic; inconsistency arises from representational anisotropy affecting routing, not from completely separate knowledge per language.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "Our method contrasts routing for sets of languages where the model correctly recalls information from languages where it fails."  
  - [section 4] Discusses prevalence of inconsistency and its shift to rarer knowledge; frames inconsistency as a tool.  
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Lost in Multilinguality..." and "Multilingual Routing..." confirm cross-lingual routing variance is a known phenomenon in MoEs.
- **Break condition:** If a fact is recalled consistently correct or incorrect across all tested languages, no contrast exists and the method cannot be applied.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A statistical contrast (Mann-Whitney U test) on router weight distributions, combined with a magnitude threshold, robustly identifies a sparse expert set causally involved in knowledge retrieval.
- **Mechanism:** For each expert per layer, the distribution of its normalized router weights (∆) across correct-language runs is compared to the distribution across incorrect-language runs using a one-tailed U-test (p≤0.05). Experts with significantly higher median ∆ in correct runs and exceeding a magnitude threshold (τ) are selected.
- **Core assumption:** The router weight (activation intensity) for a knowledge-relevant expert is systematically elevated during successful recall, and this signal persists after averaging across tokens and controlling for confounds.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 5.4] "For each expert ε in each layer l, we seek to determine whether the distribution of values {...} is higher than {...}."  
  - [section 5.5] "We add the stipulation that the difference in median between the two groups of ∆ values is above a threshold τ."  
  - [section 6.3] Shows ablation success rates (~40%) far exceeding random baselines (~1-3%), validating the statistical identification.
- **Break condition:** If the routing signal for knowledge is extremely distributed and noisy (no expert shows statistically significant difference), or if confounds (language-specific syntax, generalist experts) dominate, the method yields many false positives/negatives.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Causal ablation of the identified expert set disrupts model performance on the specific question, verifying the experts' functional necessity for that knowledge.
- **Mechanism:** Identified experts are "muted" during a forward pass by setting their router logit to the minimum value among all experts (min(z)), which forces the router to exclude them while maintaining natural weight distributions for remaining top-k experts. The model is re-evaluated on the original question across all languages.
- **Core assumption:** Deactivating the experts identified via the contrastive signal will break the model's ability to retrieve that specific fact, indicating those experts are necessary components of the knowledge retrieval pathway.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 6.1] Describes the deactivation method: "replaces the logit for the target expert z(εt) with minεz(ε)."  
  - [section 6.3] Reports "Ablation Success Rate" up to 50.1% (degrading correct answers) and low "Spurious Gain Rate" (~3-5%), showing targeted disruption.  
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Steering MoE LLMs via Expert (De)activation" (Fayyaz et al.) provide precedent for this intervention technique.
- **Break condition:** If knowledge is redundantly encoded across many experts beyond the identified set, or if the identified experts are necessary but not sufficient (missing upstream/downstream components), ablation may show minimal impact.

## Foundational Learning

- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architecture & Routing**: Why needed: XICI operates on router logits/weights from MoE layers. Understanding sparse activation, top-k routing, and the role of experts as modular units is fundamental.  
  Quick check: In a typical MoE layer with E=128 experts and k=8, what happens to the router logits of the 120 non-selected experts during a forward pass? How does the gating function transform logits into used weights?

- **Cross-Lingual Knowledge Inconsistency in LLMs**: Why needed: The entire method hinges on the existence of mixed outcomes (some languages correct, some wrong) for the same fact. Understanding why this occurs (anisotropy, data skew, routing variation) is key.  
  Quick check: Why might an LLM answer "Homo habilis" correctly in English but incorrectly (e.g., "Paleolit") in Polish for the same prehistoric tool question? What does this imply about the knowledge representation?

- **Nonparametric Statistical Hypothesis Testing (Mann-Whitney U)**: Why needed: The core identification step uses a U-test to compare distributions of expert weights between correct/incorrect language groups without assuming normality.  
  Quick check: What does a one-tailed Mann-Whitney U test with p≤0.05 tell you about the ∆(correct) vs. ∆(incorrect) distributions for a single expert? What is the null hypothesis?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Dataset -> Evaluation Engine -> Data Collection -> Preprocessing -> Attribution -> Validation; Baselines: random expert sets and random question shuffling.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Dataset preparation → Ensure questions have sufficient mixed-result languages (inconsistency). If >90% all-correct or all-incorrect, skip.
  2. Routing data collection → Run model on all language variants, save router weights for every token in every sequence. Must disable caching/batching for accurate hooking.
  3. Expert filtering → Apply layer exclusion, blacklist, and language-average subtraction to get Δ values. This step is crucial to avoid surface-level and generalist confounds.
  4. Statistical identification → For each question, partition languages (C_i vs W_i), run MWU test on each expert's Δ values, filter by τ, cap at 25, rank by U-statistic + magnitude.
  5. Causal validation → Ablate identified experts (set logit to min(z)) and re-evaluate model on all language variants. Compute ASR, SGR, RD. Compare against random baselines.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Layer exclusion boundaries: Excluding more layers (e.g., first/last 6 vs 5) reduces surface-level noise but may discard layers involved in semantic knowledge routing.
  - Magnitude threshold τ: Lower τ increases false positives (selects noisy experts), higher τ increases false negatives (misses subtle knowledge experts). Calibrated per model.
  - Per-question expert cap (25): Limits interpretability but controls false discovery rate. Higher cap may yield slightly higher ablation success but includes more irrelevant experts.
  - Contrastive condition: Using cross-lingual vs. monolingual paraphrases. Cross-lingual provides more varied routing (more languages, syntactic divergence) but requires multilingual data. Paraphrases yield fewer identified experts and lower power.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High "All Incorrect" rate (>70% of questions): Model likely lacks the knowledge; no meaningful contrast.
  - Low "Mixed Results" rate (<30% of questions): Insufficient inconsistencies for analysis.
  - Ablation Success Rate ≈ Random baseline (~2-5%): Either (a) identified experts are not truly causal, (b) knowledge is too redundantly distributed, or (c) τ/cap settings are wrong.
  - High Spurious Gain Rate (>10%): Deactivation is causing general model degradation, not specific knowledge loss. Check blacklist effectiveness.
  - All experts identified in first/last layers: Confounds not removed; revisit layer exclusion or language-average subtraction.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Inconsistency Prevalence Check: On a small sample (e.g., 50 questions × 10 languages), measure % of questions showing mixed results (some lang correct, some incorrect). Target: >40% to justify method.
  2. MWU Threshold Calibration: For a fixed set of 20 mixed-result questions, vary τ (0.001, 0.003, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05). Plot number of experts identified per question and expected ablation success rate (from a small pilot ablation). Choose τ where identified expert count stabilizes.
  3. Small-Scale Ablation Validation: For 10 questions with identified experts, perform ablation. Compute per-question ablation success. Benchmark against random expert set (same size) and random question-shuffling. Expect >20% absolute improvement over random baseline to confirm signal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **How can XICI be extended to reasoning models that generate chain-of-thought?**  
   - Basis: The authors explicitly advocate for methods to reduce signal-to-noise ratio in MoE routing interpretation, especially for reasoning-heavy models.  
   - Why unresolved: XICI evaluations were conducted with thinking disabled due to noise from verbose reasoning traces; applying to thinking models requires handling longer sequences and potentially different expert usage.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Apply XICI to models with thinking enabled, compare ablation success rates with and without thinking, and analyze routing distributions across layers to identify reasoning-specific experts.

2. **For facts that exhibit uniform cross-lingual performance (always correct or always incorrect), what alternative contrastive approaches can localize knowledge in MoE models?**  
   - Basis: The limitation section states XICI requires mixed results; uniform cases are excluded from analysis.  
   - Why unresolved: Without performance contrast across languages, the Mann-Whitney U test cannot be applied, leaving many facts unamenable to XICI.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Develop and evaluate methods using other contrasts (e.g., temporal before/after editing, synthetic input perturbations) on uniform facts to identify necessary experts.

3. **Do experts identified by XICI directly store factual knowledge or facilitate retrieval processes? The method shows necessity but not sufficiency.**  
   - Basis: The authors note that activating identified experts does not improve performance and state that XICI does not discern whether experts "store" knowledge or perform sub-functions.  
   - Why unresolved: Causal ablation only proves necessity; experts could be part of a retrieval circuit rather than the storage locus.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Combine XICI with knowledge editing: edit weights of identified experts and observe if specific facts are altered; or probe expert activations to see if they encode factual content.

## Limitations
- Model & dataset access: GLM-4.5-Air, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and the multilingual QA corpora (ECLeKTic, MultiLoKo, Global MMLU prehistory) may not be publicly available.
- Method requires cross-lingual inconsistency; facts consistently recalled correctly/incorrectly across all tested languages are excluded.
- Implementation ambiguities (deactivation strength, blacklisting criteria) can affect reproducibility and performance.

## Confidence
- Cross-lingual inconsistency as a contrastive signal: Medium
- MWU + τ identification of sparse expert set: Medium
- Ablation validation (ASR ~40%, RD >40% vs. ~3% random): Medium

## Next Checks
1. Mixed-Rate Replication: On an independent multilingual fact dataset (e.g., mMLU or new translation set), compute proportion of questions with mixed correctness across languages. A rate >35% would support XICI’s core assumption and broad applicability.
2. Parameter Sensitivity Sweep: For a fixed sample of 50 mixed-result questions, vary the magnitude threshold τ (e.g., 0.001–0.05) and the per-question expert cap (10–50). Plot ablation success rate and identified expert count versus τ; the reported τ should lie near the peak of the ASR curve while maintaining sparsity.
3. Deactivation Specificity Test: Compare the proposed min(z) ablation against (a) zeroing the expert’s output (if feasible) and (b) random expert deactivation of equal size. Measure ASR and spurious gain rate across all conditions; a consistently high ASR with low SGR specifically for the XICI-identified set would confirm that the effect stems from targeted expert removal rather than general disruption.
