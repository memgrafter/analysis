# Stance Labels Fail When They Matter Most: The Projection Problem in Stance Detection

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.24231
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24231
*   **Reference Count:** 9
*   **Primary Result:** Standard three-way stance labels (Favor/Against/Neutral) fail on texts with conflicting attitude dimensions because disagreement stems from different compression choices, not confusion; this causes a conditional crossover where label agreement collapses but dimensional agreement remains high on "hard" texts.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a fundamental flaw in how stance is annotated: compressing multi-dimensional attitudes into a single three-way label causes annotator disagreement not from misperception but from different choices about which dimensions to weight more heavily. This "projection problem" is conditional—it only activates when a text contains conflicting signals across dimensions (e.g., agreeing with the fact of climate change but opposing related policies). A pilot study on SemEval-2016 Climate Change tweets confirms this: on dimension-consistent ("easy") texts, label agreement is modestly higher; on dimension-conflicting ("hard") texts, label agreement collapses to near random while agreement on underlying dimensions soars. The authors argue that standard stance benchmarks are biased because they filter out these high-conflict cases during creation, training models to succeed on the wrong problem. The solution is a two-layer annotation scheme that records both the final label and scores for individual dimensions.

## Method Summary
The pilot test uses SemEval-2016 Task 6 tweets about "Climate Change." Tweets are stratified into "easy" (GPT-4o correctly predicts the gold label) and "hard" (GPT-4o fails) using LLM accuracy as a proxy for dimensional conflict. For each stratum, 30 tweets are sampled. Three annotators then provide a dual-layer annotation per tweet: (1) a standard three-way stance label (Favor/Against/Neutral), and (2) a Likert score (-2 to +2, with N/A) for each of four predefined dimensions (Factual, Severity, Policy, Priority). The primary analysis compares Krippendorff's α agreement: for the nominal labels and for each ordinal dimension, both within and across the two strata.

## Key Results
*   A conditional crossover pattern: on dimension-consistent ("easy") tweets, label agreement (α=0.307) exceeds average dimensional agreement (α=0.082). On dimension-conflicting ("hard") tweets, label agreement collapses (α=0.085) while dimensional agreement rises sharply (average α=0.334, with Policy at α=0.572).
*   The published SemEval-2016 benchmark systemically filtered out ~25% of tweets for failing a 60% majority rule, likely removing precisely the dimension-conflicting texts that trigger the projection problem and thereby over-representing "easy" cases.
*   Standard models trained on such filtered data may achieve high F1 without ever learning to handle the complex, multi-signal texts where stance understanding matters most.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: The Projection as Lossy Compression
The core claim is that disagreement on complex texts stems from different *compression choices*, not misperception. Annotators first perceive a multi-dimensional attitude vector (e.g., positive on Factual, negative on Policy). They then *project* this vector onto a single {Favor, Against, Neutral} label. Different weightings of the dimensions during this final step produce different labels from similar initial perceptions. The break condition is when all addressed dimensions align in valence; then any weighting yields the same label, making projection lossless.

### Mechanism 2: Conditional Activation of the Cost
The cost of projection is not constant; it activates only on dimension-conflicting texts. On consistent texts, label agreement is high because the projection is insensitive to weighting. On conflicting texts, small differences in how an annotator weighs, say, Policy versus Factual lead to different final labels, causing label agreement to plummet. Meanwhile, dimensional agreement remains high because it bypasses the lossy projection step. This structural cause is tied to text properties, not annotator competency.

### Mechanism 3: Benchmark Bias via Filtering
Standard stance benchmarks like SemEval-2016 are systematically biased toward dimension-consistent texts. Their creation process discarded any tweet where annotators couldn't reach a 60% majority on the three-way label. These discarded cases are the precise texts where the projection problem causes maximal disagreement—the dimension-conflicting ones. The surviving dataset over-represents texts where the three-way label scheme happens to work well, creating a false signal of progress.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Krippendorff's α for Agreement Analysis.**
    *   **Why needed here:** The central evidence is a crossover in α values between nominal (label) and ordinal (dimension) scales. Understanding that an ordinal α of 0.334 can be "high agreement" while a nominal α of 0.085 is "near random" for the same annotators and texts is critical.
    *   **Quick check:** Why is comparing α across nominal vs. ordinal scales valid for detecting the projection problem?
*   **Concept: Label vs. Dimensional Annotation Granularity.**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper's entire argument distinguishes the derived three-way label (a lossy projection) from the primary perceptual unit (multi-dimensional scores). Engineers must see that a label is not the ground truth attitude but a summary of it.
    *   **Quick check:** For the tweet "Climate change is real, but carbon tax will destroy the economy," what would dimensional annotations look like, and why would annotators agree on those but not on a single label?
*   **Concept: Stratified Evaluation Design.**
    *   **Why needed here:** Diagnosing the projection problem requires splitting data by a proxy for dimensional conflict (e.g., LLM error, label entropy) and reporting metrics separately. Only then does the crossover pattern emerge.
    *   **Quick check:** If you only report overall Macro-F1 on a standard stance benchmark, what specific model failure mode would you *not* detect?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
Dual-Layer Annotation Interface (collects 3-way label + N-dim scores) -> Stratification Module (splits data by hardness proxy like LLM error or annotator entropy) -> Multi-Metric Reporting Pipeline (computes α for labels/dimensions, Macro-F1 per stratum, per-dimension regression metrics)

**Critical Path:** 1) Collect new data with dual-layer scheme. 2) Compute baseline label/dimension agreement. 3) Stratify data by conflict proxy. 4) Train/evaluate model. 5) Report all metrics separately for each stratum to observe crossover.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   *Annotation cost vs. granularity:* Four dimensions × 5-point scale + label increases cognitive load. Mitigation: iterative bottom-up dimension discovery to identify only necessary, high-agreement dimensions.
*   *Backward compatibility:* Must support existing 3-way labels. Solution: store both; use majority-vote label for legacy tasks, dimension scores for new analysis.
*   *Stratification proxy reliability:* LLM accuracy is a heuristic; errors may correlate with sarcasm, not just conflict. Mitigation: validate stratum with human audit or use multi-annotator label entropy as a more direct proxy.

**Failure Signatures:**
1.  No crossover observed: label α and dimension α move together across strata, suggesting dimensions are poorly defined or the projection problem is inactive.
2.  High label α on hard texts: indicates annotator training overfitted to a single projection, suppressing minority weighting choices.
3.  Model shows high overall F1 but catastrophically low (F1 ~0) on the "hard" stratified subset. This is the primary signature of a model trained on projection-biased data.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  **Replicate Pilot on New Target:** Apply the exact dual-layer annotation (3 annotators, 4 dimensions) to a different SEM16 target (e.g., "Hillary Clinton") to test generalizability of the crossover pattern.
2.  **Implement Dimension-Level Evaluation:** For a standard stance model (e.g., BERT fine-tuned on SEM16), compute predictions for each dimension (via prompting or multi-task head) and measure correlation with human dimension averages. Does it capture some dimensions (Policy) better than others (Factual)?
3.  **Conflict-Aware Model Training:** Fine-tune a model with a loss that also enforces consistency with dimensional signals (e.g., multi-task auxiliary heads for dimension regression). Evaluate if this improves hard-stratum F1 without hurting easy-stratum performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** What empirically grounded dimensions capture stance attitudes across targets?
    *   **Basis:** The top-down four dimensions showed low agreement (e.g., Factual α=0.082), implying they may not be universal or well-defined.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No validated, cross-target set of dimensions exists; current approach is inconsistent.
    *   **Resolution evidence:** A large-scale study using free/open coding followed by factor analysis to identify high-agreement, reusable dimensions across many topics.
*   **Question:** Is the two-layer annotation scheme scalable to all SemEval-2016 targets with high reliability?
    *   **Basis:** The paper's proposal extends the pilot to all targets but provides no evidence of feasibility.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Piloting was on a single target; cross-target agreement and cognitive burden are unknown.
    *   **Resolution evidence:** Annotate all original SEM16 targets with multiple annotators using the two-layer scheme, reporting Krippendorff’s α for both layers per target.
*   **Question:** Does multi-dimensional training improve stance detection on dimension-conflicting texts?
    *   **Basis:** The paper infers that current model gains might be "projection mimicry" and suggests dimensional training as a solution.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No experiments directly compare training on 3-way labels vs. dimensional targets, especially on conflict-stratified test sets.
    *   **Resolution evidence:** Train matched models on standard labels vs. multi-dimensional targets, then evaluate their performance separately on "easy" and "hard" (conflict-stratified) test sets.

## Limitations
*   **Stratification Validity:** The easy/hard split via LLM (GPT-4o) accuracy assumes LLM errors primarily reflect dimensional conflict, but they may also stem from sarcasm, implicit stance, or dataset artifacts.
*   **Reproducibility of Pilot:** The exact 60-tweet sample IDs are not released; a new random sample may yield different α values and a weaker crossover pattern due to small sample size (n=30/stratum).
*   **Guideline Ambiguity:** The precise annotator instructions for the four dimensions are not provided, and reported low agreement on some dimensions suggests operationalizations may be unstable across replications.

## Confidence
*   **Projection Mechanism (Conceptual):** High. The two-step Perception→Projection model is logically sound, supported by the illustrative example, and aligns with literature on multi-dimensional attitudes.
*   **Conditional Crossover Pattern (Empirical):** Medium. The reported α values are compelling but based on a single, non-released sample. Replication with a new sample is needed to confirm magnitude and existence.
*   **Benchmark Bias Hypothesis:** Low-Medium. The claim that filtering removed dimension-conflicting texts is plausible and consistent with the data, but it is an inference not directly tested on the discarded tweets.

## Next Checks
1.  **Replicate Crossover on New Target:** Perform the exact dual-layer annotation (3-way + 4-dim) on a *different* SemEval-2016 target (e.g., "Hillary Clinton") using the same LLM-based stratification to test generalizability of the crossover pattern.
2.  **Validate Stratification Proxy:** Compare the LLM-accuracy split against a more direct proxy: **multi-annotator label entropy** from the original SemEval-2016 annotations (where available). Alignment between high entropy and the "hard" stratum would strengthen the link between label disagreement and dimensional conflict.
3.  **Test Model Failure Mode:** Fine-tune a standard stance model (e.g., BERT) on the standard SemEval-2016 training data. Evaluate its Macro-F1 separately on the "easy" and "hard" stratified subsets (from Check 1). A significant drop (e.g., >20 points) on "hard" texts would confirm that models fail precisely on the identified critical cases.
