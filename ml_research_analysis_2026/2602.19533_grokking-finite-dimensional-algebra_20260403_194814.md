# Grokking Finite-Dimensional Algebra

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19533  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19533  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Grokking dynamics in learning finite-dimensional algebra multiplication are strongly influenced by algebraic properties (associativity, commutativity, unitality), tensor rank, and sparsity, with generalization coinciding with the emergence of algebra-consistent latent representations.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the grokking phenomenon—sudden transitions from memorization to generalization—in the setting of learning multiplication in finite-dimensional algebras (FDAs) over finite fields. The authors introduce a tensorial framework where the algebra’s multiplication is encoded by a structure tensor C. Learning the operation reduces to recovering C from training examples. By systematically varying algebraic properties (associativity, commutativity, unitality) and tensor characteristics (rank, sparsity), they train neural networks on FDA tasks and analyze grokking delays. The results show that non-unital associative algebras grok fastest, that higher tensor rank and sparsity increase delays, and that a sharp rise in representation quality (measured by a cosine similarity probe) coincides with the onset of generalization. The work unifies prior group-grokking studies and extends the understanding of how mathematical structure shapes learning dynamics.

## Method Summary
The authors frame FDA multiplication as a supervised learning problem. For a finite-dimensional algebra A over a finite field 𝔽=ℤ/pℤ, the product of two basis-encoded elements u and v is given by the structure tensor C via u·v = C×₁u×₂v. The full dataset (all pairs of algebra elements) is split into training and test sets using a fraction r. Neural networks learn to predict the product index from the two input element embeddings. The model architecture typically comprises an embedding layer, a multi-layer perceptron (or LSTM/Transformer) processing concatenated embeddings, and a linear classifier. Training uses AdamW with weight decay, monitoring training and test accuracy. Grokking is quantified by the delay between when training accuracy first reaches 99% and when test accuracy does. To probe internal representations, the authors extract features from a penultimate layer and measure whether a linear combination of operand features can predict the product feature (cosine similarity Arep). The experiments systematically vary the algebra’s structural properties and tensor characteristics, revealing correlations with grokking dynamics.

## Key Results
- Algebraic properties strongly shape grokking: non-unital associative algebras (ac̄u) show the shortest delays, followed by non-unital non-commutative ones (āc̄u); unital algebras (any with u) grok more slowly.
- Grokking delay increases with the rank of the structure tensor’s mode‑3 unfolding and with sparsity (under a canonical basis).
- Generalization coincides with a sharp spike in representation quality (Arep), especially in low‑data regimes, supporting the hypothesis that grokking reflects the emergence of algebra‑consistent latent embeddings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Unitality imposes a strict constraint on representation space (∑λᵢRᵢ = Iₘ), reducing feasible solutions and making optimization harder, so non‑unital associative algebras grok fastest.  
**Core assumption**: Networks learn latent embeddings satisfying RᵢRⱼ = ∑ₖCᵢⱼₖRₖ; grokking occurs when these embeddings align.  
**Evidence**: Experiments on 2‑dimensional algebras over F₇ show ac̄u shortest t₄, ac̄u next, and unital categories slower (Section 5.3).  
**Break condition**: Over ℝ, structured algebras form a measure‑zero set; random sampling yields degenerate algebras, so property‑dependent delays may not appear without explicit regularization or initialization.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Higher rank of the mode‑3 unfolding and greater sparsity of C increase sample complexity and grokking delay.  
**Core assumption**: Learning FDA multiplication is equivalent to recovering C from data; tensor rank governs sample complexity as in matrix factorization.  
**Evidence**: Figure 6 shows test loss and t₄ rise with rank and sparsity; the trend holds across different mode unfoldings (Figure 12).  
**Break condition**: Sparsity is basis‑dependent; changing the basis can alter sparsity patterns, so the effect is tied to the coordinate system used for encoding data.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: A sharp increase in Arep (cosine similarity) coincides with test accuracy reaching near‑perfect levels, indicating that grokking involves the formation of algebra‑consistent latent features, particularly when training data is scarce.  
**Core assumption**: Over finite fields, discrete embeddings must be explicitly learned, creating a phase transition in representation quality that mirrors the test accuracy jump.  
**Evidence**: Figure 1 shows Arep remaining low then spiking as test accuracy approaches 1; Figure 2 shows Atest increasing with Arep across layers for small r.  
**Break condition**: Over ℝ, grokking may occur without clear representation alignment (e.g., “grokking without understanding”) due to parameter norm changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Structure tensor C defines multiplication → Embedding matrix E maps algebra elements to vectors → Encoder ϕ (MLP/LSTM/Transformer) processes concatenated embeddings → Classifier φ (linear) outputs logits → predictions.  
Probe module (linear regression) extracts representation quality Arep from penultimate‑layer features.

**Critical path**:  
1. Instantiate algebra by sampling a structure tensor C with desired properties (associativity, commutativity, unitality) via Proposition 2.2 checks.  
2. Generate full dataset D = {(u,v, C×₁u×₂v) | u,v ∈ A} and split into D_train, D_test using fraction r.  
3. Initialize model: E random, ϕ (e.g., 3‑layer MLP with ReLU, hidden size m²), φ linear (output size q).  
4. Train with AdamW (lr=0.01, weight decay=0.1, batch size 512) for up to 100 k steps, monitoring L_train, A_train, L_test, A_test every 100 steps.  
5. Periodically compute Arep using PCA on frozen penultimate‑layer features (Section D.2.2).  
6. Identify grokking steps: t₂ (A_train≈99%), t₄ (A_test≈99%); analyze correlation with Arep.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Encoder choice: MLP is simpler/faster; Transformer may better capture symmetries but is slower.  
- Embedding dimension d: Larger d improves capacity but may slow convergence; paper sets d = m²/2 for MLP.  
- Finite‑field implementation: Data are integer‑coded; model operates in ℝ but cross‑entropy forces discrete embeddings implicitly.  
- Regularization: Weight decay (0.1) encourages grokking; too high may prevent convergence, too low may cause overfitting.

**Failure signatures**:  
- No grokking: A_test plateaus <50% while A_train reaches 100%; Arep stays low (<0.5).  
- Excessive delay: t₄ ≫ t₂; likely due to high tensor rank or sparsity.  
- Unstable Arep: Representation quality fluctuates without upward trend; model may not converge to algebra‑consistent features.  
- Basis sensitivity: Sparsity effects disappear if a non‑canonical basis is used.

**First experiments**:  
1. Replicate complex numbers grokking: Use C from Example 2.1 with 𝔽₇, r=0.5, 3‑layer MLP; verify training loss dips then test loss drops sharply around t_gen.  
2. Vary training fraction r: Test r∈{0.2,0.5,0.8} on same algebra; confirm lower r increases t₄ and amplifies t₄−t₂ (Figure 5).  
3. Compare algebra categories: Sample 10 tensors each for ac̄u, āc̄u, and unital (acu) with n=2, p=7; run with r=0.3. Check that ac̄u achieves higher A_test and lower t₄ than others (Figure 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Establish theoretical bounds linking tensor rank and coherence to sample complexity for FDA learning over finite fields.
2. How do grokking dynamics evolve for algebras beyond dimension 2, and does the observed property hierarchy persist?
3. Do real‑world datasets with latent algebraic structure (e.g., molecular interactions, semantic composition) display grokking via formation of algebra‑like representations?
4. What is the minimal faithful representation dimension for a given FDA, and how does network width relate to this minimal m in affecting grokking speed?

## Limitations
- Scalability: Experiments only covered n=2, p=7; larger algebras remain untested.
- Probing robustness: Arep depends on choices of dummy element, layer, PCA size, and regression details; no ablations provided.
- Architectural narrowness: Results are based on a specific MLP configuration; unknown whether trends hold for Transformers or other optimizers.
- Causality gap: Sharp Arep rise correlates with grokking but does not establish that representation alignment causes generalization.
- Real‑number case: Theoretical arguments suggest property effects may vanish over ℝ, but no empirical validation is provided.

## Confidence
- Algebraic‑property effects: High (for small‑algebra regime)  
- Tensor rank/sparsity influence: Medium (observed trend but limited range/basis dependence)  
- Representation‑quality correlation: Medium (consistent but method‑sensitive)

## Next Checks
1. Extend experiments to larger algebras (e.g., n=3, p=5) using generative sampling of structured tensors and verify property‑dependent grokking delays.
2. Ablate the probing pipeline: vary dummy element (zero, random, fixed non‑zero), test embedding vs. hidden layers, and increase PCA components; reassess Arep‑accuracy correlation.
3. Conduct experiments over ℝ with the same architectures and hyperparameters, adding explicit algebraic regularizers if needed, to test whether algebraic‑property delays vanish as predicted.
