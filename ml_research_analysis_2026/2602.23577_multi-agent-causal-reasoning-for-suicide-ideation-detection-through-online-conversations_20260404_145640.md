# Multi-Agent Causal Reasoning for Suicide Ideation Detection Through Online Conversations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.23577
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23577
- **Reference count**: 33
- **Primary result**: MACR achieves Weighted-F1 of 0.3768 on PFA (8.9% over best baseline) and 0.5108 on SCT (12.3% gain over second-best model).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces MACR, a multi-agent causal reasoning framework to improve suicide ideation detection from online conversations. It addresses narrow interaction capture and hidden confounding biases by using a Reasoning Agent to generate counterfactual user reactions via cognitive appraisal theory, and a Bias-aware Decision-Making Agent that applies front‑door adjustment to mitigate unobserved influences. On two datasets (PFA and SCT), MACR significantly outperforms state‑of‑the‑art baselines, and ablation studies confirm that both agents are essential for performance.

## Method Summary
MACR comprises two core agents. The Reasoning Agent simulates alternative user reactions to the original conversation using four sub‑agents (Psychological Analyst, Critical Thinker, Empiricist, Synthesizer) in a three‑round debate grounded in cognitive appraisal theory. It produces *n* inferences that are embedded and clustered into *K* representative mediators. The Decision‑Making Agent then estimates the causal effect by approximating \(P(Y|do(M))\) through stratified in‑context learning: for each mediator, it retrieves risk‑stratified demonstrations and queries an LLM multiple times, aggregating by majority vote. The final prediction combines the mediator probabilities from the Reasoning Agent with the conditional risk estimates from the Decision‑Making Agent via a front‑door adjustment formula.

## Key Results
- Weighted‑F1 of 0.3768 on the PFA dataset, an 8.9% improvement over the previous best baseline.
- Weighted‑F1 of 0.5108 on the SCT dataset, a 12.3% gain over the second‑best model.
- Ablation: Removing the Reasoning Agent reduces performance by 32.7% (W‑F1), while removing the Decision‑Making Agent reduces by 7.8%, validating both components.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MACR’s efficacy stems from three intertwined mechanisms:

**1. Counterfactual Interaction Scaling via Psychological Reasoning**  
The Reasoning Agent addresses narrow interaction capture by generating diverse counterfactual user reactions to the original post, guided by cognitive appraisal theory. Four specialized sub‑agents engage in a three‑round debate to simulate alternative psychological responses. These are clustered into representative mediators whose probabilities are estimated by cluster size. *Core assumption:* Cognitive appraisal can be operationalized through LLM prompting to yield plausible, diverse reactions that simulate unlogged interactions. *Evidence:* Section 5.1 details the sub‑agent roles and clustering; neighboring work uses psychological frameworks but not multi‑agent counterfactual generation for scaling. *Break condition:* Poor clustering quality or unrealistic counterfactuals degrade the mediator representation.

**2. Hidden Confounding Mitigation via Front‑door Adjustment**  
The Bias‑aware Decision‑Making Agent treats the counterfactually augmented conversation tree as a mediator to perform a front‑door adjustment, mitigating hidden confounders (e.g., user conformity). It implements the causal formula \(P(Y|do(X)) \approx \sum_i [P(m_i^*|X) * P(Y|m_i^*, E_x[X])]\). The term \(P(m_i^*|X)\) comes from the Reasoning Agent’s clustering; \(P(Y|m_i^*, E_x[X])\) is approximated by prompting the LLM with the augmented instance and stratified demonstrations that sample the distribution \(P(x)\). *Core assumption:* The augmented tree is a valid observable mediator that intercepts all causal paths from \(X\) to \(Y\) and satisfies front‑door criteria. *Evidence:* Section 4 defines the structural causal model and derives the adjustment; Section 5.2 details the approximation via stratified demos. *Break condition:* If the mediator fails to block backdoor paths or if stratified demos poorly approximate \(P(x)\), bias persists.

**3. Collaborative Causal Decomposition**  
The two‑agent design aligns computational roles with the decomposed front‑door formula: the Reasoning Agent estimates \(P(M|X)\) and the Decision‑Making Agent estimates \(P(Y|do(M))\). This separation enforces a structured reasoning pipeline, improving robustness over monolithic end‑to‑end models. *Core assumption:* Decomposing the causal estimation into counterfactual generation and causal conditional prediction yields better performance and interpretability. *Evidence:* Section 4.2 motivates the decomposition; Section 6.5’s ablation shows removing either agent hurts performance significantly (32.7% and 7.8% drops). *Break condition:* Synchronization failures or error amplification between agents can nullify benefits.

## Foundational Learning
To understand MACR, engineers should grasp these concepts:

- **Structural Causal Models (SCMs) & Confounding**  
  *Why needed:* The framework targets unobserved confounders (e.g., user conformity) that bias the correlation between conversation features \(X\) and suicide risk \(Y\). Understanding backdoor paths is essential.  
  *Quick check:* Given a causal graph \(A \to B \leftarrow C \to D\), is \(C\) a confounder for the effect of \(A\) on \(D\)? Why or why not?

- **Front‑door Adjustment**  
  *Why needed:* This is the core bias mitigation technique. The engineer must understand the requirements for a valid front‑door mediator to comprehend why the augmented tree is chosen and how the formula debias.  
  *Quick check:* For \(X \to M \to Y\) with unobserved confounder \(U\) affecting both \(X\) and \(Y\), what two conditions must \(M\) satisfy to enable front‑door adjustment?

- **Cognitive Appraisal Theory (Lazarus)**  
  *Why needed:* This psychological theory structures the Reasoning Agent’s analysis into cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions, ensuring counterfactuals reflect how individuals process stressful social stimuli.  
  *Quick check:* In the context of a user reading a comment on their suicide‑related post, what are the three main components of a “cognitive appraisal” according to this theory?

- **Multi‑agent Collaboration & Debate**  
  *Why needed:* The Reasoning Agent’s effectiveness relies on four sub‑agents critically refining the analysis. Understanding this loop is key to diagnosing counterfactual quality and replicating the architecture.  
  *Quick check:* What is the specific role of the “Empiricist” agent in the three‑round debate process, and how does it differ from the “Critical Thinker”?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Original conversation tree \(X\) -> Reasoning Agent (4 sub‑agents -> generate \(n\) inferences -> encode with E5 -> K‑means clustering -> \(K\) representative mediators \(m_i^*\) with probabilities \(|C_i|/n\)) -> Decision‑Making Agent (for each \((x, m_i^*)\): retrieve stratified demos \(D_i\) -> LLM predicts \(Y\) with \(T\) samples -> majority vote) -> aggregate via front‑door formula for final risk \(Y\).

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate diverse counterfactuals (clustering quality is pivotal).  
2. For each cluster, construct valid \((x, m_i^*)\) pairs.  
3. Retrieve contextually relevant, risk‑stratified demonstrations.  
4. Aggregation must respect the estimated \(P(m_i^*|X)\) weights.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Counterfactual diversity vs. plausibility: More inferences (\(n\)) increase coverage but may include implausible reactions; clustering (\(K\)) trades granularity for stability.  
- LLM choice and prompting: Smaller models (Qwen) reduce cost but may limit reasoning depth; prompt design is critical for sub‑agent alignment.  
- Demo sampling strategy: Stratified sampling by cosine similarity is heuristic; truly representing \(P(x)\) requires a large, diverse corpus.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Degenerate counterfactuals: All \(m_i^*\) cluster into one or are nonsensical. *Diagnostic:* Check sub‑agent prompts and LLM outputs.  
- Demo mismatch: \(D_i\) examples are semantically dissimilar to input \(x\). *Diagnostic:* Evaluate retrieval similarity scores.  
- Aggregation error: Final probability dominated by one high‑probability \(m_i^*\) with a poor \(P(Y|do(m_i^*))\) estimate. *Diagnostic:* Inspect cluster size distribution and per‑mediator prediction variance.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablation – Reasoning Agent:** Replace with direct DA(\(X\)) prediction. Expect a large drop (>30%) confirming counterfactuals’ necessity.  
2. **Ablation – Clustering:** Use all \(n\) raw inferences without clustering (\(K=n\)). Tests whether clustering’s summarization loses signal or if diversity is paramount.  
3. **Demo Strategy Test:** Compare stratified demonstrations versus random demonstrations versus zero‑shot. Validates the necessity of the \(E_x[X]\) approximation via in‑context learning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other mental health conditions**: Can MACR be adapted to depression or anxiety, and what modifications are required for condition‑specific causal structures? The current work focuses on suicide ideation; different conditions may involve distinct interaction patterns and confounders.
2. **Finer‑grained risk assessment**: How can the cognitive appraisal process be refined to distinguish between passive and active suicidal ideation or to identify specific protective factors? The current multi‑class taxonomy is broad.
3. **Validity of front‑door assumptions**: Under what real‑world conditions does the counterfactually generated mediator satisfy the front‑door criteria, and how sensitive is the method to violations? The paper assumes the augmented tree blocks all backdoor paths, but this has not been empirically validated.
4. **Demographic fairness**: Does MACR exhibit demographic parity across different user groups (e.g., age, gender, cultural background), and how can potential biases from the LLM‑based agents be mitigated? The paper lacks fairness analysis.

## Limitations
- **Data accessibility**: The PFA and SCT datasets are recent and may not be publicly released, hindering full replication; exact conversation tree construction rules are unspecified.
- **Prompt and mediator validity**: The operationalization of cognitive appraisal theory via LLM prompts is not fully detailed, and the counterfactually augmented mediator’s causal adequacy (i.e., whether it truly blocks confounding) remains unverified by human experts or sensitivity analysis.
- **Demographic fairness**: The paper does not evaluate prediction equity across diverse user subgroups; LLM biases could propagate through the multi‑agent pipeline.

## Confidence
- **High**: The causal decomposition design (two‑agent split of the front‑door formula) is theoretically coherent and strongly supported by ablation studies.
- **Medium**: Reported performance gains are plausible, but uncertainties around dataset availability and prompt engineering lower reproducibility confidence.
- **Low**: Generalizability beyond the two evaluated datasets and the psychological plausibility of the generated mediators, as neither has been externally validated.

## Next Checks
1. **Counterfactual plausibility audit**: Recruit suicide risk assessment professionals to rate a sample of generated mediators for psychological realism and diversity; low ratings would undermine Mechanism 1’s core assumption.
2. **Front‑door assumption test**: On a held‑out subset, verify whether the mediator \(M\) satisfies the front‑door criteria (e.g., via sensitivity analysis or testing conditional independence \(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y | M\)); failure would challenge the bias‑mitigation claim.
3. **Prompt replication stress test**: Re‑run key ablations (w/o Reasoning Agent, w/o Decision‑Making Agent) using exactly reconstructed prompts from the paper’s descriptions; large performance deviations (>5% W‑F1) would indicate critical underspecification.
