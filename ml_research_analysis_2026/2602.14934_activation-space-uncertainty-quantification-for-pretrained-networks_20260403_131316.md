# Activation-Space Uncertainty Quantification for Pretrained Networks
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14934
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14934
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: GAPA enables post-hoc epistemic uncertainty for frozen pretrained networks via single-pass inference, preserving predictions exactly and achieving state-of-the-art calibration and OOD detection with minimal overhead.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the need for reliable uncertainty quantification in large pretrained neural networks without retraining or expensive sampling. GAPA replaces each activation function with a Gaussian process whose posterior mean exactly matches the original activation, thereby maintaining the backbone’s point predictions by construction. Scalable inference is achieved through a sparse variational inducing-point approximation over cached training activations combined with local k-nearest-neighbor conditioning, enabling deterministic variance propagation through the network.

Empirical evaluation across regression, classification, segmentation, and language modeling tasks shows that GAPA matches or outperforms strong baselines (e.g., last-layer Laplace, MC dropout, ensembles) in calibration metrics (NLL, CRPS, CQM) and OOD detection (AUROC, BALD), while incurring only minimal runtime overhead comparable to a MAP forward pass. Notable results include up to 0.8 nat NLL improvement on regression datasets, OOD BALD AUC up to 0.997 on MNIST/FMNIST, and OOD AUROC 0.953 on CIFAR-10 in ~3.3 seconds.

## Method Summary
GAPA is a post-hoc method that transforms frozen neural network activations into Gaussian processes. For each selected nonlinear layer, the deterministic activation ϕ is replaced by a scalar GP with prior mean m(z)=ϕ(z) and a kernel whose hyperparameters are set from empirical statistics of cached training pre-activations (no optimization). The GP is conditioned on noiseless pseudo-observations at the cached training locations, which forces the posterior mean to equal ϕ(z) exactly for any input, preserving the original network's predictions. The posterior variance at a query pre-activation z is computed analytically using the kernel and the training set; to achieve scalability, the full training set is compressed via k-means into M inducing points, and at test time only the K nearest inducing points are retrieved using FAISS, reducing per‑query cost to solving a K×K linear system.

The network is then treated as a deterministic pipeline of Gaussian random variables with mean vectors and diagonal covariance matrices. Propagation rules are derived: linear layers scale variance by the squared weights; elementwise nonlinearities use the delta method (variance multiplied by the squared derivative of ϕ at the mean); when a GAPA layer receives uncertain input from a previous GAPA, a noisy‑input Gaussian process correction (NIGP) adds an extra term λ=(ϕ'(μ))²⊙v_in. For classification, final Gaussian logits are converted to probabilities via the Laplace bridge; for regression, an additional heteroscedastic aleatoric head is trained separately to combine epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty. This design requires only a single training forward pass (to cache activations) and no weight updates or sampling at test time, offering near‑MAP inference speed.

## Key Results
- On Year, Airline, and Taxi regression datasets, GAPA reduces NLL by up to 0.8 nats compared to strong baselines.
- For OOD detection, GAPA achieves the highest BALD AUC (up to 0.997) on MNIST/FMNIST and OOD AUROC 0.953 on CIFAR-10, all in approximately 3.3 seconds per test batch—orders of magnitude faster than MC dropout or ensembles.
- On the LLaMA-3.2-3B language model, GAPA’s epistemic uncertainty on out-of-distribution text (OpenWebText) surpasses temperature‑scaling oracles, demonstrating scalability to large models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Exact Mean Preservation
**Claim:** GAPA preserves the original backbone’s point predictions exactly by construction.  
**Mechanism:** The GP activation uses a prior mean identical to the deterministic activation function ϕ(z). Conditioning on noiseless pseudo‑observations from the training activations forces the posterior mean to equal ϕ(z) for any input z, as the residual term in GP regression vanishes.  
**Core assumption:** ϕ is sufficiently smooth for GP modeling and kernel hyperparameters are fixed from empirical statistics without optimization.  
**Evidence anchors:** [Abstract]; Section 2.2 “Mean preservation.” shows µℓ(z∗ℓ)=ϕ(z∗ℓ) since ˜Yℓ=ϕ(˜Zℓ)=mℓ(˜Zℓ).  
**Break condition:** Discontinuous activations (e.g., ReLU at zero) or mismatched kernel may cause slight numerical deviations, though the theory guarantees exactness under noiseless conditioning.

### Mechanism 2: Scalable Single-Pass Inference
**Claim:** GAPA achieves scalable inference via a sparse variational inducing‑point approximation over cached training activations combined with local k‑nearest-neighbor conditioning.  
**Mechanism:** Offline, training activations are compressed via k‑means into M inducing points. At test time, for each query pre‑activation, only the K nearest inducing points are retrieved using FAISS, and a small K×K linear system is solved to compute epistemic variance, reducing complexity from O(N³) to O(log M + K³).  
**Core assumption:** Local conditioning on K nearest points provides a conservative variance upper‑bound and accurately captures epistemic uncertainty; K is kept small (e.g., 50) for efficiency.  
**Evidence anchors:** [Abstract]; Section 2.3 details k‑means preprocessing, FAISS indexing, and the local GP variance formula.  
**Break condition:** Poor inducing‑point coverage (bad k‑means) or too small K underestimates uncertainty; too large K increases compute cost sublinearly but with diminishing returns.

### Mechanism 3: Deterministic Variance Propagation
**Claim:** Deterministic variance propagation through deep networks enables single‑pass predictive uncertainty without sampling.  
**Mechanism:** GAPA layers output Gaussian summaries (mean and diagonal covariance). Propagation uses linear rules for linear layers, the delta‑method for element‑wise nonlinearities (variance scaled by squared derivative), and a noisy‑input GP correction (NIGP) for stacked GAPA layers to account for input uncertainty.  
**Core assumption:** First‑order Taylor expansion (delta method) suffices; diagonal covariance captures the key uncertainty; NIGP accurately models input‑dependent variance in subsequent layers.  
**Evidence anchors:** [Abstract]; Section 2.4 and Appendix C derive propagation rules, including NIGP for stacking.  
**Break condition:** Linearization errors may accumulate in deep networks; non‑diagonal correlations could be significant in wide layers, though empirical results show the approximation is effective.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Frozen backbone → GAPA layer (replaces activation) → subsequent deterministic layers (linear, conv, etc.) → output head. Offline: training forward pass → pre‑activation cache → k‑means clustering → inducing points → FAISS index. Test‑time: query FAISS for K nearest inducing points → solve K×K GP for variance → (mean, variance) → propagate via delta method/linear rules/NIGP → final prediction (Laplace bridge for classification or aleatoric head for regression).  

**Critical Path**  
1. **Offline:** Run a single forward pass on the full training set to cache pre‑activations at selected layers. Perform k‑means on these caches to obtain M inducing points. Compute per‑neuron kernel hyperparameters (length scale = median pairwise distance, signal variance = std). Build a FAISS index over the inducing points.  
2. **Test‑time (per sample):** Forward through the backbone up to the first GAPA layer. At that layer, compute pre‑activation z and set mean μ = ϕ(z). Use FAISS to retrieve the K nearest inducing points. For each neuron, compute the posterior variance via the RBF kernel and the K×K system. If the input to this layer has uncertainty from a previous GAPA, add the noisy‑input correction λ = (ϕ'(μ))² ⊙ v_in.  
3. **Propagation:** Propagate the Gaussian summary (μ, v) through remaining layers: linear layers scale variance by squared weights; elementwise nonlinearities scale by squared derivative of ϕ at μ; for additional GAPA layers, incorporate input variance via λ. For classification, convert Gaussian logits to probabilities using the Laplace bridge; for regression, combine with a separately trained heteroscedastic aleatoric variance head.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Memory vs. accuracy:* Larger M improves coverage and variance fidelity but increases FAISS index size and RAM consumption.  
- *Compute vs. variance quality:* Larger K yields more accurate (less conservative) variance estimates but increases per‑query cubic solve cost (K³). The paper uses K=50 as a compromise.  
- *Diagonal vs. full covariance:* Diagonal covariance simplifies propagation and scales to wide layers but neglects inter‑neuron correlations that might be important for structured outputs.  
- *Layer placement:* Earlier layers capture input‑space uncertainty and may improve calibration; later layers tend to boost OOD detection. Optimal placement is task‑dependent.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Calibration collapse:* Sudden increase in NLL or ECE indicates variance estimates are too extreme, possibly due to too small M/K or poor kernel hyperparameters.  
- *OOD detection failure:* Low AUROC/BALD suggests inducing points do not cover OOD regions or K is too small to generate variance sensitivity.  
- *Mean shift:* Drop in accuracy implies GP posterior mean deviates from ϕ(z); verify noiseless conditioning and correct kernel settings.  
- *Runtime blow‑up:* Excessive per‑sample time points to overly large M/K or inefficient FAISS configuration; monitor query latency and linear‑solve times.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. *Regression benchmark:* Evaluate on Year, Airline, and Taxi datasets. Compare NLL, CRPS, and CQM against baselines like VaLLA and LLA.  
2. *Classification OOD detection:* Use MNIST vs. FMNIST and CIFAR-10 vs. SVHN. Report accuracy (should match MAP), NLL, ECE, and OOD AUROC/BALD. Benchmark runtime against MC dropout, ensembles, and Laplace.  
3. *Language model scaling:* Attach GAPA to LLaMA-3.2-3B. Measure epistemic uncertainty on in‑distribution (WikiText-103) vs. out‑of‑distribution (OpenWebText). Compare against temperature‑scaling oracles.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Reducing Memory Footprint
**Question:** How can we reduce the memory footprint of GAPA's cached inducing activations?  
**Basis:** The conclusion identifies memory cost as the primary limitation and proposes compressed or hierarchical indexing as future work.  
**Why unresolved:** For very large datasets or many inducing points, memory becomes a bottleneck, hindering application to state-of-the-art models.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and evaluate a compression scheme that significantly reduces memory while preserving uncertainty quality across multiple benchmarks.

### Open Question 2: Structured Covariances
**Question:** Can we extend GAPA beyond diagonal covariances to capture inter‑neuron dependencies tractably?  
**Basis:** The conclusion explicitly mentions extending beyond diagonal covariances to capture structured dependencies while preserving scalability.  
**Why unresolved:** Diagonal assumption may miss useful correlations; modeling full covariance is costly, so efficient approximations are needed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Propose a scalable structured approximation (e.g., low‑rank, block‑diagonal) and demonstrate improved calibration or OOD detection on tasks with structured outputs.

### Open Question 3: Optimal Layer Placement Principle
**Question:** What principle determines the optimal layers for applying GAPA to maximize uncertainty utility?  
**Basis:** Ablations (Appendix M.1) show later layers boost OOD detection while earlier layers preserve calibration, but no general rule is given.  
**Why unresolved:** Optimal placement likely varies with architecture and data; without guidance, users must manually search, which is expensive.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Discover a predictive metric (e.g., feature sensitivity) that correlates with beneficial placement and validate across diverse models.

## Limitations
- Incomplete implementation details (e.g., exact layer placement, inducing‑point counts M, kernel hyperparameter estimation, jitter values) are not fully specified, making exact reproduction challenging.
- The method relies on strong assumptions: mean preservation requires noiseless conditioning and smooth activations; local KNN conditioning may underestimate uncertainty if inducing points poorly cover the space; the delta method may accumulate linearization errors in deep networks.
- Scalability claims depend on undisclosed FAISS configurations and memory usage; for very large models/datasets, memory and runtime overheads could be prohibitive in practice.
