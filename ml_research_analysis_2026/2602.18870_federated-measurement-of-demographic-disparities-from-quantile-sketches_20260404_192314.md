# Federated Measurement of Demographic Disparities from Quantile Sketches

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.18870  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18870  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A one-shot federated audit protocol using quantile sketches accurately estimates global demographic disparity and decomposes it into selection-induced mixture effects and cross-silo heterogeneity, with theoretical guarantees and empirical validation on three datasets.

## Executive Summary
Auditing fairness across federated data silos is challenging because local disparity estimates can be severely biased due to selection effects and silo heterogeneity. This paper introduces a communication-efficient, one-shot protocol where each silo shares only group counts and a quantile sketch of local score distributions. The server then reconstructs a consistent estimator of the global Wasserstein–Fréchet variance (a distributional disparity measure) and, for p=2, an ANOVA-style decomposition that isolates mixture and heterogeneity contributions. Theoretical analysis yields O(1/k) discretization bias and finite-sample concentration rates, while experiments on COMPAS, Adult, and Law datasets show that a few dozen quantiles suffice for accurate recovery; local audits systematically miss the global gap.

## Method Summary
The protocol operates in a single communication round. For each sensitive group s, silo j reports subgroup size n_{j,s} and empirical quantiles of the score Z on a shared grid u_ℓ = (ℓ-0.5)/k, ℓ=1..k. The server normalizes group weights α_s = n_s/n and mixing proportions π_{j|s} = n_{j,s}/n_s. It builds step-CDFs F̂_{j,s,k} from the quantiles, mixes them into the within-group distribution F̂_{×,s,k} = Σ_j π_{j|s} F̂_{j,s,k}, and inverts this mixture to obtain quantiles q̂_{×,s,ℓ}. Global barycenter quantiles q̂^{×*} are computed (weighted average for p=2, median for p=1). The disparity estimator is Ĝ_{k,p} = Σ_s α_s (1/k) Σ_ℓ |q̂_{×,s,ℓ} – q̂^{×*,ℓ}|^p. Heterogeneity Ĥ_{×,k,p} follows by integrating pairwise Cramér distances. For p=2, additional terms V_mix and V_bar use within-group barycenter quantiles q̂_{s,ℓ} = Σ_j π_{j|s} Q̂_{j,s,ℓ}. Consistency is shown: |Ĝ_{k,p} – G_p| = O_p(1/k + √(log k / n_min)) under bounded density assumptions.

## Key Results
- Quantile sketches with as few as 25–100 points recover global demographic disparity with <1% error, while local silo audits often substantially underestimate the true global gap.
- For p=2, the ANOVA decomposition (G_2 = V_mix + V_bar + R) cleanly separates the contributions of selection-induced mixing and cross-silo heterogeneity, aiding diagnosis.
- Theoretical guarantees (O(1/k) discretization bias, finite-sample rates) are empirically validated on COMPAS, Adult, and Law datasets; the required k grows with selection bias strength.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Local–Global Disparity Mismatch**  
The global score distribution for group s is a mixture ν_s = Σ_j π_{j|s} ν_{j,s}. Even if all local ν_{j,s} are identical (local parity), differing π_{j|s} generate disparity (Simpson’s paradox). Conversely, equal π_{j|s} with heterogeneous ν_{j,s} also create disparity. Formally, mismatch occurs when Z (score) depends on A (silo) either directly or through S (sensitive attribute): Z ⊥̸ A | S or Z ⊥̸ S | A. Local audits, which compare ν_{j,s} within each silo, cannot detect this.

**Mechanism 2: One-Shot Quantile Sketch Protocol**  
Each silo transmits only group counts and k-quantile sketches on a shared midpoint grid. The server reconstructs step-CDFs, forms the within-group mixture, inverts to mixture quantiles, and plugs them into a discrete Fréchet variance formula. Key properties: (i) Communication cost is O(|S|k) per silo, independent of local sample size; (ii) The midpoint grid u_ℓ = (ℓ-0.5)/k avoids unstable endpoint quantiles; (iii) Under bounded density assumptions, the discretization bias is O(1/k). The protocol avoids raw data sharing, offering a privacy benefit.

**Mechanism 3: ANOVA Decomposition for p=2**  
When p=2, squared Wasserstein distance is Hilbertian: W₂²(μ,ν) = ‖Q_μ – Q_ν‖²_{L²}. Write q_s^× = mixture quantile, q_s^* = within-group barycenter quantile, q^{×*} = global barycenter. Then  
G_2 = Σ_s α_s ‖q_s^× – q_s^*‖²  + Σ_s α_s ‖q_s^* – q^{×*}‖²  + 2 Σ_s α_s ⟨q_s^× – q_s^*, q_s^* – q^{×*}⟩.  
The first term is V_mix (mixture effect), the second V_bar (heterogeneity), and the third R satisfies |R| ≤ 2√(V_mix V_bar). This exact identity cleanly separates the two mismatch mechanisms.

## Foundational Learning

- **Wasserstein–Fréchet variance**  
  Why needed: Defines population-level disparity as the Fréchet mean (barycenter) of group score distributions in Wasserstein space, generalizing variance to distributional objects and capturing full shape differences beyond moments.  
  Quick check: How does W₂(ν_s, ν^*)² differ from a moment-based variance, and why is it appropriate for score distributions? (It measures distributional discrepancy through optimal transport, sensitive to location, spread, and shape, whereas variance only captures second moments.)

- **Quantile sketches**  
  Why needed: Provide a compact, communication-efficient summary of a distribution; with O(k) numbers one can reconstruct a step-CDF and approximate Wasserstein distances to within O(1/k) error.  
  Quick check: What is the trade-off between a uniform mid-point grid and a dynamic t-digest? How does the grid affect the O(1/k) rate? (Uniform grid yields deterministic points and clean bias analysis; t-digest adapts to density variations but complicates theoretical rates.)

- **ANOVA-style decomposition in Hilbertian geometry**  
  Why needed: For p=2, squared Wasserstein distance forms a Hilbert space structure on quantile functions (L²), enabling a variance decomposition that isolates selection-induced mixture effects from cross-silo heterogeneity.  
  Quick check: Why does the decomposition hinge on treating quantile functions as elements of L²([0,1])? What would change for p=1? (L² gives an inner product and Pythagorean expansion; for p=1 the space is not Hilbertian, so only triangle inequalities hold.)

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:  
Silos compute n_{j,s} and Q̂_{j,s,ℓ} → Server → Outputs Ĝ_{k,p}, Ĥ_{×,k,p} (and for p=2: V̂_mix, V̂_bar, R̂)

Critical path:  
1. Silo-side: local scoring → quantile computation on shared grid → transmission.  
2. Server-side: compute α_s, π_{j|s} → build step-CDFs F̂_{j,s,k} → mix to F̂_{×,s,k} → invert to mixture quantiles q̂_{×,s,ℓ} → compute global barycenter q̂^{×*} → evaluate discrete disparity and heterogeneity.

Design tradeoffs:  
- **Grid size k**: Larger k reduces discretization bias (O(1/k)) but increases communication (O(|S|k)). Empirically k≈25–100 suffices for accurate estimates.  
- **Privacy vs. accuracy**: Sharing quantiles and counts minimizes raw data exposure but does not achieve formal differential privacy; adding noise would increase bias.  
- **Weight estimation**: Small local subgroup counts n_{j,s} lead to noisy π_{j|s} and inflated error; a minimum cell size may be required in practice.

Failure signatures:  
- **Small local samples**: Quantile error dominates; the rate includes √(log k / n_min).  
- **Boundary effects**: Midpoint grid avoids 0/1 extremes; trimming may be needed if densities vanish at boundaries.  
- **Heavy-tailed/discrete scores**: The O(1/k) bias relies on bounded densities; heavy tails or discreteness can slow convergence.  
- **Zero counts**: Must ensure mixing weights sum to 1 even when some n_{j,s}=0 (skip or pad with zero weight).

First experiments:  
1. **Synthetic Beta**: Vary selection bias ρ and grid size k; show convergence of U₂(k) and demonstrate bin-averaging underestimation.  
2. **COMPAS**: Simulate 5 silos under random and selection-biased allocations; show local disparity underestimates global gap; assess Ĝ₂ and diagnostics across k.  
3. **Adult & Law**: Replicate COMPAS analysis; confirm sketch accuracy and reliability (k₉₅ heatmaps) across datasets; observe bias increases required k.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Multi-dimensional scores**  
  Extending the framework to multi-dimensional outputs (e.g., calibrated probabilities for multiple classes) would require different distributional summaries and may lose the simplicity of one-dimensional quantiles. Designing a low-communication sketch with O(1/k) guarantees for multivariate distributions remains open.

- **Heavy-tailed or discrete score distributions**  
  Theoretical guarantees assume regularity (e.g., bounded densities). Understanding behavior under heavy tails or discrete distributions, and identifying necessary modifications for robust performance, is an important direction.

- **Differential privacy augmentation**  
  While the protocol is privacy-motivated by design, quantile sketches and counts do not by themselves provide formal differential privacy. Integrating calibrated noise and analyzing the privacy–accuracy trade‑off is left to future work.

## Limitations

- The O(1/k) discretization rate assumes compact support and densities bounded away from 0 and ∞; real-world scores may be discrete or heavy-tailed, potentially degrading accuracy.
- The ANOVA decomposition for p=2 includes a remainder term R that can be non-negligible, limiting the interpretability of V_mix and V_bar as pure mixture vs. heterogeneity effects.
- The protocol does not achieve differential privacy; adding DP noise would introduce additional bias and requires trade‑off analysis.
- Local scoring functions m_j are taken as given; model misspecification could bias the final disparity estimates.
- Empirical evaluations use simulated federations; actual selection mechanisms may be more complex and less well‑captured by the copula-based simulation.

## Next Checks

- **Robustness to density violations**: Test the method on datasets with discrete scores (e.g., integer risk scales) and heavy-tailed continuous scores (e.g., income); compare empirical error decay with k to the theoretical O(1/k + √(log k / n_min)) rate.
- **Remainder R significance**: Compute the average proportion of G₂ explained by R across varying selection biases on COMPAS, Adult, and Law; if R often exceeds 10% of G₂, the decomposition’s diagnostic separation is limited.
- **Privacy-utility trade-off**: Implement a differential privacy variant by adding calibrated Laplace/Gaussian noise to transmitted quantiles and counts; for fixed k, measure the increase in absolute error |Ĝ₂ – U₂| as privacy budget ε decreases, and find ε needed for ≤1% error relative to non‑private.
