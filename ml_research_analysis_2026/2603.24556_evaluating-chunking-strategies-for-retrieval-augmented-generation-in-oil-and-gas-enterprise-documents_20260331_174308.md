# Evaluating Chunking Strategies For Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Oil and Gas Enterprise Documents

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24556
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24556
- Reference count: 26
- Primary result: Structure-aware chunking achieves highest retrieval effectiveness (MRR 0.644, NDCG@3 0.409) and lowest computational cost (911 chunks, 15MB index, 431ms retrieval), but all strategies fail on P&IDs.

## Executive Summary
This study compares four chunking strategies for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) on proprietary oil and gas documents. Structure-aware chunking, which splits documents at structural boundaries like headings and tables, yields the best retrieval metrics and lowest computational overhead. However, all text-based methods fail on Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs) due to their spatially encoded nature, indicating a fundamental limitation of current RAG systems for diagram-heavy documents. The results suggest that preserving document structure is key for efficient and effective retrieval in domain-specific text, but multimodal approaches are needed for visual documents.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate fixed-size, recursive, breakpoint-based semantic, and structure-aware chunking on a stratified corpus of 60 oil and gas documents (manuals, specifications, P&IDs). Documents are preprocessed using Azure Document Intelligence to extract structured Markdown with tables and headers. Each chunking strategy is applied, and chunks are embedded using OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large model. Separate vector indices are built in Azure Cosmos DB (HNSW) for each strategy. Retrieval performance is measured on 60 human-annotated queries using metrics like MRR and NDCG@3, while computational cost is assessed via chunk count, index size, and retrieval latency.

## Key Results
- Structure-aware chunking achieves the highest top-K retrieval effectiveness: MRR 0.644, NDCG@3 0.409.
- It also minimizes computational cost: only 911 chunks, 15MB index, and 431ms average retrieval time.
- All strategies exhibit near-zero performance on P&IDs (e.g., structure-aware NDCG@3 = 0.066), highlighting a limitation for spatially encoded documents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Structure-aware chunking improves retrieval effectiveness and reduces computational cost by preserving the inherent hierarchical layout (headings, tables) of specialized documents.
- **Mechanism:** By splitting text at structural boundaries (e.g., H1, H2, H3 headers), it creates chunks that are semantically cohesive and contextually complete, avoiding fragmentation that severs related information. This reduces the total number of chunks indexed, shrinking index size and speeding up vector search.
- **Core assumption:** Document structure (e.g., headings in manuals/specs) is a reliable proxy for semantic cohesion in domain-specific texts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "structure-aware chunking... achieves the highest retrieval effectiveness in top-K metrics... and the lowest computational cost"
  - [section 4.1] Structure-aware achieves highest MRR (0.644) and NDCG@3 (0.409).
  - [section 4.2] It produces the fewest chunks (911 vs. 10,685 for semantic) and smallest index (15MB), with fastest retrieval (431ms).
- **Break condition:** If documents lack discernible, consistent structural markup (e.g., poorly formatted scans), the method reverts to fragmented chunks like fixed-size.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Breakpoint-based semantic chunking underperforms in this domain because its embedding-driven breakpoints misalign with the technical and structural logic of O&G documents.
- **Mechanism:** The method uses a generic embedding model to compute sentence-level semantic similarity. In technical documents, crucial context is often distributed across sentences that are *structurally* linked (e.g., a table caption and its data rows) but may not be semantically similar in the embedding space, leading to inappropriate splits.
- **Core assumption:** The pre-trained embedding model captures semantic shifts that correspond to meaningful chunk boundaries for *this specific document type*.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] Semantic chunking scores lowest on high-relevance metrics (MRR 0.557, NDCG@3 0.305).
  - [section 2.2.3] "Breakpoint is inserted when the semantic distance... is higher than a set threshold, signalling a significant topic change."
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper "Is Semantic Chunking Worth the Computational Cost?" questions its general benefit, aligning with domain-specific failure.
- **Break condition:** If domain-specific terminology or layout conventions are poorly represented in the embedding model's training data, the semantic similarity signal becomes noisy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** All text-based chunking strategies fail on P&IDs because these documents encode primary meaning visually and spatially, not in linear text.
- **Mechanism:** RAG systems process extracted text streams. P&IDs contain engineering symbols, spatial relationships, and minimal descriptive text. Chunking strategies, even structure-aware, operate on the sparse, disconnected text extracted from these diagrams, which lacks the relational context needed for meaningful retrieval.
- **Core assumption:** The textual content extracted from P&IDs (via ADI) is sufficient for information retrieval about the diagram's content.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "all strategies perform poorly on visually complex Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs), revealing a fundamental limitation of text-based RAG for spatially encoded documents."
  - [section 4.3] All strategies score extremely low on P&IDs (e.g., structure-aware NDCG@3 = 0.066).
  - [section 2.1] P&IDs "combine both unstructured records and tabular components" and rely on "visual layout and structural hierarchy."
- **Break condition:** If a document's essential information resides in non-textual elements (graphics, spatial arrangement), any text-only chunking and retrieval will be ineffective regardless of strategy.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Document Input -> Preprocessing & Extraction (Azure Document Intelligence) -> Chunking Engine (four strategies) -> Embedding Model (text-embedding-3-large) -> Vector Store (Azure Cosmos DB vCore HNSW) -> Retrieval & Evaluation -> Annotation Pipeline (Label Studio)
- **Critical path:** Document → ADI Extraction → Chunking (per strategy) → Embedding → Indexing → Retrieval → Metric Calculation. Any error in extraction or chunking propagates downstream.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Chunk size vs. context: Smaller chunks increase index size/latency but may reduce noise; larger chunks preserve context but risk including irrelevant info.
  - Structural preservation vs. generality: Structure-aware excels on well-formatted docs but fails on unstructured/visual ones; semantic is generalist but domain-agnostic.
  - Computational cost vs. retrieval quality: Semantic chunking creates 10x more chunks than structure-aware, costing more storage/latency for lower top-K accuracy.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - P&ID query: Abysmal metrics (NDCG < 0.1) across all strategies.
  - Fragmentation indicator: High chunk count (>~4k) and index size (>50MB) relative to corpus (fixed/recursive in Table 2).
  - Semantic misfire: Low MRR/NDCG but potentially decent MAP—suggests relevant chunks exist but are ranked too low.
  - Over-chunking: Excessive overlap or tiny chunks (<100 chars) in the chunk distribution report.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate baseline comparison: Run the exact same 60-doc corpus through all four chunkers, embed with `text-embedding-3-large`, index in Cosmos DB HNSW, and evaluate on the provided Qrels to confirm structure-aware's top-K advantage and computational efficiency.
  2. Isolate P&ID performance: Filter the Qrels to only P&ID-originating queries, re-run retrieval, and calculate metrics per strategy. Expect all scores to drop >90%, confirming the visual/spatial limitation.
  3. Vary structure-aware granularity: Modify the header levels (e.g., split on H1 vs H1+H2) and observe the trade-off between chunk count and NDCG@3. Hypothesis: too coarse (H1-only) may miss nuances; too fine (H3) increases count with diminishing returns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can multimodal RAG architectures effectively integrate visual and spatial information from P&IDs with textual structural cues to overcome the fundamental retrieval limitations identified for spatially-encoded documents?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states that "future work must integrate multimodal models to overcome current limitations" stemming from poor performance on P&IDs.
- **Why unresolved:** The study definitively shows all text-based strategies fail on P&IDs, but provides no pathway or empirical evaluation of multimodal solutions, leaving the practical implementation and efficacy for this specific document type an open challenge.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow-up study implementing and evaluating a multimodal RAG pipeline (e.g., combining visual encoders for diagrams with text encoders for extracted labels) on the same P&ID corpus, measuring retrieval gains against the text-only baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific factors within the P&ID text extraction and representation process cause even structure-aware chunking to fail, and can these be mitigated by augmenting the input with minimal spatial metadata?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 3 shows structure-aware chunking, which excels on other documents, still yields very low scores on P&IDs. The paper attributes failure to "spatially encoded" nature but does not analyze whether the extracted text is insufficient, poorly chunked, or semantically void without spatial context.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses Azure Document Intelligence for extraction but does not report on the quality or quantity of text actually extracted from P&IDs. It is unclear if the failure is due to poor OCR/extraction, the chunking algorithm's inability to handle sparse text, or the inherent meaninglessness of isolated text fragments without their diagrammatic context.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An error analysis quantifying the text density, coherence, and semantic completeness of chunks derived from P&IDs. An ablation test adding simple spatial coordinates (e.g., region bounding boxes) as metadata to the text chunks before embedding, to see if rudimentary spatial awareness improves retrieval.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent are the performance advantages of structure-aware chunking (over semantic and recursive methods) generalizable to other enterprise domains beyond oil and gas, and to what degree are they dependent on the specific document parsing tool (Azure Document Intelligence)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study's conclusions are based on a proprietary, domain-specific corpus processed with a single commercial OCR/parsing tool (ADI). The "structure-aware" method heavily relies on ADI's markdown output with header tags, which may not be universally available or consistent across other document types or extraction tools.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes efficacy within its narrow context but does not investigate whether the results hold for documents with different structural conventions (e.g., legal contracts, scientific papers) or if a different parser producing different structural tags would yield similar benefits. The observed efficiency gains may be tied to ADI's specific output format.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A replication study applying the same four chunking strategies to a diverse, cross-domain corpus (e.g., legal, financial, academic) using multiple, varied document parsing backends (open-source and commercial), comparing the performance delta of structure-aware chunking across these conditions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can a hybrid or dynamic chunking strategy that selects or combines methods based on real-time document type classification outperform a single, uniform chunking strategy across a heterogeneous enterprise corpus?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study evaluates each strategy on the full corpus and on stratified subsets, finding that structure-aware wins overall but that recursive/fixed-size have strengths in MAP/F1. The paper notes the corpus contains "text-heavy," "table-heavy," and "diagram-heavy" documents but applies one strategy at a time globally.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis implies different document types might benefit from different chunking (e.g., structure-aware for manuals, semantic for tables?), but the research design tests strategies in isolation. It does not explore if a system that detects document type and applies the optimal strategy per type could achieve a better trade-off between top-K precision (MRR/NDCG) and recall (MAP/F1) than any single strategy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment implementing a document type classifier (using metadata or initial parsing) to route documents to their best-performing chunking strategy (as determined by the paper's per-type results), then evaluating the combined system's overall metrics against the single-strategy champions.

## Limitations
- Dependency on Azure Document Intelligence's proprietary extraction quality, which may not generalize to poorly formatted or legacy documents.
- Unspecified parameters for semantic chunking (embedding model, threshold) make its performance sensitive and hard to replicate.
- Potential bias in query generation toward text-heavy documents may exaggerate the failure on P&IDs.

## Confidence
- **Structure-aware superiority on text-heavy docs**: High
- **Semantic chunking underperformance**: Medium
- **Universal failure on P&IDs**: High
