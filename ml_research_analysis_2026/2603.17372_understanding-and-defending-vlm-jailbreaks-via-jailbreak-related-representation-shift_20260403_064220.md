# Understanding and Defending VLM Jailbreaks via Jailbreak-Related Representation Shift

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17372  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17372  
- **Reference count:** 24  
- **Primary result:** JRS-Rem reduces attack success rate (ASR) by up to 72.5% across three VLMs and seven attack datasets while preserving utility on benign benchmarks (≤0.5% drop).

## Executive Summary
Vision-language models (VLMs) remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks even when textual prompts explicitly contain harmful requests, suggesting a safety gap introduced by the visual modality. This paper identifies a "jailbreak direction" in the VLM's representation space that separates harmful-compliant internal states from refusal states. The image-induced representation shift along this direction—termed the jailbreak-related shift—predicts jailbreak success. The proposed defense, JRS-Rem, removes this shift component during inference when it exceeds a threshold. Evaluations on multiple VLMs and diverse attacks show strong protection with negligible impact on benign performance.

## Method Summary
The method begins by precomputing a unit "jailbreak direction" \(d^{(\ell)}\) for each transformer layer \(\ell\) using 50 samples: it averages hidden states for jailbreak and refusal text-only prompts, takes the difference, and normalizes. At inference, given an image–text input, the model runs two forward passes (text-only and multimodal) to obtain hidden states \(h_{\text{text}}^{(\ell)}\) and \(h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)}\). The image-induced shift is \(\Delta h^{(\ell)} = h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)} - h_{\text{text}}^{(\ell)}\). The scalar projection \(s^{(\ell)} = \Delta h^{(\ell)} \cdot d^{(\ell)}\) measures movement toward the jailbreak state. If the normalized projection \(\tilde{s}^{(\ell)} = s^{(\ell)} / \|\Delta h^{(\ell)}\|\) exceeds a threshold \(\tau = 0.2\), the component \(s^{(\ell)} d^{(\ell)}\) is subtracted from \(h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)}\), thereby steering the representation away from the jailbreak state while preserving other visual information.

## Key Results
- ASR reduction up to 72.5% compared to unprotected baselines across three VLMs and seven attack datasets.
- Consistently outperforms four state-of-the-art defense methods.
- Negligible utility loss on standard multimodal benchmarks (e.g., ≤0.5% on ScienceQA, MM-Vet).
- Jailbreak direction is sample‑efficient (computable with 50 samples) and robust across distributions (AUROC > 0.85 separating jailbreak/refusal states).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Existence of a Distinct Jailbreak State
- **Claim:** Jailbreak behavior arises because visual inputs shift the VLM's hidden state into a distinct internal "jailbreak state," separate from both benign and refusal states.
- **Mechanism:** Although VLMs recognize harmful intent (evidenced by safety warnings in text-only refusals), the visual modality causes a representation shift that moves the state into a cluster that triggers compliance rather than refusal.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM backbone has sufficient inherent safety alignment, but the visual override is the failure point.
- **Evidence anchors:** PCA visualizations (Figure 2), cosine distances to jailbreak centroid (Figure 3a), and linear probing F1 scores (Figure 3b) show separable clusters for jailbreak, refusal, and benign inputs.
- **Break condition:** If the backbone lacks basic safety alignment, the jailbreak and refusal states may not be separable, making the direction undefined.

### Mechanism 2: Jailbreak-Related Shift as Predictor
- **Claim:** The component of the image‑induced shift along the jailbreak direction (the "jailbreak‑related shift") quantifies the transition into the jailbreak state and predicts success.
- **Mechanism:** Compute \(d^{(\ell)}\) as the normalized difference between average jailbreak and refusal representations. For an input, the scalar projection \(s^{(\ell)} = \Delta h^{(\ell)} \cdot d^{(\ell)}\) captures movement along this direction. Larger \(s^{(\ell)}\) correlates with stronger steering toward jailbreak.
- **Core assumption:** The transition from refusal to jailbreak state is approximately linear and consistent across diverse harmful inputs.
- **Evidence anchors:** Shift distributions (Figure 4) show jailbreak samples have significantly larger normalized shifts than refusals across explicit, implicit, and adversarial scenarios; AUROC > 0.85 for separation (Figure 8b).
- **Break condition:** If jailbreak and refusal representations are not linearly separable, \(d^{(\ell)}\) fails to capture the transition and \(s^{(\ell)}\) becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 3: Removal of the Jailbreak‑Related Shift
- **Claim:** Subtracting the jailbreak‑related shift component from the hidden state during inference (JRS‑Rem) defends against jailbreaks with minimal utility loss.
- **Mechanism:** At the first generated token, for each layer \(\ell\), if \(\tilde{s}^{(\ell)} > \tau\), replace \(h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)}\) with \(h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)} - s^{(\ell)} d^{(\ell)}\). This preserves other visual‑semantic shifts while nullifying the jailbreak‑steering component.
- **Core assumption:** The remaining shift retains enough visual information for benign tasks but insufficient drive to enter the jailbreak state.
- **Evidence anchors:** Tables 2–4 show ASR reductions up to 72.5%; Table 5 shows negligible utility drops. Figure 7a justifies \(\tau = 0.2\) as a sweet spot.
- **Break condition:** High false positive rate on benign inputs (exceeding \(\tau\)) leads to utility loss; an inaccurate \(d^{(\ell)}\) (low robustness) reduces defense efficacy.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Jailbreak Direction Bank (precomputed \(d^{(\ell)}\)) → Shift Calculator (computes \(h_{\text{text}}\), \(h_{\text{mm}}\), \(\Delta h\), \(s\) per layer) → Threshold Gate (normalizes to \(\tilde{s}\) and compares to \(\tau\)) → Representation Corrector (applies removal if triggered) → Subsequent Layers (continue forward with corrected hidden states).

**Critical path:**  
1. Offline: Compute \(d^{(\ell)}\) for all layers \(\ell\) from 50 jailbreak and 50 refusal text‑only samples.  
2. Inference:  
   a. Run multimodal input \([I,T]\) once to obtain \(h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)}\) for all \(\ell\).  
   b. Run text‑only input \([\emptyset,T]\) once to obtain \(h_{\text{text}}^{(\ell)}\) for all \(\ell\).  
   c. For each layer \(\ell\): compute \(\Delta h^{(\ell)}\), \(s^{(\ell)}\), \(\tilde{s}^{(\ell)}\). If \(\tilde{s}^{(\ell)} > \tau\), set \(\hat{h}^{(\ell)} = h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)} - s^{(\ell)} d^{(\ell)}\); else set \(\hat{h}^{(\ell)} = h_{\text{mm}}^{(\ell)}\).  
   d. Feed \(\hat{h}^{(\ell)}\) as input to layer \(\ell+1\).  
   e. After the final layer, generate output tokens as usual.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Threshold \(\tau\):** Lower \(\tau\) increases safety but risks over‑correction (utility loss). The paper uses a heuristic \(\tau = 0.2\); optimal value may vary per model.  
- **Direction source:** Using HADES (explicitly harmful prompts) to define \(d^{(\ell)}\) generalizes to implicit/adversarial attacks, but may not be optimal for unseen domains.  
- **Layer selection:** Correction is applied per‑layer conditionally. Applying to all layers might be safer but more disruptive; the paper uses all layers where \(\tilde{s} > \tau\).

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **High ASR despite JRS‑Rem:** Indicates poor discriminability of \(d^{(\ell)}\) (low AUROC). Verify by computing AUROC on a held‑out set; if < 0.8, recompute direction with more diverse samples.  
2. **Significant utility drop:** If benign benchmarks suffer, check the distribution of \(\tilde{s}\) on benign inputs; if many exceed \(\tau\), raise the threshold to reduce false positives.  
3. **Inconsistent ASR reduction across attack types:** May occur if \(d^{(\ell)}\) lacks coverage of certain attack modes (e.g., non‑English). Include diverse samples when computing the direction.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Separability validation:** On a target VLM, collect a small mix of jailbreak, refusal, and benign samples (e.g., from HADES and LLaVA‑Instruct). Perform PCA and linear probing to confirm that jailbreak and refusal states are separable (F1 > 0.9).  
2. **Visualize jailbreak direction:** Using 50 jailbreak/refusal samples, compute \(d^{(\ell)}\) for mid‑layers (e.g., 10–20). Plot histograms of normalized shifts \(\tilde{s}^{(\ell)}\) for jailbreak vs. refusal inputs; expect a clear gap.  
3. **Threshold sweep:** Run JRS‑Rem on a jailbreak dataset (e.g., HADES‑SD) with \(\tau \in [0.05, 0.3]\). Record ASR and performance on a benign benchmark (e.g., ScienceQA). Choose \(\tau\) where ASR plateaus and accuracy begins to drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How effective is JRS‑Rem on VLMs with varying degrees of pre‑existing safety alignment in their LLM backbones?**  
   *Basis:* Limitations state that weak backbone alignment may make the jailbreak state indistinguishable, limiting effectiveness; further investigation across models with different alignment levels is needed.

2. **Can the JRS‑Rem mechanism be adapted to protect against text‑only jailbreak attacks?**  
   *Basis:* The method specifically targets visual‑induced shifts; it does not extend to pure text attacks. Whether a similar "jailbreak‑related shift" exists in text‑only scenarios is unknown.

3. **Does JRS‑Rem scale efficiently and maintain efficacy for much larger VLMs (e.g., 70B+)?**  
   *Basis:* While validated on models up to 19B parameters, scalability to significantly larger models remains unexplored; the linearity assumption and compute overhead may not hold.

4. **How robust is the pre‑computed jailbreak direction to distribution shifts (e.g., new attack types, different domains, non‑English prompts)?**  
   *Basis:* Although the direction shows consistency across similar datasets, generalization to truly out‑of‑distribution attacks has not been tested; continuous or adaptive direction updates may be required.

## Limitations
- Relies on a pre‑aligned LLM backbone; models with weak safety may lack a separable jailbreak state.  
- Jailbreak direction derived from explicit attacks (HADES); its linearity may not generalize to all implicit or adversarial attacks.  
- Fixed threshold \(\tau = 0.2\) is heuristic; model‑specific tuning may be necessary.  
- Inference roughly doubles compute (text‑only + multimodal forward passes).  
- Evaluation limited to three VLMs; broader model coverage is needed.

## Confidence
- **Separable jailbreak state exists:** High (supported by PCA, linear probing AUROC > 0.85).  
- **Jailbreak direction captures the transition:** High (consistent separation across datasets; but depends on diversity of training samples).  
- **JRS‑Rem effectiveness:** Medium‑High (ASR reduction up to 72.5% on tested benchmarks; utility preservation shown but not extensively probed).  
- **Scalability to larger models:** Low‑Medium (no evidence beyond 19B).  
- **Generalization to unseen attack domains:** Medium (some cross‑dataset robustness demonstrated, but not truly OOD).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑model generalizability:** Apply JRS‑Rem (using HADES‑derived direction) to two additional VLMs (e.g., MiniGPT‑4, BLIP‑2). Measure ASR reduction and utility on standard benchmarks.  
2. **Direction robustness to unseen attacks:** Compute the jailbreak direction from a new attack dataset (e.g., visual adversarial prompts) and test its ability to separate jailbreak/refusal states on HADES. Evaluate JRS‑Rem with this new direction.  
3. **Threshold sensitivity analysis:** On a held‑out benign set, plot the distribution of normalized shifts \(\tilde{s}\). Determine a model‑specific \(\tau\) that yields < 1% false positive rate while maintaining ASR reduction.
