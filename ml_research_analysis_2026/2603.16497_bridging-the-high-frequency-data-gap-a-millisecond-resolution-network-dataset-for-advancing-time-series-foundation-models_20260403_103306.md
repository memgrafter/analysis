# Bridging the High-Frequency Data Gap: A Millisecond-Resolution Network Dataset for Advancing Time Series Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.16497
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16497
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Current time series foundation models (TSFMs) like Chronos, TTM, and Lag-Llama fail to generalize to a novel millisecond-resolution 5G network dataset, while the Adaptive Random Forest (ARF) model significantly outperforms all TSFMs and other baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a novel, real-world, millisecond-resolution 5G wireless network dataset to address the lack of high-frequency data for evaluating time series foundation models (TSFMs). The dataset, filtered for static mobility and video streaming traffic and resampled to 100ms intervals, captures volatile, non-stationary network conditions. Comprehensive benchmarking reveals that state-of-the-art TSFMs perform poorly in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings on short-term forecasting tasks (100ms to 9.6s). In contrast, the online learning model Adaptive Random Forest (ARF) achieves superior accuracy, demonstrating that current TSFMs are ill-equipped for high-frequency, volatile data distributions due to mismatched pre-training frequencies and a static, batch-optimized nature. The work underscores the necessity of pre-training TSFMs on high-frequency data to advance their applicability to dynamic real-world systems.

## Method Summary
The study centers on a proprietary 5G network dataset containing physical (PHY) and medium access control (MAC) layer metrics (e.g., CQI, MCS, packet statistics) and downlink bitrate as the target. After filtering for specific user mobility and traffic class combinations, the raw millisecond data is resampled to 100ms intervals. For evaluation, a rolling-origin forecast protocol is adopted: models use a lookback window (5 steps) to predict a horizon (96 steps). The training (80%) and test (20%) split is performed temporally per user to prevent leakage. A suite of models is benchmarked: online adaptive (ARF), offline shallow (Random Forest, XGBoost, Ordinary Linear Regression), and TSFMs (Chronos, TTM, Lag-Llama). For multivariate forecasting with TSFMs, Chronos is adapted using a covariate regressor to model effects of the four PHY/MAC features and fine-tuned on the resulting residuals. Performance is assessed using RMSE and MAE across all generated forecast points.

## Key Results
*   TSFMs (Chronos, TTM, Lag-Llama) exhibit consistently poor performance, often performing worse than a naive baseline, in both zero-shot and fine-tuned configurations.
*   The Adaptive Random Forest (ARF) model significantly outperforms all other baselines and TSFMs, achieving the lowest error metrics (RMSE: 0.0175, MAE: 0.0130) in the multivariate forecasting setting.
*   Analysis of the dataset reveals unstable trends, weak seasonality, and heavy-tailed, spiky residuals—statistical properties that are starkly different from the low-frequency data used to pre-train existing TSFMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Distribution Shift from Pre-Training Frequencies
*   **Claim:** TSFMs fail because their pre-training data distributions (seconds to years) do not cover the statistical properties of millisecond-resolution wireless data.
*   **Mechanism:** TSFMs learn temporal patterns from low-frequency datasets. Applied to high-frequency data with unstable trends, weak seasonality, and spiky residuals, their internal representations mismatch the new distribution, causing poor zero-shot and fine-tuned performance.
*   **Core Assumption:** Pre-training data frequency is a primary determinant of TSFM generalization capability.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract states current large-scale datasets hinder capturing high-frequency nuances. STL decomposition (Section 3.2) shows unstable trends and sharp spikes unlike pre-training datasets. Corpus work notes non-stationarity challenges but not specifically for millisecond wireless data.

### Mechanism 2: Online Adaptation vs. Static Model Nature
*   **Claim:** ARF outperforms TSFMs because its incremental, ensemble-based design handles concept drift from volatile, non-stationary streams, while TSFMs are optimized for static, curated datasets.
*   **Mechanism:** ARF updates per instance via online bagging and drift detection. For data with sudden variance changes and clustered anomalies, ARF reacts instantaneously. TSFMs process fixed contexts and cannot react instantaneously to distribution shifts, even when fine-tuned in batches.
*   **Core Assumption:** Adaptive online learning is necessary for high-frequency, volatile streams where statistical properties change between training and deployment.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Section 4.4 explicitly states ARF handles concept drift. ARF is from the River library (online ML), while TSFMs use offline fine-tuning. Corpus work on federated dataset learning focuses on cross-domain, not intra-stream drift.

### Mechanism 3: Feature Utilization in Multivariate Context
*   **Claim:** In multivariate forecasting, ARF leverages the immediate physical coupling of PHY/MAC-layer features (e.g., CQI→bitrate) more effectively than TSFMs, which are tuned for broader but shallower cross-variable relationships.
*   **Mechanism:** ARF's tree-based splits naturally capture complex, non-linear interactions among the four key features. TSFMs, pre-trained on datasets with different feature semantics and relationships, may not prioritize or model these specific couplings effectively.
*   **Core Assumption:** Tree-based models can more directly exploit strong, local feature interactions present in engineered network telemetry.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** ARF outperforms Chronos in the multivariate setting (Section 4.4). Dataset features are defined as PHY/MAC metrics with known network theory relationships (Section 3.1). Corpus work on TSFM-Bench discusses multivariate evaluation but not this specific feature coupling.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Statistical Stationarity and Heteroskedasticity**
    *   **Why needed here:** The core challenge is modeling a non-stationary, heteroskedastic process. The dataset shows significant drifting mean and variance (Section 3.2, Figs 4b, 8a). Understanding these properties is prerequisite to evaluating why static models fail.
    *   **Quick Check:** Given a time series where rolling mean and standard deviation both drift significantly, which class of models (online adaptive, offline static) would you prioritize and why?
*   **Concept: Temporal Granularity and Sampling Theorem Implications**
    *   **Why needed here:** The dataset operates at 100ms resolution after downsampling. Relevant dynamics (channel fading, traffic bursts) occur at sub-second scales. Models must capture dependencies across very short lags.
    *   **Quick Check:** If a phenomenon has significant power at cycles of 200ms and 500ms, what minimum sampling rate is needed to avoid aliasing, and how might that impact a model's context window?
*   **Concept: Online vs. Batch Learning Paradigms**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper contrasts ARF's online nature with TSFMs' batch fine-tuning. This is central to explaining adaptation speed to new patterns (e.g., a sudden DDoS attack).
    *   **Quick Check:** For a streaming data source where a new pattern emerges after 10,000 samples, what are the computational and statistical trade-offs between an online learning algorithm that updates per sample versus a model periodically retrained on a sliding window?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Raw Millisecond PMs (O-RAN Base Station) -> Filtering (mobility='static', traffic='video') -> Resampling (100ms mean) -> Feature Selection (CQI, MCS, pkt_ok, pkt_nok, bitrate) -> Splitting (80:20 per user) -> Windowing (lookback=5, horizon=96) -> Evaluation (rolling-origin) -> Models (ARF, RF, XGB, OLR, Chronos/TTM/LL)

### Critical path
The most decisive path is: **Data Characterization → Baseline Model Selection → TSFM Evaluation Diagnosis**.
1.  First, characterize the dataset: STL decomposition, rolling statistics, ACF, QQ plots. Expect unstable trend/weak seasonality/spiky residuals.
2.  Second, run the baseline suite (Naive, OLR, RF, XGB, ARF). If ARF dominates, hypothesis leans toward "requires online adaptation."
3.  Third, evaluate TSFMs zero-shot then fine-tuned. If performance remains poor, hypothesis leans toward "distribution shift from pre-training frequency/domain."

### Design tradeoffs
*   **Resolution vs. Noise:** 100ms downsampling reduces overhead but may discard relevant sub-100ms dynamics. TSFMs trained on lower frequencies cannot recover this.
*   **Online Update Cost vs. Accuracy:** ARF updates per sample (low latency, memory growth). TSFMs fine-tune in batches (high compute, static post-training). For volatile networks, ARF's cost is justified.
*   **Feature Depth vs. Model Capacity:** Using 4 vs. 10 features shows ARF benefits from more relevant signals. TSFMs may need different pre-training to utilize deeper feature sets effectively.
*   **Univariate Simplicity vs. Multivariate Complexity:** Chronos is univariate; extending it via covariate regressors introduces pipeline complexity and potential error propagation.

### Failure signatures
1.  **TSFM Signal Collapse:** Predicted series is overly smooth, missing sharp spikes and step changes. RMSE high relative to Naive.
2.  **Shallow Model Static Lag:** RF/XGB predictions lag behind abrupt shifts because their trees are built on older data distributions.
3.  **Concept Drift Undercatch:** If ARF's ensemble weight for recent data is low due to `grace_period` or drift detector settings, it may temporarily mimic RF/XGB failure. Monitor tree generation rate.
4.  **Multivariate Breakdown:** If a key feature (e.g., CQI) drops to zero or becomes constant, ARF may degrade less than TSFMs which expect co-varying patterns from pre-training.

### First 3 experiments
1.  **Data Characterization Replication:** Replicate Figures 4 and 7-10 for your own subset. Calculate STL components, rolling mean/std, ACF, and QQ plots. Confirm the "unstable trend, weak seasonality, spiky residuals" pattern persists across different mobility/traffic filters.
2.  **Baseline Model Sanity Check:** Train and evaluate Naive, OLR, RF, XGB, ARF on a short univariate window (e.g., last 10k samples). Expect ARF to adapt quickest to local patterns; OLR to be worst due to linearity; RF/XGB to lag.
3.  **TSFM Zero-Shock Test:** Run Chronos-small (46M) and TTM zero-shot on the validation set with default context lengths. Measure the gap between Naive forecast and TSFM forecast. A large gap (TSFM worse than Naive) confirms the distribution shift hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1: Can parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) like LoRA improve TSFM performance on high-frequency network data?**
    *   **Basis in paper:** Appendix A.2 states novel techniques like LoRA were not considered since focus was on zero/few-shot. Future work proposes ablation studies.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Current benchmarking used only default fine-tuning; LoRA/autotuning unevaluated.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments comparing default fine-tuning and PEFT (e.g., LoRA) on the dataset, measuring zero-shot and few-shot improvements with statistical significance.
*   **Question 2: Could feature engineering and tailored preprocessing boost TSFM accuracy on high-frequency volatile data?**
    *   **Basis in paper:** Appendix A.2 notes feature engineering and preprocessing were not considered to keep comparison fair with shallow models.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Raw data was used; impact of domain-specific transformations unknown.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply preprocessing (STL residual extraction, denoising, differencing) and evaluate TSFM performance vs. raw-data baseline.
*   **Question 3: Would comprehensive hyperparameter optimization (HPO) alter the performance hierarchy and possibly narrow the gap between TSFMs and ARF?**
    *   **Basis in paper:** Appendix A.2 admits limited HPO for shallow models and default TSFMs, though some tuning for TTM/Lag-Llama was done.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Only selective tuning performed; full HPO might improve all models and change relative rankings.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform thorough HPO (e.g., Bayesian optimization) across all models and compare optimized performance; assess if ARF maintains superiority.
*   **Question 4: What architectural modifications or training objectives are necessary for TSFMs to capture unstable trends, weak seasonality, and heavy-tailed noise in millisecond data?**
    *   **Basis in paper:** Inferred from dataset analysis (Section 3.2) highlighting characteristics that challenge TSFMs, and call to incorporate high-frequency data for pre-training.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Existing TSFMs pre-trained on low-frequency data lack mechanisms for high-frequency volatility.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Design model variants (adaptive normalization, attention to spikes, specialized losses) and pre-train them on high-frequency data; evaluate against baseline TSFMs.

## Limitations
*   The dataset is not publicly available, preventing independent verification and broader analysis.
*   Hyperparameters for RF, XGB, and TSFM fine-tuning are incompletely specified, risking implementation drift.
*   Causal claims about TSFM failure rely on observational evidence; no experiments manipulate pre-training frequency or adaptation mechanisms.
*   Evaluation is restricted to static mobility and video traffic; generalizability to other network conditions or domains is unknown.

## Confidence
*   **TSFMs underperform on this high-frequency dataset:** High (consistent benchmark results across models and metrics).
*   **Failure primarily due to pre-training distribution shift:** Medium (supported by STL/rolling statistics but not proven causative).
*   **ARF’s superiority stems from online adaptation:** Medium (inferred from comparison to offline baselines; no online-TSFM variant tested).
*   **TSFMs underutilize multivariate network features:** Low (only indirect evidence from ARF’s larger multivariate gain).

## Next Checks
1.  **Pre-train a TSFM on high-frequency data:** Train a foundation model on a corpus of millisecond-resolution series (e.g., financial ticks, sensor streams) and evaluate zero-shot on the 5G dataset. Significant improvement would confirm the distribution shift hypothesis.
2.  **Implement online fine-tuning for TSFMs:** Adapt a TSFM (e.g., Chronos) to update incrementally on a rolling window of recent data and compare rolling-origin error to ARF. Parity would challenge the online adaptation necessity claim.
3.  **Ablate multivariate feature interactions:** Systematically remove or perturb key network features (CQI, MCS) and measure performance change for ARF vs. TSFMs. A larger drop for ARF would falsify the feature utilization claim.
