# Interleaved Resampling and Refitting: Data and Compute-Efficient Evaluation of Black-Box Predictors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.14218
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14218
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: Provides a computationally and data‑efficient method to bound the excess risk of black‑box predictors without full retraining, using interleaved subsampling and wild refitting.

## Executive Summary
Excess risk evaluation—estimating how much a deployed model’s performance degrades relative to the Bayes optimal—is crucial but often requires expensive retraining or large validation sets. This paper introduces a novel approach that interleaves random subsampling with wild refitting to compute high‑probability upper bounds on excess risk using only the original training data and a limited number of refits on much smaller subsets. The method constructs pseudo‑responses by adding scaled, sign‑randomized residuals from a pilot predictor, refits the black‑box algorithm on these perturbed subsets, and aggregates computable “wild optimism” terms. The resulting bounds hold under both fixed and random designs, avoid reliance on traditional complexity measures like Rademacher complexity, and scale with the covariate density ratio, a Fourier decay exponent \(v\), and the ambient dimension. Experiments on synthetic continuous and discontinuous regression tasks confirm that the bounds are tight and track the true excess risk across sample sizes and tuning choices, demonstrating the practicality of the approach for data‑ and compute‑efficient model evaluation.

## Method Summary
Given a trained black‑box model \(\hat{f}\) (the pilot) and dataset \(D = \{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\), the algorithm first computes residuals \(v_i = y_i - \bar{f}(x_i)\) (usually \(\bar{f} = \hat{f}\)) and generates independent Rademacher signs \(\varepsilon_i\). For \(K\) rounds, it randomly selects a small subset \(S_k\) of size \(m = \lfloor n^\beta \rfloor\) without replacement, and constructs two perturbed response vectors: \(\tilde{y}^+_i = \hat{f}(x_i) + \rho_k \varepsilon_i v_i\) and \(\tilde{y}^-_i = \hat{f}(x_i) - \rho_k \varepsilon_i v_i\) for \(i\in S_k\). The black‑box algorithm is refit on each perturbed subset, yielding \(\hat{f}^+_{\rho,k}\) and \(\hat{f}^-_{\rho,k}\). For each, the empirical \(L^2\) norm over \(S_k\) (the “radius”) and the wild optimism term \(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in S_k} \varepsilon_i v_i (\hat{f}^\pm_{\rho,k}(x_i)-\hat{f}(x_i))\) are recorded. If desired, an initial set of rounds can estimate the true risk radius \(r = \|\hat{f}-f^\star\|_D\) so that \(\rho_k\) can be tuned to make the radii approximately \(2r\). Finally, the average of the optimism terms over all rounds, plus a concentration term \(R_p(\delta)\) that accounts for the randomness in subsampling and noise, yields an upper bound on the empirical excess risk. A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.1) shows that when the radii are properly aligned, the average optimism tightly upper bounds the true excess risk. The method thus requires only \(K\) refits on subsets of size \(n^\beta\), dramatically reducing computational burden compared to full retraining, while providing finite‑sample guarantees under mild assumptions: a Fourier decay condition on the function class, a bounded covariate density ratio, and bounded noise.

## Key Results
- High‑probability excess risk bounds under both fixed and random designs, without needing separate validation data or explicit complexity measures like Rademacher complexity.
- Bounds scale with the covariate density ratio, the Fourier decay exponent \(v\), and the dimension, capturing the effect of data distribution and function smoothness.
- Empirical results on synthetic tasks (continuous sine regression with MLPs and discontinuous step functions with random forests) demonstrate that the computed bounds are tight and closely follow the true excess risk across various sample sizes and noise‑scale settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Wild Refitting with Pilot Residuals
- **Claim**: The excess risk can be upper‑bounded by a computable “wild optimism” derived from refitting on perturbed pseudo‑responses.
- **Mechanism**: Compute residuals \(v_i = y_i - \bar{f}(x_i)\). For each subsample, form \(\tilde{y}_i = \hat{f}(x_i) + \rho \varepsilon_i v_i\) with i.i.d. Rademacher \(\varepsilon_i\). Refit to obtain \(\hat{f}_\rho\). The resulting term \(\frac{1}{m}\sum \varepsilon_i v_i (\hat{f}_\rho(x_i)-\hat{f}(x_i))\) equals the supremum of an empirical process over an \(L^2\) ball around \(\hat{f}\) (Lemma 4.2). When \(\rho\) is tuned so that \(\|\hat{f}_\rho-\hat{f}\|_{S_k}\) reaches the appropriate radius, this supremum upper‑bounds the true optimism.
- **Core assumption**: The pilot \(\bar{f}\) is sufficiently accurate; noise \(|w_i| \le \tau\).
- **Break condition**: If the pilot is very poor, the pilot error term \(V(\cdot)\) may not be dominated by the wild optimism, leading to a loose bound.

### Mechanism 2: Interleaved Subsampling for Compute Efficiency
- **Claim**: Refitting on small random subsets reduces computational cost from \(O(n)\) to \(O(n^\beta)\) while preserving statistical validity.
- **Mechanism**: Draw \(K\) subsets \(S_k\) of size \(m=\lfloor n^\beta\rfloor\) without replacement. Lemma 4.3 shows that, under a bounded density ratio and Fourier decay, the empirical \(L^2\) norm on a subset is uniformly equivalent (up to a constant) to the full empirical norm over the function‑difference class. Averaging the wild optimism over \(K\) rounds yields a high‑probability bound with an extra term of order \(\tau/\sqrt{K}\).
- **Core assumption**: Subset size \(m\) is large enough (requires \(n\) sufficiently large); density ratio is bounded; distinct covariates are available for pilot error control via interpolation.
- **Break condition**: If \(\beta\) is too small, the norm‑equivalence constant may explode or the condition \(n \ge C(\delta)\) may be violated. Extremely large density ratios also degrade the bound’s constant.

### Mechanism 3: Harmonic Analysis and Toeplitz Operator Concentration
- **Claim**: Under a Fourier decay assumption, the uniform equivalence between full‑ and subsample empirical \(L^2\) norms follows from concentration of empirical Toeplitz operators, avoiding classical Rademacher complexity.
- **Mechanism**: Expand functions in Fourier series; the empirical \(L^2\) norm corresponds to a quadratic form of a Toeplitz operator built from the covariates. Truncating high frequencies (thanks to the decay) yields a finite‑dimensional operator. Concentration inequalities for random tensor matrices then show that the empirical operator on a random subset concentrates around its expectation, giving \(\|h\|_S^2 \le C \cdot \|h\|_D^2 + \text{error}\) uniformly over the function‑difference class. The constants involve the density ratio and the Fourier tail bound \(M_v\).
- **Core assumption**: Assumption 2.1: \(\exists v>1/2, M_v>0\) s.t. \(|\phi_f(k)| \le M_v/|k|^v\) for all \(f\in\mathcal{F}\); Assumption 2.2 (bounded density ratio); covariates lie in a compact domain.
- **Break condition**: If \(v\le1/2\), Parseval’s identity fails; if \(v>1\) the class is smoother but constants still depend on \(s_w/w\). Non‑polynomial decay would require a different envelope.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Preprocessing**: Train the pilot \(\hat{f} = \text{Alg}(D)\). Compute residuals \(v_i = y_i - \bar{f}(x_i)\) (with \(\bar{f}=\hat{f}\) by default). Generate i.i.d. Rademacher signs \(\varepsilon_i\).
2. **Iterative Loop** (repeat for \(k = 1,\dots,K\)):
   - Subsample \(S_k \subset [n]\) without replacement, size \(m = \lfloor n^\beta \rfloor\).
   - Construct two perturbed response vectors on \(S_k\):  
     \(\tilde{y}^+_i = \hat{f}(x_i) + \rho_k \varepsilon_i v_i\),  
     \(\tilde{y}^-_i = \hat{f}(x_i) - \rho_k \varepsilon_i v_i\).
   - Refit the black‑box algorithm:  
     \(\hat{f}^+_{\rho,k} = \text{Alg}(\{(x_i,\tilde{y}^+_i)\}_{i\in S_k})\),  
     \(\hat{f}^-_{\rho,k} = \text{Alg}(\{(x_i,\tilde{y}^-_i)\}_{i\in S_k})\).
   - Compute empirical norms \(a_k^\pm = \|\hat{f}^\pm_{\rho,k} - \hat{f}\|_{S_k}\) and wild optimism terms \(\text{ĄOpt}^\pm_{\rho,k,S_k} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in S_k} \varepsilon_i v_i (\hat{f}^\pm_{\rho,k}(x_i)-\hat{f}(x_i))\).
3. **Optional Radius Estimation** (Algorithm 2): Use the first \(K_1\) rounds to obtain a high‑probability estimate \(\hat{r}\) of the true risk radius \(r = \|\hat{f}-f^\star\|_D\) via inequality (5). Then, for remaining rounds, tune \(\rho_k\) (e.g., by binary search) so that \(a_k^\pm \approx 2\hat{r}\).
4. **Final Bound Assembly**: Compute the average optimism \(\overline{\text{Opt}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k (\text{ĄOpt}^+_{\rho,k,S_k} + \text{ĄOpt}^-_{\rho,k,S_k})\). Add the concentration term \(R_p(\delta)\) and the pilot error \(V^2(\bar{f})\) to obtain an upper bound on the empirical excess risk. If a bound on the population excess risk is desired, multiply by \(4\overline{w}/\underline{w}\) and incorporate the critical‑radius term from Theorem 5.1.

### Critical Path
The computational bottleneck is the initial full‑scale training of \(\hat{f}\) (once) followed by \(K\) refits on subsets of size \(n^\beta\). Data flows: \(D \to\) residuals & \(\varepsilon\) \(\to\) subsamples \(\to\) perturbed datasets \(\to\) refitted models \(\to\) norms & optimism \(\to\) aggregated bound. If Algorithm 2 is used, an initial set of rounds (say \(K_1\)) is allocated to estimating the risk radius; subsequent rounds then perform binary search over \(\rho\) to achieve the desired radii, adding a constant‑factor overhead but not changing asymptotic scaling.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Subset exponent \(\beta\)**: Smaller \(\beta\) reduces per‑round cost but increases the norm‑equivalence constant from Lemma 4.3 and may require a larger total sample size \(n\) for the theory to hold. Very small \(\beta\) can also make the radii harder to tune.
- **Number of rounds \(K\)**: More rounds decrease the sampling‑error term (\(\propto 1/\sqrt{K}\)) but linearly increase total wall‑clock time. There is a trade‑off between bound tightness and computational budget.
- **Noise‑scale \(\rho\)**: Must be large enough that the radii of the wild models reach the target value (approximately twice the true risk radius). If \(\rho\) is too small, Lemma 4.2 does not apply and the bound may be invalid. If \(\rho\) is too large, numerical instability or over‑perturbation can occur, and the mapping \(\rho \mapsto \|\hat{f}_\rho-\hat{f}\|_{S_k}\) may become non‑monotonic, complicating binary search.
- **Pilot choice**: Using the final model \(\hat{f}\) as pilot simplifies implementation but ties the residuals to its own estimation error. A separate, perhaps simpler, pilot could reduce the pilot error term \(V^2(\bar{f})\) at the cost of an additional training run.
- **Dimensionality**: The multivariate extension (Section 6) introduces a factor \((N\log N)^d\) in the norm‑equivalence constant (Lemma 6.1), where \(d\) is the covariate dimension. This constant can grow quickly with \(d\), potentially eroding the computational benefits in high dimensions.

### Failure Signatures
- **Norm equivalence violation**: If \(n\) is too small or \(\beta\) too large, the uniform equivalence from Lemma 4.3 may fail with noticeable probability, causing the translation from subsample radii to the full‑data radius to be erroneous.
- **Pilot error domination**: When the function class does not satisfy the interpolation conditions required by Theorems 4.2 and 4.3, the pilot error term \(V(\bar{f})\) may not be bounded by the wild optimism, leading to a vacuous final bound.
- **Extreme density ratio**: A large ratio \(\overline{w}/\underline{w}\) inflates the main bound by that factor, potentially rendering it useless in practice.
- **Numerical instability in \(\rho\) tuning**: For non‑convex algorithms, the relationship \(\rho \mapsto \|\hat{f}_\rho-\hat{f}\|_{S_k}\) may be irregular or non‑monotone, causing binary search to fail to locate a suitable \(\rho\) that meets the radius condition.
- **Small \(n\) and high \(\beta\)**: When the dataset is modest and \(\beta\) is close to 1, the subsample size \(m\) is nearly \(n\), defeating the purpose of computational savings while still suffering from the same theoretical constraints.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis relies on a Fourier decay condition (\(v>1/2\)), a bounded covariate density ratio, and bounded noise—assumptions that may not hold for high‑dimensional data, large language models, or unbounded noise distributions.
- The bound includes a pilot error term \(V^2(\bar{f})\) that can be substantial if the initial predictor is inaccurate, potentially weakening the guarantee. This term is controlled by interpolation properties of the function class, which are not guaranteed for many modern black‑box models (e.g., deep neural networks).
- Although the method reduces the per‑round cost by using subsets of size \(n^\beta\), the total compute still scales with the number of rounds \(K\) and the cost of training the black‑box algorithm. Moreover, the norm‑equivalence constants may degrade as \(\beta\) decreases, limiting attainable speed‑ups, especially in high dimensions where the constants involve factors that grow rapidly with \(d\).
