# Quantifying Memorization and Privacy Risks in Genomic Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08913
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08913
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** All evaluated genomic language models exhibit measurable memorization, and single-metric evaluations systematically underestimate privacy risk, necessitating a multi-vector auditing framework.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical gap: genomic language models (GLMs) lack systematic evaluation for memorization risks, which threaten privacy due to the immutable and identifiable nature of genomic data. The authors introduce a multi-vector evaluation framework combining perplexity-based detection, canary sequence extraction, and membership inference into a unified maximum vulnerability score. Testing across four GLM architectures and four genomic datasets reveals that every model shows evidence of memorization. Notably, extraction success scales with data repetition, and different models are vulnerable to different attack vectors—demonstrating that relying on any single metric obscures the full privacy risk. The work underscores the need for comprehensive, multi-attack privacy audits in genomic modeling.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a multi-vector framework to quantify memorization along three complementary attack surfaces: (1) **perplexity gap** between training and held-out data as a detection signal, (2) **canary extraction** success rate for sequences artificially inserted into the training set, and (3) **membership inference** attack accuracy using shadow models. These metrics are aggregated into a maximum vulnerability score per model. Experiments fine-tune four GLMs (DNABERT-2, SimpleDNALM, Nucleotide Transformer, Evo) on a fixed corpus of 1,000 genomic sequences, systematically varying data repetition for planted canaries to measure scaling behavior.

## Key Results
- All four GLMs exhibit measurable memorization, with maximum vulnerability scores ranging from 0.48 to 1.00.
- Evo (LoRA) achieves near-perfect canary extraction (100%) on real genomic data, while other models dominate in different attack vectors (e.g., DNABERT-2 in perplexity gap, SimpleDNALM in extraction).
- Extraction success scales monotonically with the number of data repetitions, confirming that memorization laws from NLP extend to genomic sequences.
- Membership inference attacks yield AUCs between 0.70–0.79 across all models, indicating consistent but moderate signal.
- Perplexity gaps and extraction success are decoupled: DNABERT-2 shows strong perplexity gaps but resists extraction, whereas SimpleDNALM exhibits high extraction with negligible perplexity signal.

## Why This Works
The multi-vector approach works because memorization manifests through multiple, distinct mechanisms that no single attack can capture comprehensively. Perplexity gaps reveal distributional shifts but may not indicate exact sequence regurgitation. Canary extraction directly tests for verbatim copying of specific training points. Membership inference detects statistical leakage about training set membership. By combining these, the framework exposes a broader attack surface—models can be vulnerable in one dimension while appearing safe in another, as seen with DNABERT-2 and SimpleDNALM. This triangulation prevents underestimation of privacy risk that would occur with any metric in isolation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map:** Input Genomic Corpus → Tokenization → GLM Fine-Tuning → Attack Evaluation (Perplexity Gap / Canary Extraction / Membership Inference) → Maximum Vulnerability Score
- **Critical Path:** The evaluation depends on successful fine-tuning on a corpus with planted canaries; any failure in canary insertion or attack implementation breaks the measurement.
- **Design Tradeoffs:** The maximum vulnerability score prioritizes worst-case risk (via max aggregation) over average risk, which may overstate practical threat but ensures no attack vector is ignored. The framework sacrifices granular trade-off analysis between utility and privacy for clear risk signaling.
- **Failure Signatures:** (1) Low canary extraction despite high repetition, suggesting effective defenses or architectural resistance; (2) High perplexity gap with zero extraction, indicating memorization without verbatim copying; (3) Inconsistent membership inference AUC across shadow models, implying unreliable detection.
- **First Three Experiments:** (1) Plant synthetic 100-bp canaries at varying repetition counts (1–100) and measure extraction success; (2) Compute perplexity on held-out canaries vs. training canaries to quantify gap; (3) Train shadow models on disjoint data slices to perform membership inference on canaries, recording AUC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do memorization rates and the dominant attack vectors for genomic sequences with biological structure match those observed with synthetic random canary sequences?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states: "it remains an open question whether memorization of real sequences would manifest at comparable rates or through the same vectors."
- **Why unresolved:** The evaluation used synthetic canaries with no biological structure to isolate memorization from biological plausibility, but real genomic data have motifs, conservation, and other structures that might affect memorization dynamics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments planting canaries that are realistic genomic sequences (e.g., from actual genomes, with known functional elements) and compare extraction success, perplexity gaps, and membership inference AUC to the synthetic canary baseline across multiple GLMs.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does full parameter fine-tuning of large genomic language models (e.g., 7B-parameter Evo) exhibit different memorization profiles compared to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA) or full fine-tuning of smaller models?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper notes: "we therefore cannot determine whether full parameter fine-tuning of a 7B-parameter model would change the memorization patterns observed relative to the smaller fully fine-tuned architectures." Also, Evo was only evaluated with LoRA.
- **Why unresolved:** The study compared Evo (LoRA) to smaller fully fine-tuned models, but the effect of fine-tuning strategy on a large model is conflated with model scale. Without a full fine-tuning run of Evo, we don't know if LoRA inherently reduces memorization or if the observed high vulnerability is due to scale.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train Evo with full fine-tuning on the same datasets and repeat the multi-vector evaluation. Compare maximum vulnerability scores and per-vector metrics to the LoRA version and to smaller fully fine-tuned models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does the size of the training corpus (number of sequences) affect memorization risk in genomic language models?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper acknowledges: "all experiments use a fixed corpus size of 1,000 training sequences, and memorization dynamics may shift as dataset scale increases toward the cohort sizes typical of biobank-derived fine-tuning."
- **Why unresolved:** The experiments used a small corpus (1,000 sequences) to simulate fine-tuning on small cohorts. However, real biobank fine-tuning may involve thousands to millions of sequences. Memorization laws in NLP show that larger datasets reduce memorization per example, but genomic data may behave differently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments varying the number of training sequences (e.g., 1k, 10k, 100k, 1M) on one or more GLMs, measuring maximum vulnerability scores and per-vector metrics. Determine if memorization scales with dataset size as in NLP.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How effective are privacy-preserving techniques (e.g., differential privacy, data deduplication, early stopping) at reducing memorization across all three attack vectors in GLMs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper suggests: "integrating more privacy defenses into the framework would enable direct measurement of defense efficacy against the multi-vector attack surface characterized in this work." Also, in related work, DP and deduplication are noted as defenses in NLP.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates GLMs under standard fine-tuning without any privacy defenses. Prior work in NLP shows deduplication reduces extraction, and DP reduces memorization but at utility cost. It is unknown how these defenses perform on GLMs across the multi-vector framework.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train GLMs with various privacy-preserving methods (e.g., DP-SGD, deduplicated training data, early stopping, dropout) and evaluate using the same multi-vector framework. Compare maximum vulnerability scores and per-vector metrics to undefended models.

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to four GLM architectures and four genomic datasets, restricting generalizability to larger models and diverse genomic sources.
- Canary sequences were artificially inserted; memorization behavior on naturally occurring data may differ due to biological structure.
- The maximum vulnerability score uses a simple max aggregation, potentially oversimplifying overall privacy risk and ignoring trade-offs between attack vectors.
- The study focused on extraction and membership inference; other privacy threats (e.g., model inversion, attribute inference) remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- **All evaluated GLMs exhibit measurable memorization:** High (consistent evidence across multiple independent attacks).
- **Extraction success scales monotonically with data repetition:** High (controlled experiments show clear trend).
- **Perplexity gaps and extraction success are decoupled (DNABERT-2 vs SimpleDNALM):** Medium (observed in two models; requires broader evaluation).
- **Membership inference yields AUC 0.70–0.79:** Medium (moderate signal, but performance depends on shadow model design).
- **Single-metric evaluations underestimate risk; multi-vector auditing is necessary:** High (direct comparison demonstrates contradictory cross-attack signals).

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate larger-scale genomic LMs (e.g., >1B parameters) and additional datasets (e.g., ENCODE, TCGA) to test generalizability of the multi-vector findings.
2. Compare alternative aggregation strategies for the vulnerability score (e.g., weighted sum, Pareto front) and include supplementary attacks (model inversion, phenotype attribute inference) for a more comprehensive audit.
3. Investigate memorization under natural duplication patterns in public genomic repositories, moving beyond controlled repetition of canaries, to assess ecological validity.
