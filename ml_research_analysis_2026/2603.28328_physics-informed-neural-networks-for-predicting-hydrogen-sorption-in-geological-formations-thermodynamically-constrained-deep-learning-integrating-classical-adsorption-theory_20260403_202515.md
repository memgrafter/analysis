# Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Predicting Hydrogen Sorption in Geological Formations: Thermodynamically Constrained Deep Learning Integrating Classical Adsorption Theory

## Quick Facts
* arXiv ID: 2603.28328  
* Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28328  
* Reference count: 40  
* Primary result: Achieved R²=0.9544 on held-out test set with thermodynamically constrained PINN; 98.6% monotonicity satisfaction; zero non-physical predictions; outperformed classical models (R² collapse to 0.09–0.38 on aggregated data) and random forest by 10–15% in leave-one-lithology-out validation.

## Executive Summary
Predicting hydrogen sorption in geological formations is complicated by the heterogeneity of clay, shale, and coal samples. Classical isotherm models, when fit to individual samples, achieve R² of 0.80–0.90, but fail to generalize to aggregated datasets, with R² collapsing to 0.09–0.38. To address this, we introduce a multi-scale physics-informed neural network (PINN) that integrates classical adsorption theory and thermodynamic constraints. The model employs a seven-category feature engineering pipeline generating 62 thermodynamically meaningful descriptors, a loss function that penalizes violations of saturation bounds, pressure monotonicity, and Van’t Hoff temperature dependence, and a three-phase curriculum training schedule that stabilizes learning. An ensemble of ten architecture-diverse networks provides uncertainty quantification with temperature scaling calibration.

On a dataset of 1,987 hydrogen sorption measurements across clays, shales, and coals, the PINN achieved R²=0.9544, RMSE=0.0484 mmol/g, MAE=0.0231 mmol/g on a held-out test set. It exhibited 98.6% monotonicity compliance and zero non-physical predictions (negative uptakes or exceeding saturation limits). Under leave-one-lithology-out cross-validation, the PINN outperformed a tuned random forest by 10–15% in R², underscoring the transfer value of embedding physical laws into deep learning for geoscientific prediction tasks.

## Method Summary
The method builds on seven categories of physics-informed feature engineering to transform raw material properties (e.g., surface area, pore volume, mineral composition) and process conditions (pressure, temperature) into 62 descriptors that encode thermodynamic relationships such as reduced pressure, adsorption driving force, and confinement parameters. These features serve as inputs to a neural network with a multi-scale parallel architecture: three dense layers of 64, 128, and 256 units process the data at different resolutions, their outputs are concatenated and modulated by a gating mechanism that uses pressure and temperature to emphasize thermodynamically relevant signals. The gated representation passes through a compressed backbone (dense layers 256→512→256→128 with skip connections) to a final Softplus unit that predicts hydrogen uptake (mmol/g).

Training employs a three-phase curriculum: Phase 1 (50 epochs) optimizes only the mean squared error (data loss) with AdamW (η=1.2e-3). Phase 2 (250 epochs) linearly increases the weight of the physics loss (saturation, monotonicity via automatic differentiation, Van’t Hoff compliance) while annealing the learning rate to 5e-4. Phase 3 (100 epochs) activates all constraint losses with fixed weights and a lower learning rate (1e-4). The model ensemble comprises ten members with varied widths (0.75×, 1.0×, 1.25×) and depths (3–5 layers), and their predictions are calibrated using temperature scaling. This design yields accurate, physically consistent predictions and well-calibrated uncertainty estimates across heterogeneous lithologies.

## Key Results
* R²=0.9544, RMSE=0.0484 mmol/g, MAE=0.0231 mmol/g on held-out test; 98.6% monotonicity satisfaction; zero non-physical predictions.
* Classical isotherm models' R² dropped from 0.80–0.90 (sample-specific fits) to 0.09–0.38 on aggregated datasets.
* Leave-one-lithology-out cross-validation: PINN outperformed tuned random forest by 10–15% in R².

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Multi-scale feature extraction with physics-informed gating captures heterogeneous sorption physics while enforcing thermodynamic constraints, enabling accurate cross-lithology generalization.
- **Mechanism**: Input features are processed in parallel at three scales (64, 128, 256 neurons), concatenated, and modulated by a gating mechanism that uses pressure and temperature to emphasize thermodynamically relevant signals. The physics loss embeds saturation limits, monotonicity, and bounds directly into the loss function.
- **Core assumption**: Material properties (surface area, organic content, rank) parametrize the sorption function; thus a conditional model can learn sample-specific isotherms while sharing common physics.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "A seven-category physics-informed feature engineering scheme generates 62 thermodynamically meaningful descriptors... The loss function enforces saturation limits, a monotonic pressure response, and Van't Hoff temperature dependence..."
  - [section 2.2] Classical isotherm models fail on aggregated data (R² drops from 0.80–0.90 to 0.09–0.38), proving the need for conditional learning.
  - [corpus] A directly related neighbor paper ("Adaptive Physics-Informed Neural Networks with Multi-Category Feature Engineering for Hydrogen Sorption Prediction in Clays, Shales, and Coals") validates the same methodology, indicating community uptake.
- **Break condition**: Inaccurate or missing material property data (e.g., low surface area measurements, sparse micropore volume for coals) leads to poor conditioning; the network may then violate physical bounds or show high errors, as seen in coal performance degradation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Progressive curriculum training—separating data-driven warming from physics constraint integration—ensures stable convergence and prevents the physics loss from derailing early learning.
- **Mechanism**: Three phases: Phase 1 (50 epochs) trains on data loss only; Phase 2 (250 epochs) linearly ramps physics loss from 0 to 1 while lowering learning rate; Phase 3 (100 epochs) activates all constraints. The schedule avoids early dominance of physics gradients.
- **Core assumption**: The loss surfaces of data fitting and physics enforcement differ; starting with pure data fitting locates a good region before tightening constraints.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.6] "Training proceeds through three sequential phases implementing curriculum learning: (i) data-driven warmup... (ii) physics integration... (iii) full constraints."
  - [section 4.5.3] "Removing cosine annealing warmup cycles degraded performance by 1.4%... curriculum training proved essential."
  - [corpus] Limited direct Corpus evidence; the concept aligns with general PINN best practices noted in companion papers (e.g., "Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Real-Time Gas Crossover Prediction").
- **Break condition**: If the physics weight ramps too quickly (under ~150 epochs), the network may converge to a suboptimal state where neither data nor physics objectives are satisfied, resulting in higher constraint violation rates and lower R².

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Architecture-diverse ensemble learning yields calibrated uncertainty quantification and modest accuracy gains by avoiding the near-perfect correlation of dropout-only ensembles.
- **Mechanism**: Ten ensemble members vary in width (0.75×, 1.0×, 1.25×), depth (3–5 layers), and regularization. Predictions are aggregated (mean ± std) and temperature-scaled for calibration.
- **Core assumption**: Different topologies explore distinct regions of parameter space, producing epistemic diversity that purely stochastic (dropout) variations cannot achieve.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.6] "Ensemble of ten members... architecture-diverse... dropout-only ensemble produced near-perfect inter-member correlation (r=0.9994)... architecture-diverse reduced inter-member correlation to r=0.9980 and improved R² by +0.88%."
  - [section 4.5.4] "Dropout-only ensemble failed... architectural diversity is prerequisite for meaningful epistemic uncertainty quantification."
  - [corpus] Most neighbor works employ single PINNs; this ensemble approach is a distinctive contribution.
- **Break condition**: If ensemble members share identical architecture (e.g., dropout-only), prediction variance collapses (σ≈0.002), coverage rates deviate from nominal, and uncertainty estimates become overconfident.

- **Why needed**: Simultaneous optimization of data and physics losses is unstable; separating them prevents the model from satisfying constraints at the expense of accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input → Multi-scale extraction (parallel dense layers of 64, 128, 256 units) → Gating (sigmoid modulation from pressure/temperature) → Backbone (dense layers 256→512→256→128 with skip connections) → Output (Softplus)

### Critical path
Feature engineering → Multi-scale extraction → Gating → Compression backbone → Output → Automatic differentiation for monotonicity gradient → Combined loss → Backpropagation with gradient-magnitude-based weight adaptation.

### Design tradeoffs
- Depth vs. width: More layers (5) increase capacity but risk overfitting; chosen 4-layer backbone balances representational power with dataset size (1,330 training samples).
- Physics loss weighting: Hard constraints would damage accuracy; soft penalties with adaptive balancing preserve performance while encouraging compliance.
- Ensemble size: Ten members give good diversity and calibration but increase compute; smaller ensembles risk unreliable uncertainty estimates.

### Failure signatures
- High violation rates (negative predictions, exceeding qmax, non-monotonic curves) ⇒ physics loss weight too low or gating malfunction.
- Large gap between training and CV R² ⇒ overfitting; increase dropout or L2 weight.
- Poor coverage (prediction intervals far from nominal) ⇒ ensemble diversity insufficient or temperature scaling mis-calibrated.
- Disproportionate performance drop on minority lithologies (e.g., coals) when dropout >0.10 ⇒ over-regularization harming data-limited groups.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate classical isotherm failure**: Fit each sample with Sips model; then aggregate by lithology and refit. Expect R² collapse from >0.8 to <0.4, confirming the need for conditional modeling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does the framework generalize to competitive multicomponent sorption scenarios involving hydrogen and other gases?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors state that the framework is "directly extensible to competitive multicomponent sorption" (Abstract and Conclusion), but the current study only validates pure hydrogen sorption on single-component systems.
- **Why unresolved**: Multicomponent systems introduce competitive adsorption effects, non-linear isotherm shifts, and complex thermodynamic interactions (e.g., selectivity, displacement) that are not captured by the existing single-gas constraints and loss function.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Acquire and compile experimental datasets of mixed-gas adsorption (e.g., H₂-CO₂, H₂-CH₄) on geological samples; extend the PINN architecture to handle multiple adsorbates with appropriate competitive sorption constraints (e.g., extended Langmuir models); validate predictive accuracy and constraint satisfaction against measured multicomponent isotherms.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What are the specific microstructural or compositional factors causing persistent prediction errors in high-TOC shale samples, and how can the model incorporate multi-scale heterogeneity to improve accuracy?
- **Basis in paper**: Results indicate that shale predictions exhibit larger errors for samples with total organic carbon (TOC) exceeding 3 wt% (Section 4.5.1: "Larger errors in high-TOC samples (>3 wt%)").
- **Why unresolved**: The current feature set (bulk mineralogy, TOC, surface area) may not fully resolve intra-sample heterogeneity (e.g., organic matter distribution, pore network complexity), leading to under-coverage in uncertainty quantification for certain shale types.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Perform high-resolution characterization (e.g., FIB-SEM, X-ray nano-CT) on high-TOC shale samples to identify missing predictors (e.g., organic porosity connectivity, mineral OM associations); integrate these as additional inputs or hierarchical embeddings in the PINN; assess reduction in prediction error and calibration improvement for high-TOC subsets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can thermodynamic constraints be more robustly enforced for heterogeneous geological adsorbents, given the poor reliability of classical Van't Hoff analysis in extracting consistent temperature-dependent parameters?
- **Basis in paper**: Van't Hoff analysis of multi-temperature data yielded low mean R² (0.173 ± 0.294) and high uncertainty, indicating that equilibrium constants do not follow ideal temperature dependence across samples (Section 4.4.3).
- **Why unresolved**: The current "soft penalty" approach in the PINN loss function may be insufficient for materials with distributed adsorption energies; the failure of classical parameterization suggests that single-parameter thermodynamic descriptions are inadequate for heterogeneous systems.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop alternative constraint formulations (e.g., distribution-aware penalties, neural network parameterizations of temperature dependence that learn from multi-temperature data); compare constraint satisfaction (e.g., isosteric heat consistency) against high-fidelity molecular simulations; evaluate whether improved constraints enhance cross-temperature generalization without sacrificing accuracy.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: To what extent does the engineered feature set contain redundancy, and can a reduced, more interpretable feature subset maintain predictive performance for hydrogen sorption across diverse lithologies?
- **Basis in paper**: The feature engineering generated 62 descriptors with observed multicollinearity (e.g., among surface-area-derived features), and ensemble selection retained 50 features via voting, but correlation analysis suggests overlapping physical meanings (Section 3.4 and 4.4.2).
- **Why unresolved**: Over-engineered features may increase computational cost and risk overfitting while obscuring key causal relationships; a parsimonious model aligned with fundamental sorption physics would improve interpretability and deployment efficiency.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct systematic feature ablation or sparsity-inducing regularization (e.g., L1, group lasso) on the PINN; evaluate test performance, constraint satisfaction, and feature importance stability when features are pruned by category (e.g., removing kinetic or molecular sieving descriptors); validate whether a minimal feature set captures essential lithology-specific controls identified in correlation studies.
