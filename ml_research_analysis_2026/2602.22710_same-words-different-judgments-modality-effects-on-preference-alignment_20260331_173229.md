# Same Words, Different Judgments: Modality Effects on Preference Alignment

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2602.22710
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22710
* **Reference count:** 0
* **Primary result:** Audio-based preference annotation achieves reliability comparable to text (ICC(2,k) ≈ .80 at ~9 raters) but yields systematically narrower judgments, reduced length bias, and near-chance cross-modality agreement with text; synthetic ratings align with human judgments and can predict inter-rater agreement.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether the modality (text vs. audio) through which preference annotations are collected affects the reliability and nature of judgment in preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL). Using identical dialogue content from 100 prompts, the authors collected human and synthetic ratings in both text (static side-by-side) and audio (sequential playback) formats. Key findings reveal that while aggregate reliability (measured by ICC(2,k)) is similar across modalities—reaching good levels at approximately nine raters—the two modalities produce substantively different preference judgments. Audio annotators exhibit narrower decision thresholds, are less susceptible to length-based heuristics, and their judgments appear more user-oriented, with agreement between audio and text annotators approaching chance levels. Synthetic model ratings (from GPT-4o and GPT-4o-Audio) not only align with their respective human counterparts but also predict human inter-rater agreement, suggesting a potential role in triaging ambiguous annotation pairs or partially replacing human effort.

## Method Summary
The study used 100 prompts from the PRISM dataset's unguided conversations. Each prompt's two responses were converted to audio using a single TTS voice (Kokoro, `af heart`). Human annotators (N=94 total; 48 text, 46 audio) from Prolific rated response quality on a 1–100 visual analog scale. The text interface presented both responses simultaneously; the audio interface played them sequentially with a mandatory full first listen and optional replays. Behavioral data (timing, replays) were logged. Synthetic ratings were collected per modality using GPT-4o and GPT-4o-Audio-Preview. Primary analyses employed linear mixed-effects models to compute ICC(2,1) and ICC(2,k), Krippendorff’s α, and tested fixed effects (modality, response length, presentation order) with prompt- and rater-specific random effects. Cross-modality agreement was assessed by comparing modal winners across varying decisiveness thresholds.

## Key Results
* **Reliability:** Intraclass correlation for absolute agreement (ICC(2,k)) scales similarly for text and audio, reaching ~0.80 at approximately nine raters per stimulus, indicating good aggregate reliability.
* **Modality-specific judgment:** Audio raters show significantly narrower decision thresholds (smaller rating gaps on non-tie trials), reduced length bias (smaller positive effect of character count), and higher alignment with original user satisfaction ratings from the interactive PRISM setting.
* **Synthetic utility:** AI rating discriminability (absolute score difference per prompt) positively correlates with human inter-rater agreement (ICC) within the same modality, supporting synthetic ratings for identifying ambiguous pairs; AI-human absolute error is comparable to human-human error.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
* **Claim:** Audio preference annotation yields comparable aggregate reliability to text but with narrower decision thresholds.
* **Mechanism:** Sequential auditory presentation compresses the perceptual rating gap between preferred and non-preferred responses compared to text's simultaneous side-by-side view. The inability to visually gauge length or simultaneously compare reduces the salience of extreme judgments.
* **Core assumption:** Modality shapes the cognitive comparison process; sequential intake constrains retrospective/prospective evaluation permitted by static text display.
* **Evidence anchors:** Mann–Whitney U on per-prompt rating gap (non-tie): Audio M=27.9 vs Text M=41.7, p<.001, r=.303.
* **Break condition:** If audio stimuli vary widely in acoustic quality or speaker traits beyond neutral TTS, the compressed threshold effect may dissipate or reverse due to increased noise/affect.

### Mechanism 2
* **Claim:** Modality differentially reduces susceptibility to length bias in preference judgments.
* **Mechanism:** The fixed, linear progression of audio prohibits rapid visual scanning, making length a less accessible heuristic. Audio listeners must attend to content sequentially, weakening the verbosity-quality association.
* **Core assumption:** Length bias in text is partly a visual-spatial heuristic; removing visual access attenuates it.
* **Evidence anchors:** Linear mixed-effects model: Modality×Character length interaction b=1.53, p=.045; text effect b=4.91 vs audio b=3.52 (both p<.01).
* **Break condition:** If audio clips are artificially truncated/expanded without semantic adjustment, or if listeners can scrub/seek, the length-heuristic barrier could break down.

### Mechanism 3
* **Claim:** Synthetic (AI) rating discriminability predicts human inter-rater reliability (ICC) across modalities.
* **Mechanism:** AI models producing divergent scores operate on evaluative signals that correlate with factors causing human consensus; ambiguous AI scores flag prompts where human judgments are inherently variable.
* **Core assumption:** Variance in human preferences is partially structured by semantic/content features that both humans and capable AI models detect; AI disagreement reflects true ambiguity, not random noise.
* **Evidence anchors:** Linear regression: AI differentiation predicting human ICC, B=0.014, p<.001; Spearman r=.23 (audio) and r=.38 (text), both p<.05.
* **Break condition:** If AI models are primarily text-trained and not fine-tuned for audio-specific criteria, discriminability may fail to predict audio-human ICC; relationship may be modality-specific.

## Foundational Learning
* **Concept:** Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC(2,k))
  * **Why needed:** Quantifies how agreement scales with annotator pool size; core reliability finding hinges on ICC(2,k)≈.80 at ~9 raters, moving beyond pairwise agreement percentages.
  * **Quick check:** What does an ICC(2,k) of .80 at k=9 imply about the cost-benefit of adding a 10th rater?
* **Concept:** Cross-modality agreement vs. within-modality reliability
  * **Why needed:** Paper finds high within-modality ICC but near-chance cross-modality agreement, decoupling reliability from generalizability across sensory modalities.
  * **Quick check:** Can a dataset be statistically reliable (high ICC) yet invalid for a different deployment modality? Why?
* **Concept:** Recency and length biases in sequential presentation
  * **Why needed:** Audio forces sequential presentation, inheriting recency bias and altering length bias; understanding these biases is essential to interpret the modality effect.
  * **Quick check:** How does counterbalancing presentation order across raters mitigate recency bias at the aggregate level but inflate disagreement at the individual level?

## Architecture Onboarding
Stimulus Conversion Pipeline -> Annotation Interface -> Synthetic Rating Engine -> Reliability Analytics -> Bias & Effect Models
Critical path: Stimulus Conversion → Human+AI Annotation → Aggregation (mean per clip) → ICC Calculation → Cross-Modality Agreement → Bias Analysis → AI-Human Alignment.
Design tradeoffs:
* *Cost vs. Reliability*: ~9 raters needed for good ICC, but synthetic pre-screening can triage ambiguous prompts, reducing total annotations.
* *Fidelity vs. Generalizability*: Single TTS voice controls acoustic confounds but limits generalizability to diverse speakers.
* *Sequential vs. Simultaneous*: Audio forces sequential presentation, introducing recency bias but enabling logging of replay/deliberation behaviors not accessible in text.
Failure signatures:
* ICC(2,1) remains poor (<.3) even with >5 raters → stimulus set too ambiguous or annotation guide flawed.
* Cross-modality agreement near 50% *and* prompt-specific random slopes non-significant → modality effects negligible or overpowered by noise.
* AI-human absolute error significantly higher in audio → audio model (GPT-4o-Audio) not aligned with human audio preferences or TTS quality issues.
First 3 experiments:
1. *Replicate ICC scaling*: Compute ICC(2,k) curves for your own modality (text/audio) with 5–15 raters per stimulus; confirm threshold for "good" agreement.
2. *Decision threshold test*: For non-tie trials, compare distribution of |rating_A - rating_B| between modalities; expect median gap ~20-30 (audio) vs ~30-40 (text).
3. *Synthetic triage pilot*: For a held-out set, compute AI rating difference per prompt; split prompts into "high AI diff" (top tercile) and "low AI diff"; run human ICC within each split; expect significantly higher ICC in high-diff group.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
* **Question 1:** Do the observed modality differences in preference judgments (e.g., narrower decision thresholds, reduced length bias) replicate when using audio-native datasets with natural paralinguistic features (e.g., emotional prosody, background noise) instead of TTS-generated speech?
  * **Basis:** [explicit] The authors state: "TTS-rendered speech lacks paralinguistic nuances... that may influence preference judgments in real-world audio interactions. Future work should investigate whether these findings replicate with audio-native datasets..."
* **Question 2:** How does variability in speaker identity (e.g., gender, accent, pitch) interact with semantic content to influence inter-rater agreement and modality-specific biases in audio preference judgments?
  * **Basis:** [inferred] The authors used a single static TTS voice ("af heart") to control for confounds, noting: "this limits our ability to generalize findings across different speaker identities. We do not assess how variable voice characteristics... might interact with semantic content to influence annotator preference or agreement rates."
* **Question 3:** Does audio-based preference annotation by external raters reliably approximate user satisfaction in real-time conversational interactions, as hinted by the non-significant alignment with original PRISM ratings?
  * **Basis:** [explicit] The authors note: "human-audio ratings aligned most closely with the original PRISM ratings... The non-significant result... precludes strong claims, but it is suggestive... audio-based annotation pipelines may better approximate real user satisfaction than text-based alternatives—though this finding requires replication..."
* **Question 4:** Can the reduced length bias observed in audio modality be harnessed to train less biased reward models for speech PbRL, and does this come at the cost of reduced annotation efficiency?
  * **Basis:** [inferred] The Discussion speculates: "audio-derived reward signals may produce less biased reward models than their text counterparts" and notes the "roughly 2× increase in annotation time per trial." However, no downstream reward model training or bias quantification is performed.

## Limitations
* Generalizability is limited: single TTS voice (`af heart`) and US crowdworkers may not capture diverse audio contexts or cultural biases.
* Synthetic rating setup is underspecified; exact prompts/temperature for GPT-4o/Audio are unreported, risking misalignment with paper’s conditions.
* Manual audio quality review lacks inter-rater reliability reporting; unreported subjectivity could affect stimulus set composition.

## Confidence
* **Reliability equivalence (audio vs. text ICC~0.80 at k≈9):** High. Backed by mixed-effects ICC(2,k) scaling with bootstrap CIs.
* **Narrower audio decision thresholds & reduced length bias:** Medium. Statistical anchors (Mann–Whitney, LMM interaction) are solid, but causal mechanism (sequential vs. simultaneous) is inferred, not manipulated.
* **Synthetic ratings predict human ICC and can triage:** Low. Correlation exists (r=.23–.38) but predictive utility for triaging ambiguous prompts is not experimentally validated; model version drift risk is high.
