# Countering Catastrophic Forgetting of Large Language Models for Better Instruction Following via Weight-Space Model Merging

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01538
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01538
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Merged models mitigated forgetting, e.g., SLERP (t=0.4) improved medical benchmark accuracy by 1.43% over the clinical model while SLERP (t=0.7) preserved 98.3% of the instruct model's IFEval score.

## Executive Summary
Fine-tuning large language models on medical data causes catastrophic forgetting of instruction-following ability, hindering clinical deployment. This paper addresses the problem by merging a clinical foundation model (GatorTronLlama) with a general instruct model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) using weight-space interpolation (Linear/SLERP), without gradient updates. The merged models combine clinical knowledge with preserved instruction-following, achieving better zero-shot medical accuracy, maintaining high IFEval scores, and demonstrating superior data efficiency in downstream clinical tasks—matching the performance of the clinical model fine-tuned on four times more data.

## Method Summary
The method merges two pre-trained models via parameter-space interpolation. Given a clinical model (CPT on medical text) and an instruction-tuned model (RLHF for following user prompts), the authors apply either linear interpolation (weighted average) or spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) across all layers at a mixing ratio *t* ∈ [0,1]. This generates a new checkpoint that inherits clinical semantics from the first and instruction alignment from the second. The merged checkpoints are evaluated zero-shot on medical benchmarks and IFEval. Pareto-optimal merge ratios are then selected for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on downstream clinical generation tasks (e.g., radiology summarization, discharge instruction generation), with consistent training hyperparameters across all initializations.

## Key Results
- SLERP (t=0.4) improved zero-shot medical benchmark accuracy by 1.43% over the GatorTronLlama clinical model.
- SLERP (t=0.7) preserved 98.3% of the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's IFEval score.
- After SFT, the SLERP-merged model achieved the highest composite scores across five clinical generation tasks.
- On radiology summarization, the merged model fine-tuned with only 64 examples matched the performance of the clinical model fine-tuned with 256 examples.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Disentangled Subspace Preservation
Weight-space merging mitigates catastrophic forgetting by combining parameters without gradient updates, preserving instruction-following circuits from the instruct model while injecting clinical knowledge from the foundation model. The core assumption is that instruction-following capability resides in a relatively disentangled subspace of the weights, so convex combinations do not destroy it entirely. Evidence: SLERP(t=0.7) recovered 98.3% of IFEval score while improving medical accuracy at t=0.4. Break condition: if weight spaces are orthogonal or highly conflicted, interpolation could degrade both capabilities.

### Mechanism 2: Non-Monotonic Trade-off
The performance trade-off between clinical knowledge and instruction-following is non-monotonic across interpolation ratios. Clinical performance initially increases with added instruct weight (due to better task representation alignment) but declines after a threshold as instruct weights dilute domain-specific features. Instruction-following improves monotonically but saturates. This creates a Pareto frontier with a sweet spot for combined utility. Evidence: Figure 3 shows clinical scores peaking around t=0.4–0.6 while instruction scores plateau. Break condition: if both knowledge types are highly entangled, mixing would yield strictly monotonic degradation.

### Mechanism 3: Superior Initialization for SFT
Merged models provide a better starting point for downstream SFT, accelerating adaptation to new clinical tasks. The merged weights already encode a shared representation aligning clinical semantics with instruction format, reducing the optimization steps needed compared to a purely clinical or instruct-only base. Evidence: SLERP-merged model fine-tuned on 64 examples matched the clinical model fine-tuned on 256 examples. Break condition: suboptimal merge ratios (e.g., too much instruct weight) may misalign the initialization with the clinical task distribution, requiring more data.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
GatorTronLlama (clinical) + Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (instruct) -> mergekit (Linear/SLERP) -> merged checkpoints -> zero-shot evaluation (clinical benchmarks + IFEval) -> Pareto frontier selection -> downstream SFT on clinical tasks -> final evaluation.

**Critical Path**  
1. Precompute and cache base model weights (FP16/BF16).  
2. Run merge grid (t ∈ [0,1] step 0.1) → generate 11 merged checkpoints per method.  
3. Zero-shot eval on clinical + IFEval → plot Pareto frontier.  
4. Select 2–3 frontier recipes (e.g., best clinical, best instruction retention) for downstream SFT.  
5. Run few-shot learning curves (8–512 examples) on 1–2 key tasks to validate data efficiency.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Merge ratio (t): Higher t improves instruction-following but risks diluting clinical expertise; requires task-dependent tuning.  
- Interpolation method: SLERP may preserve norms better, reducing degeneration at extreme t, but is slightly more compute than Linear.  
- Evaluation granularity: Using a single composite score may mask task-specific failures; must inspect per-task tables.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Zero-shot: Merged model significantly underperforms both parents on either clinical or instruction metrics → likely weight space conflict; try smaller t or layer-wise merging.  
- Post-SFT: Merged model shows no data efficiency gain (learning curve identical to clinical base) → merge ratio may be too low (insufficient instruct prior) or task distribution far from both parents.  
- SLERP produces NaNs: Usually due to zero-norm vectors in one model’s layer; enable epsilon or fallback to Linear for that layer.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. Replicate Figure 3: For Linear and SLERP, sweep t∈[0,1] step 0.1, evaluate Medical Avg and IFEval. Verify non-monotonic clinical curve and identify peak.  
2. Few-shot IU-Xray: Train SLERP(t=0.4) and GatorTronLlama on {8,16,32,64,128} examples. Confirm SLERP matches/beats base at 64-shot.  
3. Post-SFT catastrophic forgetting check: After full SFT on MIMIC-BHC, re-evaluate IFEval. Expect merged model to retain >90% of pre-SFT instruction score, while clinical base may drop further.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Clinical Safety Evaluation**: How do merged models compare to baselines in terms of factual correctness, hallucination rates, and clinical safety when evaluated by clinicians using structured adjudication? The current study relies on automatic metrics, leaving safety concerns unaddressed.  
- **Broader Parameter Exploration**: Does a more extensive exploration of merge parameters (finer weights, alternative merging methods) improve the Pareto frontier between clinical knowledge and instruction-following? The grid search was constrained (step 0.1) and may have missed optimal configurations.  
- **Scalability to Larger Models**: Are the benefits consistent when applied to larger-scale LLMs (e.g., >8B parameters) in clinical domains? Scaling laws might alter weight-space geometries and interpolation effects.

## Limitations
- The study uses a specific model pair (GatorTronLlama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and medical domain; generalization beyond these is unclear.
- The core "disentangled subspace" assumption is not directly validated—no neuron/layer-wise analysis confirms instruction-following circuits remain intact.
- The optimal merge ratio (t) appears task-dependent, requiring per-deployment tuning; computational costs for sweeping multiple ratios are not reported.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Disentanglement): Medium. Supported by preservation metrics and related work, but lacks ablations on weight subspace overlap.
- Mechanism 2 (Non-monotonic Trade-off): High. Directly evidenced by Figure 3 and Table 2 across both interpolation methods.
- Mechanism 3 (SFT Data Efficiency): High. Strong empirical support from Figure 4 learning curves and composite task scores.
