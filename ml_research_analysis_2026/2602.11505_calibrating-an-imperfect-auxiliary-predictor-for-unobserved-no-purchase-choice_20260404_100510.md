# Calibrating an Imperfect Auxiliary Predictor for Unobserved No-Purchase Choice
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11505  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11505  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Calibrating an imperfect auxiliary predictor using linear regression (affine) or maximum rank correlation (nearly monotone) consistently recovers unobserved no-purchase probabilities from purchase-only data, improving assortment optimization and prediction accuracy (Expedia NLL 1.7278 → 0.3347).

## Executive Summary
Firms often only observe transactional purchases, yet estimating the probability of the outside option (no purchase) is crucial for assortment optimization. This paper tackles the challenge by leveraging an imperfect black-box auxiliary predictor that estimates outside probabilities but is biased. By exploiting the structural form of the multinomial logit model—where the logit of the outside option equals γᵀz − s—the authors develop two calibration methods. Under an affine miscalibration assumption, a simple linear regression of the predictor’s logits on the inclusive value ŝ and context features z consistently recovers γ. For more general predictors that are nearly monotone, they maximize a smooth rank correlation objective to align the predictor with the true ordering, yielding consistent estimation. Theoretical finite‑sample bounds separate predictor quality from utility‑learning error. Synthetic experiments validate the theory, and a real‑world Expedia study shows dramatic reductions in negative log‑likelihood (1.7278→0.3347) and calibration error (0.2730→0.0464) using the MRC approach. The work also extends to aggregating multiple heterogeneous predictors.

## Method Summary
The paper formulates the problem of recovering unobserved no‑purchase probabilities using only purchase‑only transaction data and a biased auxiliary predictor. The key setting follows the multinomial logit (MNL) model: for a context X and assortment S, the probability of choosing the outside option is  

p0(X,S) = exp(γᵀz(X)) / (exp(γᵀz(X)) + Σ_{i∈S} exp(u_i(X))),  

where u_i are inside utilities and γ captures the effect of context features z on the outside utility. Taking logarithms yields logit(p0) = γᵀz − s(X,S), with s = log Σ_i exp(u_i) (the inclusive value). Importantly, s can be estimated from purchase data via conditional MNL (the inside utilities are identified up to an additive constant that cancels when computing s). Given an auxiliary predictor that outputs ˜p0 (hence y = logit(˜p0)), we aim to recover γ.

Two calibration algorithms are proposed. **Algorithm 1 (Linear Calibration)** assumes the bias in y is affine in z and s: y = a + bᵀz + c·s + ε. Using the purchase data to compute ŝ and extract z, we regress y on [1, z, ŝ] via (ridge‑stabilized) OLS to obtain coefficient estimates θ̂. The relationship between θ and the true γ is γ̂ = θ̂_z / (−θ̂_s); this is consistent under the affine assumption. **Algorithm 2 (MRC Calibration)** drops the affine assumption and instead relies on a weaker “nearly monotone” condition: the predictor preserves the ordering of true logits for most pairs. The method maximizes a smooth surrogate of Kendall’s rank correlation between y and the linear predictor θᵀw, w = [z, ŝ]. The maximizer θ̂ satisfies γ̂ = −θ̂_z / θ̂_s (after normalizing by θ̂_s). For multiple auxiliary predictors, the sign information from each can be aggregated, e.g., by taking a weighted average of the estimated γ’s or using a median of signs.

Calibrated γ̂ then yields an estimate p̂0 = logistic(γ̂ᵀz − ŝ), which can be plugged into assortment optimization (e.g., revenue maximization under MNL) to improve decision making. Theoretical results provide finite‑sample error bounds that decompose into a term capturing predictor quality (max bias for linear, ρ₀ for MRC) and the error from utility estimation.

## Key Results
- Synthetic experiments confirm that both calibration methods significantly improve no‑purchase probability estimation and reduce revenue suboptimality in assortment optimization.
- In the Expedia case study, MRC calibration (with neural utilities) reduces negative log‑likelihood from 1.7278 to 0.3347 and expected calibration error from 0.2730 to 0.0464 compared to the raw biased predictor.
- Robust multi‑predictor aggregation techniques enable combining heterogeneous or even adversarial sources without sacrificing calibration performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The calibration relies on the structural identity from the MNL model: logit(p0) = γᵀz(X) − s(X,S) + ε. The inclusive value s is a function of the inside utilities only and can be estimated from purchase data using standard conditional logit (MNL) up to an additive constant, because the constant cancels when considering differences. With an auxiliary predictor that delivers a noisy estimate of the logit, we can recover γ by exploiting the linear relationship in (γ,−1) with regressors [z, s]. If the predictor’s miscalibration is affine in these regressors, ordinary least squares yields a consistent estimate of γ. For more general predictors that merely preserve the ordering of true logits (nearly monotone), we instead maximize the rank correlation between the predictor logits and the linear combination γᵀz−ŝ; this recovers γ up to scale, which is then normalized using the implicit scale of ŝ. The finite‑sample analysis separates the error due to the predictor’s alignment (measured by max bias or ρ₀) from the error in estimating the inclusive values, explaining why calibration can succeed even with biased predictors.

  *Why needed*: Provides the structural identity logit(p0)=γᵀz−s, linking the unobserved outside utility to the inclusive value s.  
  *Quick check*: Derive the expression for the outside option probability from the MNL log-sum formula and verify the additive form.
- **Auxiliary Predictor**:  
  *Why needed*: Supplies estimates of outside probabilities in the absence of no‑purchase labels, albeit biased.  
  *Quick check*: Explain why the predictor’s bias must be structured (e.g., affine or monotone) for identification from purchase data alone.
- **Inclusive Value (s)**:  
  *Why needed*: Captures the overall attractiveness of the assortment and appears subtractively in the outside logit.  
  *Quick check*: Given a set of item utilities u_i, compute s = log Σ_i exp(u_i) and show how it changes if all utilities are shifted by a constant.
- **Maximum Rank Correlation (MRC)**:  
  *Why needed*: A non‑parametric calibration approach that works when the predictor is only approximately monotone in the true logits.  
  *Quick check*: Show that maximizing Kendall’s τ is equivalent to maximizing the number of concordant pairs in a dataset.
- **Finite‑Sample Bounds**:  
  *Why needed*: Quantify the error in γ̂ as a sum of predictor‑quality terms and utility‑estimation error, guiding method selection.  
  *Quick check*: Identify the role of the “max bias” b* (for linear) and the “monotonicity” ρ₀ (for MRC) in the error bound.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Transaction Data -> Utility Learner -> Inclusive Value (ŝ) & Inside Utilities (û_i)  
Transaction Data -> Feature Extractor -> z  
Auxiliary Predictor -> y = logit(˜p0)  
ŝ, z, y -> Calibrator (Linear / MRC) -> γ̂  
γ̂, z, ŝ -> Probability Estimator -> p̂0  
p̂0, û_i -> Assortment Optimizer -> Optimal Assortment  

### Critical Path
1. **Utility Learner** fits a conditional MNL to the booked sessions to obtain `û_i(X)` (up to an additive constant).  
2. **Inclusive Value Calculator** computes `ŝ(X,S) = log Σ_{i∈S} exp(û_i(X))`.  
3. **Feature Extractor** derives context features `z(X)` from `X`.  
4. **Auxiliary Predictor** (pre‑trained) outputs `y = logit(˜p0(X,S))`.  
5. **Calibrator** (either Linear Regression or MRC optimization) uses `(y, z, ŝ)` to estimate `γ̂`.  
6. **Probability Estimator** forms `p̂0 = logistic(γ̂ᵀz - ŝ)`.  
7. **Assortment Optimizer** plugs `p̂0` and `û_i` into the MNL revenue maximization to select the best assortment.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Linear vs. MRC Calibration**: Linear is convex, fast, and has simple finite‑sample guarantees but requires the affine bias assumption; MRC is more flexible (nearly monotone predictor) but involves non‑convex optimization (L‑BFGS with subsampling) and may be sensitive to initialization and subsample size.
- **Utility Model Complexity**: Linear utilities are interpretable and stable; neural utilities can capture nonlinear interactions but risk overfitting, especially with limited bookings. The choice affects the quality of `ŝ` and thus calibration error.
- **Single vs. Multi‑Predictor**: Using a single predictor is simple; aggregating multiple predictors (via weighted sign averages or median) improves robustness to heterogeneous biases but introduces aggregation hyperparameters and potential correlation issues.

### Failure Signatures
- **Linear calibration**: Residual plots of the regression show strong nonlinear patterns; out‑of‑sample NLL/ECE are substantially worse than raw predictor.
- **MRC calibration**: L‑BFGS fails to converge (large gradient norm) or converges to a solution with small |θ̂_s|, leading to unstable `γ̂`; performance degrades dramatically when the empirical rank correlation between `y` and true `η` is low (≈0).
- **Utility estimation**: If the number of bookings is too small, the estimated inside utilities become noisy, causing `ŝ` to be inaccurate; both calibration methods then produce biased `γ̂`.
- **Auxiliary predictor**: If the predictor’s monotonicity (ρ₀) is below a critical threshold, MRC fails to improve over the raw predictor; calibration error may even increase.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Does the calibration approach generalize to non-MNL choice models (e.g., nested logit)?
- **Basis**: [explicit] Lemma 1 gives η = u0 - s, which is used throughout. This decomposition is specific to MNL.
- **Why unresolved**: Other models lack such a simple additive link, so identification and bias correction via linear regression or MRC may fail.
- **Evidence**: Derive analogous identities for broader families; prove consistency; test robustness with non-MNL simulations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How to empirically verify the nearly monotone condition (Assumption 5) without ground-truth no-purchase data?
- **Basis**: [explicit] MRC guarantees require that the predictor preserves logit ordering for most pairs (small ρ0, δ0). This condition is not directly testable when η is unobserved.
- **Why unresolved**: Practitioners have no diagnostic to assess whether a given predictor is sufficiently aligned. Without this, they cannot trust MRC calibration.
- **Evidence**: Develop validation tools using side information (e.g., a small external dataset with outside choices) or robust calibration that automatically detects and mitigates violations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the optimal aggregation rule for multiple predictors in MRC, and when do the proposed heuristics (weighted average, median) achieve near-optimal performance?
- **Basis**: [explicit] Section 7.2 presents two ad-hoc aggregation strategies but offers no theoretical justification.
- **Why unresolved**: Unknown whether they are minimax optimal or how they behave under correlated biases, adversarial predictors, or varying noise levels.
- **Evidence**: Model multi-predictor sign generation statistically; derive optimal estimator; prove finite-sample bounds; compare heuristics in extensive simulations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can the non-convex MRC optimization be solved reliably at scale, and do approximate solutions still satisfy the finite-sample error bounds?
- **Basis**: [explicit] The MRC objective is non-smooth and non-convex. The analysis assumes the global maximizer is used. For large n, iterative heuristics are employed without optimality guarantees.
- **Why unresolved**: Practical implementations may converge to suboptimal points, potentially violating the bounds. The theory does not account for optimization errors.
- **Evidence**: Analyze the geometry of the MRC criterion to identify conditions for convexity or benign local minima; design scalable algorithms with certificates; empirically study convergence behavior on large real datasets.

## Limitations
Assumptions of affine or nearly monotone bias may be violated in real-world predictors, leading to biased recovery of outside utilities.  
Real-world findings on Expedia cannot be precisely reproduced without access to the exact dataset and preprocessing pipeline; even minor deviations can alter results.  
MRC calibration’s non-convex optimization with subsampling introduces sensitivity to implementation choices and may converge to suboptimal solutions in practice.
