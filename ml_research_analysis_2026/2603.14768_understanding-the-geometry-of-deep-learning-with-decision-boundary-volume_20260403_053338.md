# Understanding the geometry of deep learning with decision boundary volume

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.14768
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14768
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Decision boundary volume, estimated via Weyl’s tube formula and FGSM, serves as a geometric complexity measure; for convolutional networks smaller volumes correlate with higher test accuracy, while for fully connected networks the relationship is less stable.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a theoretically grounded method to measure the complexity of neural network decision boundaries by estimating the volume of an ε‑neighbourhood of the boundary. Leveraging Weyl’s tube formula, the volume is approximated as proportional to the boundary’s surface area for small ε. The estimation uses Monte Carlo sampling combined with fast gradient sign (FGSM) attacks to efficiently compute whether a sampled point lies within the tube. Three metrics are defined: overall boundary volume (Bvol), volume around training data (TrainBvol), and volume within gaps between classes (LAdvBvol). Experiments on MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST and CIFAR‑10 show that for convolutional architectures, TrainBvol and LAdvBvol are inversely correlated with test accuracy, indicating that smoother boundaries generalize better. For fully connected networks, the correlation is less consistent, though optimal accuracy still aligns with critical points in the volume curves. The approach remains efficient even in high dimensions due to concentration of measure effects.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins by training a neural network with a chosen architecture and dropout rate. For each model, three regions of interest are defined: the entire input space, a union of δ‑balls centred on training points, and a union of δ‑balls centred on points found by bisecting line segments between training examples of different classes (linear adversarial points). For each region, a large number of points are uniformly sampled. Each sampled point is perturbed using the fast gradient sign method (FGSM) with a small ε to produce an adversarial example. If the adversary changes the network’s prediction, the original point is considered to lie within the ε‑tube of the decision boundary. The fraction of such successes, multiplied by the region’s volume (assumed 1 for the hypercube), yields the volume estimate. The same process is repeated over multiple seeds and dropout probabilities to study the relationship between boundary volume and generalization. The method’s efficiency stems from the linear regime of Weyl’s formula and from concentration of measure, which justifies the use of ℓ∞‑bounded FGSM as a proxy for ℓ₂ distance.

## Key Results
- Proposed a Monte Carlo method based on Weyl’s tube formula and FGSM to estimate decision boundary volume in high dimensions.
- For convolutional networks on MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST and CIFAR‑10, smaller TrainBvol and LAdvBvol strongly correlate with higher test accuracy; for fully connected networks the correlation is weaker, but peak accuracy still occurs near critical points of the volume curves.
- The estimation scales well to high‑dimensional inputs due to concentration of measure, which also justifies the ℓ∞‑norm attack.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Weyl’s tube formula yields a linear approximation
The volume of an ε‑tube around a codimension‑1 manifold is given by Weyl’s polynomial, whose lowest‑degree term is linear in ε and equals ε multiplied by the manifold’s surface volume. For sufficiently small ε, higher‑order curvature terms become negligible, so the tube volume serves as an efficient proxy for the true boundary volume. This holds under the assumption that the decision boundary is a smooth (or piecewise linear approximable) manifold and the tube does not self‑intersect. Evidence appears in Section 4.2 and the abstract’s claim of theoretical justification. The approximation breaks down if ε is too large or if the boundary is highly fragmented.

### Mechanism 2: Concentration of measure enables efficient ℓ∞‑based estimation
In high‑dimensional spaces, random vectors are nearly orthogonal and distances concentrate sharply. This implies that overlapping sampling cubes become negligible and that the ratio between ℓ₂ and ℓ∞ distances to a random hyperplane concentrates around √(2/π). Since the decision boundary is locally flat near data points, the ℓ∞ distance obtained by FGSM approximates the true ℓ₂ distance up to a constant factor, making the fast attack a reliable estimator. This mechanism relies on high dimensionality and the existence of adversarial examples; it may fail in low dimensions or with vanishing gradients.

### Mechanism 3: Smoother boundaries indicate better generalization
The volume of the decision boundary reflects model complexity: a smaller ε‑tube volume corresponds to a smoother boundary, which is expected to generalize better. The paper measures local surface volumes in two informative regions—around training points (TrainBvol) and in inter‑class gaps (LAdvBvol)—and observes that for convolutional architectures these volumes indeed decrease as test accuracy increases. Dropout regularisation typically increases LAdvBvol while TrainBvol exhibits a clear minimum near optimal accuracy. For fully connected networks the relationship is less stable, suggesting that the volume‑generalisation link depends on architecture‑data alignment.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Trained network -> Sampler -> Attack engine -> Distance calculator -> Volume estimator -> Parameter tuner

**Critical path**  
1. Train network with dropout.  
2. Choose region U (full space, train‑point δ‑balls, or linear adversarial δ‑balls).  
3. Sample points uniformly from U.  
4. For each point, run FGSM to get an adversarial example.  
5. Compute ℓ∞ distance between point and adversary.  
6. Count whether the adversary changed the predicted class (hit).  
7. Repeat for many samples; estimate volume as hit fraction.  
8. For LAdvBvol, first generate linear adversarial points via bisection before step 2.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- ε must be small enough for linear regime but large enough to yield measurable hit rates.  
- δ must be smaller than minimal inter‑class distance to avoid overlapping δ‑balls, yet large enough to contain adversarial examples.  
- Monte Carlo sample size trades off between computational cost and variance (10⁵ used).  
- FGSM is fast but may fail with vanishing gradients; stronger attacks increase accuracy at higher cost.  
- ℓ∞ norm is used, justified by concentration; however, boundary curvature could misalign gradients.

**Failure signatures**  
- Near‑zero hit rate: ε too small, attack ineffective, or region far from boundary.  
- High variance across random seeds: insufficient samples; increase Monte Carlo count.  
- Non‑smooth volume curves vs. ε: curvature dominates; decrease ε.  
- Overlapping δ‑balls cause sudden jumps in volume; reduce δ.  
- For FC networks, erratic patterns may be inherent or due to instability; verify with more seeds.

**First three experiments**  
1. Train a small network on a 2‑D synthetic dataset with a known smooth boundary (e.g., concentric circles), compute estimated volume, and compare to analytic surface length.  
2. For a fixed CNN on MNIST, vary ε over a range and plot volume estimates to ensure smooth curves and consistent rankings; also vary δ to detect overlap effects.  
3. Replicate the dropout trends: train CNNs and FC nets on MNIST with dropout p∈{0,…,0.5} (10 seeds each), compute TrainBvol and LAdvBvol, and verify that CNN accuracy peaks align with minima in TrainBvol while FC patterns are less stable.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What explains the unstable correlation between decision boundary volume metrics and test accuracy for fully connected networks?** The paper notes the relationship varies across datasets and optimisation procedures but offers no analysis.
2. **How can decision boundary volume be measured reliably far from training data where FGSM fails due to vanishing gradients?** The authors suggest using different adversarial attack methods but leave this unexplored.
3. **Does the piecewise linear nature of ReLU decision boundaries invalidate Weyl’s tube formula assumptions, and how does this affect volume measurement accuracy?** The paper relies on a smooth approximation via Whitehead but does not verify its empirical accuracy.
4. **Is the inverse relationship between decision boundary volume and generalization robust across other regularizers (weight decay, batch norm) and modern architectures (ResNet, ViT)?** The study is limited to dropout and simple networks.

## Limitations
- The method assumes a smooth codimension‑1 manifold, but ReLU networks yield piecewise linear boundaries with possible kinks, questioning the validity of Weyl’s linear approximation.
- FGSM provides only an upper bound on ℓ∞ distance and can be biased by vanishing gradients; the paper does not quantify this error.
- Overlap of δ‑balls around dense training clusters may inflate volume estimates, especially in lower‑dimensional manifolds.

## Confidence
- Method correctness (Weyl + FGSM): Medium
- Volume–accuracy correlation (CNNs): Medium
- Volume–accuracy correlation (FC): Low
- Scalability and robustness: Low
