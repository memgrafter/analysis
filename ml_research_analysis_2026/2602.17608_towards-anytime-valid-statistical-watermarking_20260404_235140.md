# Towards Anytime-Valid Statistical Watermarking

## Quick Facts
- arXiv: 2602.17608
- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17608
- References: 40
- Primary result: Anchored E‑Watermarking reduces the median number of detection tokens by 13–15% on the MarkMyWords benchmark (e.g., 72 vs. 145) while maintaining generation quality comparable to state‑of‑the‑art methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Anchored E‑Watermarking, the first e‑value‑based framework for statistical watermarking of large language model outputs that supports anytime‑valid inference. By constructing a test supermartingale, it guarantees strict Type‑I error control even when the detector stops voluntarily. The method uses an anchor distribution as a prior estimate of the target distribution and derives the optimal e‑value by maximizing a worst‑case log‑growth rate. This yields a closed‑form solution that achieves the information‑theoretic lower bound on expected stopping time, scaling as log(1/α) divided by a quantity J* depending on anchor entropy and robustness radius δ. Empirical evaluation on the MarkMyWords benchmark shows up to 15% reduction in required detection tokens compared to prior baselines, without compromising text quality.

## Method Summary
Anchored E‑Watermarking operates in two phases. During generation, the system samples a seed s from the anchor distribution p0 and then draws a token v from the target distribution q via a maximal coupling (e.g., speculative decoding) that preserves the marginal distribution of q while creating detectable dependence on seeds. The detector, given a pre‑shared key that governs seed generation, computes a fixed e‑value e(v,s) for each token‑seed pair. This e‑value is constructed so that under any target distribution within a δ‑ball around p0, the process (E_t) where E_t = exp(∑_{i=1}^t log e(v_i,s_i)) is a supermartingale. The detector accumulates the log‑wealth and stops as soon as it exceeds log(1/α), outputting “watermarked”. The optimal e‑value, solving a robust max‑min problem, has a simple piecewise form that assigns a high value when v=s and a low value otherwise. The result is that the expected stopping time under the worst‑case target behaves asymptotically as log(1/α)/J*, where J* = H(p0) + (1−δ/2)log(1−δ/2) + (δ/2)log(δ/(2(n−1))). This provides a principled trade‑off between robustness (δ) and sample efficiency.

## Key Results
- The framework achieves a 13–15% reduction in the median number of tokens required for detection (e.g., 72 vs. 145 for the best baseline) on the MarkMyWords benchmark.
- Generation quality remains comparable to state‑of‑the‑art watermarking methods, preserving perceptual and linguistic metrics.
- Theoretical analysis shows the expected stopping time scales as log(1/α)/J* with J* defined by anchor entropy and robustness radius, establishing an information‑theoretic limit for sequential watermark detection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Anytime‑valid detection via test supermartingales
- **Claim**: The detector can stop at any time while rigorously controlling the false positive rate.
- **Mechanism**: The e‑value e(v,s) is chosen so that the wealth process M_t = ∏_{i=1}^t e(v_i,s_i) is a supermartingale under the null hypothesis (tokens independent of seeds). By Ville’s inequality, for any stopping time τ, P(∃t: M_t ≥ 1/α) ≤ α. This guarantees Type‑I error ≤ α even with early stopping.
- **Core assumption**: sup_{q∈Q(p0,δ)} E_{v∼q,s∼p0}[e(v,s)] ≤ 1, ensuring the supermartingale property for all admissible target distributions.
- **Evidence**: Definition 2.3 and Theorem 2.4 (Section 2.2) formalize the e‑value and Ville’s inequality; the δ‑ball definition (Section 2.1) sets the uncertainty set. The use of e‑processes for sequential detection is also validated by independent work (arXiv:2602.14286).
- **Break condition**: If e is mis‑calibrated (e.g., sup_q E[e] > 1), the supermartingale property fails and the false positive rate may exceed α after voluntary stopping. This could happen if the anchor p0 violates inf_v p0(v) > δ or if the δ‑neighborhood is misspecified.

### Mechanism 2: Optimal distortion‑free coupling
- **Claim**: The generator can maximize detection power (log‑growth rate) while keeping the watermark distortion‑free by exploiting the anchor distribution.
- **Mechanism**: Given anchor p0 and target q (assumed to lie in the δ‑ball), the generator solves an inner maximization over couplings w∈P(p0,q) of E_w[log e]. The optimal coupling w* is the maximal coupling, which can be implemented via speculative decoding: sample a seed s∼p0; then accept a proposed token v∼q with probability min(1, (1−δ/2) p0(s) / q(s)) and otherwise fall back to s. This coupling exactly preserves the token marginal q (distortion‑free) while creating dependence that maximizes the expected log‑e‑value.
- **Core assumption**: The generator knows the target distribution q and that q∈Q(p0,δ). The anchor p0 serves as the marginal for seeds.
- **Evidence**: Section 4.1 derives w* via convex duality and shows it matches the form used in SEAL; Remark 4.2 connects to speculative decoding. Definition 2.1 (distortion‑free) is satisfied because w* has marginals p0 on seeds and q on tokens.
- **Break condition**: If the maximal coupling cannot be implemented exactly (e.g., due to approximate decoding or lack of exact q), the achieved growth rate will be below the optimum J*, increasing detection time. Slight distortions may also affect text quality.

### Mechanism 3: Worst‑case log‑growth rate and sample efficiency
- **Claim**: The quantity J* = H(p0) + (1−δ/2)log(1−δ/2) + (δ/2)log(δ/(2(n−1))) governs the fundamental sample efficiency; the optimal e‑value attains this bound, leading to expected stopping time ~ log(1/α)/J*.
- **Mechanism**: The robust log‑optimality problem max_e min_{q∈Q} max_{w∈P(p0,q)} E_w[log e] admits a closed‑form solution with value J*. This represents the minimum expected log‑evidence per token under a worst‑case adaptive target and an optimal generator response. Theorem 4.3 proves that for any e‑value satisfying the supermartingale constraint, the expected stopping time under the worst‑case target is at least log(1/α)/J* asymptotically; conversely, the proposed e‑value achieves this upper bound, establishing J* as the information‑theoretic limit.
- **Core assumption**: The adversary may choose q_t adaptively at each step from the δ‑ball, while the detector commits to a fixed e‑value before the sequence begins.
- **Evidence**: The derivation of J* and optimal e* appears in Section 4.1 (Theorem 4.1). The lower and upper bounds on stopping time are proved in Section 4.2 (Theorem 4.3). Figure 2 empirically confirms convergence of the simulated stopping time to log(1/α)/J* for small α.
- **Break condition**: If the generator deviates from the optimal coupling or if the actual q_t lies outside the δ‑ball, the effective growth rate drops below J*, resulting in longer detection times. The asymptotic result assumes α → 0; for moderate α, finite‑sample effects may cause deviations.

## Foundational Learning

- **E‑values and test supermartingales**  
  - *Why needed*: They enable anytime‑valid inference, allowing detectors to stop early while controlling Type‑I error without p‑hacking.  
  - *Quick check*: What property must a stochastic process (E_t) satisfy to be a test supermartingale under H0, and how does Ville’s inequality use it to bound the false positive probability for any stopping time?

- **Robust optimization over uncertainty sets (δ‑balls)**  
  - *Why needed*: The detector must design an e‑value that works for all possible target distributions within a neighborhood of the anchor; the worst‑case analysis reduces to considering extreme points of the δ‑ball.  
  - *Quick check*: Why can the inner minimization over q be restricted to the extreme points of the δ‑ball in the log‑growth optimization? Which properties of the objective and feasible set enable this reduction?

- **Maximal coupling (and speculative decoding)**  
  - *Why needed*: The generator’s optimal strategy to embed a detectable signal while preserving the token marginal (distortion‑free) is the maximal coupling between the anchor seed marginal p0 and the target q. Speculative decoding provides an efficient implementation.  
  - *Quick check*: Given p0 and q, what is the maximal coupling, and how does speculative decoding approximate it when drawing a token from q conditioned on a seed s∼p0?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:  
Anchor distribution p0 → Watermark generator (maximal coupling) → Token‑seed pair stream → Detector (e‑value accumulator and stopping rule) → Decision (watermarked / not)

Critical path:  
1. Generator emits (v_t, s_t) at each step.  
2. Detector computes e(v_t, s_t) from the fixed e‑value function, updates cumulative log‑wealth W_t = W_{t−1} + log e(v_t, s_t).  
3. If W_t ≥ log(1/α), the detector stops and declares the text watermarked; otherwise, continue to next token.  
Theoretical guarantees: Under H0 (independent tokens and seeds), the process is a test supermartingale, so false positive rate ≤ α for any stopping rule. Under H1 with an optimal generator against a worst‑case q∈Q(p0,δ), the expected stopping time satisfies E[τ] ~ log(1/α) / J*.

Design tradeoffs:
- δ vs sample efficiency: Larger δ expands the uncertainty set, decreasing J* and increasing required tokens; smaller δ improves efficiency but risks invalidating the supermartingale if the target lies outside the ball.
- Anchor choice p0: Higher entropy H(p0) directly increases J*; the anchor should also be close to the target to keep δ modest.
- Distortion‑free vs implementation: The maximal coupling exactly preserves token marginals, but its practical approximation (speculative decoding) may introduce slight bias or quality degradation.
- Early stopping vs power: Stopping earlier reduces power; the optimal e‑value’s weighting (high for v=s, low otherwise) naturally balances this trade‑off.

Failure signatures:
- Type‑I error exceeds α on human text → e‑value not a valid supermartingale (δ too small or p0 violates inf p0(v) > δ).
- Stopping time for watermarked text is larger than predicted → generator not using optimal coupling or target q outside δ‑ball.
- Noticeable quality drop (higher perplexity, lower human rating) → distortion introduced by coupling approximation.
- Detection fails under adaptive attacks (e.g., paraphrasing) → e‑value growth becomes too slow, possibly due to insufficient δ or anchor mismatch.

First 3 experiments (as reported):
1. Synthetic two‑token verification: The authors solve the max‑min problem numerically for n=2, validate J* convergence (Figure 1), and simulate stopping times under the optimal e‑value, confirming E[τ]/log(1/α) → 1/J* (Figure 2).
2. Real‑world benchmark: Using Llama2‑7B as target and Phi‑3‑mini as anchor, the method is tested on MarkMyWords under various perturbations. Anchored E‑Watermarking reduces median detection tokens by 13–15% compared to state‑of‑the‑art baselines while maintaining comparable generation quality.
3. Temperature robustness: Additional experiments across sampling temperatures 0.3, 0.7, 1.0 show that the efficiency gains hold broadly and do not sacrifice quality (see Appendix D).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extension to flexible anchors**: Can the framework accommodate anchor distributions without a uniform lower bound (i.e., inf_v p0(v) = 0)? The current theory assumes inf p0(v) > δ.
- **Incentivized adversaries**: What are the game‑theoretic implications when attackers have additional objectives (e.g., preserving semantic quality) beyond evasion? The current minimax model may not capture such trade‑offs.
- **Choosing robustness tolerance δ**: The paper treats δ as given; a practical method to select δ (e.g., via validation data) to optimize detection efficiency is needed.

## Limitations
- The efficiency gain depends on anchor entropy H(p0); low‑entropy anchors increase the required number of detection tokens.
- The theoretical guarantee requires the target token distribution to lie within the δ‑ball of the anchor; real‑world distribution drift (e.g., due to decoding strategies or user prompts) can violate this assumption and break the supermartingale property.
- The asymptotic scaling of expected stopping time (log(1/α)/J*) holds only as α → 0; for typical α values (e.g., 0.02), finite‑sample effects may cause deviations from the predicted rate.
