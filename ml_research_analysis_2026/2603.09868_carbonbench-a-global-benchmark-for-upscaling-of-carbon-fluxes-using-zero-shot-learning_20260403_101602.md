# CarbonBench: A Global Benchmark for Upscaling of Carbon Fluxes Using Zero-Shot Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09868
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09868
- **Reference count**: 40

## Executive Summary
CarbonBench provides a standardized framework for evaluating zero‑shot spatial transfer learning in time series regression, specifically for upscaling terrestrial carbon fluxes. It aggregates 1.3 million daily samples from 567 global eddy covariance towers, harmonized with MODIS remote sensing and ERA5‑Land meteorological drivers. Dual stratified splits by IGBP vegetation and Köppen climate enforce generalization to entirely unseen ecosystems. Baseline results show temporal deep learning models (LSTM, Transformer, TAM‑RL) outperform static tree‑based methods; TAM‑RL achieves the best median GPP R² (0.631) and superior worst‑case robustness. However, NEE prediction remains poor for all models (median R² ≤ 0.384) and performance degrades severely on underrepresented biomes, especially under climate stratification. The benchmark thus establishes a rigorous, realistic testbed while highlighting fundamental challenges in spatial transfer for environmental machine learning.

## Method Summary
The task is zero‑shot spatial transfer: predict GPP, RECO, and NEE at new geographic sites given spatiotemporal drivers. The dataset includes 1 304 309 daily observations (2000–2024) from 567 flux towers, with MODIS surface reflectance (12 bands) and ERA5‑Land meteorology (150 variables) as features, plus site metadata (IGBP, Köppen). Three nested feature sets (Minimal:6, Standard:36, Full:150) enable controlled experiments. Targets are ONEFlux‑processed fluxes with quality control flags. Evaluation uses two site‑level stratified splits: IGBP (80/20 per class) and Köppen (80/20), holding out entire sites. Primary metrics are per‑site R², RMSE, and normalized MAE aggregated as 25th/50th/75th percentiles across test sites. Models range from static (XGBoost, LightGBM) to temporal (LSTM, GRU, CT‑LSTM/GRU, Transformer, Patch‑Transformer, TAM‑RL). Training employs a composite loss: MSE weighted by QC flag and inverse‑frequency IGBP/Köppen class weights, plus a flux balance constraint (NEE = RECO − GPP, α=0.1). Hyperparameters are selected via 5‑fold Köppen‑stratified CV; final predictions average 10 seeded ensembles. All features and targets are z‑score normalized using training statistics.

## Key Results
- Temporal deep learning models (LSTM, Transformer, TAM‑RL) consistently outperform static tree‑based baselines.
- TAM‑RL achieves the highest median GPP R² (0.631 under IGBP split) and superior robustness, with the best 25th percentile scores across targets, indicating fewer catastrophic failures.
- All models struggle with NEE prediction (median R² ≤ 0.384) and show severe degradation on underrepresented biomes; climate‑stratified splits (Köppen) yield greater variability than vegetation splits (IGBP).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s effectiveness arises from three synergistic design choices. First, dual stratified evaluation by IGBP vegetation and Köppen climate holds out entire sites, explicitly testing zero‑shot spatial generalization and disentangling whether failures stem from vegetation heterogeneity or climate‑driven distribution shifts. This forces models to learn ecosystem‑invariant representations rather than memorizing site‑specific temporal patterns. Second, harmonized MODIS and ERA5‑Land features—with nested Minimal/Standard/Full sets—provide a standardized, architecture‑agnostic input interface. Consistent spatial buffering and normalization eliminate confounding from feature engineering, enabling fair comparisons and controlled ablation of driver complexity. Third, quality‑weighted multi‑task training mitigates noise and imbalance in sparse flux observations. The loss weights samples by the continuous QC flag and inverse frequency of IGBP/Köppen classes, preventing dominant high‑quality temperate sites from overwhelming learning. The physics‑based constraint NEE = RECO − GPP (α=0.1) enforces target consistency, though potentially rigidly. Together, these mechanisms create a rigorous, realistic testbed for zero‑shot spatial transfer in carbon flux upscaling.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero‑shot spatial transfer learning**: Generalizing to entirely new geographic sites rather than future time steps. *Why needed*: The core task is upscaling fluxes from sparse towers to unseen locations, requiring models to extract ecosystem‑invariant patterns. *Quick check*: How would you design a split to test true spatial generalization versus temporal memorization? (Answer: hold out whole sites.)
- **Domain generalization for regression**: Handling continuous target distribution shifts across domains (e.g., sites). *Why needed*: Regression metrics like R² can mask catastrophic failures in minority domains; per‑site aggregation is essential. *Quick check*: Why might a high median R² coexist with negative R² on 25% of sites? What metrics reveal this? (Answer: Interquartile range, 25th percentile.)
- **Carbon flux components (GPP, RECO, NEE)**: Understanding that NEE = RECO − GPP. *Why needed*: Accurate prediction of both GPP and RECO is necessary for NEE; errors compound. *Quick check*: If GPP and RECO are accurate but NEE is not, what does that imply? (Answer: The model fails to capture the relationship or balance errors.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data loader → Feature encoder → Context injection → Multi‑task head → Loss → Evaluation

**Critical path**: Data harmonization → stratified split (IGBP/Köppen) → temporal windowing (W=30, stride=15) → model training with composite loss → per‑site metric computation → quantile aggregation across test sites.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Feature set: Minimal (6) vs. Standard (36) vs. Full (150). More features may benefit complex models but risk overfitting on limited site counts.
- Temporal context: Fixed window length W=30 (default) captures phenology; longer windows add noise, variable windows may adapt but complicate training.
- Metadata usage: Simple concatenation (e.g., CT‑LSTM) vs. task‑aware modulation (e.g., TAM‑RL). Modulation may improve generalization at cost of architectural complexity.

**Failure signatures**:
- NEE median R² ≤ 0.384 with often negative 25th percentile → inability to balance GPP/RECO errors.
- Wider interquartile range under Köppen splits vs. IGBP → climate‑driven shifts are harder than vegetation shifts.
- Patch‑Transformer underperformance suggests patchification may disrupt daily temporal resolution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can adversarial domain adaptation, domain‑invariant representations, or multi‑source transfer learning improve zero‑shot generalization to underrepresented biomes compared to standard supervised methods? The paper explicitly poses this as a future direction but provides no empirical evidence.
2. Does expanding from Minimal to Standard/Full feature sets improve spatial generalization, and does the benefit vary by architecture? The authors restricted baselines to Minimal features for tractability and left systematic exploration to future users.
3. Which individual IGBP vegetation types and Köppen climate classes are responsible for catastrophic failures? The paper aggregates metrics and identifies tropical/polar undersampling but lacks per‑biome breakdowns.
4. Can uncertainty quantification methods (e.g., Bayesian neural networks, conformal prediction) provide reliable, distribution‑free uncertainty estimates for predictions in data‑sparse regions, and do these estimates correlate with actual error? The baseline ensembles lack per‑prediction uncertainties, which are critical for carbon accounting.
5. Does training specialized models for specific climate zones or vegetation types (mixture of experts) reduce negative transfer and improve worst‑case performance versus a single global model? The authors question potential negative transfer but only evaluate global models.

## Limitations
- **Architectural dependency**: Details of the top‑performing TAM‑RL model are not fully described, relying on external citations, which hinders exact replication.
- **Feature set ambiguity**: The composition of the Full (150‑variable) feature set is not enumerated, limiting users’ ability to fully explore feature richness.
- **QC weighting assumptions**: The continuous QC flag is used to weight samples, but its calibration across biomes and impact on training is not validated; improper weighting could bias results.

## Confidence
- **Stratification isolates spatial transfer**: Medium confidence. The design holds out whole sites but assumes ecosystem/climate are the primary axes; site‑specific idiosyncrasies could confound.
- **Harmonized features enable fair comparison**: High confidence. Standardized preprocessing and nested feature sets reduce confounding, though spatial footprint mismatches remain a risk.
- **Quality‑weighted training mitigates imbalance**: Medium confidence. Depends on QC reliability and inverse‑frequency weighting; no ablations shown.
- **Baseline results (TAM‑RL robustness, NEE failure, climate variability)**: High confidence. Empirical findings are clear and replicated across random seeds; however, external validation on new architectures is needed.
