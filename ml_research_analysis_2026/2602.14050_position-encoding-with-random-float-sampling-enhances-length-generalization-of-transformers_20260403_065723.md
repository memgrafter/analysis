# Position Encoding with Random Float Sampling Enhances Length Generalization of Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14050  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14050  
- **Reference count**: 13  
- **Primary result**: Random Float Sampling (RFS) significantly improves length generalization on algorithmic tasks and zero-shot commonsense reasoning without fine‑tuning.

## Executive Summary
Transformer language models fail to generalize to sequences longer than those seen during training because position indices become out‑of‑distribution. This paper proposes Random Float Sampling (RFS), a simple modification that replaces discrete position indices with randomly sampled continuous values from a shared range (e.g., [0,1)) during training and uses deterministic evenly‑spaced values during inference. RFS exposes the model to diverse position distributions, mitigating the OOD problem. On algorithmic tasks (copy, reverse, addition, etc.) RFS yields dramatic gains—for example, ~80% exact‑match accuracy on inputs twice the training length versus ~20% for NoPE. On zero‑shot commonsense QA, RFS lifts out‑of‑distribution average accuracy from 34.47% to 35.78%, nearly matching the in‑distribution baseline of 35.84%. The method works with sinusoidal, RoPE, and ALiBi encodings and outperforms position interpolation and random integer sampling.

## Method Summary
RFS changes how position indices are generated for the positional encoding layer. During training on sequences of length \(n_{tr}\), the model receives \(n_{tr}\) floats sampled uniformly from \([0,1)\), sorted in ascending order and multiplied by a constant scaling factor \(L\) (e.g., 1000). These continuous indices are fed to the positional encoding (absolute sinusoidal, RoPE, or ALiBi). At inference, for any length \(n_{in}\), evenly spaced floats in \([0,1)\) (i.e., \(i/n_{in}\) for \(i=0,\dots,n_{in}-1\)) are scaled by the same \(L\). This introduces no new parameters and requires no fine‑tuning. Training hyperparameters follow standard settings: AdamW optimizer, learning rate \(3\!\times\!10^{-5}\) for algorithmic tasks and \(6\!\times\!10^{-4}\) for language modeling, batch size 64 (tasks) or 480 (LM), 40k–100k steps, gradient clipping 1.0.

## Key Results
- RFS achieves ~80% exact‑match accuracy on the copy task with test length 40 (twice the training length 20), compared to ~20% for NoPE and lower for other baselines.
- On out‑of‑distribution commonsense QA examples, RFS raises average zero‑shot accuracy from 34.47% to 35.78%, nearly matching the in‑distribution performance of 35.84%.
- RFS generalizes across sinusoidal, RoPE, and ALiBi position encodings and consistently outperforms position interpolation and random integer sampling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Standard training exposes Transformers only to a fixed set of discrete position indices (e.g., 0 to \(n_{tr}-1\)). At inference, longer sequences introduce position indices that are statistically OOD, making the learned positional representations unreliable. RFS breaks this pattern by presenting a wide variety of continuous position values during training, effectively simulating many possible sequence lengths. The sorted random floats preserve ordering while the continuous range forces the model to rely less on absolute positions and more on relative distances. Consequently, the model learns to handle arbitrary position ranges, generalizing seamlessly to unseen lengths without any modification at inference (deterministic spacing ensures consistency).

## Foundational Learning
1. **Position Encoding**  
   *Why needed*: Self‑attention is permutation‑equivariant; position encodings inject sequence order.  
   *Quick check*: Remove position encoding and observe collapse on order‑sensitive tasks (e.g., copy).  

2. **Out‑of‑Distribution (OOD) Generalization**  
   *Why needed*: Models fail when test data distribution differs from training; here, longer sequences yield OOD position indices.  
   *Quick check*: Train on max length 20, test on 40; performance drops if model overfits to position indices.  

3. **Random Float Sampling (RFS) as Distributional Augmentation**  
   *Why needed*: RFS exposes the model to a continuum of position values, simulating many lengths.  
   *Quick check*: Compare training with fixed positions vs. RFS; the latter should reduce loss variance across lengths.  

4. **Sinusoidal / RoPE / ALiBi**  
   *Why needed*: Different ways to encode positions; RFS is agnostic to the choice.  
   *Quick check*: Plug RFS into each and measure length generalization.  

5. **Length Generalization**  
   *Why needed*: Real‑world applications require handling inputs longer than training context.  
   *Quick check*: Evaluate on progressively longer sequences; a robust model maintains accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Token Embedding -> Position Index Generation (RFS: training random sorted floats, eval deterministic evenly spaced) -> Positional Encoding Layer (sinusoidal/RoPE/ALiBi) -> Transformer Stack -> Output  

**Critical path**  
For each training batch: generate per‑sequence random floats, sort, scale by \(L\); compute positional encodings; combine with token embeddings; forward through Transformer. At inference: generate evenly spaced indices, scale, encode.  

**Design tradeoffs**  
RFS adds no parameters but requires careful selection of scaling factor \(L\); too large may cause instability, too small may limit diversity. It may slightly increase training time due to random sampling, but overhead is negligible. It works across multiple PE methods, offering flexibility.  

**Failure signatures**  
- (a) No length generalization gain: likely RFS implementation error (e.g., shared positions across batch, not sorted).  
- (b) Training loss spikes/NaNs: scaling factor \(L\) too high; reduce \(L\) or normalize position values.  
- (c) Degraded in‑distribution accuracy: possible over‑regularization; reduce randomness range or adjust \(L\).  

**First experiments**  
1. Reproduce copy task: Train base Transformer (12 layers, 12 heads, 768 dim) with RFS (\(L=1000\)) on sequences ≤20; evaluate on lengths 20 and 40. Expect ~80% on 40 vs ~20% for NoPE.  
2. Ablate scaling factor \(L\) on addition task: Train with \(L\in\{10,100,1000\}\); compare stability and length generalization.  
3. Verify compatibility: Apply RFS with RoPE and ALiBi on reverse task; confirm similar gains.
