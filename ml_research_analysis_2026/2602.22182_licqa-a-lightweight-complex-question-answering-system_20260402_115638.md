# LiCQA : A Lightweight Complex Question Answering System

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.22182
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22182
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** LiCQA achieves statistically significant improvements in tie-aware metrics (up to 21.6% higher MRR, 71.8% higher Hit@5) and reduces query latency by 8 times compared to QUEST and DrQA on complex QA benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
LiCQA is an unsupervised, lightweight system for complex question answering (CQA) where answers must be synthesized from evidence spread across multiple documents. It employs a modular pipeline: a lightweight question type classifier, retrieval of candidate documents, named entity extraction, and a scoring function that combines semantic similarity (via InferSent) with document frequency to rank candidate answers. Evaluated on WikiAnswers (CQ-W) and Google Trends (CQ-T) datasets, LiCQA demonstrates superior effectiveness in tie-aware metrics and a substantial efficiency advantage over supervised and knowledge graph-based baselines, offering a practical approach for complex QA without expensive end-to-end neural training.

## Method Summary
The system processes a complex question through four main stages. First, an SVM-based question type classifier predicts a coarse answer type (e.g., PERSON, LOCATION). Second, a keyword-based retriever fetches a set of top-ranked documents (e.g., Top10). Third, a Flair NER tagger extracts named entities from these documents, which are filtered to those matching the predicted answer type via a handcrafted mapping. Fourth, for each remaining candidate entity, the system identifies sentences containing it, computes the maximum cosine similarity between the query and any such sentence using InferSent embeddings, and multiplies this score by the normalized document frequency of the entity across the retrieved set. The top-ranked entities are returned as the answer list.

## Key Results
*   On CQ-W and CQ-T, LiCQA significantly outperforms QUEST and DrQA on tie-aware metrics, achieving up to **+21.6% tMRR** and **+71.8% tHit@5** over QUEST.
*   It reduces **average query latency by approximately 8 times** compared to the neural and graph-based baselines.
*   The multiplicative combination of semantic similarity and normalized document frequency (`comb-score*`) determined via ablation to be the optimal ranking function.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** LiCQA answers complex questions by synthesizing evidence from multiple retrieved documents, rather than relying on a single document or a pre-built knowledge graph.
*   **Mechanism:** The system retrieves a set of top-ranked documents for a query. Candidate answers (named entities) are extracted from these documents. The final answer is synthesized by scoring and ranking these entities based on their contextual occurrence across the document set. This multi-document evidence aggregation is designed to handle queries where the answer is not contiguous in a single source.
*   **Core assumption:** Relevant information for a complex query is present in the top-ranked documents returned by a standard search engine, and the correct answer can be identified as a recurring named entity type that co-occurs contextually with the query terms.
*   **Evidence anchors:** The [abstract] states the system is unsupervised and works on corpus evidence for questions with answers spread across multiple documents. [Section 3] details the retrieval, extraction, and cross-document scoring pipeline.
*   **Break condition:** If the retrieved document set does not contain documents that collectively mention the key entities and relationships required to answer the query, the system cannot synthesize the correct answer.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Answer ranking is driven by a multiplicative combination of (a) semantic similarity between the query and sentences containing a candidate entity, and (b) the normalized document frequency of that entity in the retrieved set.
*   **Mechanism:** For each candidate entity `e`, a semantic score is computed (best sentence similarity using InferSent embeddings). This is multiplied by `df(e)/|D|` (the proportion of retrieved documents containing `e`). This favors entities that appear in multiple documents *and* in contexts semantically close to the query.
*   **Core assumption:** The correct answer entity will appear in a sentence with high semantic similarity to the query *and* will be mentioned in a significant fraction of the initially retrieved documents, indicating its centrality to the query topic.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [Section 3.4] explicitly states ranking is based on an arithmetic combination and reports that simple multiplication yielded the best performance in ablation studies (Table 3). [Section 5.1] concludes the best performance is with the normalized document frequency multiplied with the score.
*   **Break condition:** The multiplicative combination fails if the correct answer is a low-frequency entity (appearing in few retrieved documents) or if high-frequency entities co-occur with the query in irrelevant contexts that still yield high semantic similarity.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** The unsupervised, modular pipeline avoids expensive end-to-end neural training and large annotated datasets, achieving efficiency and effectiveness for complex QA.
*   **Mechanism:** Each component (SVM classifier, Flair NER, InferSent embeddings) uses pre-trained or lightweight models. No task-specific training on complex QA datasets is performed. The system repurposes general-purpose NLP tools within a retrieval-centric framework.
*   **Core assumption:** General-purpose NLP components provide sufficient signal for complex answer extraction when combined with corpus statistics (document frequency) and IR-style retrieval.
*   **Evidence anchors:** The [abstract] highlights the unsupervised nature and contrasts with expensive supervised systems. [Section 1] states the system bypasses the need for computational resources required by end-to-end supervised QA.
*   **Break condition:** The approach fails for complex questions requiring reasoning beyond entity type matching and contextual similarity (e.g., temporal reasoning, negation, quantitative aggregation).

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Question Type Classification**
    *   **Why needed:** To filter extracted named entities to only those matching the predicted answer type (e.g., filtering all entities to only PERSONs for "Who..." questions). This reduces the candidate answer space and improves precision.
    *   **Quick check:** What is a failure mode of mapping the classifier's coarse labels (from [25]) to Flair's OntoNotes 5 tagset via the handcrafted Table 1?
*   **Concept: Named Entity Recognition with Contextual Embeddings (Flair)**
    *   **Why needed:** To extract potential answer candidates (entities) from retrieved documents. Flair's contextual embeddings help disambiguate entity types in varied contexts, which is critical for accurate filtering by predicted answer type.
    *   **Quick check:** Why might Flair's OntoNotes 5 tagset (e.g., `ORG`, `GPE`) require a handcrafted mapping to the question classifier's label set (e.g., `LOCATION`)?
*   **Concept: Sentence Semantic Similarity (InferSent)**
    *   **Why needed:** To measure how contextually relevant a sentence containing a candidate entity is to the query. This is the core signal for determining if an entity's surrounding text is semantically aligned with the question's intent.
    *   **Quick check:** Why might InferSent (trained on SNLI) be preferred over more computationally expensive models like BERT in this efficiency-focused pipeline?
*   **Concept: Unsupervised Retrieval-Augmented Scoring**
    *   **Why needed:** The system must identify a correct answer from a set of candidate entities without any labeled examples of correct answers for the specific query. It uses corpus statistics (document frequency) as a proxy for entity salience within the retrieved set.
    *   **Quick check:** What is the intuition behind using `df(e)/|D|` (normalized document frequency), and how does it interact with the semantic similarity score in the ranking formula?
*   **Concept: Document Retrieval**
    *   **Why needed:** To provide the set of text sources (`D`) from which candidate entities and their contextual sentences are drawn. The quality and relevance of this initial retrieval set is fundamental to the entire evidence synthesis process.
    *   **Quick check:** How might the choice between using a `Top10` set versus a lower `Strata` set (e.g., `Strata-5`) impact the system's recall and overall performance, as observed in the experiments?
*   **Concept: Tie-Aware Metrics**
    *   **Why needed:** Standard QA metrics (MRR, Hit@5) can be overly punitive when multiple correct answers exist for a complex query. Tie-aware metrics (tMRR, tHit@5) are used to evaluate systems that return a set of plausible answers, giving partial credit if any correct answer is ranked highly.
    *   **Quick check:** Why are tie-aware metrics particularly important for evaluating LiCQA's approach of returning a top-5 list of candidate entities for complex questions?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Question Type Classifier (SVM/USE) -> Document Retriever (Keyword IR) -> Answer Extractor & Filter (Flair NER + Type Mapping) -> Answer Scorer (InferSent + df) -> Answer Ranker (comb-score* multiplication) -> Top-5 Answer List
*   **Critical path:** The predicted question type from the Classifier directly controls the filtering of entities extracted by the NER module. The final ranking depends on both the semantic similarity score computed by InferSent and the document frequency statistic from the Retriever's output set.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Speed vs. Expressivity:** Using `max-score` (best single sentence) and `InferSent` favors speed over potentially richer context aggregation (`avg-score`).
    *   **Precision vs. Recall in NE Filtering:** The handcrafted type mapping (Table 1) reduces noise but may filter out correct answers if the mapping is incomplete or the QC misclassifies.
    *   **Parameterization:** The system uses a fixed multiplicative combination (`comb-score*`) after ablation, simplifying deployment but forgoing adaptive weighting (`𝛂, 𝛃`) that might suit some query types better.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **High tMRR but low P@1:** System places correct answers in the top-5 but ties or ranks them lower than incorrect ones at rank 1.
    *   **Poor performance on specific strata (e.g., Strata-5):** Indicates sensitivity to the quality of the initial document retrieval; lower-ranked strata may lack sufficient relevant evidence.
    *   **Low tHit@5:** System fails to place *any* correct answer in the top 5, indicating a fundamental breakdown in evidence aggregation or type filtering.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Validate QC → NE Filtering Pipeline:** Take a sample of 20 known complex questions with known answer types. Run the QC, extract NEs from a relevant document, and apply the Table 1 mapping. Measure if the gold answer entity is retained in the filtered set.
    2.  **Isolate Scoring Function Impact:** On a held-out set of 50 questions with known gold entities, compute the separate `score(e)` (InferSent max-similarity) and `df(e)/|D|` values. Correlate each component individually with gold answer rank to understand their contributions before multiplication.
    3.  **Latency Profiling:** Measure time per query for each pipeline stage (QC, NER, InferSent encoding, scoring/ranking). Confirm that NER and sentence encoding dominate runtime and that total time aligns with the reported ~8x speedup over QUEST/DrQA.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can more advanced embedding models, such as those learning entity and concept representations from knowledge bases, improve LiCQA's answer extraction and scoring?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states future work will explore such embedding models to improve the answer extraction module.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The paper uses InferSent and Sentence-BERT but does not test embeddings explicitly trained for entities or concepts (e.g., Wikipedia2Vec).
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative evaluation integrating entity-aware embeddings into the scoring stage on the benchmark datasets, measuring changes in tMRR, tP@1, and tHit@5.
*   **Open Question 2:** To what extent does LiCQA's performance depend on the specific characteristics of the WikiAnswers and Google Trends datasets, and how well does it generalize to other complex question datasets or domains?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Evaluation is restricted to two curated complex question sets (CQ-W, CQ-T). The paper notes a scarcity of diverse complex QA testbeds.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No evaluation on other complex QA benchmarks (e.g., HotpotQA, QALD) or domain-specific corpora is performed.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Running LiCQA on additional established complex QA datasets and comparing performance; analyzing failure cases to identify dataset-specific biases.
*   **Open Question 3:** What is the theoretical or empirical rationale for the multiplication-based combination (`comb-score*`) of semantic similarity and normalized document frequency outperforming weighted addition (`comb-score+`) in ranking candidate answers?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The paper empirically finds that `comb-score*` (multiplication) yields the best performance but provides no analysis or hypothesis for why this works better than a tuned linear combination.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The effectiveness of the multiplicative scoring function is treated as an observed result without deeper explanation.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study or analytical experiment that varies the relationship between the two score components and correlates scoring behavior with answer correctness.
*   **Open Question 4:** How robust is the handcrafted mapping between the question classification labels (from [25]) and the OntoNotes 5 named entity types, and does it introduce a systematic source of error?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly states the mapping is "not learned or validated."
    *   **Why unresolved:** The mapping is static and simplistic; there is no error analysis on how mis-mappings affect answer recall or evaluation of the table's coverage and ambiguity.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** A manual or automated audit of a sample of incorrectly answered questions to quantify errors attributable to failed type mapping. Redesigning the mapping and measuring performance change.

## Limitations
*   **Data Access:** Reliance on the QUEST dataset (CQ-W/CQ-T with Top10/Strata documents) and the 5500 annotated questions from [25] for training the SVM classifier. Public availability is unclear, hindering full reproduction.
*   **Implementation Specifics:** Unspecified versions/configurations for critical components (InferSent checkpoint, Flair OntoNotes model) and the precise formulas for tie-aware metrics introduce reproducibility risk.
*   **Evaluation Scope:** Results are demonstrated on only two datasets. Generalization to other complex QA benchmarks, domains, or question types (e.g., requiring quantitative or temporal reasoning) is not established.

## Confidence
*   **Effectiveness improvements (tMRR/tHit@5 vs QUEST/DrQA):** Medium. Results are promising but based on a narrow evaluation scope and face reproducibility obstacles.
*   **Efficiency gains (8× latency reduction):** High. The timing difference is straightforward to measure, and the pipeline uses lightweight, pre-trained components.
*   **Design rationale (unsupervised corpus-evidence approach, multiplicative scoring):** Medium. Internal ablations support these choices, but external validation and deeper theoretical understanding are lacking.
