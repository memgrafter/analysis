# Concept Influence: Leveraging Interpretability to Improve Performance and Efficiency in Training Data Attribution

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14869
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14869
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Concept‑based attribution (Concept Influence and its efficient approximations) matches or exceeds classical influence functions in identifying data that drive unwanted LLM behaviors, achieves >20× speedup, and filters just 5% of OASST1 to reduce the "evil" score from 2.3% to 0.8% while preserving instruction‑following.

## Executive Summary
Training data attribution (TDA) explains model behavior by identifying influential training examples, but existing gradient‑based methods are computationally expensive and often biased toward surface‑level lexical similarities. This paper introduces Concept Influence, which redirects attribution from individual test examples to semantic directions—such as linear probes or sparse autoencoder (SAE) features—that capture high‑level behaviors like "evil" or "sycophancy". Concept Influence generalizes influence functions by replacing the test‑loss gradient with the gradient of the model’s activation along the concept vector. The authors further derive two efficient approximations, Vector Filter and Projection Difference, which bypass Hessian inversion and, in the case of Vector Filter, also avoid generating outputs on the training set. Evaluated on emergent misalignment benchmarks and the OASST1 post‑training dataset, concept‑based methods attain comparable or superior performance in pinpointing harmful data while being orders of magnitude faster; Vector Filter alone yields over 20× speedup. Moreover, filtering a small subset of OASST1 (top 5% safest examples) reduces harmful behavior dramatically without sacrificing instruction‑following quality. The work thus demonstrates that leveraging interpretability not only clarifies model behavior but also enhances the scalability and efficacy of TDA.

## Method Summary
Concept Influence modifies the standard influence function by conditioning on a concept vector \(v\) instead of a test example’s loss gradient. For a target behavior defined by \(v\) (e.g., a linear probe or an SAE feature at layer \(\ell\)), the influence of a training example \(z_{\text{train}}\) is  
\(I_v(z_{\text{train}}) = \nabla_\theta f_\ell^v(x_{\text{test}})^\top H^{-1} \nabla_\theta L(z_{\text{train}})\),  
where \(f_\ell^v(x_{\text{test}}) = \langle v, a_\ell(x_{\text{test}})\rangle\) measures the model’s activation along \(v\), and \(H\) is the Hessian of the training loss. This formulation attributes training points based on their effect on the model’s representation of the concept. Two computationally cheaper approximations are derived: (1) **Projection Difference** \(I_v \approx v^\top (a_\ell(x_{\text{train}}, y_{\text{train}}) - a_\ell(x_{\text{train}}, y'_{\text{train}}))\), where \(y'_{\text{train}}\) is a counterfactual output from the base model; and (2) **Vector Filter** \(I_v \approx v^\top a_\ell(x_{\text{train}}, y_{\text{train}})\), which requires only a forward pass on the original model. Both avoid explicit Hessian inversion; Vector Filter also eliminates the need to generate finetuned outputs. Furthermore, influence scores can be aggregated over SAE features to produce interpretable, concept‑level analyses. The approach integrates with existing TDA pipelines and can be applied to any layer with a meaningful concept vector.

## Key Results
- **Vector Filter achieves >20× speedup** compared to classical influence functions (Table 1), making TDA feasible on full‑scale datasets.
- **Filtering OASST1 using Vector Filter** reduces the harmful "evil" score from 2.3% to 0.8% while retaining the full dataset’s instruction‑following performance (MTBench score 67).
- **Concept‑based methods match or exceed influence functions** across emergent misalignment benchmarks in identifying data that induce unwanted behaviors, especially when concept vectors are well‑aligned with the target.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Concept Influence works by reorienting the attribution target from a specific test example to an abstract semantic direction. In standard influence, the influence of a training point on a test loss is computed via the gradient \(\nabla_\theta L(z_{\text{test}})\). Concept Influence replaces this with the gradient of the model's activation along a concept vector \(v\): \(\nabla_\theta \langle v, a_\ell(x_{\text{test}})\rangle\). This shift means the “query” is the behavior itself—not a particular prompt—so influential training points are those that shape the model's capacity to activate that concept. The method assumes that such concept vectors (obtained via linear probes or SAEs) are stable proxies for the behavior, and the paper’s formalization (Section 3.2) and experiments confirm its effectiveness.

The efficiency gains stem from two approximations that lead to Vector Filter and Projection Difference. Starting from the full formula \(I_v = \nabla_\theta f_\ell^v(x_{\text{test}})^\top H^{-1} \nabla_\theta L(z_{\text{train}})\), approximating \(H \approx I\) and the cross‑input kernel \(M \approx I\) reduces the influence to \(\nabla_\theta f_\ell^v(x_{\text{test}})^\top \nabla_\theta L(z_{\text{train}})\). Further using the relationship \(\nabla_\theta L(z_{\text{train}}) \propto [a_\ell(x_{\text{train}}, y_{\text{train}}) - a_\ell(x_{\text{train}}, y'_{\text{train}})]\) (valid for SGD fine‑tuning) yields Projection Difference \(v^\top(a_{\text{orig}} - a_{\text{finetuned}})\). Dropping the counterfactual term gives Vector Filter \(v^\top a_{\text{orig}}\). These avoid Hessian inversion entirely, and Vector Filter eliminates per‑example generation, achieving >20× speedup (Table 1). The approximations hold well on in‑distribution data, but degrade under significant distribution shift (e.g., attributing GSM8K mistakes to “evil”), indicating the Hessian’s role in capturing counterfactual parameter changes (Figure 2).

Beyond individual scores, Concept Influence enables interpretable aggregation by grouping examples via SAE features. Summing influence over examples that strongly activate a given SAE feature reveals which high‑level semantics (e.g., “historical oppression”, “conspiracy theories”) are linked to the target behavior. This SAE‑based grouping, demonstrated in Figure 4, yields more coherent and directly relevant insights than the lexical clusters often surfaced by standard influence functions. The quality of SAE features is critical; monosemantic features provide clear groupings, while noisy or polysemantic features could undermine interpretability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Influence Functions**  
  *Why needed*: Concept Influence generalizes the classical influence function; understanding its Hessian‑based derivation and the role of the up‑weighting effect is essential to appreciate the modification.  
  *Quick check*: What is the core mathematical approximation in an influence function, and what does the Hessian capture about the loss landscape?

- **Linear Probes & Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)**  
  *Why needed*: These supply the interpretable directions (\(v\)) that define the target concept. Their quality directly determines whether attribution aligns with the desired behavior.  
  *Quick check*: What is the difference between a linear probe (e.g., TCAV) and an SAE feature as a “concept vector”, and what are the trade‑offs?

- **Hessian Approximation (EK‑FAC)**  
  *Why needed*: Full Hessian computation is infeasible for large LLMs; EK‑FAC makes gradient‑based influence estimation scalable. Grasping its assumptions is key to evaluating computational claims.  
  *Quick check*: What does the EK‑FAC approximation assume about the structure of the Hessian, and why does it make influence estimation scalable for large models?

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Data & Labels -> Base Model -> Concept Extractor -> Attribution Engine -> Aggregation & Filtering -> Evaluation Loop

#### Critical Path
The typical pipeline: train a model on a dataset; obtain a concept vector \(v\); compute influence scores for all training examples; filter or rank the data; retrain on the subset and evaluate. For gradient‑based methods (Concept Influence, Influence Functions), the dominant cost is building and applying the Hessian via EK‑FAC. Efficient approximations like Vector Filter skip the Hessian entirely, and Vector Filter also avoids generating finetuned outputs on the training set, reducing attribution to a forward pass per example.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- *Accuracy vs Speed*: Full Concept Influence (with Hessian) is most accurate, especially under distribution shift; Vector Filter is fastest but less robust when the training distribution differs from the base model.
- *Concept Quality*: Supervised probes require labeled data for the concept; SAEs are unsupervised but may lack a perfect feature for every abstract behavior, affecting attribution validity.
- *Interpretability*: Individual example scores can be noisy; aggregating over SAE clusters provides higher‑level, more semantically coherent insights.

#### Failure Signatures
- High correlation of attribution scores with lexical or bag‑of‑words baselines suggests the concept vector is not capturing deep semantics (weak probe or shallow SAE feature).
- Performance collapse on out‑of‑domain tasks (e.g., GSM8K Mistakes → Evil) indicates that the first‑order approximations (H≈I) are insufficient; the Hessian‑based effect is needed.
- SAE cluster attributions show inconsistent influence signs or no clear separation between aligned and misaligned data, implying the SAE features are not monosemantic.
- Filtering based on scores does not change downstream behavior after retraining, meaning the attribution does not predict true influence (possibly due to poor concept vector or non‑linear effects).

#### First 3 Experiments
1. **Synthetic Emergent Misalignment Filtering**: Finetune on a mixed dataset (e.g., 50% “evil”, 50% benign). Compute attribution for an “evil” concept vector. Remove top‑K most (or least) influential examples and retrain. Evaluate “evil” score via an LLM judge. Expected: removing top‑K reduces evil score; removing bottom‑K increases it (Figure 2).
2. **Efficiency Benchmark**: Time attribution on 1,000 examples for all methods. Report runtime, demonstrating Vector Filter’s >20× speedup over full gradient‑based methods (Table 1).
3. **Correlation & Clustering Analysis**: Compute pairwise Spearman correlations between method scores. Expect two clusters: vector‑based (Vector Filter, Projection Difference, Concept Influence) vs. gradient‑based (Influence Functions) (Figure 3). ## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: *Can Concept Influence be extended to richer mechanistic representations beyond linear probes and SAE features?* The authors note that the framework naturally accommodates circuits or other mechanistic decompositions, but evaluating such representations on TDA benchmarks remains future work. Evidence: The paper mentions this in its future directions, but no experiments with richer representations are provided.
- **Open Question 2**: *How can group‑level attribution be made more principled and robust compared to individual datapoint attribution?* The current SAE‑based grouping is heuristic; a theoretical foundation for optimal grouping and robustness analyses across datasets are lacking. Evidence: The authors identify developing principled group‑level attribution as an important direction.
- **Open Question 3**: *What explains the empirical trade‑off between gradient‑based influence (robust to distribution shift) and projection‑based methods (fast but less robust), and can efficiency be improved without sacrificing robustness?* The trade‑off is observed but not formally characterized. Evidence: The discussion notes the out‑of‑domain degradation of approximations but offers no analysis of why the Hessian matters or how to design better approximations.
- **Open Question 4**: *To what extent do base model representation geometry versus finetuning dynamics contribute to the effectiveness of concept‑based attribution?* The paper consistently uses concepts derived from the base model to attribute finetuned behavior, without disentangling whether the effect stems from base representations or finetuning changes. Evidence: The authors call for systematic comparisons between base and finetuned models.

## Limitations
- **Concept vector dependence**: Attribution correctness relies on the quality of the concept vector (probe or SAE feature). Poorly trained probes or polysemantic SAE features can render the scores meaningless.
- **Evaluation reproducibility**: The “evil” score depends on an LLM judge whose model, prompts, and weighting are not fully disclosed, making exact replication difficult and susceptible to score drift.
- **Approximation brittleness**: The efficient approximations (Vector Filter, Projection Difference) degrade under distribution shift, limiting their use in scenarios where the training distribution differs significantly from the base model.

## Confidence
- *Concept Influence improves attribution quality by focusing on semantic directions*: Medium  
- *Efficient approximations (Vector Filter, Projection Difference) achieve large speedups with minimal accuracy loss in-distribution*: Medium  
- *SAE‑based aggregation yields interpretable, concept‑level insights*: Low–Medium
