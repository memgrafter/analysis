# Removing Planner Bias in Goal Recognition Through Multi-Plan Dataset Generation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14691
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14691
- Reference count: 5
- Primary result: Introduces a multi-plan dataset generation method using SymK to mitigate planner bias and proposes VCS; shows landmark recognizer accuracy drops from 99% to 76% at 10% observability under high resilience threshold, exposing hidden vulnerability.

## Executive Summary
Goal recognition benchmarks often suffer from planner bias because they rely on a single heuristic-generated plan per goal. This paper addresses the issue by constructing unbiased datasets: for each goal hypothesis, multiple distinct optimal plans are generated via a top‑k planner (SymK), and partial observations are sampled from each. A new metric, Version Coverage Score (VCS), measures a recognizer’s ability to succeed across all plan variants. Evaluation of a state‑of‑the‑art landmark‑based recognizer on the resulting benchmark reveals substantial performance degradation under low observability and high resilience thresholds. For instance, at 10% observation level, accuracy falls from 99% to 76% when the resilience threshold increases from 0.0 to 1.0, and positive predictive value plummets from 95% to 13%. These results demonstrate that prior datasets overestimated recognizer capability and that robustness to plan variation is a critical yet overlooked aspect.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a pipeline to create resilient goal recognition benchmarks. Starting from a classical planning domain and a set of goal hypotheses (generated by a HypothesisGenerator), they employ a top‑k planner (SymK) to produce k distinct optimal plans for each hypothesis. For each plan, they then sample a partial observation sequence at a specified observability level O (percentage of actions retained), optionally injecting noise by replacing a fraction N of retained actions with other plausible actions. This yields k variants of the same goal recognition task, each with a different observation sequence. A recognizer is evaluated on all variants; the Version Coverage Score (VCS) is the fraction of variants where the true goal is correctly inferred. By varying O, N, and a resilience threshold T (the minimum VCS required for a hypothesis to be considered solved), the dataset probes a recognizer’s robustness to planner bias. The pipeline is applied to the Ramirez & Geffner (2009) benchmarks, and a landmark‑based recognizer is tested, showing clear performance declines as O decreases and T increases.

## Key Results
- At 10% observability, accuracy decreases from 99% (resilience threshold 0.0) to 76% (threshold 1.0) and PPV drops from 95% to 13%.
- The multi‑plan dataset uncovers vulnerabilities in the landmark‑based recognizer that were not evident in traditional single‑plan benchmarks.
- Performance degradation is more pronounced under lower observability and higher resilience thresholds, indicating reliance on planner‑specific heuristics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach combats planner bias through three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **Multi‑plan dataset generation** – Using SymK to generate k diverse optimal plans per goal hypothesis removes the idiosyncratic imprint of any single heuristic planner. A robust recognizer must generalise across alternative optimal behaviours. If SymK fails to produce truly distinct plans, this mitigation collapses.

2. **Version Coverage Score (VCS)** – VCS evaluates a recognizer on all k variants of a hypothesis and computes the fraction of correct inferences. The resilience threshold T demands that a recognizer succeed on at least a T‑fraction of variants, making the metric sensitive to variability across plan versions. If variants are poorly curated (e.g., duplicates), VCS can become misleading.

3. **Exposure of hidden vulnerabilities** – When combined with low observability, the multi‑plan dataset forces recognizers to rely on generalisable features rather than planner‑specific patterns. The landmark‑based recognizer shows sharp performance drops as T increases, revealing a weakness that single‑plan benchmarks concealed because all test sequences came from the same planner’s solution space.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Original planning problem (Υ, G) → HypothesisGenerator → Set of goals G → For each g ∈ G: SymK(g, k) → Ψ_g (k distinct plans) → For each p ∈ Ψ_g: Select(p, O, N) → Ω_{g,p} (observations) → TaskAssembler(Υ, G, Ω_{g,p}) → Goal recognition task → Recognizer R → VCSEvaluator(Γ_g, R, T) → VCS score.

Critical path: From a base planning problem Ξ → for each hypothesis g: generate k plans via SymK → for each plan: sample clean/noisy Ω → evaluate R on all variants → compute VCS per threshold T → aggregate over the dataset.

Design tradeoffs:
- SymK domain dependency: Works for classical STRIPS planning but not richer models; choosing k trades diversity vs. computational cost.
- Random observation dropout: Simple but may produce unrealistic partial plans; adversarial selection (e.g., dropping landmark actions) could be more challenging but requires domain knowledge.
- VCS threshold T: Higher T demands greater robustness but reduces the number of hypotheses passing the filter, potentially harming dataset size.

Failure signatures:
- SymK returns fewer than k distinct plans for many hypotheses → dataset has insufficient variant count.
- Recognizer’s accuracy and PPV collapse at low O and high T (as in Table 1) → strong reliance on planner‑specific patterns.
- Spread remains ~1.0 across all thresholds → recognizer defaults to single‑hypothesis output, making VCS trivial (0 or 1).

First 3 experiments:
1. **Dataset generation replication**: Run Algorithm 1 on all problems from Ramirez & Geffner (2009) with k=5, O∈{10,30,50,70,100}, N=0 and 30. Verify each hypothesis yields exactly 5 plan variants (unless SymK exhausts options).
2. **Baseline recognizer evaluation**: Implement or reuse Pereira et al.’s landmark‑based recognizer. For each generated task, compute the set of hypotheses it returns as true. Tabulate accuracy, PPV, spread per (O,N) as in Table 1.
3. **Resilience curve construction**: For each O, compute VCS across thresholds T∈[0.0,1.0]. Plot accuracy/PPV vs. T, compare against results on the original single‑plan dataset to visualise the bias exposure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Can using diverse planners generate less biased goal recognition datasets? The authors suggest exploring diverse planners in future work.
- **Question 2:** What alternative action selection strategies for partial observability better model real‑world scenarios? Future work proposes non‑random methods for dropping or modifying actions.
- **Question 3:** Do learning‑based or probabilistic goal recognizers show similar resilience degradation to planner bias? The evaluation was limited to one landmark‑based recognizer; other SOTA methods remain untested.
- **Question 4:** How does the number of plan versions (k) affect the Version Coverage Score’s effectiveness? No sensitivity analysis on k was performed; the optimal value is unknown.

## Limitations
- SymK may fail to generate sufficiently diverse plans for many hypotheses, weakening the bias mitigation.
- The noise injection process is not precisely defined, raising doubts about the realism of generated observations.
- The exact implementation and hyperparameters of the landmark recognizer used in evaluation might differ from those reported, affecting reproducibility.

## Confidence
- Multi‑plan dataset generation reduces planner bias: **Medium**
- Version Coverage Score measures resilience: **Medium**
- SOTA landmark‑based recognizer shows significant degradation at low observability: **High** (contingent on correct reproduction)
