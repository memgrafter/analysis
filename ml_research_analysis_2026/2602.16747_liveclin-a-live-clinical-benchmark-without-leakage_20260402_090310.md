# LiveClin: A Live Clinical Benchmark without Leakage

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16747  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16747  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: LiveClin reveals top models (o3, GPT‑5) achieve only 35.7% case accuracy, well below human chief physicians, uncovering significant failure modes in longitudinal clinical reasoning.

## Executive Summary
Static medical benchmarks suffer from data contamination and rapidly become clinically outdated, failing to evaluate model performance in real-world settings. LiveClin addresses this by introducing a live, continuously updated benchmark constructed from contemporary peer‑reviewed case reports, refreshed every six months. Using an AI‑human pipeline (generator‑critic agents + 239‑physician validation), it transforms authentic cases into 6,605 multi‑turn, multimodal questions that simulate the full clinical pathway. Evaluation of 26 models shows even the best struggle, with identified weaknesses in mid‑pathway synthesis and context retention. The framework provides a scalable, cost‑effective (~$42k per update) standard for developing clinically reliable medical AI.

## Method Summary
LiveClin builds its benchmark by sourcing recent case reports from PubMed Central, classifying them into a fine‑grained clinical taxonomy, and stratifying sampling to ensure diversity. An AI pipeline (a Generator agent creates initial questions, a Critic iteratively refines them for correctness and difficulty, and a Judge pre‑screens) produces candidate exam sets. These are then rigorously validated by a network of 239 physicians across specialties in a two‑phase process (annotation followed by senior inspection). The final benchmark comprises 1,407 cases with 3–6 sequential, multimodal questions each, simulating the evolving clinical encounter. The entire dataset is regenerated biannually to maintain freshness and resist contamination.

## Key Results
- Leading models (o3, GPT‑5) achieve only **35.7% case accuracy** (all questions per case correct), far below human chief physicians.
- **Open‑source models are narrowing the gap** with proprietary ones on certain reasoning subtasks.
- Evaluation reveals distinct **failure modes**: models struggle with mid‑pathway information synthesis and late‑stage context loss over long dialogues.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Freshness & Anti‑Contamination
- **Claim**: Regular refreshment with recent, unseen clinical cases reduces data contamination and knowledge obsolescence.
- **Mechanism**: The benchmark sources peer‑reviewed case reports published within the past six months and updates entirely every January and July. This temporal separation places evaluation data outside most models’ training cutoffs, preventing memorization and ensuring clinical currency.
- **Break condition**: Model trainers could ingest the benchmark pre‑release; if update cycles become irregular or contemporary reports are already in training corpora, contamination resistance degrades.

### Mechanism 2: Sequential Clinical Reasoning Simulation
- **Claim**: Multi‑turn, context‑maintaining questions evaluate integrated, longitudinal reasoning rather than isolated fact retrieval.
- **Mechanism**: Each case yields 3–6 multiple‑choice questions aligned to clinical stages (presentation, diagnosis, treatment, etc.). Models must retain and integrate information from prior turns and newly added multimodal data (images, tables), mirroring a real patient encounter.
- **Break condition**: Insufficient context window length or poorly linked questions could reduce the evaluation to independent snapshots, losing sequential complexity.

### Mechanism 3: Human‑AI Collaborative Quality Assurance
- **Claim**: The generator‑critic‑judge pipeline, followed by extensive physician screening, yields high‑quality, challenging questions at sustainable scale and cost.
- **Mechanism**: An o3‑powered Generator creates initial items; a Critic agent iteratively refines them (up to 10 cycles) for factual correctness and cognitive complexity; a Judge agent conservatively pre‑screens; finally, 239 licensed physicians verify every item in two phases. This balances automation with expert oversight.
- **Break condition**: Decline in physician availability or drop in Critic quality could reduce throughput or correctness; the conservative rejection principle may also limit yield.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Case Source (PMC) -> Classification Module (gpt‑4.1) -> Sampling Module -> Exam Generation (Generator -> Critic loop) -> Pre‑screening (Judge) -> Physician Network (Annotation -> Inspection) -> Final Assembly -> Evaluation Harness

- **Critical path**:  
  1. Acquire raw case reports from PMC.  
  2. Parse narratives; extract images/tables.  
  3. Classify into Level‑1/2/3 taxonomy.  
  4. Stratified sample ~2,150 cases.  
  5. Generate exam sets (Generator).  
  6. Run Critic loop until convergence or discard.  
  7. Judge pre‑screen; pass to physicians.  
  8. Physician annotation → inspection → consensus.  
  9. Final stratified sampling to 1,407 cases / 6,605 questions.  
  10. Deploy to evaluation harness; monitor via private leaderboard.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *AI vs. Human Effort*: AI enables frequent, low‑cost updates but risks errors; extensive physician review ensures quality but limits scalability.  
  - *Breadth vs. Depth of Taxonomy*: Fine‑grained (72 clusters) provides diagnostic feedback but strains sparse‑group resources; sparse groups were merged for reliability.  
  - *Multimodality vs. Complexity*: Including images/tables increases realism and challenge but complicates generation and evaluation.  
  - *Update Frequency vs. Sustainability*: Biannual full replacement guarantees freshness but imposes recurring ~$42k / 1.8k person‑hour cost; the AI‑human workflow makes this sustainable.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - High trivial ratio (≥20% of questions answered correctly by all weak models).  
  - Low physician pass rate (<80% after verification).  
  - High inter‑physician disagreement (>15% requiring >2 loops).  
  - Large performance drift (>5 points) across successive live sets.  
  - Resource overruns (cost/time exceeding budget).

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Temporal contamination pilot: Evaluate models on case sets from pre‑ vs. post‑cutoff periods to quantify score gaps.  
  2. Ablation of generation workflow: Compare full pipeline against Generator‑only and physician‑only on accuracy, cost, difficulty.  
  3. Full model evaluation: Run LiveClin on 26 models to establish rankings, analyze error patterns across stages/modalities, and identify failure modes.

## Limitations
- Generator‑critic pipeline may harbor undetected biases; physician reviewer pool composition is not detailed.  
- PMC case reports may underrepresent rare diseases and multimorbidity.  
- Biannual updates could still face contamination if models ingest post‑release or if cases appear in pre‑training corpora.

## Confidence

## Next Checks
1. *Contamination audit*: Evaluate models with known cutoffs on LiveClin vs. a static benchmark; LiveClin should show a larger performance gap correlated with cutoff dates.  
2. *Sequential necessity test*: Compare model performance on the original multi‑turn format versus a single‑turn version (all information upfront); a significant drop confirms sequential difficulty.  
3. *Longitudinal quality monitoring*: Recruit an independent physician cohort to re‑annotate random question samples from each update cycle; stable pass‑rates over cycles validate pipeline consistency.
