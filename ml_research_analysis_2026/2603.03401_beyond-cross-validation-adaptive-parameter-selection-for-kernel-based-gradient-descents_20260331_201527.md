# Beyond Cross-Validation: Adaptive Parameter Selection for Kernel-Based Gradient Descents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03401
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03401
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The paper introduces a hybrid selection strategy (HSS) combining hold-out with a Lepskii-type backward selection principle based on empirical effective dimension, achieving optimal generalization error bounds for kernel-based gradient descent across various kernels, target functions, and error metrics (L2, Linfinity).

## Executive Summary
Kernel-based gradient descent (KGD) is a powerful nonparametric method, but its performance heavily depends on the number of iterations. Traditional parameter selection via cross-validation lacks a clear bias‑variance analysis and can fail under covariate shift or unbounded outputs.  
This work proposes a hybrid selection strategy (HSS) that merges a data‑splitting hold‑out approach with a theoretically grounded backward stopping rule based on empirical effective dimension. The algorithm adaptively chooses the iteration count without discarding training data for validation during the final run. The authors prove that KGD with HSS attains minimax‑optimal rates for a wide range of kernels (polynomial, exponential, finite‑rank) and both L2 and L∞ error metrics, adapting to the smoothness of the target function and the kernel’s capacity. Numerical experiments on synthetic and real‑world datasets confirm that HSS consistently outperforms existing methods—including hold‑out, AIC, BIC, balancing principle, Lepskii principle, early stopping, and discrepancy principle—in prediction accuracy, computational efficiency, and robustness to covariate shift.

## Method Summary
HSS is formalized in Algorithm 1. Given a dataset \(D\), a subset \(L\) is randomly split into a training set \(D_{tr}\) (70%) and a validation set \(D_{val}\) (30%). The eigenvalues of the full kernel matrix \(K\) on \(D\) and of \(K_{tr}\) on \(D_{tr}\) are computed to obtain empirical effective dimensions \(N_D(\lambda)\) and \(N_{D_{tr}}(\lambda)\). Using these, the quantities \(W_{D,t}\) and \(W_{D_{tr},t}\) are pre‑computed for \(t=1,\dots,|D|\). A safe upper bound \(T\) on the iteration count is derived from a high‑probability inequality involving an auxiliary term \(U_{D,t,\delta}\). KGD is then run on both the full data and the training subset up to \(T\), producing iterates \(f_t\).  

For each candidate constant \(C_j\) from a predefined logarithmic set, the backward selection principle (BSP) is applied on the training runs: the largest \(t\) satisfying  
\(t\|f_{t+1}-f_t\|_{D_{tr}} + \sqrt{t}\|f_{t+1}-f_t\|_{K} \ge C_j\, W_{D_{tr},t}\)  
is selected as \(\hat{t}_j\). The constant that yields the smallest validation error (L2) on \(D_{val}\) is chosen, denoted \(C_{j^*}\). Finally, BSP is executed on the full data with constant \(C_{j^*}\) to obtain the final iteration \(\hat{t}^*\) and predictor \(f_{\hat{t}^*}\). The core innovation lies in using the weighted increment—interpreted as a bias‑variance surrogate—against a threshold proportional to the empirical effective dimension, enabling automatic, theoretically sound stopping.

## Key Results
- **Optimal rates:** HSS achieves minimax‑optimal generalization errors for kernels with polynomial, exponential, and finite‑rank eigen‑decay, adaptively recovering the smoothness index \(r\) and capacity index \(s\) in both L2 and L∞ norms.
- **Superior empirical performance:** Compared to hold‑out, AIC, BIC, balancing principle, Lepskii principle, early stopping, discrepancy principle, and fixed‑budget baselines, HSS yields lower prediction errors (L2 and L∞) and often terminates earlier, reducing computational cost.
- **Robustness to covariate shift:** Under increasing KL divergence between training and test distributions, HSS degrades less than traditional hold‑out, demonstrating stability beyond standard i.i.d. settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Backward Selection via Empirical Effective Dimension  
**Claim:** The backward selection principle (BSP) uses empirical effective dimension to quantify iteration increments, enabling a data‑driven stopping rule that balances bias and variance.  
**Mechanism:** For each iteration \(t\), compute the weighted increment  
\(t\|f_{t+1}-f_t\|_D + \sqrt{t}\|f_{t+1}-f_t\|_K\)  
and compare it to a scaled empirical effective dimension \(\tilde{C}\, W_{D,t}\). The largest \(t\) satisfying the inequality is selected. The empirical effective dimension \(N_D(t-1) = \operatorname{Tr}\bigl[(t-1)|D|I + K)^{-1}K\bigr]\) captures the effective number of eigenfunctions contributing to the approximation at iteration \(t-1\).  
**Core assumption:** The increment behaves like the sum of bias and variance terms, which are controlled by \(W_{D,t}\) (Proposition 4).  
**Evidence anchors:** Definition 1 and the role of \(N_D(t-1)\) (Section 2.1); Proposition 2 links \(W_{D,t}\) to bias‑variance bounds (Section 3.2). If \(\tilde{C}\) is too small or too large, BSP defaults to \(T = \max t\), losing adaptivity (Figure 2).

### Mechanism 2: Hybrid Constant Selection via Hold‑Out  
**Claim:** HSS circumvents the need for an optimal constant \(\tilde{C}\) by using a small validation set to select it, combining bias‑variance analysis with hold‑out.  
**Mechanism:** A subset \(L \subset D\) is split into \(D_{tr,L}\) and \(D_{val,L}\). For each candidate constant \(\hat{C}_j\), BSP is run on \(D_{tr,L}\) to get \(\hat{t}_j\). The constant minimizing validation error on \(D_{val,L}\) is chosen, then applied to the full dataset \(D\) to select \(\hat{t}^*\).  
**Core assumption:** The optimal constant \(\tilde{C}\) is independent of data size (Theorem 6), so selecting it on a subset generalizes.  
**Evidence anchors:** Algorithm 1 details the procedure (Section 2.1); Theorem 6 and Corollary 7 guarantee optimality if \(\min \hat{C}_j \ge \tilde{C}_0\) (an empirical condition). Too small \(L\) yields noisy validation error; too large reduces computational benefit; theory requires \(L \le |D|/U^{1/3}\) for complexity bounds.

### Mechanism 3: Optimal Generalization Error Bounds  
**Claim:** KGD with HSS achieves minimax‑optimal generalization error bounds that adapt to kernel eigenvalue decay (capacity index \(s\)) and target function regularity \(r\), across L2, L∞, and RKHS norms.  
**Mechanism:** The backward stopping rule aligns with the theoretical optimal iteration count \(t_0\) (Corollary 3), which depends on the kernel’s eigenvalue decay. The effective dimension \(N_D(t-1)\) implicitly estimates this decay, allowing adaptive selection.  
**Core assumption:** The target function \(f_\rho\) lies in the range of \(L_K^r\) (Assumption 2) and noise satisfies the Bernstein condition (Assumption 1).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.3 (Theorem 6, Corollaries 7–8) prove optimal rates for polynomial, exponential, and finite‑rank kernels and L∞ robustness; Section 4 experiments confirm HSS matches backward selection under L2 and outperforms hold‑out under L∞.

## Foundational Learning
- **RKHS and Kernel Operators**  
  *Why needed:* KGD operates in an RKHS; learning guarantees rely on the integral operator \(L_K\) and its eigenvalues.  
  *Quick check:* Given a Mercer kernel \(K\), define the RKHS norm \(\|f\|_K\) and the operator \(L_Kf = \int f(x)K_x\,d\rho_X\).

- **Empirical Risk Minimization and Gradient Descent in RKHS**  
  *Why needed:* KGD minimizes empirical \(L_2\) risk via gradient descent in the RKHS; its iterates have operator representations (A3).  
  *Quick check:* Derive the gradient of \(L(f) = \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i=1}^{|D|}(y_i - f(x_i))^2\) in the RKHS, leading to the update (3).

- **Effective Dimension and Capacity**  
  *Why needed:* The empirical effective dimension \(N_D(\lambda)\) estimates the effective number of eigenfunctions below threshold \(\lambda\), encoding kernel capacity.  
  *Quick check:* For kernel matrix eigenvalues \(\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_{|D|}\), compute \(N_D(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{|D|}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i + \lambda|D|}\).

- **Bias‑Variance Decomposition for Iterative Algorithms**  
  *Why needed:* HSS controls generalization error by bounding the noise‑free iterate difference (increment) as a surrogate for bias + variance.  
  *Quick check:* Write \(\|f_{t+1} - f_t\|_* \le \text{(bias term)} + \text{(variance term)}\) using Proposition 4.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data split → Eigenvalue computation → Effective dimension precomputation → Compute sudden stopping value \(T\) → Run KGD on training subset and full data → For each candidate constant, apply backward selection on training runs to get \(\hat{t}_j\) → Select constant \(j^*\) minimizing validation error → Apply backward selection on full data with chosen constant to obtain final \(\hat{t}^*\) → Output final predictor.

**Critical path:**  
- Eigenvalues must be computed before precomputing \(W_{D,t}\) and before KGD runs.  
- Backward selection requires running KGD up to \(T\) for each candidate constant on the training subset; this is the computational bottleneck.  
- Constant selection depends on validation error, so splitting precedes final backward selection.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *\(L\) vs. computational load:* Smaller \(L\) speeds up constant selection but increases variance of validation error; theory suggests \(L \le |D|/U^{1/3}\), while empirically \(L \approx 0.6|D|\) works well.  
- *Candidate set \(\mathcal{C}_U\):* Too few risks missing the optimal constant; too many increases cost. A log‑uniform search (e.g., \(C_0=100\), \(q=0.9\), \(U=20\)) balances.  
- *Backward vs. early stopping:* Backward search requires a full run to \(T\), while early stopping (e.g., ESR) may stop earlier but lacks adaptivity to \(r\). HSS’s overhead is dominated by the eigendecomposition, not the number of iterations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Condition (10) never holds (left side always below threshold) → all \(\hat{t}_j = T\). Diagnose by plotting left‑hand side vs. \(t\); reduce \(C_j\) if values are consistently below the threshold line.  
- Validation error curve flat or noisy → \(\mathcal{C}_U\) may not contain the optimal constant. Expand \(\mathcal{C}_U\) (increase \(C_0\) or use more candidates).  
- \(T\) computed too small (e.g., \(T=1\)) because \(U_{D,t,\delta}\) exceeds threshold early; check \(U_{D,t,\delta}\) values and consider a larger \(\delta\) (e.g., 0.1).  
- Eigenvalue computation fails or is inaccurate for large \(|D|\); consider approximate solvers (randomized SVD) but expect noise in effective dimension which may destabilize stopping.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **BSP feasibility:** Fix \(|D|\), vary \(\tilde{C}\) on synthetic data; confirm L2 and L∞ errors exhibit a clear minimum and that the selected iteration number varies smoothly with \(\tilde{C}\).  
2. **HSS vs. baselines:** Compare HSS with hold‑out, balancing principle, Lepskii principle, early stopping, discrepancy principle, AIC, BIC on L2 and L∞ across increasing \(|D|\); verify HSS matches backward selection under L2 and outperforms hold‑out under L∞.  
3. **Covariate shift robustness:** Train on uniform \([0,1]^d\), test on \([0,b]^d\) with \(b>1\); measure performance degradation relative to KL divergence; confirm HSS degrades less than hold‑out.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Tightening the pessimistic constant \(\tilde{C}_0\):** The theoretical bound includes \(\tilde{C}_0 = 32\sqrt{2}(1+\beta)(\kappa M+\gamma)\), which may be pessimistic. A more delicate analysis could reduce it, potentially improving practical parameter selection.  
- **Extension to distributed/federated learning:** HSS currently assumes centralized data. Adapting it to distributed settings (e.g., each local agent selects constants based on its own local data) while preserving optimal generalization and ensuring privacy remains open.  
- **Adaptive selection for spherical data with deterministic sampling:** Many spherical datasets (e.g., satellite data) use deterministic sampling. Designing a fully adaptive KGD parameter selection that exploits the spherical geometry and deterministic design is an interesting direction for future research.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter tuning (step size \(\beta\), constant candidate set \(C_0\), confidence \(\delta\), subset size \(L\)) lacks theoretical guidance, often requiring dataset‑specific adjustments.  
- Full eigendecomposition costs \(O(|D|^3)\), limiting scalability; approximate eigensolvers introduce noise that may affect the empirical effective dimension and thus the stopping decision.  
- Theoretical assumptions (Mercer kernel, compact domain, bounded outputs, Bernstein noise) may not hold in real‑world applications, potentially invalidating the optimality guarantees.

## Confidence
- **Backward selection principle based on empirical effective dimension:** Medium  
- **Hybrid constant selection combining hold‑out:** Medium  
- **Overall optimality and superior empirical performance of HSS:** High
