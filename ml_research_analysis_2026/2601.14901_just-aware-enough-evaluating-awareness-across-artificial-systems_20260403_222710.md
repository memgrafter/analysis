# Just aware enough: Evaluating awareness across artificial systems

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.14901
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14901
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Intermediate awareness levels in a robot swarm yielded optimal efficiency (Lee et al., 2026).

## Executive Summary
The paper argues that current debates on AI consciousness are too contested and theoretically loaded to serve practical evaluation needs. Instead, it proposes *awareness*—a system’s capacity to process, store, and use information for goal‑directed action—as a more methodologically tractable target. The authors outline four desiderata for any evaluation framework (domain‑sensitivity, scalability, multidimensionality, ability‑oriented prediction) and present a structured approach based on five provisional dimensions (spatial, temporal, metacognitive, agentive, self‑awareness). Each dimension is linked to specific action‑perception abilities, which are probed with domain‑adapted task batteries to generate multidimensional awareness profiles. These profiles enable fair comparison across heterogeneous AI systems, regardless of architecture or scale. An empirical illustration using a robot swarm (Lee et al., 2026) demonstrates the “just aware enough” principle: intermediate levels of awareness produced the highest efficiency, suggesting that optimal performance may not require maximal awareness. The work thus provides a principled method for assessing AI capabilities that can guide design, oversight, and policy.

## Method Summary
The proposed evaluation framework operationalizes awareness through three linked elements: (i) **Dimensions** that partition the space of relevant informational domains (e.g., spatial, temporal); (ii) **Abilities** as stable, generalizable action‑perception dispositions within each dimension; and (iii) **Task batteries** that probe these abilities with paradigms adapted to the system’s sensorimotor and architectural constraints. For a given system and domain, the evaluator first selects the most salient dimensions, then defines concrete abilities aligned with those dimensions, and finally designs a set of tasks (with multiple variants that test reliability, robustness, and flexibility) to measure performance. The results are aggregated into a normalized profile vector that represents the system’s degree of awareness across dimensions. This profile can be compared across different configurations of the same system, between similar architectures, or even between fundamentally heterogeneous systems, because the focus is on functionally equivalent abilities rather than raw task scores.

## Key Results
- Awareness, defined as context‑sensitive information processing for goal‑directed action, provides a tractable and gradable alternative to consciousness for AI evaluation.
- The framework satisfies four desiderata (domain‑sensitivity, scalability, multidimensionality, ability‑oriented prediction) and uses five provisional dimensions to generate comparative awareness profiles.
- Empirical illustration (Lee et al., 2026) shows that intermediate awareness levels in a robot swarm yield optimal efficiency, supporting the “just aware enough” principle.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Shifting the evaluation target from "consciousness" to "awareness" enables principled, comparative assessment of heterogeneous AI systems.
*   **Mechanism:** By defining awareness functionally—as a system's context-sensitive information processing abilities for goal-directed action—the framework sidesteps intractable metaphysical debates about phenomenal experience. It replaces a binary, agent-centric, and theoretically fraught concept with a gradable, system-centric, and operationally focused one. This makes cross-architecture comparison possible.
*   **Core assumption:** The primary near-term need is to evaluate and compare system *capabilities* for design and oversight, not to resolve the hard problem of consciousness.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] The paper states awareness provides a "more productive and methodologically tractable alternative" for evaluating AI, avoiding the "conceptual baggage" of consciousness.
    *   [section 2] Argues debates on AI consciousness are mired in confusion, theoretical disagreement, and lack consensus, making them ill-suited for urgent practical evaluation needs.
    *   [corpus] **Weak anchor.** Neighboring papers (e.g., "Intelligence as a Measure of Consciousness", "Preliminaries to artificial consciousness") actively pursue consciousness evaluation, highlighting the field's theoretical division and the novelty of the awareness-as-alternative claim.
*   **Break condition:** If a consensus emerges on a validated, theory-neutral measure of consciousness that demonstrably outperforms awareness evaluations for design and oversight.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** A structured, multidimensional profile based on domain-adapted task batteries can fairly compare awareness across disparate systems.
*   **Mechanism:** The framework operationalizes evaluation through three linked elements: (i) **Dimensions** (e.g., spatial, temporal) that partition the space of relevant informational domains; (ii) **Abilities** (e.g., navigation, sequencing) as stable, generalizable action-perception dispositions within dimensions; and (iii) **Task batteries** that probe these abilities with paradigms adapted to each system's sensorimotor and architectural constraints. A system's performance across this battery yields a multidimensional "profile."
*   **Core assumption:** Meaningful cross-system comparison is possible by evaluating functionally equivalent abilities through system-sensitive tasks, even when surface features differ radically.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 4.1] Explicitly outlines the three-part structure (dimensions, abilities, tasks) and its role in satisfying the desiderata (domain-sensitivity, multidimensionality).
    *   [section 4.2] Details five provisional dimensions and notes they are "conceptually separable" yet expected to overlap in practice.
    *   [section 4.3] Describes how profiles enable four types of comparison (single-system variations, congruent systems, heterogeneous systems, biological organisms).
*   **Break condition:** If it is proven that core awareness abilities cannot be validly isolated into separable dimensions or that task adaptation inevitably distorts the measured ability beyond meaningful comparison.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** An ability-oriented evaluation (focusing on reliable, non-coincidental success across contexts) predicts task performance and generalizes better than single-task metrics.
*   **Mechanism:** The framework distinguishes between isolated *performance* (a one-off success, possibly gaming or constrained) and *ability* (a disposition to reliably succeed across an appropriate range of conditions). Evaluation must use a battery of varied tasks to establish reliability, robustness, and flexibility, thereby approximating a system's genuine capacities. This aligns with the "just aware enough" principle: optimal system performance may correlate with intermediate, task-appropriate levels of ability, not maximal information processing.
*   **Core assumption:** Ethical oversight and robust design require knowledge of a system's generalizable competencies, not just its peak performance on a benchmark.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3.4] Argues forcefully against task-oriented evaluation for anything but highly constrained systems, advocating for ability-oriented measures that account for reliability, competence vs. performance, and non-coincidence (citing Harding & Sharadin, 2024).
    *   [section 5] States awareness requires "reliable, non-coincidental abilities that generalise across appropriate variations in context," contrasting with a "brittle, coincidental" thermostat.
    *   [section 4.3] References the Lee et al. (2026) empirical finding: "intermediate levels of awareness yielded optimal efficiency," demonstrating the "just aware enough" principle.
*   **Break condition:** If empirical studies show that single, well-designed task metrics are perfectly predictive of real-world deployment success and failure modes for all relevant system types.

## Foundational Learning
### Awareness as Capacity vs. Transient
*   **Why needed:** The paper adopts the *capacity view* (a standing disposition) for its framework. Understanding this choice is crucial, as the *transient view* (episodic states) would lead to different evaluation strategies (state-detection vs. ability-profiling).
*   **Quick check:** Does the profile method measure what a system *can* do in principle (capacity), or what it *is doing* right now (transient)? Which view is assumed in section 4?

### The Four Desiderata (Domain‑Sensitivity, Multidimensionality, Scale‑Neutrality, Ability‑Orientation)
*   **Why needed:** These are the non‑negotiable constraints that shaped the proposed solution. Every design choice in the framework (dimensions, tasks, profiles) is a response to one or more of these criteria.
*   **Quick check:** Why would a universal, one‑size‑fits‑all test battery fail the "domain‑sensitive" and "deployable at different scales" desiderata?

### Abilities as Multi‑Track Dispositions (Harding & Sharadin)
*   **Why needed:** This philosophical notion defines the unit of measurement. It's what separates a true awareness capability from a fluke or a brittle, overfitted response. It directly addresses the "reliable, non‑incidental" requirement.
*   **Quick check:** How does the definition of an ability ("it would reliably succeed at φ when it *tries* under appropriate conditions") differ from simply measuring average success rate on a fixed dataset?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The evaluation pipeline flows as follows:

**Domain Analyzer** → **Dimension & Ability Mapper** → **Task Battery Generator** → **Profiler & Comparator** → **Just Aware Enough Analyzer**

**Domain Analyzer:** Takes system's operational context (e.g., warehouse logistics, surgical assistance). Outputs key constraints (sensors, actuators, goal structure) and candidate relevant dimensions.  
**Dimension & Ability Mapper:** Maps the five provisional dimensions (or others) to specific, testable abilities *for this domain* (e.g., for spatial: "localize via landmark recognition," "plan path avoiding dynamic obstacles").  
**Task Battery Generator:** For each ability, designs a set of 3+ task variants. Variants systematically vary conditions (lighting, noise, obstacle layout, partner mismatch) to test reliability, robustness, flexibility. Tasks must be implemented using the system's native I/O.  
**Profiler & Comparator:** Executes battery, aggregates performance metrics per ability/dimension (not just accuracy, but e.g., degradation under perturbation, generalization gap). Generates a normalized profile vector. Contains comparator logic for single-system configs, similar architectures, and heterogeneous systems.  
**Just Aware Enough Analyzer:** (Optional) Correlates profile vectors with primary system performance metrics (e.g., efficiency, safety incidents) to identify non‑linear relationships and potential "sweet spots" of awareness.

### Critical Path
Domain Analysis → Ability Specification → Task Design → Execution → Profile Generation → Comparison/Analysis.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Breadth vs. Depth:** Evaluating all five dimensions in depth is expensive. Tradeoff: select 2‑3 *salient* dimensions based on domain analysis.
- **Task Fidelity vs. Comparability:** Highly ecologically valid tasks (real warehouse) may not be comparable to simulated tasks for a medical AI. Tradeoff: use abstract task *principles* (e.g., "track a target through occlusions") with concrete, system‑specific implementations.
- **Profile Resolution vs. Practicality:** More dimensions/abilities yield finer‑grained profiles but increase evaluation cost. Start with a minimal viable profile (e.g., Spatial + Temporal + one other).

### Failure Signatures
- **Task Overfit:** System excels on battery but fails in novel real‑world scenarios. (Insufficient ability generalization test).
- **Profile Myopia:** Evaluation shows high scores in all dimensions, but system is unsafe in deployment. (Missed emergent interactions between dimensions or un‑evaluated critical failure modes).
- **Scale Confusion:** Treating a swarm's collective behavior as an individual robot's ability, or vice‑versa, leading to invalid attribution.
- **Anthropomorphic Benchmarking:** Using human‑centric task designs for a non‑bio‑inspired architecture, measuring the wrong thing.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Intra‑System Calibration:** Take a modular robot (e.g., with SLAM, simple planner, fault sensor). Disable/enable components to create 4‑5 configurations. Run a battery targeting Spatial (navigation mazes with obstacles) and Bodily Awareness (simulated faults). Plot efficiency vs. profile. Expected: find an intermediate optimum ("just aware enough").
2. **Congruent System Comparison:** Evaluate two LLM‑based dialogue agents (same architecture, different training data/tasks) on dimensions of Metacognitive (confidence calibration, self‑correction) and Agentive (recognizing user's communicative intent via dialogue acts). Compare profiles. Expected: differences in metacognitive profile correlate with trust scores in human trials.
3. **Heterogeneous System Proof‑of‑Concept:** Profile a delivery drone swarm (Spatial: flocking, collision avoidance; Temporal: coordinated timing) and a static medical image classifier (Spatial: lesion localization; Temporal: none; Metacognitive: uncertainty estimation on ambiguous scans). Use a common abstract principle (e.g., "detect and respond to relevant change in environment") to generate domain‑specific tasks. Expected: Both show high Spatial awareness but in fundamentally different instantiations (dynamic vs. static spatial reasoning), visible in profile.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Under what specific contextual conditions does increasing a system's awareness profile optimize versus degrade its task performance (“just aware enough” principle)?**  
   *Basis:* The Lee et al. (2026) illustration found intermediate awareness levels in a robot swarm yielded optimal efficiency, but the authors state this principle needs broader validation.  
   *Why unresolved:* The principle is based on a single intralogistics scenario; it is unclear how generalizable the optimal awareness threshold is across domains, system types, or task structures.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic experiments varying awareness dimensions (spatial, temporal, etc.) across multiple, diverse task environments (e.g., collaborative assembly vs. competitive foraging) to map performance curves and identify domain‑specific inflection points where added awareness ceases to be beneficial.

2. **What is the appropriate evaluative scale (individual component vs. collective) for systems with emergent, distributed architectures, and how do awareness profiles at different scales relate?**  
   *Basis:* Section 3.3 frames this as an open methodological challenge: “what is the appropriate scale for evaluation?” It notes that evaluating individual drones may miss swarm‑level competencies, while treating every collection as unified may obscure important differences.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper provides no criteria for determining when a collective should be treated as the unit of analysis. It explicitly avoids resolving the “special composition question.”  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Empirical studies comparing awareness profiles measured at the agent‑level and the collective‑level for the same multi‑agent system, using the same task battery. Correlating these profiles with emergent collective outcomes would reveal which scale best predicts functional success.

3. **How can the proposed ability‑oriented evaluation be operationalized to reliably distinguish genuine, generalizable awareness abilities from narrow task‑specific competence or performance artifacts (e.g., gaming, memorization)?**  
   *Basis:* The framework emphasizes evaluating “abilities” rather than performance (Section 3.4, 4.1), drawing on Harding & Sharadin (2024). However, it leaves the concrete design of “task batteries that probe these abilities in species‑ and system‑sensitive ways” entirely open, acknowledging this as a non‑trivial implementation challenge.  
   *Why unresolved:* Without specific, validated task paradigms and metrics (e.g., for robustness, flexibility, generalization), the ability‑oriented approach risks being applied inconsistently, potentially failing to avoid false positives (coincidental success) and false negatives (constraint‑masked competence).  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Development and cross‑validation of standardized task batteries for key awareness abilities (e.g., temporal prediction, fault monitoring) across multiple artificial systems within a domain. Success would be demonstrated by these batteries reliably differentiating between systems with known mechanistic differences (e.g., a reactive controller vs. a predictive model) and correlating with performance under novel perturbations.

4. **To what extent are the five provisional dimensions (spatial, temporal, metacognitive, agentive, self‑awareness) psychologically or computationally separable versus integral in artificial systems?**  
   *Basis:* The authors propose five dimensions as “conceptually separable” (page 13) and note that “separable dimensions are simpler to work with, integral dimensions would prove theoretically informative.” However, they state that their interdependencies are an open question: “whether some dimensions are more fundamental… or whether this set admits of further refinement.”  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper presents a conceptual schema but provides no empirical data on the statistical independence or co‑dependence of these dimensions across real‑world AI/robotic systems. This is critical for validating the dimensional structure and for efficient evaluation (can dimensions be tested independently?).  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Factor analytic studies measuring performance across a wide array of tasks designed to tap each proposed dimension across a diverse set of artificial systems. Evidence of separable factors would support the current schema; evidence of strong, systematic couplings (integrality) would necessitate a revised, potentially hierarchical, dimensional model.

## Limitations
- The five provisional dimensions lack standardized operational definitions and validated task batteries, hindering direct replication.
- No quantitative method is provided to equate awareness degrees across fundamentally different substrates (e.g., neural vs. swarm), risking invalid cross‑system comparisons.
- Empirical support is limited to a single swarm study; the "just aware enough" principle and generalizability to other domains remain unverified.

## Confidence
- Awareness as a tractable alternative to consciousness: **Medium**
- Multidimensional profiling enables fair cross‑architecture comparison: **Medium**
- Ability‑oriented evaluation predicts task performance & "just aware enough": **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Standardize a battery for two dimensions** (e.g., spatial and temporal) and apply it to at least three distinct AI systems (embodied robot, LLM, game‑playing agent) to assess profile consistency and comparative validity.
2. **Test predictive power:** Correlate awareness profiles (across multiple dimensions) with real‑world deployment metrics (efficiency, safety incidents, human‑robot teamwork quality) in a controlled field study.
3. **Probe boundary conditions:** Systematically vary a single system's capabilities (e.g., add/remove memory modules) to see if profile changes match expected "sweet spot" and whether the relationship generalizes across task domains.
