# Predicting first-episode homelessness among US Veterans using longitudinal EHR data: time-varying models and social risk factors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02731  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02731  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Time-varying EHR representations with clinician-informed condition persistence rules and social/behavioral factors improved discrimination (PR-AUC gains 15-30%) and concentrated risk (top 1% PPV 11.65-13.80% at 12 months).

## Executive Summary
The study developed and evaluated models to predict first-episode homelessness among US Veterans using longitudinal electronic health record (EHR) data from over 4.3 million patients. Comparing static versus time-varying representations of clinical and social risk factors, the authors found that time-varying models incorporating clinician-defined condition persistence rules (e.g., chronic vs. episodic) significantly improved discrimination. Adding social and behavioral factors (SBFH) further boosted performance. Although overall positive predictive values were modest, risk scores were highly concentrated, with the top 1% of predicted risk capturing nearly 10-15% of future cases. Classical gradient boosting (XGBoost) and transformer-based models (ModernBERT) performed best; large language models (LLMs) showed smaller racial performance gaps despite lower discrimination. The work supports the value of longitudinal EHR modeling and targeted outreach for homelessness prevention.

## Method Summary
The authors used national VA EHR data for veterans with ≥1 visit in 2016 (N=4,276,403), excluding those with prior homelessness. Predictors included demographics, service utilization, mental/physical/substance disorders (ICD-10), and social/behavioral factors (Z-codes, ADI). The outcome was first homelessness in 2017, assessed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. They created static (ever/never) and time-varying representations, where time-varying features aggregated data into half-year or quarterly intervals and applied clinician-informed persistence rules (chronic persistent, recurrent time-limited, ever-history, episodic) to model condition trajectories. Models trained included elastic net, random forest, XGBoost (tabular), masked language models (ModernBERT, BioClinicalBERT), and LLMs (Llama, OpenBio) using natural language prompts. A 92/3/5 train/validation/test split was used, with downsampling for class balance. Hyperparameters were tuned on validation PR-AUC. Evaluation focused on PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and risk-tier metrics (top 0.5%, 1%, 5% sensitivity, specificity, PPV, O/E). Subgroup fairness by race, age, ethnicity was examined.

## Key Results
- Time-varying representations with persistence rules consistently outperformed static models and ablations without persistence.
- Adding SBFH to demographics plus clinical codes improved PR-AUC by 15–30% across prediction windows.
- Best models: time-varying ModernBERT at 3 and 9 months; XGBoost at 6 and 12 months.
- In the top 1% risk tier, PPV increased from 3.93–4.72% at 3 months to 11.65–13.80% at 12 months, capturing 9.76–14.72% of future cases.
- LLMs underperformed in discrimination but exhibited smaller racial performance gaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Clinician-informed condition persistence rules applied to time-varying EHR representations improve discrimination for first-episode homelessness prediction.
- **Mechanism:**(raw visit data → aggregated intervals → clinician-defined "active duration" rules). Diagnoses/social risks are not simply binary indicators for an interval but are "filled forward" according to their expected clinical course (e.g., chronic conditions remain active indefinitely; episodic conditions reset each interval). This creates trajectories that better capture underlying disease/social need burden than naive aggregation.
- **Core assumption:** Clinician-specified persistence windows (e.g., "recurrent time-limited: 2 quarters") approximate the true prognostic window of conditions influencing homelessness risk more accurately than treating each interval as independent.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "incorporating clinically informed time-varying features improved PR-AUCs in all but one head-to-head comparison with static representations" and "ablating the condition persistence rules... reduced performance".
  - [section 2.2] Table 1 shows time-varying + persistence generally outperforms time-varying without persistence (ablation) and static models.
  - [section 4.2] Details the four persistence modes (chronic persistent, recurrent time-limited, ever-history, episodic) and clinician derivation.
- **Break condition:** If the observation window is too short relative to the true persistence window of a condition, rules may over-smooth or misrepresent recency effects. Performance gains may diminish for prediction horizons shorter than the persistence timeout.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Incorporating routinely collected Social and Behavioral Factors of Health (SBFH) into longitudinal models yields consistent gains in discrimination beyond demographics and clinical codes.
- **Mechanism:** (structured EHR Z-codes/stop codes → time-varying SBFH features → model input). SBFH captures proximal social determinants (housing problems, legal issues, violence) that are direct precipitants of homelessness, complementing distal clinical comorbidities.
- **Core assumption:** VA EHR's structured social risk documentation, while incomplete, contains signal about current instability that is temporally aligned with homelessness onset and not fully collinear with clinical diagnoses.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "incorporating social and behavioral factors... improved PR-AUC by 15-30%".
  - [section 2.2] Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 4 show stepwise PR-AUC gains when adding SBFH to demographics+clinical codes across models/windows, with largest absolute gains at longer horizons (6-12 months).
  - [section 2.4] SHAP analyses show housing/violence problems as frequent top positive predictors, supporting their discriminative role.
- **Break condition:** If SBFH are severely under-coded or documented primarily during crisis visits, they may introduce selection bias or leakage (e.g., homelessness-coded visit itself triggers SBFH capture). Gains may not generalize to settings with less systematic SBFH screening.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Model risk scores are highly concentrated, enabling efficient targeting: the top 1-5% of predicted risk captures a disproportionate share of future homelessness cases with very high specificity.
- **Mechanism:** (model outputs → rank ordering → top-P tier selection). Even with modest PR-AUC, the rank ordering places most future cases in a small high-risk tail, due to the extreme outcome rarity (~1% at 12mo) and the model's ability to separate signal from noise.
- **Core assumption:** The decision problem is triage/intervention targeting, not perfect prediction. A modest lift in the highest-risk percentile is operationally valuable if it concentrates scarce outreach resources.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "In the top 1% risk tier... captured 9.76-14.72% of future cases" with "specificity 99%".
  - [section 2.3] Table 2 shows PPV rising from ~4% (3mo) to ~14% (12mo) at top 1%, with O/E ratios 5-15×, confirming strong enrichment.
  - [discussion] "small, high-risk tiers (top 1-5%) can substantially concentrate future homelessness risk while preserving very high specificity".
- **Break condition:** If implementation costs of outreach are extremely high or harms of false positives are severe, even a 14% PPV may be insufficient. Concentration relies on a stable risk distribution over time; concept drift could degrade tier performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Temporal Representation in EHRs**
  - *Why needed here:* The core methodological innovation is moving from static "ever/never" features to time-varying sequences with persistence rules. Understanding how raw timestamps are aggregated (quarterly/half-year) and how conditions are carried forward is essential to interpret model inputs and results.
  - *Quick check question:* If a veteran has a documented substance use disorder in January 2016 and no further mentions, how would a "recurrent time-limited, T=2 quarters" rule affect their feature vector in the H2 (July-Dec) interval?
- **Concept: Extreme Class Imbalance & Evaluation Metrics**
  - *Why needed here:* Outcome prevalence is 0.32-1.19%. ROC-AUC can be misleadingly high; PR-AUC is the primary metric. Understanding why PPV is low despite enrichment, and why sensitivity/PPV trade-offs differ by risk-tier size, is crucial for operational interpretation.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does a model with PR-AUC=2% at 3 months (prevalence 0.32%) represent meaningful performance, and what does that imply for the top 1% risk tier's PPV?
- **Concept: SHAP Values for Multivariable Interpretability**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper uses Kernel SHAP to rank predictor importance. This requires understanding that SHAP values are *contribution to model output* in a *specific multivariable context*, not causal effects. They explain "what the model uses" not "what causes homelessness".
  - *Quick check question:* Why might "being married" appear as a negative predictor in SHAP analyses, and how should that be interpreted given known protective effects of social support?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data pipeline: VA CDW EHR → cohort definition (2016 visits, no prior homelessness) → feature extraction (79 predictors across 6 domains) → outcome ascertainment (2017 homelessness indicators). Feature engineering: Static vs. time-varying aggregation (quarterly/half-year) → apply clinician-informed persistence rules (fill strategy) → generate interval-specific binary/count features. Model inputs: Tabular (for ML) or text prompt (for MLM/LLM) organized by domain/time. Prompt templates differ by prediction window (M months). Model classes: Classical ML (Elastic Net, RF, XGBoost); Masked LM (ModernBERT, BioClinicalBERT); LLM (Llama, OpenBioLoRA). Training/validation: 92/3/5 split (patient-level), downsampled training for class balance, hyperparameter search (ML) or fixed config (transformers/LLMs), validation PR-AUC for early stopping/model selection. Evaluation: Test-set PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and risk-tier metrics (top 0.5%, 1%, 5%: sensitivity, specificity, PPV, O/E). Subgroup fairness (race/age/ethnicity) via PR-AUC heterogeneity tests and worst-group analysis.

**Critical path:**
1. Define cohort & outcome (ensures no leakage from future homelessness codes into 2016 observation).
2. Implement persistence framework *before* train/val/test split (applies only within observation window).
3. Select temporal granularity (quarterly vs half-year) via validation PR-AUC per model/window.
4. Train models with identical splits for fair comparison.
5. Evaluate on test set with bootstrapped CIs; conduct subgroup analyses with reliability filters (≥20 events per subgroup).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Time-varying vs. static: Time-varying captures trajectories but increases sparsity; persistence rules add interpretability but are heuristics.
- Tabular vs. text-based models: Tabular (XGBoost) is data-efficient and interpretable; text-based (ModernBERT) may capture interactions but requires careful prompt engineering and more compute.
- PR-AUC vs. operational metrics: PR-AUC optimizes ranking; for outreach, one cares about top-tier PPV and sensitivity. Model selection may differ by use-case.
- Fairness trade-off: Models minimizing racial PR-AUC gaps (e.g., Llama) may have lower worst-group PR-AUC. Choice depends on equity objective.

**Failure signatures:**
- Calibration failure: Even with good PR-AUC, predicted risk percentages may not match observed rates in top tiers (check PPV vs. predicted probability deciles).
- Subgroup collapse: Very low/zero events in a subgroup (e.g., American Indian veterans) yield unreliable estimates; monitor CI width and event counts.
- Temporal instability: Using pre-2016 data, but patterns may shift post-pandemic or after EHR transitions (noted as limitation).
- SBFH under-ascertainment: If key social risks (e.g., eviction) are rarely coded, model reliance on available Z-codes may misrepresent true social risk.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Ablate persistence rules:** Train time-varying models *without* the fill strategy (TV w/o Fill Strategy in Table S2) to isolate the contribution of clinician-informed persistence over naive aggregation.
2. **Incrementally add SBFH:** Train each model class with (Demographics) → (Demographics+Clinical Codes) → (Full: +SBFH) to quantify the incremental PR-AUC gain from social factors, separately by prediction window.
3. **Compare model classes on top-tier PPV:** For the 12-month horizon, compute PPV at top 1% and top 5% for all time-varying models. Identify which model maximizes case capture (sensitivity) at a fixed specificity (~99%), as this directly informs outreach efficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can social and behavioral factors of health (SBFH) be more completely captured from EHR data to improve homelessness risk prediction?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note that SBFH from structured EHR fields likely undercapture true social risks and state that "richer SBFH ascertainment (for example, via NLP and LLM-based extraction from clinical text)" is needed.
- **Why unresolved:** Under‑ascertainment may lead to outcome and predictor misclassification, limiting model performance and generalizability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Studies applying natural language processing or large language models to extract SBFH from narrative clinical notes, followed by head‑to‑head comparison of prediction models with and without these extracted features.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the optimal condition persistence rules (mode and timeout T) for different diagnoses in time‑varying EHR representations?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors write: "we evaluated only a limited set of temporal granularities and condition persistence rules; although these rules improved discrimination in most settings, they remain approximations and may over‑smooth some conditions. Refining and externally validating persistence choices is an important area for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** Persistence parameters were chosen a priori via clinician consultation; different timeouts may better reflect the natural history of conditions and improve predictive accuracy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation across multiple candidate T values for each condition (or condition groups) using validation data, possibly combined with external validation on independent cohorts to identify the most effective persistence configurations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the net clinical benefit of using homelessness risk scores to guide targeted outreach, and how should they be integrated into clinical workflows?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors call for "decision‑analytic evaluation such as decision curve analysis and net‑benefit frameworks" and "impact evaluations co‑designed with veterans, clinicians, and housing partners" to ensure equitable and useful implementation.
- **Why unresolved:** Positive predictive values remain modest (e.g., 3.93%–13.80% at the top 1%), creating uncertainty about whether screening yields a favorable benefit‑to‑burden ratio compared to alternative interventions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Decision‑curve analysis to determine optimal risk thresholds, prospective implementation trials measuring housing stability outcomes and resource use, and human‑centered design studies to create usable EHR‑embedded decision support tools.

## Limitations
- Predictor code lists and persistence rule assignments not fully disclosed, blocking exact replication.
- Generalizability of SBFH gains uncertain due to potential under‑ascertainment and outcome‑induced coding leakage.
- Findings may not generalize post‑2016 due to temporal shifts in EHR practices or veteran populations.

## Confidence
- **Time‑varying + persistence rules improve discrimination:** High (consistent ablation and head‑to‑head results).
- **SBFH adds incremental predictive value:** Medium (supported by SHAP and stepwise gains, but reliant on VA‑specific coding completeness).
- **Risk concentration enables efficient targeting:** High (robust top‑tier PPV/O/E across horizons).
- **LLMs reduce racial performance gaps:** Low (observed in subgroup tests but with overall poor discrimination; may be artifact of regularization or prompt design).

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate persistence ablation:** Independently train time‑varying models *without* clinician persistence rules (naive forward‑filling) to confirm the measured performance delta is solely attributable to the persistence framework.
2. **Test for SBFH leakage:** Re‑run analyses excluding any SBFH (Z‑code) entries recorded *after* the first homelessness‑coded visit in 2017 to assess whether gains are inflated by outcome‑induced coding.
3. **Assess temporal transportability:** Apply the final model to a hold‑out veteran cohort from 2018‑2019 to evaluate degradation in PR‑AUC and top‑tier PPV, quantifying the impact of potential concept drift.
