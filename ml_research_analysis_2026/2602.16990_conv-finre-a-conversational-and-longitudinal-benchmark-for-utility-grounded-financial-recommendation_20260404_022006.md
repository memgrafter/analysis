# Conv-FinRe: A Conversational and Longitudinal Benchmark for Utility-Grounded Financial Recommendation

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.16990
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16990
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Multi-view evaluation reveals a persistent trade-off: LLMs with high utility-based alignment often exhibit low behavioral recovery, and vice versa, demonstrating that behavior-only evaluation is insufficient for financial advice.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Conv-FinRe, a novel benchmark for evaluating language models on financial recommendations within longitudinal, conversational contexts. The core innovation is a multi-view evaluation framework that disentangles a model's alignment with observed user behavior from its alignment with a normative, utility-grounded "rational" advisor. By generating four distinct reference rankings—User Choice, Rational Utility, Market Momentum, and Risk Sensitivity—from the same underlying data, the benchmark diagnoses whether a model mimics noisy user actions, optimizes a mathematically derived utility, chases trends, or prioritizes safety. Evaluations on multiple LLMs reveal a fundamental tension: achieving high utility-based quality often comes at the cost of behavioral mimicry and risk sensitivity, highlighting critical limitations in single-view evaluations for high-stakes domains.

## Method Summary
The benchmark pipeline first ingests market data and user profiles from a simulation tool. It then uses inverse optimization—fitting a regularized Multinomial Logit model to a user's longitudinal stock-selection sequence—to estimate stable, person-specific risk parameters (λ, γ). These parameters define the Rational Utility ranking (maximizing mean return minus risk penalties) and the Risk Sensitivity ranking (minimizing the risk penalty alone). A separate ranking based on recent returns defines Market Momentum. These, along with the actual User Choice ranking, serve as references.

Simulated conversational dialogues are constructed, weaving together onboarding interviews, historical interaction turns, market snapshots, and messages from three "expert" bots (each advocating one of the first three views). For each step, an LLM is prompted with this context to produce a ranked stock list. Finally, model outputs are evaluated using uNDCG (vs. Utility), MRR/HR@K (vs. User Choice), and Kendall’s τ (vs. each expert ranking), with scores aggregated to profile the model's advisory strategy.

## Key Results
*   **A fundamental trade-off exists:** Models like Llama-3.3-70B achieve near-optimal utility alignment (uNDCG up to 0.97) but show poor behavioral recovery (HR@1 down to 0.54), while others like Qwen2.5-72B excel at mimicking user choices but neglect long-term utility and risk.
*   **Risk alignment is particularly low:** Even utility-aligned models exhibit weak τ(Risk) scores (e.g., 0.17 for Llama-3.3-70B), indicating a systemic failure to balance return objectives with downside protection.
*   **Expert alignment patterns diagnose strategy:** DeepSeek-V3.2 shows the most balanced profile across Utility, Momentum, and Risk, while others are strongly coupled to momentum or user empathy, providing a diagnostic spectrum of advisory biases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multi-View Disentanglement
*   **Claim:** Creating four divergent reference rankings from the same data disentangles behavioral imitation from decision quality by providing orthogonal diagnostic signals.
*   **Mechanism:** The views represent distinct advisory principles: descriptive (User Choice), normative (Rational Utility), trend-following (Market Momentum), and risk-averse (Risk Sensitivity). A model's pattern of alignment across these views diagnoses its underlying heuristic—e.g., high User Choice alignment but low Rational Utility alignment indicates overfitting to noisy, short-sighted behavior.
*   **Core Assumption:** Observed user choices are a noisy and potentially suboptimal indicator of long-term utility; separating this signal from normative, momentum, and risk signals allows for principled diagnosis.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] "...evaluates LLMs beyond behavior matching... multi-view references that distinguish descriptive behavior from normative utility..." ; [section 4.1] Direct comparison of top models shows inverse correlations between uNDCG and HR@K, proving the views are not redundant.
*   **Break Condition:** If the expert rankings become highly correlated (e.g., during a bull market where high-momentum assets also have high utility), the diagnostic power collapses as τ scores become collinear.

### Mechanism 2: Inverse Optimization for Latent Preferences
*   **Claim:** Inverse optimization of longitudinal choices reliably infers a user-specific, time-invariant risk preference (λ, γ) that grounds a personalized utility function without explicit labeling.
*   **Mechanism:** Assuming a rational actor model with Gumbel noise (Multinomial Logit), the user's sequence of choices is used to minimize a regularized negative log-likelihood loss. The estimated (λ, γ) then parameterizes the Rational Utility and Risk rankings.
*   **Core Assumption:** A user's noisy, real-world selections can be explained by a stable, risk-sensitive utility maximization process. The model correctly identifies the latent parameters from behavioral noise.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [section 3.2] Full specification of the utility function U(s) and the probabilistic choice model P(s*|λ,γ). [section 3.3.2] Validation via user summaries yields a high average agreement score of 7.8/9.
*   **Break Condition:** If user preferences are non-stationary (λ_t, γ_t change) or driven by unmodeled factors (ethics, liquidity), the inferred parameters will not represent a stable utility, corrupting the references.

### Mechanism 3: Expert Alignment Scores for Strategy Diagnosis
*   **Claim:** Measuring Kendall’s τ alignment between model output and three expert rankings (Utility, Momentum, Risk) provides a clear, aggregated diagnostic of the model's advisory strategy and biases.
*   **Mechanism:** For each model output ranking π_i,t, compute τ(π_util), τ(π_mom), τ(π_safe). The pattern of these scores—e.g., high τ(Momentum) & τ(Utility) but low τ(Risk)—reveals if the model is momentum-chasing, risk-neglecting, or balanced. The Expert Alignment Score (EAS) aggregates this profile.
*   **Core Assumption:** The three expert rankings (sort by U, momentum, R) represent distinct, non-redundant advisory philosophies whose alignment scores are a valid proxy for model heuristics.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [section 3.4] Definition of EAS. [section 4.2] Analysis shows clear clusters: Llama-3.3-70B aligns with Utility/Momentum, DeepSeek-V3.2 is balanced, and Llama3-XuanYuan aligns with User Choice.
*   **Break Condition:** If the expert rankings are highly correlated in the dataset, τ scores become statistically indistinguishable, preventing diagnosis of distinct strategies.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Utility Theory in Finance**
    *   **Why needed here:** The normative reference is defined by U(s) = μ - λσ² - γDrawdown. Interpreting uNDCG scores and diagnosing model failures (e.g., ignoring risk) requires understanding this mean-variance-drawdown trade-off.
    *   **Quick Check:** If a model's rankings perfectly match the Rational Utility view, what specific financial objective is it optimizing, and which investor trait (λ or γ) most influences its selections for volatile stocks?
*   **Concept: Inverse Optimization for Preference Elicitation**
    *   **Why needed here:** This method generates the latent utility reference from observed behavior. Understanding the Multinomial Logit assumption and regularized loss is crucial for trusting the inferred (λ, γ) parameters.
    *   **Quick Check:** What role does the Gumbel noise in Eq. 4 play, and how does the L2 regularization term (α ∥(λ,γ)∥²) in the loss prevent overfitting to a single anomalous choice?
*   **Concept: Multi-View Evaluation & Diagnostic Metrics**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper's thesis is that behavior-only evaluation is insufficient. Engineers must design and interpret uNDCG, MRR/HR@K, and τ against conflicting references to build a diagnostic profile.
    *   **Quick Check:** For a model with high uNDCG (0.97) but very low τ(Risk) (0.17), what two competing principles is it violating, and what might this imply for its advice during a market downturn?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
User Trajectory & Profile -> Inverse Optimization -> (λ, γ) -> Generate Expert Rankings -> Simulate Dialogue -> LLM Generates Ranking -> Compute Multi-View Metrics -> Aggregate & Analyze

**Critical path:** The quality of all downstream evaluation hinges on the validity of the inverse optimization step. Flawed (λ, γ) corrupts the Utility and Risk references, making all subsequent diagnostics invalid.

**Design tradeoffs:**
1.  **Utility Function Complexity:** The simple linear U = μ - λσ² - γDrawdown is tractable but omits preferences like skewness or liquidity. Adding terms increases realism but complicates inverse optimization and risks overfitting short trajectories.
2.  **Expert Definition Rigidity:** The three experts are hard-coded heuristics (sort by U, momentum, R). This ensures clean diagnostics but lacks the nuance of human experts or variable LLM-based advisors.
3.  **Conversation Realism vs. Control:** Simulated dialogues use templates for reproducibility but may be less ambiguous and challenging than real human-advisor chats, potentially making context easier for LLMs to parse.
4.  **Trajectory Length:** Using 23-step sequences is a compromise between capturing longitudinal dynamics and the practical limits of simulation and inverse optimization stability.

**Failure signatures:**
*   **Inverse Optimization Failure:** Estimated (λ, γ) are extreme or counter-intuitive vs. user questionnaire. Individual summary validation scores may be low (<3), even if the aggregate is 7.8.
*   **Model Collapse:** Near-random scores (<0.5 uNDCG, τ~0) indicate the model fails to use the conversational context or market signals.
*   **Single-View Overfit:** High HR@1 (>0.8) coupled with very low τ(Risk) (<0.1) is the core "behavioral overfitter" failure mode—perfect mimicry of noisy choices but neglect of risk.
*   **Context Ignorance:** Step-wise Δ uNDCG is flat (~0), showing the model does not improve its utility alignment by incorporating longitudinal history H_i,1:t-1.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Baseline Replication & Sanity Check:** Run the full benchmark with the provided random baseline and default models (e.g., Llama-3.3-70B). Verify aggregate scores match Table 4 and 5 within expected variance to confirm the pipeline.
2.  **Ablation: History Context:** For a top model (e.g., GPT-4o), remove the longitudinal dialogue history H_i,1:t-1 at every step. Re-run and compare uNDCG and EAS to full-context results to isolate the value of conversation for preference discovery.
3.  **Single-View Failure Mode Induction:** For a utility-aligned model (e.g., Llama-3.3-70B), instruct it: "Prioritize matching the user's most recent pick above all else. Ignore long-term risk." Re-run to confirm HR@1 increases significantly while τ(Risk) and uNDCG drop, demonstrating benchmark sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** How can we develop LLMs that effectively reconcile the tension between utility-based alignment and behavioral mimicry in financial recommendations?
    *   **Basis:** The paper reveals a persistent trade-off: high uNDCG correlates with low HR/τ(Risk), and no model excels on all axes (Section 4.1).
    *   **Why Unresolved:** Root causes (training objectives, data biases) are not investigated; no model breaks the trade-off.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Ablation studies varying architectures, multi-task losses, and training data to isolate factors that break the trade-off with statistical significance on both utility and behavioral metrics.
*   **Question 2:** To what extent can LLMs track and adapt to users’ latent risk preferences in longitudinal conversations, and what limits their ability?
    *   **Basis:** Step-wise analysis shows utility alignment gains plateau after initial steps (Section 4.3, Figure 2 & 3), attributed to noise.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** The mechanism behind the plateau (context limits, ineffective encoding, inherent noise) is not diagnosed.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Controlled experiments with synthetic trajectories having known, time-varying preferences; measure correlation between model internal representations and ground-truth preferences across steps.
*   **Question 3:** Does the multi-view evaluation framework generalize to other domains where observed behavior may not reflect true utility (e.g., health, education), and what adaptations are needed?
    *   **Basis:** The paper positions this as a general problem beyond finance (Section 1) but does not test generalization.
    *   **Why Unresolved:** Other domains have different normative models and confounders; the four-view structure may not translate.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Replicate in a new domain (e.g., medical adherence) by defining analogous views and validating with experts; evaluate LLMs to see if similar tensions appear.
*   **Question 4:** How reliable are the latent risk preferences inferred via inverse optimization from short behavioral trajectories?
    *   **Basis:** Parameters (λ, γ) are estimated from 23-step sequences; validation via summaries yields aggregate score 7.8/9 (Section 3.3.2).
    *   **Why Unresolved:** Validation is post-hoc and subjective; 23 steps may be insufficient for stable estimates; regularization (α) is not justified.
    *   **Resolution Evidence:** Test-retest reliability with repeated simulation sessions; comparison with alternative elicitation methods; larger user studies for stability and external validity.

## Limitations
*   **Data & Reproduction Barriers:** Exact conversation prompts and raw per-step user decision trajectories are not publicly released, forcing approximations for inverse optimization and simulation.
*   **Sample & Horizon Constraints:** Only 10 users with 23-step trajectories may under-represent population diversity and preference stability, limiting generalizability.
*   **Reference Definition Heuristics:** The expert rankings are simplistic, hard-coded sorts. Their potential correlation in certain market regimes reduces diagnostic specificity, and the linear utility function may omit key investor preferences (e.g., skewness, liquidity).

## Confidence
*   **Multi-view disentangling claim:** Medium. Internal evidence shows divergent alignments, but independence of expert views across market regimes is unverified.
*   **Inverse optimization stability claim:** Low. No ground truth for (λ, γ); validation is aggregate and masks individual failures; short sequences may not yield stable parameters.
*   **Expert alignment diagnostic claim:** Medium. Clear statistical patterns exist across models, but heuristic expert definitions may be correlated, limiting diagnostic specificity.

## Next Checks
1.  **Inverse optimization recovery test:** Simulate synthetic MNL choices with known (λ, γ). Vary trajectory lengths (10, 23, 50) and noise levels. Apply the paper's inverse optimization and report correlation between true and estimated parameters, plus proportion where utility/risk rankings match ground truth.
2.  **Reference correlation analysis:** For every user and step, compute Spearman correlations among the four reference rankings (Choice, Utility, Momentum, Risk). Summarize the distribution (mean, max, tail). If any pair consistently exceeds 0.85, the disentangling premise is weakened.
3.  **Expert definition robustness:** Replace heuristic experts with LLM-prompted experts (e.g., ask GPT-4 to rank by "long-term value," "recent trend," "downside protection"). Recompute EAS for all models and compare rank correlations with heuristic-based scores. Inconsistency would signal sensitivity to expert framing.
