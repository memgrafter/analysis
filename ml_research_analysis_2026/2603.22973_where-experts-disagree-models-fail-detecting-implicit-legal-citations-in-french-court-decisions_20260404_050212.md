# Where Experts Disagree, Models Fail: Detecting Implicit Legal Citations in French Court Decisions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22973
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22973
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: An unsupervised multi-model consensus method achieves 76% precision at k=200 for detecting implicit statutory citations, but model errors concentrate on cases where human annotators disagree (33% of dataset).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the novel task of detecting implicit statutory citations in French civil court decisions—instances where a court applies a rule from the Civil Code without explicitly citing the article. A benchmark of 1,015 passage-article pairs reveals low expert agreement (κ=0.33), with 43% of disagreements centered on the fact-law boundary. Supervised classifiers achieve only moderate performance (F1=0.70) and 68% of false positives occur on the minority (33%) of cases where annotators initially disagreed. By reframing the problem as a top‑k retrieval task and combining predictions from multiple language models via consensus, an unsupervised ranking approach reaches 76% precision at k=200 (34% recall), with remaining false positives often legally ambiguous rather than clear errors. The work underscores the impact of legal “open texture” on NLP systems and suggests that annotator disagreement signals inherent difficulty.

## Method Summary
Data is drawn from 182,155 first‑instance civil court decisions (Judilibre). Motivation sections are split into ≤100‑token chunks. Explicit citation pairs are extracted using regex and TF–IDF similarity to fine‑tune a JuriBERT bi‑encoder (Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss). This bi‑encoder encodes all 2,836 Civil Code articles and, for each chunk, retrieves the top‑5 nearest articles (L2 distance threshold 0.574), excluding any explicitly cited. Candidates are filtered with OpenAI’s o3 model using a strict prompt (doubt → NO), retaining only positives, yielding 1,015 pairs for expert annotation (YES/NO). For supervised classification, frozen encoders (legal and general‑domain) extract features; simple heads (logistic regression/MLP) are trained via 5‑fold CV grouped by decision ID. An ensemble stacks the best single‑model predictions with a meta‑learner. The unsupervised ranking combines four low‑bias LLM binary outputs, a cross‑encoder score, and lexical similarity (TF‑IDF/BM25) using heuristic weights, prioritizing consensus and breaking ties with the cross‑encoder.

## Key Results
- Supervised ensemble F1 = 0.70.
- 68% of false positives fall on the 33% of cases with annotator disagreement.
- Unsupervised method attains 76% precision at k=200 (34% recall); remaining false positives often correspond to legally ambiguous passages.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Model errors concentrate on cases where human annotators disagree, indicating that disagreement signals inherent ambiguity.
- **Mechanism:** When annotators disagree (κ=0.33, 33% of cases), the underlying fact‑law boundary is genuinely uncertain. Models over‑predict on these ambiguous instances, leading to a higher false‑positive rate. This pattern is consistent across all architectures, suggesting they lack robustness to legal “open texture.”
- **Core assumption:** Annotator disagreement reflects noise‑free uncertainty rather than random annotation error.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “68% of false positives fall on the 33% of cases where the annotators disagreed.”
  - [section 6.1]: Table 10 shows false‑positive rate 1.7× higher on disputed cases; Table 11 reports significant odds ratios for seven models; Figure 3 visualizes the gap.
  - [corpus]: Corpus evidence is weak; no close neighbors directly address human‑model disagreement in legal NLP.
- **Break condition:** If future models show no error concentration on disagreement cases, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Reframing detection as a top‑k ranking task and using multi‑model consensus yields high precision in an unsupervised setting.
- **Mechanism:** Zero‑shot LLMs exhibit varying positive bias. Intersecting the positive predictions of the least‑biased models creates a strong signal: cases agreed upon by multiple models are more likely true positives. Ranking by consensus (plus lexical and cross‑encoder scores) prioritizes reliable hits.
- **Core assumption:** Model agreement correlates with true relevance more than any single model’s confidence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “reframing the task as top‑k ranking and leveraging multi‑model consensus yields 76% precision at k = 200.”
  - [section 5.3]: Table 7 shows unsupervised ensemble AP = 0.67 (+0.23 over random); Table 8 gives precision@200 = 76% with 34% recall; Figure 2 compares gain vs. random.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like GreekBarBench use ranking, but consensus fusion is not directly replicated.
- **Break condition:** If consensus does not correlate with precision or adding models lowers performance, the ranking mechanism collapses.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Adversarial filtering with a strong model (o3) produces a benchmark that emphasizes edge cases where current methods struggle.
- **Mechanism:** By retaining only pairs that o3 labels positive, the dataset becomes enriched for examples that fooled a high‑performing model. This creates a harder test bed, exposing systematic weaknesses (e.g., on the fact‑law boundary).
- **Core assumption:** Failures of a state‑of‑the‑art model are informative about the limits of the broader class of similar architectures.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.4]: “We applied adversarial filtering… We only selected the pairs where o3 predicted the implicit use of an article.”
  - [section 6.1]: The supervised ensemble’s false positives concentrate on disputed cases, which are over‑represented due to the filter.
  - [corpus]: Evidence weak; neighbor “Artemis” uses LLM‑assisted detection but not adversarial filtering.
- **Break condition:** If an unbiased random sample yields a different error distribution (e.g., no concentration on disagreement), the adversarial filter’s utility is limited.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Implicit statutory citation detection  
  - *Why needed here:* The entire study targets this novel task: determining whether a court passage applies a Civil Code article without explicit reference. Misunderstanding the task would invalidate all annotations and experiments.  
  - *Quick check:* Given a court excerpt and an article, what evidence would justify a “YES” label? (Hint: judicial reasoning that adopts the article’s rule without naming it.)

- **Concept:** Adversarial dataset construction  
  - *Why needed here:* The benchmark’s difficulty stems from selective sampling using o3. Recognizing its bias (preferring cases where o3 errs) is essential for interpreting results and generalizing beyond French Civil Code.  
  - *Quick check:* How might adversarial filtering inflate performance on a held‑out set if test examples share artifacts with the filter?

- **Concept:** Ranking‑oriented evaluation (top‑k)  
  - *Why needed here:* The paper shifts from binary classification (F1) to precision@k, which better matches legal practitioners’ need to skim a manageable candidate list. This reframing unlocks practical utility even with moderate classification scores.  
  - *Quick check:* Why is precision@200 more meaningful than overall accuracy for a lawyer researching implicit citations?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  1. Data ingestion: Collect French court decisions from Judilibre API.  
  2. Chunking: Split motivation sections into ≤100‑token pieces.  
  3. Bi‑encoder training: Fine‑tune JuriBERT on explicit citation pairs (masked legal references) using Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss; build FAISS index of article embeddings.  
  4. Candidate generation: For each chunk, retrieve top‑5 articles; remove any explicitly cited or low‑similarity pairs.  
  5. Adversarial filtering: Query o3 (strict prompt) on candidates; keep only positives.  
  6. Annotation: Legal experts label YES/NO (with sub‑categories).  
  7. Supervised ensemble: Extract features from multiple frozen encoders (legal/general); train meta‑learner (stacking logistic regression) on 5‑fold CV.  
  8. Unsupervised ranking: Combine four low‑bias LLM binary predictions, cross‑encoder scores, and lexical similarity (TF‑IDF/BM25) into a weighted sum; break ties with cross‑encoder.

- **Critical path:** Train bi‑encoder → generate implicit candidates → apply o3 filter → annotate → train supervised ensemble → design unsupervised ranking.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Bi‑encoder*: efficient retrieval vs. less expressive than cross‑encoder.  
  - *Frozen encoders*: avoid over‑fitting small dataset vs. limit adaptation.  
  - *Adversarial filtering*: increases benchmark hardness vs. introduces bias toward o3‑like reasoning.  
  - *Supervised vs. unsupervised*: supervised yields higher recall but needs labels; unsupervised trades some recall for high precision without annotation.  
  - *Ensemble complexity*: stacking improves performance but reduces interpretability.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Disproportionate false positives on cases with annotator disagreement (Table 10).  
  - Calibration error: models overconfident on ambiguous instances (Table 12).  
  - Lexical overmatching: false positives when chunk text verbatim mirrors statute (e.g., Article 606 quoted in lease).  
  - Adversarial bias: models sharing o3’s reasoning patterns may appear weaker on this benchmark.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Bi‑encoder validation:** Compute recall@k for retrieving explicit citations on held‑out decisions; ensure >95% true matches within threshold (0.574 L2).  
  2. **Supervised classification:** Perform 5‑fold CV for each frozen encoder + head; select top configurations (e.g., SAUL‑7B, LLaMA‑3.1‑8B).  
  3. **Unsupervised ranking evaluation:** Compute Average Precision and precision@200 for the ensemble of four LLMs + cross‑encoder + lexical baselines; compare against random and单品 methods (Table 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the difficulty of implicit citation detection reflect genuine legal ambiguity (Hart’s “open texture”) or auxiliary factors like syntactic complexity or lexical rarity?
2. Is the concentration of model errors on human-disagreement cases an artifact of adversarial filtering, or would it appear in a naturally sampled corpus?
3. Can the unsupervised multi-LLM consensus ranking be improved by optimizing weights rather than using heuristics, and will it maintain the same error pattern?
4. What is the true prevalence of implicit statutory citations in French first-instance court decisions?
5. Are expert disagreements over implicit citations semantically meaningful (e.g., corresponding to distinct legal reasoning patterns) or mere annotation noise?

## Limitations
- Low inter‑annotator agreement (κ=0.33) introduces fundamental ambiguity; 43% of disagreements involve the fact‑law boundary.
- Adversarial filtering with o3 likely over‑represents edge cases, threatening external validity and potentially biasing error analysis.
- Critical implementation details (e.g., exact regex patterns, o3 prompt parameters, unsupervised weight rationale) are missing, hindering full reproducibility.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (errors concentrate on annotator disagreement): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (top‑k ranking with multi‑model consensus): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (adversarial filtering produces a hard benchmark): Low

## Next Checks
- Replicate dataset without o3 filtering – Randomly sample top‑5 bi‑encoder candidates instead of o3‑positive pairs; compare error distributions and model performance to isolate the filter’s impact on hardness and the observed disagreement error concentration.
- Multi‑annotator labeling study – Collect 5+ expert labels for 500+ pairs to estimate a probabilistic gold standard; then correlate disagreement magnitude (e.g., variance) with model error rates, controlling for lexical overlap, to test if disagreement is a reliable proxy for irreducible ambiguity.
- Full‑range unsupervised evaluation on random pairs – Draw a random set of all possible chunk‑article pairs (bypassing any filtering), compute precision‑recall curves for k up to 1000, and ablate each signal (LLM consensus, cross‑encoder, lexical) to quantify individual contributions and robustness of the ranking approach.
