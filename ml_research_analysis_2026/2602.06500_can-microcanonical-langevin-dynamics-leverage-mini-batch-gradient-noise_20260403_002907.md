# Can Microcanonical Langevin Dynamics Leverage Mini-Batch Gradient Noise?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.06500
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06500
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Preconditioned SMILE (pSMILE) successfully scales microcanonical Langevin dynamics to large-scale Bayesian inference using mini-batch gradients, overcoming bias and instability to achieve state-of-the-art performance on high-dimensional tasks while being robust to hyperparameters.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of applying microcanonical Langevin Monte Carlo (MCLMC) to large-scale Bayesian inference where full-batch gradients are infeasible. Naively using mini-batch gradients introduces two critical failure modes: a bias arising from anisotropic gradient noise and numerical instabilities in high dimensions. To address these, the authors propose a preconditioning scheme that uses exponential moving averages of gradient mean and per-coordinate standard deviation to render the mini-batch noise locally isotropic, thereby eliminating the bias-induced drift. They further develop an energy-variance-based adaptive tuner that automatically adjusts the step size and enforces stability through a guardrail based on a Gamma model of absolute energy changes. The resulting algorithm, preconditioned SMILE (pSMILE), combines these ideas with a second-order minimal-norm Omelyan integrator and MAP warm-start. Empirical evaluations on UCI regression and large-scale deep learning benchmarks (CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, Imagenette with ViT, nanoGPT) show that pSMILE matches or outperforms full-batch MILE and strong baselines like scale-adapted SGHMC, while demonstrating considerable robustness to hyperparameter choices.

## Method Summary
pSMILE modifies the SMILE integrator by first preconditioning the mini-batch gradient to have isotropic noise. It maintains exponential moving averages (α=0.01) of the gradient mean and per-coordinate standard deviation across minibatches; the gradient is scaled by √d/||σ|| and divided by σ+ε to equalize variance. This eliminates the drift bias caused by anisotropy.

The step size η is adapted online based on the energy change ΔE between successive states, computed using a full-batch (or very large minibatch) log posterior to approximate the true energy. An EMA (β=0.01) of |ΔE| estimates a Gamma distribution; its lower and upper quantiles (a=0.1, κ=0.98) define a guardrail (reject and reinitialize if |ΔE| exceeds the threshold) and adaptation rules (increase η if |ΔE| below lower quantile, decrease if above upper). The deterministic core uses a 5-stage second-order Omelyan integrator with coefficients b₁=0.1931833, a₁=0.5, b₂=0.6136333. Chains are warm-started from MAP estimates, run in parallel ensembles (e.g., 8 chains), and predictions are averaged (BDE). The full algorithm is detailed in Algorithm 1 and Section F.3 of the paper.

## Key Results
- pSMILE matches or exceeds the full-batch MILE sampler and scale-adapted SGHMC on multiple high-dimensional benchmarks, including CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18, 11.2M parameters) and Imagenette (ViT, 22M parameters).
- The algorithm shows significant robustness to hyperparameter misspecification, maintaining competitive performance across a range of step sizes and batch sizes.
- pSMILE achieves state-of-the-art effective sample size and predictive accuracy for Bayesian inference with large neural networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that microcanonical Langevin dynamics assume isotropic gradient noise; when using mini-batches, the noise becomes anisotropic, leading to a systematic bias in the sampled distribution. The preconditioning step rescales each coordinate by its estimated noise standard deviation, effectively whitening the gradient noise and restoring the isotropic assumption required for unbiased sampling. This is complemented by the energy-variance adaptation: by tracking the distribution of absolute energy changes, the algorithm can detect when the step size is too large (risk of instability) or too small (inefficient exploration) and adjust accordingly. The guardrail mechanism acts as a safety net, rejecting moves that would cause excessive energy jumps and resetting momentum to avoid pathological states. The minimal-norm Omelyan integrator provides a symplectic, volume-preserving discretization that respects the underlying Hamiltonian dynamics while being efficiently implementable with the preconditioned gradients. Together, these components allow pSMILE to retain the theoretical benefits of MCLMC while scaling to large datasets via mini-batches.

## Foundational Learning
- **Microcanonical Langevin Monte Carlo (MCLMC)**: A deterministic integrator for stochastic differential equations that preserves the microcanonical ensemble, avoiding discretization error inherent in HMC. *Why needed*: Provides a stable, volume-preserving discretization for Langevin dynamics. *Quick check*: Energy should remain nearly constant over long runs if step size is appropriate.
- **Gradient noise isotropy**: The assumption that mini-batch gradient noise has equal variance in all directions. *Why needed*: Anisotropic noise induces bias in MCLMC. *Quick check*: After preconditioning, compute per-coordinate variance of gradients; they should be roughly equal.
- **Preconditioning via EMAs**: Using exponential moving averages to estimate per-coordinate gradient variance. *Why needed*: Enables cheap online whitening without full covariance. *Quick check*: Monitor that σ estimates converge to stable values during warm-up.
- **Energy-variance adaptation**: Fitting a Gamma distribution to absolute energy changes to set step size. *Why needed*: Automates tuning and maintains numerical stability. *Quick check*: The rejection rate should hover around the target (e.g., 2% for κ=0.98).
- **Minimal-norm Omelyan integrator**: A second-order symmetric integrator with minimal coefficient magnitude. *Why needed*: Reduces numerical error while preserving symplecticness. *Quick check*: Compare energy drift against Euler; Omelyan should be smaller.
- **Exponential moving average (EMA)**: Recursive averaging with decay. *Why needed*: Provides efficient, streaming estimates of statistics. *Quick check*: Verify that EMAs smooth out noise and track running means.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data → Minibatch Sampling → Gradient Computation → Update_g_bar_sigma (EMA) → Precondition_g → Compute_norm_e → Integrator_Stages → Update_θ_Pi → Full-batch Energy Evaluation → Compute_ΔE → Update_energy_EMA → Guardrail_check → Step_size_adaptation → (Optional) Momentum_renorm → Repeat

**Critical path**: The loop progresses from minibatch gradient to preconditioning to deterministic integration to energy evaluation and adaptation; the guardrail may trigger a rejection resetting momentum and reverting parameters.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Diagonal vs full preconditioning: Diagonal is cheap but may not capture curvature; full matrix would be more accurate but costly in high dimensions.
- Full-batch energy vs large-minibatch approximation: Full-batch gives accurate ΔE but expensive; large minibatch reduces cost but introduces extra noise potentially harming adaptation.
- EMA decay α, β: Small values give stable estimates but slow response; larger values adapt quickly but increase variance.
- Guardrail threshold κ: Higher values allow larger steps (faster mixing) but risk instability; lower values are safer but conservative.
- Integrator order: Second-order Omelyan is a good balance; higher-order schemes would require more gradient evaluations per step.

**Failure signatures**:
- Rejection rate >10%: η too large or guardrail too sensitive.
- NaNs or exploding loss: numerical overflow, often from σ ≈ 0 or excessively large η.
- Very low effective sample size (ESS) per gradient: poor mixing, possibly due to too small η or over-preconditioning.
- Persistent anisotropy in posterior samples: check per-coordinate marginal variances; imbalance suggests preconditioning failure.
- Energy drift not centered around zero: integrator step size may be mismatched with η.

**First experiments**:
1. Validate gradient noise isotropy: compute per-coordinate variance of gradients over many random minibatches; after preconditioning, coefficient of variation (std/mean) should be low (<0.1).
2. Tune guardrail parameters: run short chains varying κ (0.95–0.99) and a (0.05–0.2); record rejection rate, average LPPD, and ESS. Identify stable region that targets ~2% rejection.
3. Ablate stochastic updates: if the paper includes optional half-step stochastic/tangent updates at integrator boundaries, disable them and compare mixing (ESS) and predictive log-likelihood on a benchmark (e.g., Airfoil) to assess their contribution.

## Limitations
- Unclear whether ΔE should be computed with full-batch log-posterior or a large minibatch; using a mini-batch could bias the guardrail and adaptation.
- Ambiguity in "zeroing the momentum" after rejection: setting momentum to zero violates the unit-sphere constraint; proper renormalization is needed but not specified.
- The optional half-step stochastic/tangent updates mentioned in Section F.3 are not detailed for pSMILE; their omission might affect theoretical properties.
