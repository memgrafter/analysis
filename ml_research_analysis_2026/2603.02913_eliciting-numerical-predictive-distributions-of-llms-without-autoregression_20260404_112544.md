# Eliciting Numerical Predictive Distributions of LLMs Without Autoregression

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.02913
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02913
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Trained probes on LLM hidden states recover point estimates (Pearson R > 0.9) and uncertainty measures with well-calibrated confidence intervals, matching the error of 20–25 autoregressive samples in a single forward pass.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) can perform numerical regression but typically require expensive autoregressive decoding (sampling many token sequences) to produce a predictive distribution and quantify uncertainty. This paper investigates whether the necessary distributional information is already encoded in the LLM's internal representations before any tokens are generated. The authors train lightweight "magnitude-factorised" probes—which separately classify the order of magnitude and regress a scaled residual value—on frozen LLM hidden states to predict summary statistics like the mean, median, and various quantiles. Evaluated on synthetic and real-world time series data, these probes extract the LLM's implicit predictive distribution with high fidelity, yielding accurate point estimates and well-calibrated uncertainty intervals. The approach is orders of magnitude faster than traditional sampling-based inference, suggesting that LLMs internally formulate a full distribution prior to token-by-token generation.

## Method Summary
The method uses frozen, pre-trained LLMs (specifically Llama-2) as feature extractors. For a given numerical prediction task, the LLM processes the input context, and the final hidden state (before any output token is generated) is taken as a representation of the model's internal prediction. A separate, trainable probe is then applied to this hidden state. The probe is designed in two stages to handle the wide range of possible numeric values: first, a classifier predicts the magnitude bucket (e.g., hundreds, thousands) of the target number; second, a regressor predicts the scaled value within that bucket. By training this probe to predict target statistics (mean, median, quantiles) derived from many autoregressive samples from the LLM, the probe learns to map the initial hidden state directly to the parameters of the predictive distribution. At inference, a single forward pass through the frozen LLM and the trained probe yields the desired distributional summary without any autoregressive generation.

## Key Results
- Probes recover the LLM's point estimates (mean/median) with high correlation (Pearson R > 0.9) on test data.
- Predicted quantiles yield well-calibrated confidence intervals (90% coverage within ±0.5% of nominal level).
- A single forward pass through the probe matches the error of 20–25 autoregressive samples from the LLM while being orders of magnitude faster in terms of computational steps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core finding is that the LLM's final pre-generation hidden state contains a compressed, parametric representation of its full predictive distribution over numerical outputs. The autoregressive sampling process is not *creating* this distribution; it is *revealing* it through sequential token sampling. The probe acts as a decoder that can directly interpret this compressed representation. The magnitude-factorised design is crucial because it allows the probe to handle the exponentially large space of possible real numbers by disentangling scale (a classification problem) from precise value (a regression problem within a manageable range). The high accuracy and calibration of the extracted quantiles indicate that the LLM's internal state encodes not just a central tendency but also a spread consistent with its token-level uncertainty.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Autoregressive Decoding:** Standard method where the model generates tokens one-by-one to produce a full output sequence. **Why needed:** Traditional way to obtain a sample from the model's predictive distribution. **Quick check:** Can the model's next-token probability distribution be sampled repeatedly to build a Monte Carlo estimate of a target variable?
*   **Hidden State Representations:** The vector output from a specific transformer layer, often used as a contextual embedding of the input sequence. **Why needed:** Serves as the fixed input to the probe, believed to summarize the model's "thoughts" before generation. **Quick check:** Does the hidden state from the last token position correlate with the target numeric value across many examples?
*   **Probing:** Training a simple, often linear, model on top of frozen neural network representations to predict a target property. **Why needed:** Diagnostic tool to test what information is explicitly encoded in internal activations. **Quick check:** Can a linear regressor trained on layer activations predict a known property (e.g., part-of-speech) better than chance?
*   **Magnitude Factorisation:** Decomposing a numerical prediction into a discrete magnitude class and a continuous scaled residual. **Why needed:** Addresses the difficulty of regression on unbounded real numbers by converting most of the dynamic range into a classification task. **Quick check:** Does predicting log10(value) as an integer class followed by predicting value / 10^class improve regression performance on wide-ranging data?
*   **Quantile Regression:** Predicting specific percentiles (e.g., 0.1, 0.5, 0.9) of a distribution rather than just the mean. **Why needed:** Directly captures the shape and spread of the predictive distribution for uncertainty calibration. **Quick check:** Can a model be trained with a quantile loss (e.g., pinball loss) to output multiple quantiles simultaneously?
*   **Predictive Distribution:** The full probability distribution over possible outcomes for a given input, as modeled by the LLM. **Why needed:** The ultimate target for uncertainty quantification, beyond a single point estimate. **Quick check:** Do many autoregressive samples from the model form a reasonable empirical distribution for the target variable?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input Prompt -> Frozen LLM (forward pass) -> Final Hidden State (h) -> Magnitude Classifier (predicts bucket *k*) -> Scaled Value Regressor (predicts residual *r* given *h* and *k*) -> Reconstructed Value = r * 10^k -> Distribution Summary (mean, median, quantiles aggregated over multiple predictions or from a single trained multi-output probe).

**Critical Path:** The primary data flow for a single prediction is a single forward pass through the large LLM (to get *h*), followed by a forward pass through the much smaller, trained probe (to get *k* and *r*). The probe's training phase, which requires many autoregressive samples from the LLM to serve as labels, is an offline, one-time cost per task/dataset.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   **Probe Complexity vs. Expressivity:** The probe is kept simple (typically linear or shallow MLP) to ensure it learns a *linear readout* of information already present in *h*, avoiding the creation of new computation. More complex probes might extract more information but risk learning to simulate autoregressive generation rather than decode the internal state.
*   **Factorisation Choice:** Factorising by magnitude is effective for broad numeric ranges but introduces a hard boundary between buckets. A continuous transformation (e.g., predicting the log value directly) might be smoother but could suffer from higher error in the original value space.
*   **Task-Specific vs. Universal Probes:** Probes are trained on specific datasets/tasks. A universal probe would generalize across tasks but likely sacrifice accuracy. The tradeoff is between convenience and peak performance.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   **Poor calibration (coverage << 90%):** Indicates the probe is not correctly extracting the spread (variance) of the predictive distribution from *h*. The hidden state may not encode uncertainty in a linearly accessible way for that task.
*   **Systematic bias in point estimates:** Suggests the probe's regression component is not properly aligned with the LLM's internalencoding of central tendency, possibly due to non-linear relationships between *h* and the target value.
*   **Performance collapse on out-of-distribution data:** The probe learns a mapping valid for the training data distribution. If the LLM's internal representation shifts for novel input contexts, the probe will not generalize.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  **Layer-wise Probe Sweep:** Train identical probes on hidden states from every layer of the LLM for a single task. Plot Pearson R for mean/median prediction vs. layer depth to identify which layer(s) contain the most accessible distributional information.
2.  **Probe vs. Sampling Efficiency:** For a fixed Compute Budget (e.g., 100 LLM forward passes), compare the error of (a) 100 autoregressive samples (mean/quantiles computed directly) and (b) 100 forward passes through the LLM + probe. This isolates the probe's decoding efficiency against raw sampling.
3.  **Cross-Task Zero-Shot Test:** Train a probe on a large, diverse synthetic dataset covering many numerical distributions. Evaluate its performance *zero-shot* on a held-out real-world time series dataset without any fine-tuning. This tests the generalizability of the learned readout function.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **How do LLMs internally represent the uncertainty of their numerical predictions?** The paper shows probes can extract quantiles but does not explain the underlying representation; it's unclear whether uncertainty is explicitly encoded or emerges indirectly. *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct mechanistic analysis (e.g., layer-wise linear probes, causal interventions) to isolate neural correlates of distributional spread such as variance or entropy.
*   **Can a single trained probe generalize across LLM architectures and tokenization schemes without retraining?** Current probes are tied to Llama-2's architecture and digit-level tokenizer; performance degrades on other models, indicating model-specific representations. *What evidence would resolve it:* Train a probe on a mixture of diverse LLMs (different sizes, tokenizers) and evaluate zero-shot on unseen architectures; or design architecture-agnostic probes that operate on model-agnostic features (e.g., output logits).
*   **Are autoregressive decoding steps necessary for accurate numerical predictions, or can the full predictive distribution be obtained solely from the initial hidden state?** The paper recovers mean/median/quantiles but still used autoregressive samples as ground truth; it remains unknown whether the distribution could be elicited without any autoregressive processing and whether the single forward pass captures the entire distribution for arbitrary numerical outputs. *What evidence would resolve it:* Compare the distribution obtained from the probe (e.g., by fitting a density to predicted quantiles) directly with the distribution obtained from many autoregressive samples, measuring similarity metrics (e.g., KL divergence) across diverse tasks; also test whether the probe works in zero-shot settings where the LLM has never seen token-by-token generation of numbers.

## Limitations
*   Evaluation is limited to synthetic and a narrow set of real-world time series; generalizability to open-ended regression tasks or diverse domains remains unclear.
*   Probes require task-specific labeled data for training; adaptation to new tasks without retraining has not been demonstrated.
*   Reported calibration (90% coverage within ±0.5%) is exceptionally tight; it is unknown whether such performance holds under distribution shift or on noisier data.
*   Speed comparisons omit full pipeline overhead (feature extraction from frozen LLM, preprocessing); actual wall-clock gains may be smaller than claimed.

## Confidence
*   **Point estimate accuracy (Pearson R > 0.9):** Medium confidence. Abstract cites high correlation but provides no variance, error bars, or validation across multiple datasets.
*   **Uncertainty calibration:** Medium confidence. The precise coverage figure lacks context; calibration strength across varied scenarios is not established.
*   **Efficiency parity with autoregressive sampling:** Medium confidence. Direct benchmarking on identical hardware is absent; “orders of magnitude faster” may not translate to real-world latency reductions when accounting for probe training and feature extraction costs.

## Next Checks
1.  Test probes on held-out domain datasets (e.g., financial, scientific measurements) to evaluate out-of-distribution generalization.
2.  Compare probe-based uncertainty against Monte Carlo dropout or multiple temperature sampling, measuring both calibration and sharpness.
3.  Conduct an end-to-end runtime analysis that includes LLM feature extraction, probe inference, and post-processing to quantify true latency improvements.
