# Bidirectional Reward-Guided Diffusion for Real-World Image Super-Resolution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07069
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07069
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Bird-SR achieves state-of-the-art perceptual quality on real-world image super-resolution benchmarks while maintaining structural fidelity.

## Executive Summary
Real-world image super-resolution (SR) using diffusion models often suffers from distribution shift when models trained on synthetic paired data are applied to real low-resolution images. This paper introduces Bird-SR, a bidirectional reward-guided diffusion framework that reframes SR as a trajectory-level preference optimization problem. Bird-SR jointly optimizes a forward process on synthetic data—leveraging relative rewards and structural losses at early diffusion steps—and a reverse process on real-world data—using reward feedback with semantic alignment at later steps—balanced by a dynamic fidelity-perception weighting.

On four real-world SR benchmarks, Bird-SR consistently outperforms existing methods across perceptual metrics such as MUSIQ, MANIQA, ClipIQA, and LIQE, while preserving structural consistency. A user study confirms strong preference for Bird-SR in both realism and fidelity. Ablations demonstrate the contributions of the bidirectional optimization, dynamic weighting, and individual loss components, validating the approach's effectiveness.

## Method Summary
Bird-SR formulates super-resolution as trajectory-level preference optimization. The method jointly optimizes a forward process on synthetic data (using relative rewards and structural loss at early diffusion steps) and a reverse process on real-world data (using reward feedback with semantic alignment at later steps), balanced by a dynamic fidelity-perception weighting.

During training on synthetic pairs, the diffusion model is guided at early timesteps with a combination of relative reward signals (comparing intermediate samples) and structural constraints (e.g., edge consistency) to preserve geometrical accuracy. For real-world low-resolution images, the reverse diffusion process is fine-tuned at later timesteps using feedback from pre-trained perceptual quality models, enhancing realism. The dynamic weighting smoothly modulates the influence of fidelity versus perception throughout the diffusion trajectory, allowing the model to transition from structure-preserving early steps to detail-enhancing later steps.

## Key Results
- Bird-SR consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods on four real-world benchmarks in perceptual metrics (MUSIQ, MANIQA, ClipIQA, LIQE).
- Structural consistency is maintained, as evidenced by lower structural losses compared to competitors.
- A user study shows strong preference for Bird-SR in both realism and fidelity over other methods.
- Ablations validate the effectiveness of the bidirectional optimization, dynamic weighting schedule, and the proposed loss components.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The bidirectional approach effectively addresses the distribution shift by decoupling the optimization of early and late diffusion stages. Early steps are anchored on synthetic data, which provides reliable ground truth for structural fidelity, while later steps are adapted to real-world conditions using reward guidance from perceptual metrics. This separation prevents the model from mixing conflicting signals within the same timestep and allows each stage to specialize: the forward process learns to maintain geometric correctness, and the reverse process learns to inject realistic textures. The dynamic weighting ensures a smooth transition between these objectives, avoiding abrupt changes that could degrade either fidelity or perception. As a result, Bird-SR bridges the gap between synthetic training and real-world deployment, achieving improved perceptual quality without sacrificing structural integrity.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Synthetic Data (HR, LR) -> Forward Diffusion -> UNet (noise predictor) -> Losses: Diffusion loss + λ_early*(Relative Reward + Structural Loss) at early t.
Real Data (LR) -> Sample noise -> Reverse Diffusion -> UNet -> Loss: λ_late*(Reward feedback) at late t.
Dynamic Scheduler γ(t) -> modulates λ_early and λ_late across timesteps.
Condition Encoder -> injects LR into UNet (e.g., via latent conditioning or concatenation).
Pre-trained Reward Models -> compute perceptual scores from predicted HR.
Pre-trained Feature Extractors -> for structural loss (e.g., edge detectors).

### Critical Path
1. During training, alternate between synthetic batch and real batch (or combine them).
2. For synthetic batch: forward diffusion adds noise to HR; UNet predicts noise; compute standard diffusion loss; at timesteps t < T_early, compute additional reward and structural terms; backpropagate combined loss.
3. For real batch: start from random noise, condition on LR; perform reverse diffusion steps; at timesteps t > T_late, compute reward from intermediate HR estimate; backpropagate reward loss.
4. The dynamic γ(t) scales the respective additional losses at each step.
5. All losses jointly update the UNet and conditioning encoder parameters.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Reward model choice**: Off-the-shelf IQA models are convenient but may bias toward certain distortions; fine-tuning on target domain could improve alignment at the cost of generalization.
- **Dynamic weighting**: The schedule and magnitude of γ(t) significantly affect the fidelity-perception balance; requires careful tuning and may be dataset-sensitive.
- **Bidirectional training complexity**: Training on two data types (synthetic and real) doubles computational overhead and may cause gradient interference if not properly balanced.
- **Structural loss design**: Using simple losses like pixel MSE at early steps is less effective; feature-based or edge losses add computational cost but improve structure.
- **Inference cost**: Reward guidance during reverse diffusion (if applied at sampling) increases inference time due to additional forward passes through reward models.

### Failure Signatures
- **Over-perceptualization**: Excessively high reward weights yield unrealistic textures, color shifts, or hallucinated details that break scene coherence.
- **Structural degradation**: Insufficient structural loss or too early transition to perception leads to blurry edges, distorted geometry, or loss of fine details.
- **Training instability**: Abrupt changes in γ(t) or mismatched loss scales cause oscillations, exploding gradients, or mode collapse.
- **Reward misalignment**: If the reward models underrate certain types of detail (e.g., natural textures), the output may become overly smooth or exhibit artifacts to game the metric.
- **Domain mismatch**: The synthetic forward training distribution might not fully cover real LR variations, causing poor generalization if the reverse process cannot fully compensate.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Ablation of Bidirectional Optimization**: Train ablations: forward-only (synthetic with full losses, no real branch), reverse-only (real branch only), and full Bird-SR. Evaluate on real-world benchmarks to quantify the contribution of each branch and their synergy.
2. **Dynamic Weight Sensitivity Analysis**: Systematically vary γ(t) schedules (e.g., linear ramp-up, constant, sigmoid) and magnitudes. Measure impact on trade-off metrics (e.g., MUSIQ vs. SSIM) to identify robust configurations and highlight sensitivity.
3. **Reward Model Selection Study**: Compare performance using individual reward models (MUSIQ, MANIQA, ClipIQA, LIQE) and their combinations. Determine which metrics best drive perceptual improvement and whether multiple rewards provide complementary gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to four real-world benchmarks; generalization to other degradation types (e.g., sensor noise, varied blur) remains uncertain.
- The dynamic fidelity-perception weighting scheme introduces hyperparameters that may require careful tuning; no robustness analysis is provided.
- Reward guidance relies on off-the-shelf perceptual metrics that imperfectly correlate with human judgment, possibly introducing systematic bias.
