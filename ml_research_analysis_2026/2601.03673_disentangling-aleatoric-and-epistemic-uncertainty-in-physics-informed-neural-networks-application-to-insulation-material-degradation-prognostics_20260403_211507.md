# Disentangling Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty in Physics-Informed Neural Networks. Application to Insulation Material Degradation Prognostics

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.03673
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03673
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A heteroscedastic Bayesian PINN (B-PINN) jointly captures aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, reducing total RMSE by 57.3% versus vanilla PINN and improving uncertainty calibration (CRPS/NLL) on real transformer insulation degradation data.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a key limitation of standard Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) for prognostics: they are deterministic or model only epistemic uncertainty, lacking the full uncertainty quantification required for risk‑aware asset management. It introduces a heteroscedastic Bayesian PINN (B‑PINN) framework that integrates Bayesian neural networks with physics‑based residual enforcement and an input‑dependent noise model. This yields complete predictive distributions—quantifying both reducible (epistemic) and irreducible (aleatoric) uncertainty—for spatiotemporal transformer insulation ageing estimation. Evaluated on operational data from a solar plant transformer, validated with finite‑element simulations, the method significantly outperforms deterministic PINNs, dropout PINNs, and homoscedastic B‑PINNs in both predictive accuracy (RMSE) and uncertainty calibration (CRPS, NLL). A sensitivity analysis identifies moderate residual collocation points (10k) and full boundary sampling as optimal for uncertainty performance. The explicit disentanglement enables more reliable ageing predictions and informed maintenance decisions.

## Method Summary
The proposed heteroscedastic B‑PINN replaces the deterministic network in a standard PINN with a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) approximated via variational inference. The architecture features two output heads: one predicts the mean temperature field μ̂(x,t), the other predicts an input‑dependent aleatoric variance σ²̂(x,t) (modeled via a softplus activation). The training loss combines three components: (1) a Kullback‑Leibler (KL) divergence term regularizing the variational posterior toward a Laplace prior; (2) heteroscedastic negative log‑likelihood (NLL) losses for initial/boundary condition data and physics residuals evaluated at collocation points via automatic differentiation; and (3) optional weighting coefficients (λ₀, λ_b, λ_r) to balance these terms. At inference, multiple weight samples are drawn from the variational posterior; epistemic uncertainty is estimated as the variance of predicted means across samples (cEU), while aleatoric uncertainty is the average of predicted variances (dAU). Total predictive variance is their sum.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy & calibration:** Total RMSE of 1.09, representing a 57.3% improvement over vanilla PINN (2.55) and a 42.6% reduction versus heteroscedastic d‑PINN (1.90). CRPS and NLL scores are superior to all baselines, indicating better‑calibrated uncertainty.
- **Optimal sampling:** Sensitivity analysis shows CRPS is optimized with moderate residual collocation points (N_r = 10,000) and full boundary sampling (N_b = 11,520), confirming a trade‑off between physics enforcement and model flexibility.
- **Uncertainty disentanglement:** The heteroscedastic B‑PINN produces spatially varying aleatoric uncertainty bands that adapt to the temperature profile, while epistemic uncertainty remains more uniform—enabling clearer interpretation of uncertainty sources for risk‑aware prognostics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Bayesian inference with physics enforcement
**Claim:** Bayesian inference over network parameters, combined with physics‑based residual enforcement, yields a posterior distribution that captures epistemic uncertainty while adhering to physical laws.  
**Mechanism:** The B‑PINN uses variational inference to approximate the posterior over weights q(θ|ϕ). The loss (ELBO) combines a KL divergence term (complexity cost) with expected negative log‑likelihoods of the data, including the physics residual r(x,t) evaluated at collocation points. This joint optimization shapes the posterior such that sampled parameters produce solutions consistent with both data and the PDE.  
**Core assumption:** The true posterior can be approximated by a tractable variational family (e.g., Gaussian), and the PDE provides a noise‑free constraint that reduces epistemic uncertainty.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Section 2.3 defines the B‑PINN loss, explicitly integrating residual likelihoods.  
- Section 2.2 describes the variational inference objective.  
- Abstract states integration of BNNs with physics‑based residual enforcement.  
**Break condition:** If residual weighting (λ_r) is poorly chosen or collocation points are sparse/misplaced, the posterior may not enforce the PDE adequately, leading to physically inconsistent epistemic uncertainty (high variance where PDE is violated).

### Mechanism 2: Heteroscedastic aleatoric modeling
**Claim:** Modeling aleatoric uncertainty as an input‑dependent (heteroscedastic) variance yields predictive distributions that are sharper and better calibrated than assuming constant (homoscedastic) noise.  
**Mechanism:** The network has a second output head for σ²̂(x,t). During training, the NLL for each data point uses this local variance, allowing the model to assign higher noise to regions with observed variability (e.g., rapid temperature changes) and lower noise elsewhere. This contrasts with homoscedastic modeling, which averages noise globally.  
**Core assumption:** Aleatoric noise is conditionally Gaussian with variance a deterministic, learnable function of (x,t).  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Section 2.4 defines heteroscedastic NLL (Eq. 20) and contrasts it with homoscedastic modeling.  
- Table 3 shows heteroscedastic B‑PINN achieves lower miscalibration area (0.1956 vs 0.2135) than homoscedastic variant.  
- Figure 7(b) illustrates spatially varying aleatoric bands adapting to the temperature profile.  
**Break condition:** With extremely sparse data or insufficient network capacity, the variance function may underfit (degenerate σ̂) or overfit (capturing noise instead of true aleatoric variability).

### Mechanism 3: Collocation point balance
**Claim:** The quantity and distribution of collocation points critically determine the trade‑off between reducing epistemic uncertainty (via physics enforcement) and over‑constraining the model (which inflates learned aleatoric uncertainty).  
**Mechanism:** Residual collocation points (N_r) enforce the PDE across the domain, acting as a regularizer that squeezes epistemic flexibility. However, overly dense residual sampling can overfit the physics, forcing the model to explain any residual error via aleatoric noise (higher dAU). Boundary condition points (N_b) stabilize edges; insufficient N_b lets epistemic uncertainty propagate inward.  
**Core assumption:** The PDE is a correct, noise‑free representation; its enforcement via residuals is the primary lever for epistemic uncertainty reduction.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Section 3.3 and Figure 10 show CRPS optimal at N_r=10k and degrades at N_r=20k.  
- Figure 10 shows CRPS improves with more boundary samples up to full coverage.  
- Figure 10 shows minimal CRPS change when increasing initial condition samples (N_i).  
**Break condition:** If the PDE is misspecified (e.g., wrong form), stronger enforcement (high N_r) will force the BNN to learn incorrect dynamics, increasing aleatoric variance to compensate for systematic residual error.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Variational Inference for Bayesian Neural Networks
- **Why needed here:** The B‑PINN uses variational inference to approximate the intractable posterior P(θ|D) over network weights, enabling tractable sampling for epistemic uncertainty estimation.
- **Quick check question:** How does the ELBO loss balance fitting the data (including physics residuals) against staying close to the prior, and what role does the KL divergence term play in controlling model complexity?

### Concept: Physics‑Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Automatic Differentiation
- **Why needed here:** PINNs embed the PDE (heat diffusion) into the loss via automatic differentiation of the network output û(x,t;θ) to compute residuals r(x,t). This is the core mechanism for enforcing physics.
- **Quick check question:** In the PINN loss, what is the purpose of the collocation points (x_f,t_f), and how does automatic differentiation differ from finite‑difference approximations in this context?

### Concept: Aleatoric vs. Epistemic Uncertainty Decomposition
- **Why needed here:** The paper’s key advance is disentangling these two sources. Epistemic (model uncertainty) is reducible with more data/physics; aleatoric (data noise) is irreducible and must be modeled as heteroscedastic for realistic prognostics.
- **Quick check question:** Using the law of total variance, how would you interpret a scenario where total predictive variance is high but the epistemic component (variance of means across posterior samples) is low?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Input (x,t) -> Bayesian backbone (2 hidden layers, 50 neurons each, tanh; variational Gaussian posterior q(θ|ϕ) with Laplace prior) -> Output heads [μ_̂(x,t), σ²_̂(x,t)] -> Physics residual block (autodiff computes derivatives, forms r(x,t) = ∂²μ_̂/∂x² + (1/k)q(x,t) - (1/α)∂μ_̂/∂t) -> Loss aggregator (IC/BC NLL using σ²_̂, residual NLL using fixed σ_r, plus KL divergence weighted by λ₀, λ_b, λ_r) -> Monte Carlo inference (sample K weight sets θ^(k) from q, compute cEU = Var(μ̂^(k)), dAU = mean(σ²̂^(k)), total σ²_TU = cEU + dAU).

- **Critical path:** Forward pass computes μ_̂ and σ²_̂ -> autodiff computes residual r(x,t) -> NLL losses for IC/BC/residuals using σ²_̂ (or σ_r) -> KL divergence added -> total loss backpropagated to update variational parameters ϕ=(μ,σ). At test time, sample θ^(k) -> forward passes -> aggregate statistics.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **VI vs. Dropout (d‑PINN):** VI is more principled but slower; dropout often overestimates epistemic uncertainty (wider bands).  
  - **Heteroscedastic vs. Homoscedastic:** Heteroscedastic adapts to local noise (better calibration) but risks overfitting variance with scarce data; homoscedastic is simpler and more stable.  
  - **Residual weighting (λ_r):** High λ_r strongly enforces physics, reducing epistemic error but may inflate aleatoric variance; low λ_r risks physics violation.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High CRPS/NLL with low RMSE: overconfident miscalibration (e.g., homoscedastic B‑PINN).  
  - Epistemic uncertainty spikes at boundaries: insufficient boundary samples (low N_b).  
  - Exploding/vanishing aleatoric variance σ²_̂: training instability; check learning rate, softplus activation, λ_r magnitude.  
  - d‑PINN long‑horizon degradation: dropout fails under extrapolation (e.g., t=50h RMSE 3.35 vs B‑PINN 0.45).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** Implement vanilla PINN and homoscedastic B‑PINN; verify they reproduce RMSE/CRPS trends in Table 2 (e.g., vanilla PINN RMSE ~2.55).  
  2. **Uncertainty disentanglement demo:** Train heteroscedastic B‑PINN; at fixed t (e.g., 25h), plot predictive mean ±2σ_TU and separately plot cEU(x) and dAU(x); verify dAU varies with x (temperature profile) while cEU is more uniform.  
  3. **Sensitivity sweep on N_r and N_b:** Train multiple heteroscedastic B‑PINNs varying N_r (5k, 10k, 20k) and N_b (25%, 50%, 100% of BC samples); measure CRPS on held‑out grid; expect CRPS minimum at N_r=10k and N_b=100%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Alternative Bayesian inference methods:** Can HMC, SVGD, or physics‑informed priors further improve UQ robustness compared to VI? *Basis:* Paper states future work; *unresolved:* No comparative study of posterior approximation schemes.  
2. **Generalization of sampling strategies:** Do optimal N_r=10k and full boundary sampling generalize beyond 1D heat diffusion? *Basis:* Sensitivity analysis only on transformer PDE; *unresolved:* Not tested on other PDE types (hyperbolic, elliptic, higher‑dimensional).  
3. **Theoretical explanation for d‑PINN failure:** Why do heteroscedastic dropout PINNs produce over‑dispersed, inaccurate uncertainties at long horizons? *Basis:* Empirical observation of degraded d‑PINN performance; *unresolved:* No theoretical analysis linking dropout masks to variational posteriors under physics regularization.  
4. **Uncertainty propagation to RUL:** How does disentangled uncertainty from the thermal model affect reliability of remaining useful life (RUL) distributions through the empirical ageing formula? *Basis:* Ageing model is deterministic; uncertainty propagation not analyzed; *unresolved:* No validation of RUL calibration against failure data.  
5. **Performance under data scarcity/non‑stationarity:** Does the heteroscedastic B‑PINN maintain advantages with <100 measurements or under non‑stationary conditions (e.g., sensor drift)? *Basis:* Dataset has 5760 samples over 4 days; *unresolved:* No data ablation or non‑stationarity tests.

## Limitations
- **Generalization unclear:** The optimal sampling strategies and disentanglement performance are demonstrated on a single real‑world dataset; transferability to other physical systems or PDEs remains unknown.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Exact values for Laplace prior scale λ and residual weight λ_r are not disclosed; their impact on the epistemic/aleatoric trade‑off is unexplored.
- **Mechanism isolation:** The paper does not disentangle the individual contributions of Bayesian inference, heteroscedastic modeling, and residual weighting to the overall performance gain.
