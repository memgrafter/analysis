# Consensus Group Relative Policy Optimization for Text Generation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03102
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03102
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes C-GRPO to distill MBR decoding into training, enabling single-pass generation at test time with comparable or better quality than multi-sample MBR reranking.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the high inference cost of consensus-based decoding (e.g., Minimum Bayes Risk) by training a policy to directly produce high-utility outputs. It introduces Consensus Group Relative Policy Optimization (C-GRPO), which uses self-consensus calculated from on-policy samples as a reward signal within the GRPO framework. Theoretically, the expected gradient aligns with that of the MBR objective, and empirical results on machine translation and summarization show performance matching or exceeding MBR decoding while eliminating its repeated sampling overhead. The method consistently outperforms reference-free baselines like standard GRPO with random rewards.

## Method Summary
C-GRPO replaces reference-based consensus utilities with self-consensus computed from a group of outputs sampled from the current policy. For each candidate in the group, its utility is the average task-specific similarity (e.g., BLEURT) to all other candidates. These utilities are normalized within the group to form a group-relative advantage. The policy is updated via a GRPO-style objective using importance ratios, without a KL penalty. Training thus distills the expected-utility objective of MBR into the policy, allowing single-pass generation at test time. Key hyperparameters include group size (16–32), temperature 0.7, and LoRA for efficient adaptation.

## Key Results
- C-GRPO achieves average COMET scores exceeding MBR decoding on WMT 2024 translation tasks for both Llama and Mistral models.
- On XSum summarization with ROUGE-Lsum, C-GRPO improves Llama from 0.361 to 0.419, while MBR decoding shows no gain.
- On the JBBQ QA bias benchmark, C-GRPO improves accuracy for both Mistral (26.8%→34.4%) and Llama (42.6%→49.0%), surpassing self-rewarding baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** C-GRPO distills the MBR objective into a trainable policy by replacing reference-based consensus with self-consensus computed from on‑policy samples.
- **Mechanism:** For a prompt, sample a group \(G\) from the current policy. Compute each output’s consensus utility as the average task-specific similarity to all outputs in \(G\). Normalize these utilities across the group to obtain an advantage \(A_i = (R_i - \text{mean}(G))/\text{std}(G)\). The policy gradient is estimated via the GRPO objective: \(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_i \rho_i(\theta) A_i\), where \(\rho_i(\theta)\) is the importance ratio.
- **Core assumption:** The self-consensus utility is an unbiased or low-bias estimator of the expected MBR utility for moderate group sizes; the group-relative advantage directionally matches the true MBR gradient.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “distill Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) decoding into training by formulating the consensus utility as a group-relative objective within GRPO.”
  - Section 3: Formal definition of C-GRPO and its components.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under standard assumptions, the expected gradient of C-GRPO aligns with the gradient of the MBR objective, providing a convergence guarantee.
- **Mechanism:** In an ideal surrogate, the GRPO gradient estimator’s expectation equals \(\alpha \nabla_\theta L_C(\theta)\) with \(\alpha>0\). The group mean decomposes into the sample’s own utility and the mean of others; the latter acts as an independent baseline that vanishes in expectation. The scalar \(1/\sigma_G\) acts as a preconditioner. Stochastic gradient ascent then yields sub-linear convergence.
- **Core assumption:** Assumptions 3.1: (1) \(L\)-smooth objective; (2) bounded gradient variance; (3) absolute continuity of policies; (4) independence of the normalization factor \(1/\sigma_G\). Group size \(G \ge 2\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section 3.1: Theorem 3.3 (gradient alignment) and Theorem 3.4 (convergence bound).
  - Proof sketch showing how the baseline cancels in expectation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** C-GRPO amortizes the computational cost of MBR decoding; after training, a single forward pass yields outputs comparable in quality to multi-sample reranking.
- **Mechanism:** Training steers the policy to produce outputs that are high in expected consensus utility. Because the policy itself embodies the selection rule, test-time generation requires only one sample per input, eliminating repeated sampling and scoring.
- **Core assumption:** The policy has sufficient capacity to represent the MBR optimum and the self-consensus signal is a sufficient proxy for the true expected utility.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “C-GRPO successfully achieves performance comparable to MBR decoding without the associated inference-time overhead.”
  - Section 4.2–4.3: Results showing C-GRPO matching or exceeding MBR on translation and summarization while using single-pass generation.

## Foundational Learning

  - **Why needed:** C-GRPO explicitly aims to distill the MBR objective; understanding its formulation (expected utility over the model distribution) is foundational.
  - **Quick check:** Write the formula for the MBR utility of a candidate \(y\) given prompt \(q\).
- **Concept:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).
  - **Why needed:** C-GRPO is an instance of GRPO; the group-relative advantage is the key mechanism that replaces a reward model.
  - **Quick check:** How is the advantage \(A_i\) computed in GRPO (Equation 2)?
- **Concept:** Policy gradient methods (importance-sampling based).
  - **Why needed:** The GRPO objective builds on the policy gradient theorem; the estimator \(\rho_i(\theta)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_i)\) appears in the update.
  - **Quick check:** What does the importance ratio \(\rho_i(\theta)\) represent?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Sampler -> Group outputs -> Pairwise utility computation -> Per-output consensus utility -> Group mean/std -> Advantage normalization -> GRPO loss computation -> Policy update.
- **Critical path:** Training loop: sample group \(G\) from old policy -> compute pairwise task utilities (e.g., BLEURT) -> average per output to get \(R_i\) -> compute group mean \(\mu_G\) and std \(\sigma_G\) -> advantage \(A_i = (R_i - \mu_G)/\sigma_G\) -> maximize \(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_i \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|q)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i|q)} A_i\). Inference: single sample from final policy.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Group size \(G\): Larger groups improve utility estimates but increase compute; may affect advantage stability.
  - Utility function choice: Must reflect task quality (e.g., COMET for MT, ROUGE for summarization); learned metrics may have biases.
  - C-GRPO vs C-Dr.GRPO: Dr.GRPO subtracts output length and adjusts denominator, improving stability at the cost of more conservative updates.
  - Reference-free vs reference-based: C-GRPO avoids gold references during training, but the utility function may still be trained on references indirectly.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Mode collapse: all outputs in a group become nearly identical → consensus utility loses discriminative power (monitor diversity via self-BLEU).
  - Divergence: loss spikes, advantages explode → likely too large learning rate or high reward variance.
  - Test-time degradation: if policy overfits to group-wise ranking, single-sample quality may fall short of MBR; evaluate with small candidate sets during training as a proxy.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. WMT 2024 machine translation (English→Japanese/Chinese/German) using COMET: C-GRPO and C-Dr.GRPO achieve average COMET > MBR decoding on both Llama and Mistral.
  2. XSum summarization with ROUGE-Lsum: C-GRPO (Llama) improves over base (0.361→0.419) and MBR (0.361), while Mistral shows modest gain.
  3. JBBQ (Japanese Bias Benchmark for QA): C-GRPO improves base model accuracy (Mistral 26.8%→34.4%; Llama 42.6%→49.0%) and surpasses self-rewarding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does C-GRPO’s advantage over MBR decoding hold for larger models (>8B), long-form text generation, and human-annotated quality? The paper states broader validation is left to future work.
- Why does C-GRPO substantially improve ROUGE-Lsum on XSum while MBR decoding does not, despite using the same BLEURT utility? The paper speculates but does not analyze the cause.
- Under what conditions does standard C-GRPO underperform MBR decoding (e.g., Llama on En–Zh/En–De), and how does Dr.GRPO reliably close this gap? The paper does not diagnose the failure modes of plain C-GRPO.

## Limitations
- Implementation details for handling near-zero group standard deviation (ε value/clipping) are unspecified, risking unstable advantages.
- The exact modified advantage formula for C-Dr.GRPO (length/std subtraction) is not fully detailed; reproduction requires cross-referencing external work.
- Baseline prompts and output parsing for JBBQ and self-rewarding are only partially described, potentially affecting comparison validity.
- Computational scaling for pairwise utility computation (O(G²)) with large groups is not addressed; optimizations are omitted.

## Confidence
- **Core distillation mechanism** (self-consensus replaces reference-based consensus): High confidence – mechanism is clearly defined and anchored in GRPO literature.
- **Theoretical gradient alignment & convergence**: High confidence – assumptions and theorems are explicitly stated, though independence of \(\sigma_G\) is an approximation.
- **Amortization of MBR cost** (single-pass quality matches multi-sample reranking): Medium confidence – empirical results support it, but over-optimization to the training utility could harm generalization.
- **Generalization to QA** (JBBQ improvements): Medium confidence – results are promising but limited by prompt sensitivity and narrow benchmark scope.

## Next Checks
1. **Advantage distribution stability:** During training, log per-batch mean absolute advantage and proportion of near-zero advantages. If >20% of advantages are <0.01, increase temperature or group size to restore signal.
2. **Utility-metric correlation:** On a held-out set, track both training utility (BLEURT) and target metric (COMET/ROUGE) every 100 steps. If BLEURT rises while target metric plateaus or falls, reduce learning rate or stop earlier to avoid over-optimization.
3. **Group size sensitivity:** Ablate group size (e.g., 8, 16, 32) measuring final target metric and advantage variance. Confirm gains plateau and instability does not rise sharply with larger G, validating the well-behaved \(\sigma_G\) assumption.
