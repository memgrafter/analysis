# IDEA2: Expert-in-the-loop competency question elicitation for collaborative ontology engineering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01344
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01344
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Semi-automated, expert-in-the-loop workflow that yields up to 92.7% competency question acceptance and under one minute review time per question.

## Executive Summary
IDEA2 tackles the bottleneck in competency question (CQ) elicitation for ontology engineering by introducing a semi-automated, expert-in-the-loop workflow. It leverages large language models (LLMs) to generate and iteratively reformulate CQs from source documents, while domain experts validate and refine them through a collaborative Notion dashboard. The system includes full provenance tracking to maintain traceability. Evaluated on scientific data (AnIML) and cultural heritage domains, IDEA2 achieved high CQ acceptance rates (92.7% and 73.3%) and reduced average review time to under a minute per CQ. Post-task surveys indicated high usability and expert engagement.

## Method Summary
IDEA2 employs a four-phase iterative workflow. First, it extracts initial CQs from source documents (e.g., user stories, XML schemas) using an LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) with prompts designed to produce atomic and appropriately abstract questions. Second, these CQs are published to a Notion-based dashboard where domain experts review them, voting accept/reject, adding tags and free-form comments. Third, the expert feedback is used to drive an LLM-based reformulation process that modifies rejected or unclear CQs. Fourth, all steps are recorded with PROV-O-compliant provenance metadata, exportable as JSON-LD. The loop repeats until convergence criteria (e.g., acceptance threshold, iteration limit) are met. A minimal reproduction requires setting up the GitHub repository, obtaining a Gemini API key, creating a Notion workspace with the provided template, and running the extraction-validation-reformulation cycle.

## Key Results
- Final CQ acceptance rates: 92.7% (AnIML) and 73.3% (cultural heritage).
- Iterative reformulation boosted acceptance in the cultural heritage stress test from 69.6% to 85.7%.
- Average expert review time was less than one minute per CQ, demonstrating efficiency.
- Post-task surveys reported high usability and sustained expert engagement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
IDEA2 succeeds by combining the scalability of LLMs with the domain expertise of humans in a tight feedback loop. The initial LLM extraction rapidly generates a broad set of candidate CQs, overcoming the tedious manual drafting typically required. Experts then validate these CQs in a familiar, low-friction Notion interface, ensuring domain relevance. Their specific feedback (votes, comments) directly informs the LLM reformulation step, which refines or regenerates CQs to address identified issues. This iterative co-creation converges on high-quality CQs while keeping expert effort manageable. Provenance tracking throughout provides transparency and supports auditability, building trust in the final set. The approach effectively distributes cognitive load: LLMs handle pattern-based generation, while experts focus on judgment and nuanced refinement.

## Foundational Learning
**Competency Questions (CQs)**: Natural language queries that define the knowledge an ontology must capture. *Why needed*: They serve as requirements to guide ontology development and validation. *Quick check*: Can you write a CQ that is both answerable by the ontology and meaningful to domain stakeholders?

**Ontology Engineering**: The disciplined process of designing, building, and maintaining ontologies. *Why needed*: Provides structured, machine-readable knowledge representations for domain interoperability. *Quick check*: What are the core elements of an ontology (e.g., classes, object properties, data properties) and how do they relate?

**Large Language Models (LLMs)**: Generative AI models trained on vast text corpora. *Why needed*: They enable rapid, automated extraction and reformulation of domain-specific text, scaling the CQ elicitation process. *Quick check*: How can an LLM be prompted to generate questions that are both domain-specific and ontologically useful?

**Expert-in-the-Loop**: A human-AI collaboration paradigm where domain experts guide, correct, and validate AI outputs. *Why needed*: Ensures that automatically generated artifacts (like CQs) remain aligned with real-world domain semantics. *Quick check*: Why can't LLMs operate fully autonomously in high-stakes knowledge engineering tasks?

**Provenance Tracking (PROV-O)**: A W3C standard for representing the lineage of data and processes. *Why needed*: Captures who did what, when, and why, enabling reproducibility and accountability in ontology development. *Quick check*: What key provenance entities (e.g., agents, activities, entities) would you record for a CQ's lifecycle?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Source Documents -> LLM Extraction -> Notion Dashboard (Expert Validation) -> Expert Feedback -> LLM Reformulation -> Convergence Check -> Provenance Store -> Final CQs.

Critical path: The essential sequence is: extract CQs from documents → present to experts → collect feedback → reformulate CQs → repeat until acceptance criterion met.

Design tradeoffs: The use of Notion simplifies UI development and leverages experts' familiarity, but ties the system to a specific platform with API limitations and potential template drift. Relying on a single LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) risks vendor lock-in and prompt sensitivity; however, it simplifies integration. The iterative loop may prolong the process if expert response times are slow.

Failure signatures: 1) Low CQ acceptance rate (<50%) after several iterations suggests poor extraction prompts or ambiguous source material. 2) Notion webhook errors or missing database properties indicate integration misconfiguration. 3) Experts consistently providing vague comments (e.g., "not good") without specifics points to inadequate guidance or fatigue.

Three first experiments:
1. **Smoke test**: Run the extraction script on a 1-page document, confirm CQs appear in the Notion dashboard with correct properties.
2. **Validation loop**: Have one expert review and comment on 5 CQs, then trigger reformulation; inspect whether reformulated CQs address the feedback.
3. **Provenance audit**: Export the provenance JSON-LD after a couple of iterations and verify that each CQ’s history correctly links to its source document, expert votes, and reformulation steps.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: What real-time feedback assistance mechanisms (e.g., NLP-based prompting) can effectively improve the specificity and actionability of domain experts' comments during CQ validation?  
*Basis in paper*: [explicit] The authors state they "aim to integrate NLP techniques to assess expert comments in real-time, prompting for clarification if feedback is too generic." (Section 6, Conclusions)  
*Why unresolved*: The current workflow accepts free-form comments without quality control, risking vague feedback that hinders effective reformulation.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Implement a prototype module that analyzes comment specificity (e.g., length, presence of concrete references) and triggers prompts; evaluate whether such assistance increases acceptance rates of reformulated CQs compared to a baseline.

**Open Question 2**: How can an expert consensus model that preserves disagreement be designed to capture genuine domain ambiguity, and does it lead to more comprehensive and nuanced CQ sets than majority voting?  
*Basis in paper*: [explicit] The authors plan to "move beyond simple majority voting by adopting 'disagreement-aware' consensus models (e.g., CrowdTruth), treating expert divergence not as noise, but as a signal of genuine domain ambiguity to be preserved." (Section 6, Conclusions)  
*Why unresolved*: Majority vote may overlook minority but valid perspectives; disagreement may reflect underlying complexities that are currently discarded.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Compare a CrowdTruth-based aggregation against majority vote across multiple domains, measuring diversity of accepted CQs, coverage of domain concepts, and expert satisfaction.

**Open Question 3**: Which intervention strategies (e.g., thematically organized batches, gamification elements) are most effective at reducing annotator fatigue and maintaining high-quality feedback during extended CQ validation sessions?  
*Basis in paper*: [explicit] The authors discuss "Sustaining a high level of expert engagement ... could implement strategies to maintain engagement, such as presenting CQs in smaller, thematically organised batches. Moreover, the integration of gamification elements ... could be explored." (Section 5, Discussion)  
*Why unresolved*: Engagement may decline over time, affecting data quality; no empirical evidence exists on what works best in this context.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Conduct a user study across conditions (batched vs. full list, with vs. without gamification), tracking metrics like time per CQ, comment depth, and dropout rates to identify the most sustainable approach.

## Limitations
- Prompt engineering details for LLM extraction and reformulation are omitted, introducing potential variability in CQ quality and hindering exact replication.
- Expert selection criteria and compensation are undocumented, which may affect feedback consistency, engagement levels, and ultimately the acceptance rates reported.
- The system's reliance on Notion as a third-party platform creates integration fragility due to API limits, template versioning issues, and potential service outages.

## Confidence
- **Final CQ acceptance rates and review time reductions**: Medium (results are promising but depend on unreported prompt tuning and expert characteristics).
- **Workflow structure (four-phase loop, provenance tracking)**: High (explicitly described and conceptually sound).
- **Reformulation benefit in cultural heritage stress test**: High (empirical comparison within the same domain shows clear improvement).
