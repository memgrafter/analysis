# Pharmacology Knowledge Graphs: Do We Need Chemical Structure for Drug Repurposing?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01537
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01537
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Removing the graph attention-based drug encoder and relying solely on topological embeddings combined with ESM-2 protein features increased drug-protein PR-AUC from 0.5631 to 0.5785 while reducing VRAM usage by 93% (5.30 GB to 353 MB).

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the necessity of explicit chemical structure information in knowledge graph-based drug repurposing. Under strict temporal validation using a pharmacology graph from ChEMBL (5,348 entities, 20,015 edges) with biologically verified hard negatives, the authors benchmarked embedding models and graph neural networks. The core finding is that removing the chemical structure encoder (a graph attention network) and using only topological embeddings with pre-trained protein features (ESM-2) yields higher performance (PR-AUC 0.5785 vs. 0.5631) and drastically lower memory consumption (353 MB vs. 5.30 GB). Further experiments show that data volume is a more significant bottleneck than model complexity, and external validation confirms 42.9% of top novel predictions. These results challenge the prevailing emphasis on molecular graph convolutions and suggest that for approved drugs, network topology and high-fidelity target representations suffice for state-of-the-art repurposing, enabling more accessible and efficient models.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a heterogeneous knowledge graph from ChEMBL v36, comprising drugs, proteins, and indications (5,348 nodes, 20,015 edges). To simulate real-world prediction, they applied a temporal split (pre-2022 vs. post-2022) and created biologically verified hard negatives by balancing random negatives with failed assays and failed clinical trials. The baseline model employed a heterogeneous GNN: drug nodes were encoded by a graph attention network (GAT) over molecular graphs, proteins used fixed ESM-2 embeddings projected to 256 dimensions, and indications had learnable embeddings. Three layers of GraphSAGE-style mean aggregation propagated messages across relation types, and relation-specific MLPs scored link probabilities. Key ablations replaced the GAT drug encoder with learnable embeddings (no structure) or Morgan fingerprints. The primary evaluation metric was PR-AUC for drug-protein links under transductive settings. Additional experiments varied training data volume (25%-100%) and model size (1.66M-9.75M parameters), and external validation assessed top predictions against FDA labels.

## Key Results
- Ablating the GAT drug encoder (topology + ESM-2 only) increased drug-protein PR-AUC to 0.5785 (+0.015) and cut VRAM by 93% (5.30 GB → 353 MB), whereas Morgan fingerprints lowered PR-AUC to 0.5286.
- Data volume is the primary bottleneck: halving the training data reduced performance more than halving the model size (1.66M params on full data > 3.44M params on half data).
- External validation corroborated 6 of 14 top predictions (42.9%) as legitimate therapeutic indications.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Topological Signal Supersedes Chemical Structure
- **Claim:** Topological network structure combined with high-fidelity target representations (ESM-2) provides a superior signal for drug-protein link prediction compared to explicit chemical structure encoders.
- **Mechanism:** The model learns embeddings where drugs are positioned based on shared graph neighborhoods (co-interacting proteins, co-indicated diseases). A drug's likelihood of interacting with a novel protein is inferred from its proximity to other drugs known to bind that protein ("guilt by association"). This topological signal captures system-level pharmacological patterns that static 2D/3D structural features may fail to represent and can introduce noise.
- **Core assumption:** For approved drugs with known clinical profiles, the accumulated relational evidence in the knowledge graph is a more robust predictor of new interactions than molecular graph convolutions.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Ablation 2 (No Drug Graphs) achieved the highest drug-protein PR-AUC (0.5785) vs. full GAT model (0.5631) and Morgan fingerprint replacement (0.5286).
  - VRAM usage dropped from 5.30 GB to 353 MB by removing the GAT encoder.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism would be invalidated if the performance gain from ablating structure was not reproducible on a different, larger pharmacology KG or if it disappeared in an inductive setting with novel molecular entities.

### Mechanism 2: Data Volume Outweighs Model Complexity
- **Claim:** Data volume (graph density/coverage) is a much tighter performance bottleneck than model parameter count for knowledge graph-based repurposing.
- **Mechanism:** The model's ability to generalize is constrained by the diversity and completeness of relational patterns in the training graph. More edges provide more contexts for learning distinct entity neighborhoods. Scaling parameters beyond a modest threshold (~2.44M) yields diminishing returns because the model rapidly reaches its capacity to memorize topological patterns; the limiting factor becomes insufficient patterns in the data.
- **Core assumption:** The pharmacological knowledge graph has relatively low intrinsic complexity that can be captured by a medium-sized model, and the primary challenge is exhaustive coverage of drug-target-indication interactions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - A 1.66M-parameter model trained on 100% data (0.5216) outperformed a 3.44M model trained on 50% data (0.5140). Scaling to 9.75M parameters degraded performance (0.5349).
  - Performance declines rapidly with data reduction but remains relatively flat with parameter reduction.
- **Break condition:** This scaling law fails if a significantly more complex relational pattern (e.g., multi-hop pathway reasoning, temporal dynamics) is introduced that requires a proportionally larger model, even with full data.

### Mechanism 3: Molecular Graph Convolutions Dominate Computational Cost
- **Claim:** The dominant computational cost in multi-modal GNNs for pharmacology KGs is driven by molecular graph convolutions (GAT), not by large pre-computed protein embeddings (ESM-2) or the GNN's weight matrices.
- **Mechanism:** Message-passing over atom/bond subgraphs for each drug node is computationally intensive in both memory (storing intermediate node states) and operations (edge-wise attention). In contrast, a fixed 2560-d ESM-2 vector is a single static tensor per protein; projecting it is a cheap linear layer. The model's weight matrices map efficiently to GPU matrix multiplication units.
- **Core assumption:** The cost of per-edge, per-layer neighborhood aggregation for dense molecular subgraphs outweighs the cost of handling large feature dimensions in a batched, matmul-optimized framework.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - VRAM use dropped from 5.30 GB to 353 MB by removing the GAT encoder.
  - Dropping ESM-2 saved only ~8 MB (353 MB → 345 MB).
  - The GAT message-passing over atom and bond subgraphs consumes roughly 93% of the entire memory footprint.
- **Break condition:** This cost analysis would shift if protein embeddings were made dynamic (e.g., fine-tuning ESM-2) or if the drug representation moved to a larger, pre-computed fingerprint that dominates memory.

## Foundational Learning

### Transductive Link Prediction
- **Why needed:** The task is to predict new edges for drugs and proteins already present in the training graph. This aligns with real-world repurposing where the drug already exists and the model must generate embeddings for it from historical data.
- **Quick check question:** If a drug appears only in the test set (post-2022), what must happen to it during the temporal split procedure, and why does this matter for evaluation?  
  *Answer: It is moved to the training set as a "cold-start" entity to ensure it has an embedding; otherwise, it cannot be meaningfully ranked.*

### Biologically-Verified Hard Negatives
- **Why needed:** Random negative sampling is unrealistically easy and inflates metrics. Using failed assays (inactives) and failed clinical trials (Phase 2/3) creates a challenging, clinically relevant decision boundary that better reflects repurposing difficulty.
- **Quick check question:** What is the key danger of relying only on random negative sampling for drug-indication link prediction, and how does the paper's 33/33/33 mix mitigate this?  
  *Answer: Random negatives are implausible pairs; a model only needs to distinguish positives from random noise, not from plausible failures. The mix includes clinically hard negatives to teach fine-grained discrimination.*

### Temporal Validation & Data Leakage
- **Why needed:** To simulate real-world prediction, training must use only knowledge available before a test interaction was discovered/approved. This prevents the model from cheating by learning from future information embedded in the graph.
- **Quick check question:** For a drug-protein edge with an assay date of 2023, what determines whether it is in the training or test set? What happens if the drug or protein has no pre-2022 edges?  
  *Answer: Timestamp > 2022 → test set. Cold-start entities with no pre-2022 edges are moved to training to ensure they have an embedding.*

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Input Layer: Tri-modal graph (Drugs, Proteins, Indications). Drugs initially have molecular graphs (SMILES → RDKit atom/bond features) or (in ablation) learnable embeddings. Proteins have fixed ESM-2 embeddings. Indications have learnable embeddings.  
Encoder Layer (Ablated): Standard GNN uses a GAT to encode drug molecular graphs into 256-d vectors; ablation replaces this with direct learnable drug embeddings (identity features).  
Projection Layer: Linear projections for Protein (2560-d → 256-d) and Indication (32-d → 256-d).  
Message Passing Backbone: 3 layers of heterogeneous GraphSAGE-style mean aggregation across relation types.  
Prediction Heads: Relation-specific MLPs (2-layer) that score concatenated node embeddings for link prediction (drug-protein, drug-indication).

### Critical Path
1. **Data Construction:** Build KG from ChEMBL 36 with strict temporal split and biologically-verified hard negatives (failed assays/trials). This is the most crucial and error-prone step.  
2. **Baseline Training:** Train the Standard GNN (GAT + ESM-2) to establish a performance and VRAM baseline.  
3. **Feature Ablation:** Run the key experiment: remove the GAT drug encoder, using only learnable drug embeddings + ESM-2. Measure ΔPR-AUC and ΔVRAM.  
4. **Scaling & Validation:** Perform data/parameter scaling experiments. Validate top external predictions against FDA labels/literature.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Topology vs. Structure:** Sacrificing explicit chemical structure (GAT) for pure topological signal reduces VRAM by 93% and can improve performance, but may lose fine-grained SAR signals for very similar compounds.  
- **Data vs. Parameters:** Investing effort in comprehensive data integration (more edges, more node types) yields better returns than increasing model depth/width beyond ~2.5M parameters.  
- **Computational Cost vs. Ceiling:** The Blackwell GNN (InfoNCE, AttentionHeteroConv) sets the predictive ceiling but at 36.8 GB VRAM. The 353 MB ablated model achieves ~95% of this ceiling with consumer hardware.

### Failure Signatures
- **VRAM Spike:** If VRAM usage remains ~5 GB after removing the GAT encoder, the drug embeddings are not truly ablated (e.g., Morgan fingerprint MLP is still active).  
- **Performance Collapse on Ablation:** If removing GAT drastically hurts performance (PR-AUC drop >0.1), the graph topology may be too sparse or the protein features (ESM-2) insufficient; the model relies heavily on molecular similarity.  
- **External Validation Fails:** If <20% of top-ranked novel predictions are verifiable, the model is likely overfitting to spurious graph correlations or the negative sampling strategy is flawed.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate Ablation 2:** Train the Standard GNN architecture with `drug_encoder=None` (using learnable drug embeddings) and `protein_features=ESM-2`. Confirm VRAM ~353 MB and drug-protein PR-AUC >0.57.  
2. **Data Scaling Re-run:** Using the ablated model (353 MB), train on 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% of the training edges. Plot PR-AUC vs. data fraction. Expect monotonic improvement with no plateau, confirming data is the primary bottleneck.  
3. **Negative Sampling Sanity Check:** For a handful of held-out drug-protein test edges that are true positives, manually verify they are not present in the dynamic negative pool during training. A leakage here would invalidate all PR-AUC scores.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Results are based on a single moderate-sized ChEMBL-derived KG; performance may drop on denser, multi-source graphs or with different drug/protein ontologies.
- **Temporal validation specifics:** The impact of "cold-start" entity handling (moving pre-2022 orphan drugs/proteins to training) on reported metrics is unclear.
- **Negative sampling calibration:** The 33/33/33 mix (random, failed assays, failed trials) lacks reported calibration (e.g., whether failed trials are appropriately matched for disease stage and target class).

## Confidence
- **Topological embeddings superiority (vs. chemical structure):** High (strong ablation evidence with internal validation; external validation sample small).
- **Data volume as primary bottleneck:** Medium (scaling experiments clear but limited to one architecture; break condition not tested).
- **GAT as dominant computational cost:** High (direct VRAM measurements across ablations conclusive for this setup).

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate Ablation 2 on a larger, multi-source pharmacology KG** (e.g., integrating DrugBank, CTD, Pharos). Confirm if removing drug structure encoders retains or improves PR-AUC and VRAM savings.
2. **Test the ablated model (no drug graphs) in an inductive setting** with truly novel chemical scaffolds (drugs with no pre-split edges). Quantify performance decay versus the full GAT model.
3. **Audit negative sampling leakage:** For 100 random held-out drug-protein test edges, trace all training edges incident to the drug or protein within the temporal window. Ensure no indirect paths (e.g., drug→disease→protein) create a transitive positive signal that could leak into training.
