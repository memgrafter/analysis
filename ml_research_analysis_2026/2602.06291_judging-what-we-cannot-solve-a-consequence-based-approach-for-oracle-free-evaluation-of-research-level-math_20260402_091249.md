# Judging What We Cannot Solve: A Consequence-Based Approach for Oracle-Free Evaluation of Research-Level Math

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06291
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06291
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CBU consistently surpasses reward models, generative reward models, and LLM judges; for GPT-OSS-120B it raises Acc@1 from 67.2 to 76.3 and AUC from 71.4 to 79.6.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the bottleneck of evaluating solutions to research-level mathematics problems without access to ground-truth answers. It introduces Consequence-Based Utility (CBU), a method that scores a candidate solution by measuring its ability to improve a solver’s performance on a set of related, verifiable “neighborhood” problems. Evaluated on the EXPERTMATH benchmark of 192 expert-authored problems, CBU consistently outperforms reward models, generative reward models, and LLM judges. It also maintains a larger solver–evaluator gap even on extremely difficult questions, preserving separation between correct and incorrect solutions. Error analysis shows CBU more reliably penalizes solutions with incorrect reasoning, unjustified compression, or unwarranted references. The results demonstrate that downstream transfer performance is a robust signal for ranking solution quality in oracle-free settings.

## Method Summary
CBU evaluates a candidate solution C for a target problem Q by first constructing a set of neighborhood problems N(Q) that are methodologically related to Q but independently verifiable. For each neighbor Q* in N(Q), a solver model Mθ is prompted with (Q, C) as context and generates an answer for Q*. This is repeated over multiple independent rollouts (e.g., T=64). A verifier v, assumed reliable, checks each generated answer. The utility of C is the average verification accuracy across all neighbors and rollouts: U(C) = (1/|N(Q)|T) Σ_{Q*∈N(Q)} Σ_{t=1}^T v(Q*, C_t). Candidates are then ranked by U(C). The method assumes a correct solution provides transferable methodological guidance, while an incorrect one does not; neighborhoods must be in a “sweet spot” of difficulty—solvable with a correct hint but not trivially solvable without it.

## Key Results
- CBU significantly outperforms LLM judges, reward models, and generative reward models on EXPERTMATH; for GPT-OSS-120B it increases Acc@1 from 67.2 to 76.3 and AUC from 71.4 to 79.6.
- CBU maintains a larger solver–evaluator performance gap even on extremely difficult questions, whereas LLM-judge performance degrades.
- Error analysis: CBU assigns above-mean scores to wrong solutions far less frequently (0.08–0.14) compared to LLM judges (0.53 for Qwen models), and it more often penalizes unjustified compression (71.4% of fooled cases) and incorrect reasoning (68.8%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Transferable Method Signal
A candidate solution’s correctness is signaled by its ability to improve the solver’s downstream accuracy on related neighborhood problems. The solver Mθ is conditioned on (Q, C) to solve each Q* in N(Q); utility U(C) is the average accuracy. The core assumption is that neighborhood problems are methodologically linked to Q: solving Q makes Q* straightforward, but Q* does not trivially solve Q. The “sweet spot” ensures Q* is neither too easy (solver succeeds regardless of C) nor too hard (solver fails even with correct C). Evidence: Formal definition of U(C) and empirical results showing CBU’s sensitivity to neighborhood difficulty (Figure 3). Break condition: if linkage fails or difficulty is mis‑calibrated, the utility signal collapses.

### Mechanism 2: Robust Evaluator–Solver Gap
CBU evaluates solutions indirectly via their effect on other problems, preserving correct–wrong separation even when direct inspection by LLM judges fails. LLM judges assess a single reasoning trace’s plausibility and degrade on subtle, coherent wrong solutions. CBU’s indirect “unit test” mode remains informative because a flawed method, even if superficially coherent, will not consistently improve performance across diverse neighbors. Evidence: Figure 3 shows CBU’s mean score gap remains stable as target difficulty increases, while LLM‑judge’s gap collapses. Break condition: if wrong solutions transfer poorly and correct ones transfer well regardless of difficulty, the gap would not increase with difficulty; the observed trend suggests CBU’s signal strengthens where direct inspection fails.

### Mechanism 3: Penalizing Unreconstructable Solutions
CBU more reliably penalizes solutions with incorrect reasoning, unjustified compression, or unwarranted references because such flaws prevent the solution from being faithfully reused as a demonstration. A solver conditioned on such a flawed C will not consistently produce correct answers for Q*, yielding low utility. In contrast, LLM judges can be swayed by stylistic polish or authority cues. Evidence: Error analysis (Figure 5) shows CBU assigns above‑mean scores to wrong solutions far less often than judges; 71.4% of cases where a judge was fooled but CBU downranked involved unjustified compression, and 68.8% involved incorrect reasoning. Break condition: if the solver is powerful enough to fill gaps in flawed C, or if neighborhoods are so similar that the same mistake replicates, low utility may not indicate a flawed C.

## Foundational Learning
- **In‑Context Learning (ICL) as a Valuation Mechanism**: CBU treats a solution as an in‑context exemplar; the change in solver performance on a new task indicates the exemplar’s utility. *Why needed:* Without ground truth, we rely on proxy effects; ICL provides a natural way to measure transfer. *Quick check:* If both correct and incorrect solutions are provided as context for a new problem, what does ICL predict about relative answer correctness? What factors could cause this prediction to fail? (e.g., solver ignores context or problem is too hard/easy.)
- **Transfer Learning & Method‑Level Generalization**: CBU assumes correctness includes a transferable *method*, not just the final answer. A correct method should generalize to neighborhood variants. *Why needed:* This distinguishes CBU from answer‑matching or consistency voting that only compare outputs. *Quick check:* Design a pair of neighborhood questions where a solution with a *specific incorrect method* for the target nevertheless yields a correct answer for the neighbor by coincidence. Why would this be a failure mode for CBU?
- **Evaluation Without Ground Truth (Oracle‑Free Validation)**: The core problem is ranking solutions without a correctness oracle. CBU is a specific heuristic for this setting. *Why needed:* Standard reward models require pairwise preferences or answer supervision; in oracle‑free research math such data is unavailable. *Quick check:* What are the key design differences between a trained reward model (trained on preferences) and CBU (an inference‑time heuristic)? Why might a trained RM fail in this setting?

## Architecture Onboarding
The CBU pipeline consists of the following components connected in sequence:

Target Problem Queue -> Neighborhood Generator -> Solver Executor -> Local Verifier -> Utility Aggregator -> Ranker

- **Critical path:** Process starts with a target problem Q and its candidate solutions. The Neighborhood Generator produces N(Q). For each candidate C, the Solver Executor runs multiple rollouts on each neighbor Q* conditioned on (Q, C). The Local Verifier judges each rollout answer. The Utility Aggregator computes average accuracy across neighbors and rollouts to produce bU(C). Finally, the Ranker orders candidates by bU.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Neighborhood Construction Cost vs. Quality: Expert‑written neighborhoods are high‑quality but expensive; automated generation is cheap but may lack methodological linkage. Trade‑off between reliability and scalability.
  - Rollout Count T vs. Confidence: More rollouts reduce variance in bU. Paper uses T=64; T≥8 keeps normalized error <0.05. Choice depends on solver stochasticity and needed discrimination.
  - Solver Model Mθ Choice: Mθ must be strong enough to solve Q* with a correct hint but not so strong it solves unconditionally. Using the same model for candidate generation and solving introduces potential style‑matching bias but is computationally efficient.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Neighborhood dilution: Too many or redundant neighbors average out the signal; unrelated neighbors produce noise.
  - Solver saturation: If the solver unconditionally solves >90% of neighbors, conditional lift from C becomes invisible; CBU fails on easy neighborhoods.
  - Verifier brittleness: If the verifier itself is an LLM‑judge, biases are reintroduced; v must be trusted and low‑variance (e.g., formal prover, deterministic script).
  - Candidate pool composition: CBU ranks within a pool only; if all candidates share a flaw, a flawed one might still rank high; it does not produce absolute correctness.

- **First 3 experiments (validation checks):**
  1. **Stability vs. Rollout Count:** For fixed (Q, {C_i}, N(Q)), compute bU(C) for T ∈ {4,8,16,32,64}. Plot range‑normalized absolute error of bU(T) vs. bU(64). Confirm convergence behavior matches Figure 6.
  2. **Neighborhood Difficulty Calibration:** For a sample of N(Q), compute solver’s unconditional accuracy on each Q*. Discard Q* where accuracy >0.9 (too easy) or <0.1 (too hard). Verify retained neighbors have intermediate difficulty (e.g., 0.2–0.7).
  3. **Baseline Comparison Sanity Check:** On a small pilot set (e.g., 20 problems), run CBU and also score candidates with a simple LLM‑judge (default prompt). Compute pairwise rank correlation (Spearman’s ρ). Expect moderate correlation; near‑perfect correlation suggests degenerate neighborhoods or verifier.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can we fully automate the creation of neighborhood questions that are both closely related to a target problem and verifiable without human experts? *Basis:* Future work on improving fully automated neighborhood generation. *Why unresolved:* Current methods require faculty‑written variants or rely on model agreement with limited expert validation; fully automated generation remains unreliable for research‑level mathematics. *What evidence would resolve:* An end‑to‑end system that generates neighborhoods accepted by domain experts as valid and yields CBU scores consistent with those from human‑curated neighborhoods.
- **Open Question 2:** Can CBU be effectively generalized to non‑mathematical STEM domains (e.g., physics, biology) where solution quality can be assessed via downstream transfer? *Basis:* Future work on extending beyond mathematics. *Why unresolved:* CBU exploits formal verifiability and clear correctness in mathematics; other domains may lack well‑defined neighborhoods or verifiable rollouts. *What evidence would resolve:* Successful adaptation of CBU to at least one non‑math domain, showing correlation with expert judgments or task‑specific metrics.
- **Open Question 3:** How well does CBU evaluate solutions on genuinely open research problems where even the correct answer is unknown? *Basis:* Future work on evaluating effectiveness on open problems. *Why unresolved:* EXPERTMATH contains unsolved problems but evaluation still relies on expert solutions for targets; true open problems lack any ground truth, making standard validation impossible. *What evidence would resolve:* A study on contemporary open problems where multiple experts agree on plausibility rankings, and CBU scores align with that consensus.
- **Open Question 4:** What is the optimal difficulty range for neighborhood questions (“sweet spot”) and how can we automatically select it for a given target problem? *Basis:* Inferred from CBU’s dependence on neighborhood difficulty. *Why unresolved:* The sweet spot is qualitatively described but not quantified; selection currently depends on manual tuning or heuristics, which may not generalize. *What evidence would resolve:* A theoretical or empirical model that predicts the ideal difficulty interval based on target problem properties and solver capability, validated across diverse problems.
- **Open Question 5:** To what extent does the choice of solver model (Mθ) for the CBU rollouts affect the utility scores and recommendations? *Basis:* Inferred from model‑dependent aspects of neighborhood construction and difficulty calibration. *Why unresolved:* All experiments use the same model for candidate generation and as the solver; using a different solver could change discriminative power, but this dependence is unexplored. *What evidence would resolve:* Systematic evaluation using multiple solvers of varying strength for rollouts, showing whether CBU rankings remain stable across solver choices or can be calibrated.

## Limitations
- Neighborhood construction is a major bottleneck: expert‑written neighborhoods are costly; automated methods may lack methodological linkage, limiting scalability and reliability.
- CBU’s performance depends critically on the “sweet spot” difficulty of neighbors; mis‑calibration (too easy or too hard) collapses the utility signal.
- The method ranks solutions within a pool only; it does not produce an absolute correctness score and can be misled if all candidates share a common flaw.

## Confidence
- Performance on EXPERTMATH: High (strong empirical results with clear baselines)
- Robust evaluator–solver gap on difficult questions: Medium (trend shown on one benchmark; generalizability unproven)
- Penalizing flawed reasoning: Medium (compelling retrospective error analysis; mechanism not independently tested)

## Next Checks
1. **Neighborhood Construction Ablation:** Systematically vary neighborhood generation methods (expert‑written, template‑based, LLM‑generated) on a held‑out set. Measure CBU’s rank correlation with ground truth to isolate the contribution of linkage quality versus problem count.
2. **Cross‑Benchmark Difficulty Analysis:** Apply CBU to multiple math benchmarks with varying difficulty distributions. Verify that performance gains correlate with the neighborhood’s conditional difficulty gap (solver accuracy without vs. with a correct hint), testing the “sweet spot” hypothesis universally.
3. **Cost‑Utility Scaling Study:** For a fixed computational budget, compare CBU (with optimal T and |N|) against scaling up a strong LLM‑judge (e.g., more voting models, better prompts). Quantify the trade‑off between ranking accuracy and total inference cost to establish practical viability.
