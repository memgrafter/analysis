# Extracting Consumer Insight from Text: A Large Language Model Approach to Emotion and Evaluation Measurement

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15312
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15312
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LX achieves 81% macro-F1 on open-ended survey responses and >95% on third-party annotated Amazon/Yelp reviews, outperforming GPT-4 Turbo, RoBERTa, and DeepSeek.

## Executive Summary
Accurately measuring consumer emotions and evaluations from unstructured text is a core challenge in marketing. This research introduces the Linguistic eXtractor (LX), a fine-tuned large language model trained on consumer-authored text directly paired with self-reported ratings of 16 consumption-related emotions and four evaluations (trust, commitment, recommendation, sentiment). Using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), LX achieves high accuracy (81% macro-F1 on survey holdouts, >95% on Amazon/Yelp reviews) and significantly outperforms several baselines. Application to online retail clickstream data shows LX-derived emotions predict product ratings and purchase behavior, with most emotional effects mediated by ratings but some (e.g., discontent, peacefulness) exerting direct influence. A no-code, cost-free web app enables scalable deployment for researchers and practitioners.

## Method Summary
The authors collected 2,523 open-ended survey responses (US, CA, UK, AU) where respondents described a consumption experience and then immediately completed validated scales for 16 emotions (Richins 1997) and four evaluations (Morgan & Hunt 1994). This pairing created training data where the text is guaranteed to be authored by the person whose self-reported state is measured. The data was formatted as instruction-tuning examples, with prompts specifying the detection task and responses limited to option letters (e.g., "A"/"B" for binary emotions, ternary labels for evaluations). A Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct model was fine-tuned using LoRA (rank 16, α 32) in a single multi-task process across all 20 constructs. To handle severe class imbalance, oversampling was applied. Performance was evaluated using macro-F1 on held-out test sets, with comparisons to zero-shot baselines like GPT-4 Turbo and RoBERTa.

## Key Results
- LX achieves 81% macro-F1 on open-ended survey responses and exceeds 95% on Amazon and Yelp reviews, significantly outperforming GPT-4 Turbo, RoBERTa, and DeepSeek.
- Application to clickstream data reveals that LX-derived emotions predict product ratings and purchase behavior, with most effects mediated by star ratings but some emotions (discontent, peacefulness) having direct, unmediated influence on purchase decisions.
- The model's multi-task design allows a single fine-tuned model to accurately detect all 16 emotions and 4 evaluations, supported by a no-cost, no-code web app that enables scalable practical use.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Parameter-efficient fine-tuning via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).**  
Claim: LoRA enables accurate model specialization without prohibitive computational costs.  
Mechanism: LoRA freezes the pre-trained LLM weights and decomposes the weight update matrices into two low-rank matrices, drastically reducing trainable parameters (10,000× reduction, section 4.2.4) while preserving representational capacity. This makes fine-tuning feasible on a 3B-parameter base model.  
Core assumption: The essential knowledge for detecting marketing constructs resides in a low-dimensional subspace of the pre-trained model’s weight space.  
Evidence anchors: LoRA configured with rank r=16 and α=32 (section 4.2.4); 3B base model chosen for inference cost (section 4.3).  
Break condition: If the required capacity exceeds the low-rank subspace (r too small), the model fails to converge (macro-F1 < 0.70), especially on minority emotion classes.

**Mechanism 2: Training on consumer-authored text aligned with self-reports.**  
Claim: Direct alignment with consumers’ own scale responses yields construct-valid labels and avoids inferential errors from third-party coders.  
Mechanism: Survey design forces respondents to write open-ended text and then immediately select emotions/evaluations from validated scales. This temporal pairing guarantees the text is authored by the person whose internal state is measured.  
Core assumption: A consumer’s self-report on a validated scale is a valid ground truth for the emotional/evaluative state reflected in their contemporaneous description.  
Evidence anchors: Explicit contrast with third-party annotation (42.1% disagreement rate, Khan et al. 2025, section 4.1); critique of dictionary/transformer models not validated against self-reports (section 2).  
Break condition: If respondents misrepresent or misintrospect (social desirability bias), labels become noisy, degrading performance on socially sensitive emotions (e.g., shame, envy).

**Mechanism 3: Multi-task, single-model learning across related constructs.**  
Claim: Joint training on all 20 perception tasks allows the model to learn shared linguistic patterns of consumer perception, enhancing generalization.  
Mechanism: A single instruction-tuning process across all tasks forces the model to develop representations capturing the broader semantic space of consumer language, rather than overfitting to task-specific quirks.  
Core assumption: Linguistic cues for different consumption-related emotions and evaluations overlap sufficiently (e.g., positive valence markers for joy/contentment).  
Evidence anchors: Unified model architecture and prompt format (table 4); LX outperforms task-specific fine-tuned models across all tasks (table 5).  
Break condition: If tasks are semantically antagonistic (e.g., “cheap” positive for price but negative for quality), joint training causes interference, leading to poor performance on conflicting evaluations.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)**  
*Why needed here:* Provides the technical means to adapt a large LLM to a specific, multi-label classification task without full fine-tuning, core to the cost-free tool claim.  
*Quick check:* What two matrices does LoRA decompose the weight updates into, and why does a low rank matter?

**Concept: Construct Validity in Marketing Scales**  
*Why needed here:* Ensures model outputs correspond to theoretically meaningful marketing constructs (Richins’ emotions, Morgan & Hunt’s trust), justifying the contribution to marketing research versus generic NLP.  
*Quick check:* Why is alignment with established marketing scales (e.g., 7-point Likert items) more valid than using third-party review annotations?

**Concept: Multi-Task Learning for Classification**  
*Why needed here:* Enables a single model to handle 20 related classification tasks efficiently, leveraging shared signal to achieve high macro-F1.  
*Quick check:* How does the prompt design (table 4) allow a single model to perform both binary (emotion present) and ternary (positive/neutral/negative) classifications?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
CSV Upload -> Column Selection -> Perception Selection -> Prompt Engineering -> Fine-Tuned LLM (Llama 3.2-3B + LoRA) -> Output Parser -> CSV Output -> Email Download

**Critical path:**  
Data preparation (CSV file) -> Select text column and desired perceptions -> Server-side model inference -> Generate output CSV -> Email with download link to user.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Accuracy vs. Speed/Cost:** 3B model is smaller/faster; fine-tuning on domain data closes gap with larger models, accepting a minor accuracy tradeoff for accessibility.  
- **Specificity vs. Generalization:** Fine-tuning on survey data risks overfitting to that generation style; generalizability tested on Amazon/Yelp but not on novel domains (e.g., social media).  
- **Granularity vs. Complexity:** Detecting 20 constructs adds value but increases model size and risk of task interference; multi-task approach chosen for efficiency.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Model-level:** Low confidence scores or inconsistent predictions; high error on rare emotions (envy, loneliness, romantic love) despite oversampling.  
- **System-level:** Upload failures for non-UTF8 CSVs, memory errors for large files, or no email due to form submission errors.  
- **Domain shift:** Sharp macro-F1 drop on text styles not in training (e.g., tweets, formal language), indicating limited linguistic coverage.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Benchmark replication:** Run exact test sets from Study 1 (survey holdout) and Study 2 (Amazon/Yelp holdout) through the web app to confirm reported F1 scores (tables 5, 7, 8) match production output.  
2. **Boundary testing:** Input short, sarcastic, or ambiguous texts (e.g., "Great, another broken product!") and verify appropriate emotion/sentiment predictions, checking negation and mixed-emotion handling.  
3. **Output verification:** Upload a CSV with mixed texts, select all emotions and evaluations, and validate output CSV contains binary dummies for all 16 emotions and ternary (-1/0/1) values for all 4 evaluations plus word count, per the user manual.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Cross-category variation:** How do meanings and impacts of specific emotions vary across product categories and contexts? LX trained on broad retail reviews; emotional signals and behavioral consequences may differ in wellness, tech, or luxury domains.  
2. **Longitudinal dynamics:** How can LX be applied to longitudinal data, post-crisis brand commentary, or repeat consumption contexts to track emotional evolution? Study 3 uses static snapshots; temporal emotional trajectories and their predictive power remain unexplored.  
3. **Multilingual generalizability:** Does LX generalize to non-English languages? Training and validation were exclusively English; cross-linguistic emotional expression and cultural norms may affect accuracy.  
4. **Multimodal extension:** Can LX be extended to multimodal inputs (images, audio) and firm-generated marketing content? Current text-only focus poses technical and validity challenges for integration.  
5. **Psychological mechanisms:** What mechanisms explain why certain emotions (e.g., discontent, peacefulness) exert direct effects on purchase behavior independent of ratings? Paper speculates (e.g., credibility mismatches) but does not test mediating pathways.

## Limitations
- **Data Access Barrier:** The core survey dataset (2,523 responses) is not public; replication requires recollecting data with identical prompts/scales, risking demographic and linguistic distribution shifts.  
- **Domain Shift Risk:** Generalization to novel consumer text domains (e.g., social media, short messages, non-English) is unproven and may degrade significantly.  
- **Annotation Ground Truth:** Reliance on self-reported labels assumes perfect introspection and honesty; social desirability or recall bias introduces unknown noise, especially for socially sensitive emotions.

## Confidence
- **LoRA Efficiency:** High (well-specified hyperparameters, 10,000× parameter reduction, reproducible).  
- **Self-Report Label Validity:** Medium (theoretical contrast with third-party annotation, but no external gold standard for true emotional state).  
- **Multi-Task Generalization Benefit:** Medium (strong macro-F1 across 20 tasks, but task interference for minority classes not fully ruled out).

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark replication:** Using a newly collected survey-style dataset (N≈500), fine-tune Llama-3.2-3B with LoRA (r=16, α=32) and confirm macro-F1 on holdout set is within ±0.03 of the reported 81%.  
2. **Boundary condition testing:** Input 100 short, sarcastic, or ambiguous consumer texts into the deployed web app (or replicated model) and verify predictions align with human-interpreted intent, focusing on negation and mixed-emotion detection.  
3. **Cross-domain stress test:** Apply the final LX model to 500 product-related tweets or Instagram captions (a non-training domain) and compare macro-F1 to zero-shot GPT-4 Turbo; a >10% absolute drop for LX would confirm domain shift limitations.
