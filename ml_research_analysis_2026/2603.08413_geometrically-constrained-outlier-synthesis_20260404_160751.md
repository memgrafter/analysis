# Geometrically Constrained Outlier Synthesis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08413  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08413  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: GCOS achieves state-of-the-art near-OOD detection with average AUROC 93.47% across four datasets, reducing FPR95 dramatically (e.g., to 1% on Colored MNIST).

## Executive Summary
Deep neural networks exhibit overconfidence on out-of-distribution (OOD) samples, particularly in challenging near-OOD scenarios where outliers share the same semantic domain as in-distribution data. Existing virtual outlier synthesis methods rely on simplistic parametric assumptions about the feature space. Geometrically Constrained Outlier Synthesis (GCOS) addresses this by generating virtual outliers in the hidden feature space that respect the learned manifold structure. It uses PCA to identify low-variance off-manifold directions and a conformal shell defined by Mahalanobis distance quantiles to produce boundary samples of appropriate difficulty. A contrastive loss then promotes separability in the energy score space. GCOS achieves state-of-the-art performance on near-OOD benchmarks, with an average AUROC of 93.47% across four datasets, significantly outperforming prior methods like NCIS (91.97%). It reduces FPR95 dramatically, e.g., to 1% on Colored MNIST from 32.5% with no regularization and 18.5% with VOS. Feature visualizations confirm GCOS places outliers to tightly flank decision boundaries around data clusters.

## Method Summary
GCOS is a training-time regularization framework for OOD detection. Each epoch, after a warm-up phase, it performs online calibration: features from a held-out calibration set compute class-conditional PCA and Mahalanobis scores; the 95th and 99th percentiles of these scores define inner and outer boundaries (a conformal shell) per class. During batch training, a feature buffer accumulates ID features; once full, PCA on the buffer yields low-variance directions. For each class and direction, binary search finds deviations α such that the Mahalanobis score of the synthetic point equals the calibration quantiles. α is sampled from the resulting interval and used to generate an outlier in feature space. A contrastive loss on energy scores (with an adaptive margin) is added to the classification loss to push apart ID and synthetic outlier scores. The model is trained end-to-end with this regularization, using the same backbone for feature extraction and classification.

## Key Results
- Average AUROC of 93.47% across four near-OOD datasets, beating prior best (NCIS) by 1.5%.
- FPR95 reduced to 1% on Colored MNIST (from 32.5% with no regularization and 18.5% with VOS).
- Feature visualizations show outliers placed tightly flanking decision boundaries, confirming effective geometric synthesis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: GCOS generates outliers by moving in low-variance principal component directions, which approximate off-manifold directions.  
**Mechanism**: PCA is applied to class-conditional features; the eigenvectors with smallest eigenvalues define directions where the data has low variability. Perturbations along these directions yield points that are statistically unlikely yet remain near the data centroid, creating challenging synthetic outliers.  
**Core assumption**: The smallest-variance principal components span the normal space orthogonal to the data manifold; moving along them produces points that are off-manifold without being arbitrarily far.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
  - [Abstract]: "uses PCA to identify low-variance off-manifold directions"  
  - [Section 3]: "The smallest variability in the data... moving along them produces unlikely points while still remaining near the data centroid"  
  - [corpus]: Neighboring papers (e.g., VOS, Dream-OOD) rely on parametric distributions or pixel-space generation, not feature-space geometry.  
**Break condition**: If the feature manifold is highly nonlinear or the PCA subspace poorly approximates the normal space (e.g., due to high noise or insufficient samples), generated points may be trivial or overlap with ID data.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: A conformal shell defined by Mahalanobis distance quantiles adaptively controls the magnitude of outlier synthesis, producing "hard negatives" of appropriate difficulty.  
**Mechanism**: For each class, Mahalanobis scores are computed on a held-out calibration set. The 95th and 99th percentiles become inner (α_inner) and outer (α_outer) boundaries. For a given low-variance direction, binary search finds the deviations α that place a candidate point exactly on these score thresholds. Outliers are sampled uniformly from α ∈ [α_inner, α_outer].  
**Core assumption**: The Mahalanobis distance is monotonic along the generated direction; the quantiles define a region of points that are unlikely but not impossibly far, avoiding trivially easy or impossibly hard outliers.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
  - [Abstract]: "conformal shell defined by the empirical quantiles of a nonconformity score"  
  - [Section 3]: "α_inner is the minimum deviation required such that the nonconformity score... equals the lower threshold"  
  - [corpus]: Conformal prediction has been used for uncertainty quantification but not for training-time outlier synthesis.  
**Break condition**: Non-monotonicity of the score along the direction or a calibration set that is not exchangeable with training data can cause the shell to misalign, yielding outliers that are either indistinguishable or obviously OOD.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: A contrastive regularization loss on nonconformity scores (e.g., energy) directly promotes separability between in-distribution and synthetic outliers in the score space.  
**Mechanism**: Given a batch of ID features Z_id and synthesized outliers Z_ood, compute positive scores S_pos = S(Z_id | class) and negative scores S_neg = S(Z_ood). The loss L_reg = mean( ReLU( S_pos − S_neg + m ) ) minimizes S_pos and maximizes S_neg with an adaptive margin m (derived from batch statistics of S_pos). This pushes the score distributions apart.  
**Core assumption**: Optimizing this loss shapes the model’s internal representations so that the chosen score (energy or Mahalanobis) becomes more discriminative for OOD detection.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
  - [Abstract]: "contrastive loss then promotes separability in the energy score space"  
  - [Section 3]: Equation (5) and accompanying text define L_reg with adaptive margin.  
  - [corpus]: Contrastive losses are common in outlier exposure (e.g., VOS) but GCOS couples them with geometry-driven synthesis.  
**Break condition**: If the selected score space poorly correlates with true OODness (e.g., energy landscape is noisy early in training), the loss may fail to improve separability or could even harm classification accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**  
  Why needed here: PCA decomposes the covariance of class features to extract the dominant (high-variance) and residual (low-variance) subspaces. GCOS uses the low-variance subspace to define off-manifold directions for outlier synthesis.  
  Quick check: "After applying PCA to the feature vectors of a single class, what geometric property do the eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues represent about the data distribution?"

- **Mahalanobis Distance**  
  Why needed here: Mahalanobis distance measures deviation from a class mean while accounting for class covariance. GCOS uses it as a monotonic nonconformity score to set synthesis magnitude (the conformal shell) and optionally as a regularization loss.  
  Quick check: "How does Mahalanobis distance incorporate the covariance structure of the data, and why is this particularly important in high-dimensional feature spaces?"

- **Conformal Prediction (Quantile-based heuristic)**  
  Why needed here: The conformal shell uses empirical quantiles of a score on a calibration set to adaptively define outlier difficulty. This avoids manual threshold selection and ensures generated outliers are neither too easy nor too hard.  
  Quick check: "In conformal prediction, what does the (1−α) quantile of the nonconformity scores on a calibration set represent, and how does it control the false positive rate in hypothesis testing?"

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Backbone (f_θ) extracts features -> Classifier head (h_ϕ) produces logits. Features also feed a feature queue (Q_train) and PCA module. PCA module uses calibration data to compute M_calib and Q_train to compute M_train. Conformal shell calculator uses M_calib's Mahalanobis scores to get q_inner, q_outer. Outlier synthesizer uses M_train and q thresholds to generate z_ood. z_ood and ID features are scored (energy) by the scoring module; contrastive loss module uses these scores to compute L_reg. Training loop combines cross-entropy loss and λ·L_reg.

### Critical path
- **Epoch-level**: After warm-up, run D_calib through current model → compute M_calib per class → compute Mahalanobis scores → extract quantiles (q_inner, q_outer).  
- **Batch-level**: Forward pass → get Z_id → update Q_train. If Q_train full and epoch ≥ E_start, compute M_train from Q_train. For each class and each low-variance direction, call FindBoundaryAlpha (using M_calib’s quantiles as targets) to get α boundaries, sample α, produce z_ood. Compute energy scores for Z_id and z_ood, compute L_reg with adaptive margin, add to total loss, backpropagate.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Score choice for synthesis**: Mahalanobis is used because it is monotonic along directions; energy is non-monotonic and unsuitable for the binary search.  
- **Covariance estimation**: Shared across classes (more robust with few samples) vs. per-class (more specific). Per-class may improve performance when data is abundant.  
- **Direction sampling**: Average direction (single outlier per class) reduces compute but may lose diversity; per-direction (multiple) increases outlier count and diversity.  
- **Regularization weight λ**: Balances ID classification accuracy vs. OOD robustness; too high may harm ID performance.

### Failure signatures
- **Noisy PCA**: Small calibration sets yield unreliable eigenvectors, causing outliers to be meaningless.  
- **Non-monotonic score**: If energy were used for synthesis, FindBoundaryAlpha would not converge; GCOS avoids this by using Mahalanobis for synthesis.  
- **Distribution shift**: If calibration data differs from training data (e.g., covariate shift), quantiles misrepresent appropriate outlier difficulty.  
- **Stale feature queue**: If Q_train is not refreshed often, M_train becomes outdated, leading to misaligned synthesis directions.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Visual sanity check**: Train on MNIST, project final features with UMAP. Overlay GCOS outliers; they should appear in low-density regions near decision boundaries, not far from any class cluster.  
2. **Ablation of conformal shell**: Replace adaptive α boundaries with a fixed multiple of the class’s feature standard deviation. Compare OOD detection (AUROC, FPR95) on Colored MNIST. The version with the conformal shell should achieve lower FPR95.  
3. **Sensitivity to variance threshold η**: Vary η (e.g., {0.8, 0.85, 0.9, 0.95}) on a single dataset. Expect performance to peak around η=0.9 and degrade as η→1 because too few low-variance components remain for effective synthesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: How can conformal hypothesis testing for OOD detection be improved to achieve both high detection accuracy and formal statistical guarantees?  
**Basis**: [explicit] The authors present exploratory results using conformal prediction for OOD detection in Section 6, noting that "the use of conformal hypothesis testing as an OOD detection head... yields mixed results" and that "the technique demonstrates promising potential and represents an emerging area for further research." They also state that "if the community succeeds in making the procedure more consistent and accurate while retaining conformal guarantees, it could establish a framework..."  
**Why unresolved**: Current conformal OOD detection shows competitive performance only on simple datasets like Colored MNIST but collapses to near-random on others (e.g., Stanford Dogs, Retinopathy), failing to achieve practical utility despite offering statistical guarantees.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: A systematic study varying the nonconformity score (beyond Mahalanobis/Energy), calibration set size, and significance level on diverse near-OOD benchmarks; development of a method that learns a calibrated score function within the conformal framework to boost power while controlling type I error.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: Can the nonconformity score function in GCOS be learned end-to-end instead of manually specified, and would such automation improve OOD detection performance?  
**Basis**: [explicit] The authors remark that "the manual choice of the nonconformity score function is reminiscent of handcrafted filters in the early days of computer vision" and note that "recent methods have explored ways to integrate uncertainty-aware mechanisms directly into the learning process." However, GCOS relies on predetermined scores (Mahalanobis for synthesis, Energy for regularization).  
**Why unresolved**: The paper does not explore learning the score; it manually selects between Mahalanobis and Energy based on monotonicity and other properties. An optimal score might combine geometric and confidence information adaptively per dataset or class, potentially yielding better separability.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Designing a parametric score function (e.g., a neural network) that takes features and outputs a nonconformity score, trained jointly with the classifier using the GCOS regularization objective, and evaluating on near-OOD benchmarks to see if automatic learning surpasses hand-crafted scores.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: What is the theoretical relationship between the Mahalanobis distance in feature space and the energy-based confidence score, and can this relationship be formalized to guide the design of OOD regularization objectives?  
**Basis**: [inferred] The authors observe that optimizing a Mahalanobis-based geometric regularization loss (L_reg(Mahalanobis)) effectively reduces energy scores for ID samples and increases them for outliers, but the reverse—optimizing an energy-based loss—does not regularize the Mahalanobis distance. They provide intuitive explanations (geometric compactness leading to confident logits, linear boundaries coupling geometry and confidence) but no formal analysis.  
**Why unresolved**: The intuitive explanation suggests a deep connection but lacks mathematical rigor; without formalization, it is unclear whether the observed asymmetry holds generally across architectures and datasets, or how to exploit it for better regularization.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Theoretical derivation linking Mahalanobis distance to the expected energy under a model (e.g., assuming Gaussian class-conditional distributions in feature space); empirical verification across diverse architectures (ResNet, ViT) and datasets; ablation studies that decouple geometry and confidence to test causality.

### Open Question 4
**Question**: To what extent does the GCOS framework generalize to non-image data modalities (e.g., text, graphs) and to tasks beyond classification, such as regression or structured prediction?  
**Basis**: [inferred] GCOS is evaluated exclusively on image classification benchmarks (Colored MNIST, MVTec, Stanford Dogs, Retinopathy). The core synthesis mechanism relies on PCA in the penultimate feature space, assuming that off-manifold directions correspond to low-variance principal components and that a linear subspace model is appropriate. These assumptions may not hold for text embeddings (which often lie on curved manifolds) or graph representations (which are non-Euclidean).  
**Why unresolved**: The paper does not test GCOS on non-image data; thus, its applicability to other domains is unknown. The reliance on linear PCA could be a limiting factor if the feature manifolds are highly nonlinear or if the concept of "off-manifold" differs.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Applying GCOS to OOD detection tasks in NLP (e.g., using BERT features for sentiment classification with OOD text) and graph neural networks (e.g., molecular property prediction with OOD compounds), comparing performance against baselines and analyzing whether PCA-based synthesis remains effective or requires manifold-specific adaptations.

## Limitations
- The method relies on PCA to identify off-manifold directions, which may fail if the feature manifold is highly nonlinear or if the PCA subspace poorly approximates the normal space due to high noise or insufficient samples.
- The conformal shell assumes monotonicity of the Mahalanobis score along the generated direction; violations due to distribution shift or non-monotonicity lead to poor outlier placement.
- The choice of nonconformity score for synthesis and regularization is manual; an inappropriate combination (e.g., using energy for synthesis) degrades performance.

## Confidence
- GCOS achieves state-of-the-art near-OOD detection performance (average AUROC 93.47% across four datasets): **High**  
- The proposed geometric synthesis (PCA + conformal shell) generates effective virtual outliers: **Medium**  
- The contrastive regularization in energy space is responsible for improved separability: **Medium**
