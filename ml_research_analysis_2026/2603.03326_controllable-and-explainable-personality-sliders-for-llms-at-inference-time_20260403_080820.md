# Controllable and explainable personality sliders for LLMs at inference time

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.03326
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03326
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Sequential Adaptive Steering (SAS) enables simultaneous, interference-free control over multiple Big Five personality traits in LLMs at inference time, outperforming naive vector addition and establishing a Pareto frontier of trait adherence versus model coherence.

## Executive Summary
Current methods for tailoring LLM personas require expensive retraining, while naive inference-time steering by adding activation vectors fails for multiple traits due to destructive interference. This paper introduces Sequential Adaptive Steering (SAS), a method that orthogonalizes personality steering vectors by training each subsequent probe on residuals shifted by prior interventions. Validated across Llama-3, Mistral, and Qwen models on the Big Five traits, SAS achieves precise, concurrent control over multiple dimensions with minimal coherence loss, significantly surpassing naive baselines.

## Method Summary
SAS proceeds in three phases: (1) For each personality trait, extract mid-layer residual activations from a labeled dataset (BIG5-CHAT) and train a linear logistic regression probe. (2) Select an intervention layer per trait via the maximal Fisher Ratio across middle layers. (3) Train probes sequentially: the first probe is trained normally; each subsequent probe *k* is trained on a mixed dataset that includes unsteered activations and activations already steered by predecessor probes (with scaling factors α sampled from a stable range). This forces later probes to adapt to the subspace remaining after earlier interventions, yielding orthogonal vectors. At inference, all trained probes are added at their respective layers with calibrated α values for every token generated. α bounds are determined per trait via grid search to maximize trait shift while constraining perplexity increase (<50%) and F1 drop (<25%) on standard benchmarks.

## Key Results
- SAS enables simultaneous, independent steering of multiple Big Five traits (e.g., Extraversion and Neuroticism), whereas naive multi-vector addition causes severe interference and coherence collapse.
- SAS dominates naive baselines on goal adherence (LLM-as-a-Judge trait scores) and model coherence (perplexity, benchmark F1), establishing a clear Pareto frontier.
- The method is validated across Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen2.5-7B, demonstrating broad applicability within the 7B–8B parameter range.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that naive linear addition of independently trained steering vectors fails because trait representations in the residual stream are not orthogonal; activating one dimension inadvertently alters others. SAS addresses this by making the vectors orthogonal *by construction*. Training a later probe on data already perturbed by earlier probes forces it to learn a direction that primarily influences its target trait *within the subspace not already controlled*. This sequential residual training aligns the vectors with a nested set of subspaces, minimizing cross-talk. The stable α range used during training teaches the probe to remain effective across the anticipated shift magnitudes, preventing overfitting to extreme perturbations that would harm coherence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear Probes for Concept Detection**: A linear classifier (logistic regression) trained on model activations to separate data by a categorical label (e.g., high/low trait). *Why needed*: Provides a directional vector in activation space that, when added, steers the model toward the target class. *Quick check*: Probe accuracy on held-out activation data should be significantly above chance (~0.5 for binary).
- **Fisher Ratio for Layer Selection**: A statistic (mean difference over variance) comparing class activation distributions to identify layers most discriminative for a concept. *Why needed*: Pinpoints the intervention layer where the trait signal is strongest and most isolated from lower-level syntax or higher-level token prediction. *Quick check*: Fisher Ratio should show a clear peak in middle layers, not monotonically increasing or decreasing.
- **Residual Stream Steering**: Modifying the residual activations (pre-activation values) at a specific layer during forward passes. *Why needed*: Residual connections allow a localized change to propagate through the entire model, influencing final output. *Quick check*: Single-trait steering should show a monotonic relationship between steering scale (α) and judge-assessed trait score.
- **Sequential Residual Training**: Training a later concept's probe on data already perturbed by earlier probes. *Why needed*: Encourages orthogonality by minimizing the second probe's direction in the subspace already targeted. *Quick check*: Cosine similarity between SAS-trained vectors for different traits should be low (<0.2), contrasting with high similarity for naive vectors.
- **α Calibration via Grid Search**: Systematically searching for a scaling factor range that balances trait shift against coherence metrics. *Why needed*: Prevents excessive intervention that would degrade generation quality. *Quick check*: The selected α_max should induce a large trait score increase but keep perplexity within 1.5× baseline and F1 within 75% of baseline.
- **LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation**: Using a powerful LLM (e.g., GPT-4) to score generated text on trait dimensions via structured prompts. *Why needed*: Provides a scalable, consistent proxy for human personality assessment. *Quick check*: Judge scores for unsteered baseline should align with known population averages for the model's pre-training persona.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: Input Prompt -> Model Forward Pass (with intervention at selected layer: original_activation + α * probe_vector) -> Generated Output -> Judge/Coherence Evaluation. Probe Training Pipeline: Labeled Dataset -> Activation Extraction -> Probe Training (sequential for SAS) -> Layer Selection (Fisher Ratio) -> α Calibration.
- **Critical Path**: The success hinges on the sequential probe training step. If residual training data does not adequately cover the stable α range of predecessors, vectors will not be properly orthogonalized, leading to interference.
- **Design Tradeoffs**: 
  - *Intervention Layer*: Earlier layers may affect more abstract traits but risk syntactic disruption; later layers risk being too close to token prediction for coherent steering. Mid-layers offer a balance.
  - *α Range*: A wider range during SAS training improves robustness but risks including unstable regions that harm coherence; the calibration step constrains deployment.
  - *Linear Probes*: Simple and interpretable but may miss nonlinear trait manifestations; the paper explicitly leaves nonlinear extensions as future work.
- **Failure Signatures**:
  - High cosine similarity (>0.3) between final SAS vectors indicates failed orthogonalization.
  - Multi-trait evaluation shows one trait shifting as desired while another remains unchanged or shifts inversely.
  - Perplexity increases sharply (>2×) at α values far below the calibrated α_max, suggesting probe instability.
- **First Experiments**:
  1. **Single-Trait Sanity Check**: For Extraversion on Llama-3-8B, extract activations, train a probe, select layer via Fisher Ratio, sweep α from -2 to 2, and confirm monotonic increase in judge-assigned Extraversion score with minimal perplexity rise.
  2. **Naive Multi-Trait Failure**: Train independent probes for Extraversion and Neuroticism. Apply both at their layers with fixed α (e.g., α chosen from single-trait max). Expect significant perplexity increase and non-independent trait scoring.
  3. **SAS Orthogonalization**: Train Extraversion probe first. Train Neuroticism probe on data steered by Extraversion (with random α in a tentative stable range). Evaluate: Neuroticism score should shift independently of Extraversion, and perplexity should be much lower than in the naive case for equivalent total shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does SAS remain effective and coherent when applied to models with 70B+ parameters?
- Can nonlinear steering controllers (e.g., MLP-based probes) improve multi-trait control and orthogonalization beyond linear SAS?
- Does SAS generalize to steerable attributes beyond the Big Five, such as safety alignment or writing style?
- How do larger, more diverse training datasets and deeper layer-wise stability analysis affect SAS robustness and optimal intervention points?
- Can SAS be adapted for closed-source models that lack white-box activation access?

## Limitations
- α sampling distribution during SAS training is unspecified, requiring ad hoc assumption.
- Exact layer selection window (start/end indices) for a given architecture is not detailed, leaving implementation ambiguity.
- Scope of token steering (all tokens vs. prompt-only) is not clarified.
- Evaluation relies on LLM-as-a-Judge, whose alignment with human perception is not validated.
- Generalizability beyond the Big Five personality traits and 7B–8B scale models is untested.

## Confidence
- **Simultaneous control without interference**: Medium (depends on correct α range and layer selection, which are underspecified).
- **Outperformance over naive steering**: High (empirical results are consistent across three distinct model families).
- **Pareto frontier dominance**: Medium (α calibration stability and exact reproducibility could affect the precise frontier shape).
- **Core orthogonalization mechanism (sequential residual training)**: High (theoretical justification is sound and failure modes are clearly predicted).

## Next Checks
1. Perform a comprehensive layer sweep on Llama-3-8B for all five traits, computing Fisher Ratios across all middle layers, to verify that the automatically selected layer is a robust peak and not an artifact of a narrow window.
2. Ablate the α sampling range used during SAS training (e.g., [-0.5,0.5], [-1,1], [-2,2]) and measure resulting vector orthogonality (cosine similarity) and multi-trait independence to identify the minimal stable range.
3. Conduct a judge consistency study: re-evaluate a subset of generations using two additional judges (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) to assess score correlation and potential judge bias.
