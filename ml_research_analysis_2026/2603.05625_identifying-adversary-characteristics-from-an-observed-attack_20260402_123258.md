# Identifying Adversary Characteristics from an Observed Attack
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05625  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05625  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The framework achieved significant percent error reduction (PER) in recovered attacker parameters: median PER 99.14% (Linear Regression), 13.35% (Logistic Regression), and 25.25% (MLP) over 100 trials.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of inferring an attacker’s characteristics (knowledge, capability, objective) from a single observed attack perturbation on a machine learning model. The authors propose a domain-agnostic framework that models the identification task as a probabilistic reverse‑optimization: the unknown attacker parameters are latent variables, and the defender maximizes the posterior probability given prior beliefs and the observed attack. This bi‑level optimization searches over (K, C, O) while repeatedly solving the inner attack problem. For linear models the inner problem admits a closed‑form solution, making the overall objective a convex quadratic; for non‑linear models the inner attack is approximated via projected gradient descent.

Across three model‑attacker configurations, the method achieves substantial error reduction over prior‑only baselines. On linear regression (repulsive attacker) the median percent error reduction (PER) reaches 99.14% (max 99.65%). For logistic regression and MLP (both attractive attackers) the median PER is 13.35% and 25.25% respectively, though with higher variance. The results indicate the approach is most reliable for linear models, while performance on non‑linear models varies due to optimization challenges and potential attacker suboptimality.

## Method Summary
The framework constructs a probabilistic model where the true attacker parameters (K, C, O) are latent. Given a defender’s trained model and an observed attack α_obs, the defender seeks the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate:

\[
(\hat{K},\hat{C},\hat{O}) = \arg\max_{K,C,O} \log p(K,C,O) + \log p(\alpha_{\text{obs}} \mid \alpha_{\text{opt}}(K,C,O)).
\]

Here \(p(K,C,O)\) is a Gaussian prior (typically centered at reasonable defaults), and \(p(\alpha_{\text{obs}} \mid \alpha_{\text{opt}})\) is a likelihood term that penalizes the mismatch between the observed attack and the attacker’s optimal attack under the candidate parameters, often taken as \(\|\alpha_{\text{obs}} - \alpha_{\text{opt}}\|_2^2\). The optimization is bi‑level: the outer loop updates (K, C, O), and for each candidate the inner loop computes \(\alpha_{\text{opt}} = \arg\max_\alpha \mathcal{L}(\alpha; K, C, O)\) subject to the attacker’s capability constraints \(C\). For linear regression with a repulsive attacker (parameterization 1) the inner attack has a closed‑form solution (scaled principal singular vector), reducing the entire problem to a quadratic program. For logistic regression and MLP (attractive attackers), the inner attack is solved approximately via projected gradient descent (PGD), and the outer parameters are optimized with Adam, requiring gradients through the inner solution. The trade‑off between prior trust and evidence is controlled by a weight λ (set to 0.1 in experiments). The recovered parameters are evaluated by whether their optimal attack closely matches α_obs, measured by Percent Error Reduction (PER) relative to a prior‑only baseline.

## Key Results
- **Linear Regression (repulsive attacker):** Median PER = 99.14% (max 99.65%) over 100 trials.  
- **Logistic Regression (attractive attacker):** Median PER = 13.35% (max 84.56%).  
- **Multi‑Layer Perceptron (attractive attacker):** Median PER = 25.25% (max 71.68%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The framework resolves the fundamental non‑identifiability of attacker parameters by treating (K, C, O) as latent variables within a probabilistic model, using prior beliefs to select the most probable explanation.
- **Mechanism:** The defender posits a prior \(p(K,C,O)\) and maximizes the posterior \(p(K,C,O|\alpha_{\text{obs}}) \propto p(K,C,O) \cdot p(\alpha_{\text{obs}}|\alpha_{\text{opt}}(K,C,O))\). This searches for attacker parameters whose optimal attack \(\alpha_{\text{opt}}\) is most likely to produce the observed \(\alpha_{\text{obs}}\), combining prior expectations and evidence.
- **Core assumption:** \(\alpha_{\text{obs}}\) is a noisy realization of \(\alpha_{\text{opt}}\) under true parameters; the prior is well‑specified or overfitting is controlled by λ.
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 3.2 proves non‑identifiability for the linear case; Equation 20 defines the bi‑level objective. Related work on inverse reinforcement learning similarly models adversaries as utility optimizers.
- **Break condition:** A severely misspecified prior (mean far from truth, narrow variance) or extreme unmodeled attacker suboptimality can cause the posterior mode to converge to an incorrect (K, C, O) tuple.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The framework operationalizes reverse‑engineering as a bi‑level optimization problem, where an outer loop searches over attacker parameters and an inner loop computes the corresponding optimal attack.
- **Mechanism:** The outer optimization updates estimates (K, C, O) to maximize the combined objective. For each candidate, the inner optimization solves the attacker’s problem \(\alpha_{\text{opt}} = \arg\max_\alpha \mathcal{L}(\alpha; K, C, O)\) subject to constraints \(C\). The outer objective then measures mismatch between \(\alpha_{\text{obs}}\) and \(\alpha_{\text{opt}}\) (likelihood term) and deviation from the prior; λ balances the two.
- **Core assumption:** The inner optimization is solvable to sufficient precision; the gradient of the outer objective w.r.t. (K, C, O) can be approximated (e.g., via implicit differentiation or finite differences).
- **Evidence anchors:** Equations 20–24 (Section 3) explicitly define the bi‑level structure; Section 4 describes using Adam with inner attack optimization at each epoch.
- **Break condition:** The inner optimization may fail to converge to a high‑quality \(\alpha_{\text{opt}}\) for complex non‑linear models, especially with poor initialization, leading to noisy gradients and unstable outer learning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** For linear models, the bi‑level problem simplifies to a tractable, primarily quadratic optimization due to analytical solutions for the inner attack and Gaussian priors.
- **Mechanism:** With a linear defender \(f(x)=M x\) and a repulsive attacker under Mahalanobis constraints, the inner problem has a closed‑form: \(\alpha_{\text{opt}} = c \cdot G^{-1} s_1\), where \(s_1\) is the leading right singular vector of \(W^{1/2} M G^{-1}\). This allows the likelihood term to be expressed directly in terms of \(M, W, C\), transforming the overall objective into a sum of squared Frobenius norms (quadratic forms).
- **Core assumption:** The defender model is linear; the attacker solves the inner problem exactly; Gaussian priors have identity covariance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Lemma 3.3 and Appendix C derive the linear reduction; Section 4.1 attributes superb performance and low variance to this analytical tractability.
- **Break condition:** The simplification is specific to the linear attacker/defender pair; non‑linear models lack closed form, requiring iterative inner attacks and resulting in non‑convex, high‑dimensional optimization with higher variance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Adversarial Machine Learning Taxonomy (K, C, O):**  
  *Why needed:* The framework models attackers by their Knowledge (model belief \(M\)), Capability (perturbation constraints \(C\)), and Objective (loss function \(W\) or target class). This parameterization defines the search space.  
  *Quick check:* For an attractive logistic regression attacker, what do the sets \(K\), \(C\), and \(O\) concretely contain? (Answer: \(K\): attacker’s belief about model weights; \(C\): perturbation set, e.g., box bounds; \(O\): target class vector.)

- **Bi‑level Optimization:**  
  *Why needed:* The core algorithm is a bi‑level problem: outer loop over (K, C, O), inner loop over attacks. Understanding bi‑level optimization is essential to grasp the training dynamics and challenges.  
  *Quick check:* What is the role of the \(\lambda\) parameter in the bi‑level objective, and what trade‑off does it control? (Answer: \(\lambda\) balances the prior log‑likelihood (regularization) against the log‑likelihood of the observed attack; it trades off trust in prior beliefs versus trust in the observed evidence.)

- **Probabilistic Modeling of Latent Variables:**  
  *Why needed:* The true attacker parameters are latent; inference is performed via MAP estimation using a prior and a likelihood model. Comfort with defining and combining distributions is required.  
  *Quick check:* Why can’t the defender simply solve \(\alpha_{\text{obs}} = \alpha_{\text{opt}}(K, C, O)\) for \((K, C, O)\), and how does the probabilistic formulation address this? (Answer: The equation is generally non‑identifiable—many parameter tuples yield the same optimal attack. The prior picks the most probable explanation among equivalent solutions.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Attacker Parameter Sampler/Initializer → Inner Attack Solver → Outer Objective Evaluator → Outer Optimizer

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize \((K, C, O)\) from prior distribution.  
2. Inner Solver computes \(\alpha_{\text{opt}}\) for the current parameters.  
3. Outer Objective Evaluator computes \(\text{Loss}_{\text{outer}} = \lambda \cdot \text{prior\_deviation} + \|\alpha_{\text{obs}} - \alpha_{\text{opt}}\|^2\).  
4. Outer Optimizer updates \((K, C, O)\) to minimize \(\text{Loss}_{\text{outer}}\).  
5. Repeat until convergence or maximum epochs.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Accuracy vs. Compute:* Exact inner solutions (linear case) yield stable, fast convergence but are limited to specific model families. Approximate inner attacks (non‑linear) generalize but introduce bias and require more outer steps.  
- *Prior strength (\(\lambda\)):* High \(\lambda\) stabilizes training for noisy/non‑linear cases but may bias results away from the observed evidence; low \(\lambda\) trusts the attack more but risks overfitting to a possibly suboptimal \(\alpha_{\text{obs}}\). Tuning \(\lambda\) is critical.  
- *Parameterization choice:* The representation of \(K\) (e.g., weight matrices \(M\) vs. layer‑wise parameters \(W^\ell\)) affects problem dimensionality and conditioning, influencing optimization speed and success.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Linear case:* PER near 0% or negative, or large \(\|\alpha_{\text{obs}}-\alpha_{\text{opt}}\|\) after convergence, indicating poor prior initialization or implementation errors in the analytical branch.  
- *Non‑linear cases:* High variance in PER across trials with same prior, outer loss plateauing at a high value, inner attack solver failing to converge or producing \(\alpha_{\text{opt}}\) where \(f(x+\alpha_{\text{opt}})\) does not match target class, signifying non‑convex traps or insufficient inner optimization fidelity.  
- *General:* Recovered \((K, C, O)\) exhibit nonsensical values (e.g., negative constraint bounds, weight matrices with extreme norms), pointing to unregularized optimization or exploding gradients.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Sanity check – Linear, perfect prior:** Sample true attacker parameters very close to the prior mean; generate an attack; run the framework. Expect PER ~100%, fast convergence, and low final \(\|\alpha_{\text{obs}}-\alpha_{\text{opt}}\|\). Validates the pipeline.  
2. **Main linear experiment:** Sample true parameters from the prior distribution; generate an attack; run the framework over 100 trials; report median and max PER. Tests robustness to prior misspecification.  
3. **Non‑linear stress test:** Use logistic regression on PenDigits. Intentionally weaken the prior (larger covariance) or set \(\lambda\) very low (e.g., 0.01). Expect higher variance and some trials with PER < 0, demonstrating sensitivity to prior strength and optimization difficulty in non‑linear settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What specific factors cause high variance in recovered attacker parameters for non‑linear models?** The paper identifies three potential contributors (model non‑linearity, attacker suboptimality, optimization challenges) but does not isolate their individual effects.  
2. **How can the framework be extended to infer attacker characteristics from a campaign (sequence) of attacks instead of a single attack?** Processing multiple attacks jointly could exploit temporal patterns and improve accuracy.  
3. **What are the fundamental information‑theoretic limits on recovering attacker parameters (K, C, O) from a single observed attack?** The non‑identifiability proof shows equivalence classes but does not quantify the maximum extractable information under different assumptions.

## Limitations
- Reliability degrades for non‑linear models because the inner attack must be approximated (e.g., via PGD), introducing bias and sensitivity to hyperparameters.  
- Critical implementation choices (PGD step count, handling of positive definite constraint matrices, continuous surrogate for discrete objectives) are not fully specified, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- The bi‑level optimization is non‑convex and sensitive to prior covariance scaling and λ, requiring careful tuning and stabilization tricks.

## Confidence
- **Non‑identifiability resolution via probabilistic modeling:** High  
- **Bi‑level optimization effectiveness:** Medium (analytical for linear, approximate for non‑linear)  
- **Empirical PER reduction:** Medium for Linear Regression; Low‑Medium for Logistic Regression & MLP  

## Next Checks
1. **Inner solver fidelity:** Vary PGD steps (50, 200, 1000) and step size for logistic/MLP; measure PER variance and outer loss stability to determine required inner accuracy.  
2. **Prior sensitivity:** Sweep λ ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10} and prior covariance scale (σ² = 0.1, 1, 10); assess impact on PER recovery stability and convergence.  
3. **Objective formulation:** Compare alternatives to the softmax surrogate for attractive attackers (e.g., hard‑margin or cross‑entropy loss); evaluate effects on target class accuracy and PER.
