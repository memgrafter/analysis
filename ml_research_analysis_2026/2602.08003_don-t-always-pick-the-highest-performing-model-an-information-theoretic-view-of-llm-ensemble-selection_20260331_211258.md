# Don't Always Pick the Highest-Performing Model: An Information Theoretic View of LLM Ensemble Selection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08003  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08003  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A greedy mutual information selection algorithm that accounts for correlated errors consistently outperforms accuracy-based baselines under a fixed query budget, with notable gains in the mid-budget range.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of selecting a subset of large language models (LLMs) for ensembling under a fixed query budget, where simply picking the top-k most accurate models fails due to correlated errors. The authors formulate the task as maximizing the mutual information between the true label and the selected models' predictions. They model correlated errors using a Gaussian-copula, derive an error floor when many correlated models are combined, and propose a greedy algorithm that estimates and balances accuracy, prediction redundancy, and structured error correlation. Evaluations on MEDMCQA, MMLU, and IMDB show the method consistently surpasses accuracy-based and mRMR-style baselines, especially for moderate ensemble sizes.

## Method Summary
The approach converts multi-choice QA into binary queries (e.g., “Is answer X correct?”) and gathers predictions from a pool of LLMs. Using an 80% training split, it estimates mutual information terms via smoothed frequency counts. The core is a greedy selection that iteratively adds the model maximizing the conditional mutual information gain Δ(j|S)=I(Y;Xj|XS), decomposed into model accuracy, prediction redundancy, and error correlation. For each selected subset, the final aggregator is a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator built from the empirical conditional distribution P(Y|XS) on the training data, with Laplace smoothing. The process is repeated for increasing budgets k, and test error is measured on the held-out 20% split.

## Key Results
- The greedy MI method reduces error relative to top-k accuracy baselines in the mid-budget range (e.g., 16.3% vs. 17.0% error at k=5 on MEDMCQA).
- Improvements are consistent across MEDMCQA, MMLU, and IMDB, even when model errors are highly correlated.
- The Gaussian-copula model accurately reproduces pairwise and higher-order joint error probabilities and reveals an inherent error floor when many correlated models are pooled.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Mutual Information Maximization for Ensemble Selection
The optimal subset under a fixed budget is shown to maximize I(Y;XS). Under independent errors, this reduces to picking the k most accurate models. When errors correlate, the marginal gain of adding a model j decomposes into three terms: (i) its standalone accuracy I(Y;Xj), (ii) redundancy with the current ensemble I(Xj;XS), and (iii) the unique information provided by its error pattern I(Ej;ES). The greedy algorithm approximates the NP‑hard optimal selection by maximizing this decomposition at each step. The label‑invariant error assumption (Ej ⊥ Y given D) underpins the decomposition. Evidence comes from Theorem 4.3 and the consistent empirical gains over accuracy‑only and mRMR (redundancy‑penalized) baselines. The approach breaks if MI estimates are noisy (e.g., small training splits) or if errors depend strongly on Y (violating Ej ⊥ Y).

### Mechanism 2: Gaussian‑Copula Model for Correlated LLM Errors
A Gaussian‑copula latent variable Z∼N(0,Σ) models binary error indicators Ej=1(Zj<τj). Under equicorrelation ρ, the MAP error as the ensemble size grows converges to Φ(Φ⁻¹(1−α)/√ρ)>α, creating an error floor even with infinitely many models. This analytically explains why adding highly correlated models fails to improve beyond a point. The model successfully fits both pairwise and higher‑order error joint probabilities on the datasets (per Figures 4,10–11,15–16,20–21). Evidence is theoretical (Theorem 4.4) and empirical. The framework breaks if the true correlation matrix is far from equicorrelated or if the dependence is non‑Gaussian (e.g., tail‑dependent copulas), which could alter the floor or the greedy score’s penalty term.

### Mechanism 3: Greedy Mutual Information Selection
The algorithm (Algorithm 1) starts with S=∅ and repeatedly picks j∉S maximizing Δ(j|S), estimated from the training split with Laplace smoothing (Algorithm 2). The MAP aggregator (Algorithm 3) then combines predictions by empirical P(Y|XS). The greedy procedure is efficient (O(mk) MI evaluations) and leverages the decomposition from Theorem 4.3, which under mild assumptions (label‑invariant errors, submodularity of I(Y;XS|D)) yields a (1−1/e) approximation guarantee. Empirically, it outperforms Top‑k, Term‑1 (accuracy‑only), and Terms 1+2 (mRMR) across budgets. Failure modes include high variance in estimated I(Ej;ES) for large k (due to many rare prediction patterns) and label‑dependent errors that violate the decomposition’s assumptions.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Mutual Information
Why needed: It quantifies how much the ensemble’s joint predictions reduce uncertainty about the true label, serving as the optimization objective.  
Quick check: If I(Y;XA)=0.3 bits and conditional gain I(Y;XB|XA)=0.1 bits, then I(Y;XA,XB)=0.4 bits. The redundancy of XB is I(XB;XA)=I(Y;XB)−I(Y;XB|XA)=0.2 bits.

### Concept: Gaussian‑Copula Model
Why needed: It flexibly models correlated binary errors while preserving arbitrary marginal accuracies, enabling the closed‑form error floor and the estimation of error‑correlation terms in the greedy score.  
Quick check: Under uniform correlation ρ and accuracy α>0.5, the error floor as ρ→1 becomes Φ(Φ⁻¹(1−α)/√1)=1−α (i.e., the worst single‑model error). This suggests that ensembles of nearly identical models confer little benefit.

### Concept: Greedy Submodular Maximization
Why needed: The greedy MI selection relies on I(Y;XS|D) being monotone submodular to guarantee near‑optimality.  
Quick check: For a monotone submodular function f under a cardinality constraint k, the greedy algorithm achieves at least (1−1/e)≈63% of the optimal value. Under the paper’s assumptions (D independent, conditional independence), I(Y;XS|D) satisfies these properties, justifying the greedy approach.

## Architecture Onboarding

Binary Converter -> Model Pool -> Data Split (80% train, 20% test) -> MI Estimator -> Greedy Selector -> MAP Aggregator -> Evaluation

Greedy Selector iterates: for k=1…budget, compute Δ(j|S) for all candidates using MI Estimator output (smoothed frequencies on train), select j maximizing Δ, update S. After final S chosen, MAP Aggregator builds conditional table P(Y|XS) from train predictions. At test time, query only models in S, aggregate via MAP, compute error.

Design tradeoffs include: Laplace smoothing vs. bias in MI estimates; Gaussian‑copula simplicity vs. potential mis‑fit of heterogeneous correlations; greedy speed vs. optimality; MAP optimality vs. sparsity of prediction patterns at large k.

Failure signatures: unstable MI scores across random splits; copula‑empirical joint discrepancies; class‑conditional error imbalance causing non‑label‑invariant errors; MAP saturation when many test patterns are unseen in training.

First 3 experiments (as implemented in the paper):
1. Fit Gaussian‑copula to full dataset and compare pairwise/higher‑order joint error probabilities to empirical ones (Figures 4,10–11, etc.).
2. On MEDMCQA, compare Greedy MI against Top‑k, Term‑1, and Terms 1+2 under MAP for budgets k=1…12 (Figure 5, Table 4).
3. Repeat main comparison on MMLU and IMDB to assess cross‑dataset robustness (Figures 6,12; Tables 8,12).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the framework be extended from binary to multi‑class or multi‑label tasks? The binary case simplifies error correlation; multi‑class requires new theory for decomposition.
2. Are there dependency structures beyond Gaussian‑copula that more accurately capture LLM error correlations and improve selection? Real errors may exhibit tail dependence or asymmetry.
3. Can robust, low‑variance mutual information estimators replace empirical frequencies to maintain performance for larger ensembles (higher k)? Current smoothing struggles as 2^k patterns grow.
4. Can the latent prompt difficulty variable D be estimated from observed outputs to enable instance‑conditional ensemble selection that reduces average query cost? The paper treats D theoretically only.

## Limitations
- Gaussian‑copula assumes exchangeable correlations, potentially misrepresenting heterogeneous LLM error structures.
- Empirical mutual information estimates are noisy with limited training data, harming greedy ordering and MAP aggregation.
- Binary MCQA conversion may introduce label noise and class imbalance.
- Experiments cover only three datasets and a limited set of modern LLMs, limiting generalizability.
- Greedy algorithm lacks a formal submodularity guarantee; the theoretical error floor relies on equicorrelation.

## Confidence
- C1 (MI objective): Medium – decomposition is sound under label‑invariant errors, which may not hold perfectly.
- C2 (Gaussian‑copula & error floor): Medium – fits data reasonably but exchangeability assumption is strong.
- C3 (Greedy MI effectiveness): High – consistent, statistically significant gains across datasets and mid‑budget ranges.

## Next Checks
1. *Higher‑order copula validation:* For each dataset, compute total variation distance between empirical and copula‑predicted 3‑way error joint probabilities; if >0.1, explore non‑Gaussian copulas.
2. *Training‑size robustness:* Downsample training split to 20%, 50%, 80% of original size, rerun pipeline, and record greedy MI’s relative error vs. Top‑k; identify size where advantage vanishes.
3. *Label‑invariant error test:* Compute class‑conditional errors on training data; if any model’s error difference >5%, estimate Λj(S) and augment greedy score to test impact on test error.
