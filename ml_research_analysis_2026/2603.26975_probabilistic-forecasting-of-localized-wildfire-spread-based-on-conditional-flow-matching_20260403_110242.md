# Probabilistic Forecasting of Localized Wildfire Spread Based on Conditional Flow Matching

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26975  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26975  
- **Reference count:** 6  
- **Primary result:** A conditional flow matching surrogate model generates fast, probabilistic 3‑hour fire arrival time ensembles, achieving a Sørensen–Dice coefficient of 0.8–0.9 on single‑step forecasts and 0.87 average Dice over 24‑hour recursive predictions.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a surrogate model for probabilistic wildfire spread forecasting that trades off physics‑based fidelity for speed and uncertainty quantification. Trained on outputs from high‑resolution WRF‑SFIRE simulations, the model uses **conditional flow matching** to learn the mapping from current fire extent, weather, terrain, and fuel conditions to a distribution of future fire arrival times over 3‑hour intervals. It produces ensembles of plausible scenarios at a fraction of the computational cost of the physics‑based simulator. Evaluated on 1,200 test cases, the model demonstrates strong spatial agreement (Dice 0.8–0.9) and maintains performance in 24‑hour recursive forecasts, while providing pixel‑wise uncertainty estimates.

## Method Summary
The task is to learn the conditional distribution \( P(Y|X) \) of fire arrival time maps \( Y \) given input tensors \( X \) (20 channels: burned area, wind components, temperature, humidity, terrain, and 14 fuel categories) over a 128×128 grid at 25 m resolution. The model is a **conditional flow matching** network built from a U‑Net architecture (adapted from DDPM). During training, pairs \((X, Y)\) are sampled; for a random \( t \in (0,1) \) and noise \( z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \), the interpolated state \( y_t = z(1-t) + y \) is fed to the network along with \( X \) and \( t \). The network predicts a velocity field \( v_t \), trained to match the vector \( y - z \) via mean squared error. After training, sampling proceeds by integrating the ODE \( dy_\tau/d\tau = v_\tau(y_\tau, X) \) from \( \tau=0 \) to \( 1 \), starting from random noise \( z \), to generate an ensemble member. The training dataset comprises 14,000 samples extracted from 140 WRF‑SFIRE simulations, with extensive augmentation and normalization of each input modality.

## Key Results
- Strong spatial accuracy in single‑step forecasts: Sørensen–Dice coefficient between 0.8 and 0.9 across test cases.
- Recursive 24‑hour predictions remain accurate: average Dice of 0.87, probability of detection 0.85, false alarm ratio 0.07.
- Substantial computational speed‑up compared to WRF‑SFIRE, enabling ensemble generation for localized prediction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Conditional flow matching provides a deterministic way to train a **normalizing flow** by minimizing the distance between the model’s learned velocity field and the conditional probability paths of the data distribution. This allows direct ODE‑based sampling without the complexity of variational objectives. The U‑Net backbone effectively captures spatially varying patterns in the gridded data, while the conditioning on \( X \) ensures predictions adhere to local drivers (weather, fuel, terrain). The probabilistic output emerges from integrating the learned velocity field from different random initial noise samples, yielding an ensemble that reflects uncertainty in fire spread given the input state.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conditional flow matching:** A simulation‑free method to train continuous normalizing flows by regressing a conditional velocity field. *Why needed:* Avoids intractable likelihoods and enables efficient ODE sampling. *Quick check:* Verify that training loss decreases and that sampled outputs cover the data manifold.
- **U‑Net for image‑like regression:** A fully convolutional encoder‑decoder with skip connections. *Why needed:* Preserves spatial resolution while aggregating context; standard for segmentation‑like tasks. *Quick check:* Confirm receptive field covers the 128×128 domain and that skip connections are present.
- **Multimodal input normalization:** Separate standardization/scaling for each channel (wind, temperature, fuel one‑hot, etc.). *Why needed:* Stabilizes training across heterogeneous features. *Quick check:* Check that pre‑computed global statistics are applied consistently during training and inference.
- **ODE integration for sampling:** Solving \( dy/d\tau = v_\tau(y, X) \) from \( \tau=0 \) to \( 1 \). *Why needed:* Transforms noise into a valid sample following the learned conditional distribution. *Quick check:* Use a simple Euler solver; if samples are noisy, try a higher‑order solver.
- **Ensemble forecasting:** Multiple samples from \( \mathcal{N}(0,1) \) yield a predictive distribution. *Why needed:* Quantifies uncertainty in fire spread. *Quick check:* Compute pixel‑wise standard deviation across ensemble; should vary spatially with input complexity.
- **Spatial evaluation metrics:** Sørensen–Dice, POD, FAR for binary fire extent. *Why needed:* Measures overlap between predicted and observed burned areas. *Quick check:* Threshold arrival times to get binary masks at a given forecast horizon before computing metrics.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input tensor X (20 channels) → Normalization (per sample/global) → U‑Net (with time \( t \) embedding injected via adaptive group norm or channel concat) → Velocity field \( v_t \) (1 channel) → ODE solver (Euler) → Sampled arrival time map Y. During training, Y is interpolated with noise \( z \) to form \( y_t \); at inference, \( z \) is drawn fresh per sample.

**Critical path:**  
1. Data loader fetches \((X, Y)\), normalizes.  
2. Sample \( t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \), \( z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \).  
3. Compute \( y_t = z(1-t) + y \).  
4. Concatenate \( y_t \) to \( X \) (or merge with conditioning) and add \( t \) embedding.  
5. Forward through U‑Net to get \( v_t \).  
6. Compute loss \( \| v_t - (y - z) \|^2 \).  
7. Backpropagate, update EMA weights.  
8. For sampling: draw \( z \), integrate ODE from \( \tau=0 \) to \( 1 \) using \( v_\tau \).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Model capacity vs. compute:** Deeper/wider U‑Net improves expressivity but increases memory and ODE integration cost.  
- **Deterministic vs. probabilistic training:** Flow matching gives a deterministic velocity field; randomness only at sampling time. This may underrepresent aleatoric uncertainty if the conditional distribution is multimodal.  
- **Patch‑based training vs. full domain:** Training on 128×128 patches reduces memory but may lose large‑scale dependencies; the paper uses patches but applies to localized forecasts.  
- **ODE solver choice:** Euler is fast but may require many steps for accuracy; higher‑order solvers improve sample quality but increase compute.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Training loss plateaus at a high value → model too small or conditioning ineffective.  
- Generated samples are blurry or identical across different \( z \) → model ignoring noise or collapsing to a mode.  
- Spatial Dice below 0.8 on validation → data normalization mismatch or insufficient model capacity.  
- Ensemble pixel‑wise standard deviation is uniform → model not capturing input‑dependent uncertainty.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Loss convergence sanity check:** Train for 1,000 iterations; plot training/validation loss. Should decrease smoothly.  
2. **Sample diversity inspection:** Generate 10 ensembles for a single \( X \); visualize mean and std. Maps should vary, especially in wind‑driven direction.  
3. **Normalization validation:** For a held‑out batch, de‑normalize outputs and compare distributions of arrival times against ground truth histograms; check for systematic shifts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can domain decomposition methods be designed to maintain spatial consistency and proper information exchange across adjacent subdomains when applying the conditional flow matching model locally, thereby preventing artificial discontinuities in predicted fire spread?
2. In what ways can the conditional flow matching surrogate model be coupled with real‑time data assimilation frameworks to ingest observational data (e.g., satellite fire perimeters) and sequentially correct forecasts, and what impact does this have on forecast skill?
3. How does the omission of key state variables such as fuel moisture content and vertical atmospheric profiles affect the conditional distribution learned by the model, and can additional data sources be incorporated to mitigate these gaps while preserving computational efficiency?
4. Are the uncertainty estimates (pixel‑wise standard deviation) provided by the ensemble predictions well‑calibrated relative to actual forecast errors, and can they be refined to produce reliable probabilistic forecasts?

## Limitations
- **Dataset access:** The WRF‑SFIRE simulation data (140 runs, 14k samples) and derived normalization statistics are not public, hindering direct replication.  
- **Architecture details:** The exact U‑Net design (depth, channel counts, attention placements, conditioning injection) is unspecified beyond “adapted from DDPM,” risking implementation divergence.  
- **Recursive prediction drift:** The claim of stable 24‑hour performance lacks analysis of error accumulation across many 3‑hour steps; metrics are averaged over a single rollout.

## Confidence
- **Computational efficiency vs. physics‑based model:** High (explicit speed‑up measured).  
- **Single‑step spatial accuracy (Dice 0.8–0.9):** Medium (depends on data distribution; metrics from a held‑out set but without public data verification).  
- **Recursive 24‑hour performance:** Low (single trajectory average reported; no per‑step error analysis or multi‑trajectory stress test).

## Next Checks
1. **Architecture audit:** Re‑implement the U‑Net using the canonical DDPM design (Ho et al. 2020) with 128×128 inputs; confirm parameter count (~35M) and flow matching loss convergence on a small synthetic dataset.
2. **Normalization replication:** Apply the described preprocessing (wind divided by global std, temperature/humidity standardized, terrain shifted by per‑sample min then globally standardized, one‑hot fuel) to a public fire spread dataset; train a minimal model and verify stable loss curves.
3. **Recursive stability test:** Perform 10‑step (30‑hour) rollouts on 50 held‑out samples; plot Dice, POD, and FAR per step to detect systematic degradation beyond the reported 24‑hour average.
