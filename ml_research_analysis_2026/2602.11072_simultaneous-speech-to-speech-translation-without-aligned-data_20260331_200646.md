# Simultaneous Speech-to-Speech Translation Without Aligned Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11072
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11072
- Reference count: 33
- Primary result: Hibiki-Zero achieves state-of-the-art simultaneous speech-to-speech translation (28.7–35.0 ASR‑BLEU, 2.8–3.1 LAAL latency) without word‑level aligned data and preserves speaker similarity (50–70 SIM).

## Executive Summary
Hibiki-Zero is a simultaneous speech‑to‑speech translation system that eliminates the need for word‑level aligned data. It first trains a base model on coarse sentence‑level alignments created by inserting synthetic silences and smoothing them with a TTS system. Then a reinforcement learning (RL) strategy with BLEU‑based process rewards refines the translation policy to optimize the latency‑quality trade‑off. Evaluated on five X‑to‑English tasks, Hibiki‑Zero attains SOTA results: 28.7–35.0 ASR‑BLEU, 2.8–3.1 seconds LAAL latency on short utterances, and 50–70 speaker similarity, outperforming prior work. The system also adapts efficiently to Italian using under 1 000 hours of speech data, matching Seamless quality while preserving speaker identity. All code, model weights, and a multilingual benchmark are publicly released.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three stages. (1) **Base training**: A multistream RQ‑Transformer model is trained on massive multilingual speech (≈40 k hours per language) using coarse sentence‑level alignments. Alignments are generated by inserting random silences at sentence boundaries and punctuation; a TTS system (“Natural Pauses TTS”) then smoothes these silences to produce natural target speech. (2) **Fine‑tuning**: The model is lightly fine‑tuned on a small language‑specific dataset (<200 h) with synthetic pauses to adapt acoustic and linguistic representations. (3) **Reinforcement learning**: GRPO is applied with BLEU‑based *process rewards* computed on partial outputs. The reward combines a prefix‑level BLEU against the translation of processed source sentences and the full‑sequence BLEU, weighted by α. This optimizes the policy for desired latency‑quality trade‑offs. Speech is generated using the Mimi codec (residual vector quantization) and the multistream architecture supports true streaming inference.

## Key Results
- ASR‑BLEU: 28.7–35.0 on five X‑to‑English tasks.
- Latency (LAAL): 2.8–3.1 seconds on short‑form utterances.
- Speaker similarity (SIM): 50–70, significantly higher than baselines.
- Italian adaptation (<1 000 h) matches Seamless quality/latency while preserving speaker identity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Eliminating word‑level alignment by using coarse sentence‑level alignments and silence insertion is sufficient to train a base simultaneous translation model.
- **Mechanism:** Sentence‑aligned parallel speech data is prepared by inserting random silences at the sentence level (δ_i) and at punctuation (µ) to create diverse, non‑monotonic alignments. A TTS system with natural pause generation (“Natural Pauses TTS”) smooths these silences using voice transfer. This dataset trains the multistream model to map source speech tokens to target speech/text tokens without requiring precise word boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** A model exposed to diverse, coarse temporal offsets during supervised training can learn a sufficiently flexible mapping that reinforcement learning (RL) can subsequently refine for specific latency targets.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We first train on sentence‑level aligned data to learn speech translation at high latency... eliminates the need for word‑level alignments entirely."
  - [section 3.2] Detailed equations and description of silence insertion (δ_i ~ U(0, δ·d_i), µ) and Natural Pauses TTS.
  - [corpus] Hibiki (paper neighbor 91919) used synthetic word‑level alignments; Hibiki‑Zero's Table 1 superiority suggests coarse alignments + RL are more effective.
- **Break condition:** If the inserted silences are too long/frequent or the Natural Pauses TTS fails to produce smooth transitions, the base model may learn unnatural timing patterns that RL cannot correct, degrading speech naturalness.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A reinforcement learning strategy using GRPO with BLEU‑based *process rewards* (computed on sentence prefixes) effectively optimizes the latency‑quality trade‑off without word‑level supervision.
- **Mechanism:** From a fixed old policy π_θ_old, multiple output sequences (Y^i) are generated. For each frame t, a *process reward* r^i_t is computed as a weighted sum (α) of: (1) BLEU between the model’s partial text output up to t and the ground‑truth translation of the *corresponding source sentences* processed so far (y_t), and (2) the full‑sequence BLEU. These rewards are normalized per frame across the group and summed into advantages R^i_t, which drive a clipped PPO‑style (GRPO) update on the audio token predictions.
- **Core assumption:** BLEU scores on sentence prefixes provide a meaningful, dense learning signal that correlates with partial translation correctness, enabling the policy to learn precise “when to speak” decisions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] Equations 3–8 define process rewards, advantage calculation, and GRPO objective. "We exploit the sentence‑level aspect... to design a simple reward system based on BLEU score only."
  - [section 4.7, Figure 4] Ablation shows α directly controls the latency‑quality trade‑off; Figure 7 (A) shows using full‑reference prefixes harms latency optimization.
  - [corpus] Seed LiveInterpret 2.0 (paper neighbor 60225) uses complex multi‑stage RL with outcome/process rewards and encounters stability issues; Hibiki‑Zero's simpler single‑reward system is more stable.
- **Break condition:** If the reference text y_t is inaccurate (e.g., ASR errors in source transcript) or the sentence segmentation is poor, the process reward signal becomes noisy, leading to reward hacking or unstable training. The α hyperparameter must be tuned to balance the noisy intermediate signal against the stable final signal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A multilingual base model trained on massive coarse‑aligned data can be efficiently adapted to a new input language with <1000 hours of speech data through fine‑tuning followed by RL.
- **Mechanism:** The base model (pre‑trained on 4 languages, 40k hrs each) undergoes language‑specific supervised fine‑tuning on a small (<200 h) coarse‑aligned dataset for the target language (Italian). This adapts its acoustic and linguistic representations. Then, the same RL procedure (GRPO with process rewards) is applied to optimize latency/quality for this new language.
- **Core assumption:** The base model’s representations are sufficiently language‑agnostic and its multistream architecture is universally applicable, such that limited language‑specific data can specialize it effectively.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.6] "We demonstrate that our model can be adapted to support a new input language with less than 1000h of speech." Table 3 shows Italian adaptation matches Seamless quality/latency with better speaker similarity.
  - [section 4.2.3] Base training uses a "multilingual‑to‑English" data mixture (French, Spanish, Portuguese, German).
  - [corpus] Related multilingual ST work (Seamless, neighbor 166259) typically requires large in‑language data; Hibiki‑Zero's data efficiency is a key differentiator.
- **Break condition:** For typologically distant languages or those with very different phonetics/syntax from the base set, <1000 h may be insufficient for the fine‑tuned model to overcome interference from the base languages’ representations, leading to degraded quality or inability to learn appropriate timing.

## Foundational Learning

#### Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) & Multistream Modeling
- **Why needed here:** The RQ‑Transformer (Défossez et al., 2024) models audio as multiple discrete code streams (Q=16 levels) across time. This is core to Hibiki‑Zero’s ability to generate high‑fidelity speech incrementally and handle the “duplex” nature of simultaneous S2ST (listening and speaking streams).
- **Quick check question:** How does the acoustic delay (Section 3.1.2) between semantic and acoustic token streams affect the model’s ability to generate natural, coherent speech in real‑time?

#### Process Rewards in Reinforcement Learning
- **Why needed here:** Unlike outcome rewards (only at sequence end), process rewards provide intermediate feedback based on partial outputs. This is critical for learning fine‑grained timing policies in simultaneous translation where each “decision” (to speak or listen) has immediate consequences on both quality and latency.
- **Quick check question:** Why is using the ground‑truth translation of *processed source sentences* (y_t) as reference for partial BLEU more effective than using the full reference translation (as in Ablation A)?

#### Coarse Data Alignment via Synthetic Silence
- **Why needed here:** Bypasses the intractable problem of collecting human interpretation data with word‑level alignments. Creates a scalable, language‑agnostic supervision signal for the base model.
- **Quick check question:** What is the risk of over‑shifting the target speech (δ too large) versus under‑shifting (δ too small) during coarse alignment, and how does Natural Pauses TTS mitigate this?

## Architecture Onboarding

#### Component Map
1. **Input/Output:** Streaming Mimi codec (encoder → discrete tokens AX, decoder ← tokens AY).
2. **Core Model:** RQ‑Transformer with:
   - *Temporal Transformer:* Processes all tokens (source AX, target AY, text stream W) up to current time t, producing context Z_t.
   - *Depth Transformer:* For each time t, autoregressively models the Q codebook levels (q=1..Q) for target AY and (during training) source AX, conditioned on Z_t and previous audio token.
3. **Training Stages:**
   - *Stage 1 (Base):* Supervised training on coarse‑aligned sentence‑level data (high latency).
   - *Stage 2 (Fine‑tune):* Light supervised tuning on “Natural Pauses” synthetic data.
   - *Stage 3 (RL):* GRPO loop: generate G samples from π_θ_old, compute process rewards, update π_θ.
4. **Reward Engine:** Computes partial text transcripts (from W stream), aligns with source sentence segmentation, calculates BLEU‑based r_t.

#### Critical Path
Audio → Mimi Tokens (AX) → RQ‑Temporal(Z_t) → RQ‑Depth(AY, W) → Audio Output. RL updates (π_θ) target the Depth Transformer’s audio token predictions (AY) based on advantages from the Reward Engine.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- *Latency vs. Quality:* Controlled primarily by RL hyperparameter α (Eq 3). Lower α favors low latency (process reward weight ↑), higher α favors quality (final BLEU weight ↑).
- *Data Efficiency vs. Generality:* Multilingual base model provides strong initialization but risks negative transfer. Fine‑tuning is needed for new languages.
- *Compute vs. Performance:* 3B parameter model. RL is memory‑intensive (store G=4 sample trajectories). Distillation before RL reduces cost.
- *Inference Speed:* Multistream architecture enables true streaming/batching on GPU, but depth‑wise autoregression (Q=16) per frame adds sequential cost.

#### Failure Signatures
- **High Latency / Late Start:** Base model not exposed to early‑start examples during coarse training (δ_i too large). Fix: Reduce δ hyperparameter.
- **Poor Speech Quality / Robotic Voice:** Failure of Natural Pauses TTS or insufficient conditioning. Check: Speaker similarity (SIM) metric drops.
- **RL Instability:** High variance in BLEU during training, loss collapse. Likely causes: α too low (noisy rewards), n_w too small (reward frequency too high), or insufficient base model competence.
- **Language Interference:** After new language adaptation, performance drops on original languages (see Appendix Table 6). Mitigate: Use language embeddings or more balanced multilingual fine‑tuning.

#### First 3 Experiments
1. **Base Model Sanity Check:** Evaluate the model after Stage 2 (Fine‑tuned) on the validation set *without* RL. Expect high BLEU (>50 on train‑style data) but high latency (LAAL >5s). This confirms the base model learns the coarse mapping.
2. **RL Hyperparameter Sweep:** Run short RL experiments (100–500 updates) varying α (e.g., 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7) and n_w (e.g., 4, 8, 16). Monitor validation BLEU and text LAAL. Goal: Find α that moves the quality/latency Pareto frontier from the base model’s point.
3. **New Language Adaptation Pipeline:** For a held‑out language, execute the full pipeline: (a) create <100 h coarse‑aligned synthetic data, (b) fine‑tune base model for 1k steps, (c) run RL (2000 updates). Compare final LAAL/BLEU to the base model’s performance on that language pre‑adaptation. Expect significant latency reduction with maintained quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Controlling accent intensity:** The paper notes there is no way to control the intensity of the source language accent in the generated speech. Adding accent conditioning during training or inference could resolve this.
2. **Robustness to sentence segmentation errors:** The method relies on sentence boundaries and punctuation for coarse alignment and process rewards. Performance under inaccurate segmentation is unknown; evaluating with corrupted segmentation would provide evidence.
3. **Generalization to typologically distant languages:** Experiments are limited to European languages. It remains unclear if the approach works for languages with different word orders (e.g., SOV) or agglutinative morphology; testing on such languages is needed.
4. **Minimal data requirements for new languages:** Adaptation with 850 h of Italian data is successful, but the lower bound of required parallel speech data is not established; systematic scaling studies (10 h, 50 h, 100 h, 500 h) would determine the threshold.

## Limitations
- The “Natural Pauses TTS” system (Zeghidour et al., 2025) is referenced but not detailed, hindering accurate reproduction of synthetic target speech generation.
- Reinforcement learning hyperparameters (α, n_w, GRPO coefficients) are chosen empirically without a clear tuning procedure; their optimal values may vary across languages and data scales, making the latency‑quality trade‑off sensitive.
- The evaluation covers mainly short‑form English‑centric tasks; long‑form, spontaneous speech and non‑English target languages are less explored, limiting claims about general applicability.

## Confidence
