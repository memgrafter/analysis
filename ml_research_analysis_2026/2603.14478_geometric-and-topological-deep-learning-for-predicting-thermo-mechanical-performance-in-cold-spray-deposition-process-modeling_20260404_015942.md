# Geometric and Topological Deep Learning for Predicting Thermo-mechanical Performance in Cold Spray Deposition Process Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14478
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14478
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Graph-based deep learning surrogates (GraphSAGE, GAT) predict cold spray particle impact responses with R² > 0.93, while Chebyshev spectral and TDA-MLP models perform poorly (negative R² for several targets).

## Executive Summary
This study develops geometric deep learning surrogate models to predict thermo-mechanical outcomes of cold spray particle impacts from finite element simulation data. By representing each simulation sample as a node in a k-nearest-neighbor graph constructed in normalized input feature space, the models exploit spatial similarity between process conditions. GraphSAGE and a Geometric Attention Network (GAT) achieve high predictive accuracy (R² > 0.93) across all five output targets, with GAT reaching a peak R² of 0.97 for maximum plastic strain. In contrast, Chebyshev spectral graph convolution and topological data analysis-augmented MLP models yield negative R² values, underscoring the importance of appropriate inductive biases for this problem.

## Method Summary
The authors generated a dataset of 100 finite element simulations of single-particle cold spray impact, varying three input parameters—particle velocity (400–900 m/s), temperature (300–600 K), and friction coefficient (0.1–0.5)—and recording five thermo-mechanical outputs (e.g., maximum plastic strain, temperature, stress). Each simulation sample was treated as a node in a graph built by connecting it to its k-nearest neighbors in normalized input feature space, with edge weights computed via a Gaussian kernel. Four geometric deep learning models were trained to minimize mean squared error: GraphSAGE (mean aggregation), GAT (attention-weighted aggregation), ChebSpectral (Chebyshev spectral convolution), and TDA-MLP (topological descriptors concatenated with inputs and processed by an MLP). Models were evaluated on a held-out test set using R², MAE, and MSE.

## Key Results
- GraphSAGE and GAT consistently achieve R² > 0.93 across all output targets; GAT peaks at R² = 0.97 for maximum plastic strain.
- Chebyshev spectral (ChebSpectral) and topological data analysis-augmented MLP (TDA-MLP) yield negative R² values for several targets, indicating catastrophic failure.
- The k-NN graph representation successfully exploits local smoothness in the input-output mapping, enabling high accuracy for spatial GNNs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Representing simulation samples as nodes in a k-nearest-neighbor graph of the input feature space enables the model to exploit local smoothness in the underlying physical response surface.
- **Mechanism:** Similar process parameters (velocity, temperature, friction) yield similar thermo-mechanical outcomes. By connecting each sample to its nearest neighbors in the normalized input space, the graph encodes this spatial similarity. Graph neural networks (GraphSAGE, GAT) then perform neighborhood aggregation, allowing each node's prediction to be informed by the outputs of its proximate parameter combinations. This propagates information along the manifold of the data, capturing local regularity that a point-wise MLP cannot.
- **Core assumption:** The mapping from (velocity, temperature, friction) to each output target is locally smooth and continuous within the sampled parametric domain.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Each input sample was treated as a node in a k-nearest-neighbour feature-space graph, enabling the models to exploit spatial similarity between process conditions during training."
  - [section 3.2] 2D contour projections and 3D response surfaces show smooth, well-resolved gradients in the velocity-temperature planes for PEEQ and temperature, confirming local smoothness when the dominant parameter (velocity) is included. The deterioration when velocity is projected out underscores that smoothness is a local, multi-dimensional property.
- **Break condition:** If the true response surface contains sharp, discontinuous transitions or isolated "islands" of behavior within the parameter space that are not captured by the sampling density, the k-NN graph will connect physically dissimilar samples, corrupting the neighborhood aggregation. The poor performance of ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP suggests they are less robust to such local irregularities or sparsity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Attention-based neighborhood weighting (GAT) further improves performance over simple mean aggregation (GraphSAGE) by learning to emphasize the most relevant neighbors for predicting a given output.
- **Mechanism:** GAT computes attention scores for each edge, allowing the model to assign higher importance to neighbors whose feature vectors or responses are more predictive of the target. Edge weights are also modulated by the Gaussian kernel distance, combining learned importance with geometric proximity. This adaptive weighting is critical for outputs with complex, non-uniform sensitivity (e.g., maximum von Mises stress, which showed highly corrugated response surfaces).
- **Core assumption:** Not all neighbors within a fixed k-radius contribute equally to the prediction; their relevance is target-dependent and can be learned from data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "GAT attaining peak performance of R² = 0.97 for maximum plastic strain."
  - [section 2.5] The GAT equation (5) defines the attention mechanism, and the text notes: "these attention scores are further modulated by edge weights derived from geometric distances, allowing the network to emphasize more relevant neighbours."
  - [section 3.3] Performance tables show GAT consistently matches or exceeds GraphSAGE, with the largest margin on the most complex target (max_peeq), where R²=0.9700 vs. 0.9414.
- **Break condition:** If the training data is extremely sparse, attention mechanisms risk overfitting to noise by placing excessive weight on a few poorly supported neighbors. The benefit also diminishes if the response is truly uniform across neighbors (e.g., deformation ratio, where GraphSAGE and GAT performed similarly).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Chebyshev spectral graph convolution and topological data analysis augmentation failed because they impose inappropriate inductive biases for this specific dataset's topology.
- **Mechanism:** 
  - **ChebSpectral:** Operates in the spectral domain, assuming the graph signal (output value) is smooth over the graph structure. It approximates convolution using Chebyshev polynomials of the Laplacian. This biases the model toward low-frequency, globally smooth variations. The cold spray response surfaces, however, exhibit localized sharp ridges (e.g., in von Mises stress) and velocity-dominated thresholds (PEEQ), which contain high-frequency components that spectral filters struggle to capture without many polynomial orders, leading to poor generalization.
  - **TDA-MLP:** Augments the input feature vector with topological descriptors (e.g., persistence homology proxies) meant to capture global connectivity of the point cloud. This loses the explicit local neighborhood relationships encoded in the graph. The MLP then processes a global summary statistic, which is insufficient to model the strong local, non-linear dependencies between neighboring process parameters.
- **Core assumption (ChebSpectral):** The target variable varies smoothly over the graph Laplacian, with no high-frequency oscillations. **Assumption (TDA-MLP):** Global topological features of the parameter cloud are primary drivers of the output, outweighing local neighbor effects.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP performed considerably worse, yielding negative R² values for several targets."
  - [section 3.3] Tables 3-6 show ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP have much higher MSE/MAE and negative R² for avg_peeq, max_temp, and deformation_ratio.
  - [section 3.2] Response surface visualizations reveal significant local variation and non-monotonicity (e.g., Figure 12 for von Mises stress), contradicting a smoothness assumption. TDA-MLP's failure suggests global shape alone is uninformative without local geometry.
- **Break condition:** For ChebSpectral, break occurs if the graph signal has high-frequency content (sharp transitions) relative to the polynomial order K. For TDA-MLP, break occurs if the output is primarily determined by local parameter interactions rather than global manifold shape.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Inductive bias in geometric deep learning**
  - **Why needed here:** The choice of model (GraphSAGE, GAT, ChebSpectral, TDA-MLP) embodies different assumptions about how the input space relates to the output. Understanding these biases explains the performance hierarchy.
  - **Quick check question:** If two simulation points have nearly identical velocity and temperature but very different friction, should their predicted plastic strain be more similar or less similar than two points with identical friction but different velocity? Which model best captures that intuition?

- **Concept: Graph construction as a design choice**
  - **Why needed here:** The k-NN graph in feature space is the foundational representation. Its properties (connectivity, edge weights) directly enable or constrain what the subsequent GNN can learn.
  - **Quick check question:** How would the model's performance change if the graph were built using a fixed distance radius instead of k-NN, given the varying density of samples in the parameter space?

- **Concept: Non-linear response surface topology**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's 3D visualizations prove the relationships are highly non-linear and parameter-dependent (velocity dominates). This justifies complex models and rules out simple linear regressors.
  - **Quick check question:** Based on Figure 7c and 9c, why is a model that only sees (temperature, friction) likely to fail at predicting max_peeq or max_temp, even if it's a powerful MLP?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Pipeline (Abaqus simulation scripts → parametric dataset) → Graph Builder (normalize inputs → compute k-NN graph with Gaussian weights) → Model Zoo (GraphSAGE, GAT, ChebSpectral, TDA-MLP) → Trainer (minimize MSE) → Evaluator (compute R², MAE, MSE; generate plots).
- **Critical path:** Simulation data generation → Graph construction (pivotal) → Model training → Evaluation. The graph construction step is the pivotal architectural decision that differentiates this approach from standard regression.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **k in k-NN:** Small k → very local, high variance, sensitive to noise. Large k → oversmooths, mixes dissimilar samples in sparse regions. Must balance based on data density.
  - **Edge weighting:** Gaussian kernel provides smooth decay but is parametric (σ). An unweighted graph is simpler but loses distance information.
  - **Model complexity:** GraphSAGE (mean) is simpler and more stable. GAT adds attention parameters, risking overfit on small data (N=100) but can capture nuanced relationships.
  - **Spectral vs. Spatial:** ChebSpectral requires a fixed graph and is less interpretable; spatial methods (SAGE, GAT) are more direct and showed superior empirical results here.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Negative R²** for ChebSpectral/TDA-MLP on most targets: Indicates catastrophic model mismatch. For ChebSpectral, likely too few polynomial orders (K) to capture high-frequency variations, or inherent unsmoothness. For TDA-MLP, indicates global topological features are irrelevant or misleading.
  - **High variance in pred vs. actual plots** (e.g., ChebSpectral scatter in Fig 16-19): Model cannot generalize the local manifold structure.
  - **Poor performance on specific targets:** If a model fails on max_temp but does well on deformation_ratio, it suggests sensitivity to the output's smoothness. Deformation_ratio is velocity-dominated and smoother; max_temp may have more complex local interactions.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Graph validation:** Compute the correlation between edge weight (Gaussian distance) and absolute difference in output values for all connected pairs. A strong positive correlation (closer nodes → more similar outputs) validates the graph construction hypothesis. Do this for each output target.
  2. **Attention interpretability (GAT):** Extract attention weights from a trained GAT model. Visualize the attention scores vs. Euclidean distance. Do high-attention edges correspond to closer neighbors? Are there cases where the model attends to a farther neighbor? Inspect those specific parameter tuples to see if they correspond to known complex regimes (e.g., intermediate velocity in Figure 12).
  3. **Ablation - k and σ:** Systematically vary k (e.g., 3, 5, 10, 15) and σ (scale of Gaussian) for GraphSAGE and GAT. Plot R² vs. k and σ. Identify a robust plateau. This tunes the core representational hypothesis (neighborhood size) and checks sensitivity. Expect best k to reflect the local intrinsic dimensionality of the data manifold (likely 2-3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How can the geometric deep learning framework be extended to multi-particle cold spray deposition and microstructure prediction?
   - **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states that the findings provide a foundation for extending the framework to multi-particle deposition scenarios and microstructure-level property prediction in future work.
   - **Why unresolved:** The current work only addresses single-particle impact; multi-particle interactions and microstructure evolution introduce additional complexities not captured.
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of graph representations where nodes represent individual particles and edges capture inter-particle interactions, and inclusion of microstructure descriptors as prediction targets.

2. **Question:** Why do ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP models perform significantly worse than GraphSAGE and GAT for cold spray surrogate modeling?
   - **Basis in paper:** The results show ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP yielding negative R² values for several targets, while GraphSAGE and GAT achieve R² > 0.93. The authors explicitly state that ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP performed considerably worse.
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper reports performance but does not analyze the underlying reasons; possible causes include incompatibility of spectral/topological methods with the highly nonlinear and velocity-dominated response surfaces.
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of feature representations, ablation studies on graph connectivity, and testing on controlled datasets to isolate the failure modes.

3. **Question:** How sensitive are the model predictions to the choice of graph construction parameters (k, σ) in the k-NN feature-space graph?
   - **Basis in paper:** The methodology uses a k-NN graph with Gaussian weights but does not investigate sensitivity to k or σ. The feature space visualizations indicate non-uniform data density, suggesting potential sensitivity.
   - **Why unresolved:** The choice of k and σ determines neighborhood relationships and could affect model performance, especially in regions of sparse data.
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation of model performance across a range of k and σ values, possibly with adaptive choices based on local density.

4. **Question:** What is the minimum dataset size needed for reliable predictions, and how does performance scale with the number of simulations?
   - **Basis in paper:** The study uses 100 simulation cases but does not analyze data efficiency. The authors note that exhaustive simulation is infeasible, implying limited data.
   - **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether 100 samples are sufficient for generalizable models or if performance would improve with more data; learning curves are not provided.
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct learning curve experiments by training on progressively larger subsets of data and plotting error metrics against dataset size.

## Limitations
- Small dataset (100 simulation samples) limits generalizability and risks overfitting; coverage of parameter space is uncontrolled.
- Graph construction relies on Euclidean distance in normalized feature space, which may not reflect true output similarity when one parameter (velocity) dominates the response.
- Hyperparameters (k, σ, architecture) are not fully reported; performance may be sensitive to these choices.
- Poor performance of ChebSpectral and TDA-MLP could stem from unoptimized settings rather than inherent incompatibility; conclusions about their unsuitability are tentative.
- Findings are specific to single-particle cold spray; generalizability to other processes or multi-particle scenarios is unknown.
