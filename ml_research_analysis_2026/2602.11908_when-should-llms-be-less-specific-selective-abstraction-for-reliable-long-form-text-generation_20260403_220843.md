# When Should LLMs Be Less Specific? Selective Abstraction for Reliable Long-Form Text Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11908
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11908
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Selective Abstraction reduces factual errors in long-form generation by replacing low-confidence atomic claims with abstracted high-confidence alternatives, lowering AURC by up to 27.73% versus redaction.

## Executive Summary
Long-form text generation by LLMs is plagued by factual errors. Existing selective methods either abstain entirely or remove entire statements, discarding useful information. This paper introduces Selective Abstraction (SA), a fine-grained approach that replaces uncertain factual claims (atomic statements) with less specific but higher-confidence alternatives. SA decomposes responses into atomic claims, elicits confidence scores, generates a sequence of abstractions for low-confidence atoms, selects the most specific abstraction above a threshold, and reconstructs a coherent output. Evaluated on two benchmarks across six models using a Wikipedia fact-checking agent and an information-theoretic coverage measure, atom-wise SA consistently outperforms baselines like redaction, inline generation, and self-revision, achieving up to 27.73% reduction in AURC and lower risk at matched coverage. The authors also propose a calibration algorithm to set thresholds that guarantee a target risk with high probability.

## Method Summary
The Selective Abstraction pipeline comprises four stages: (1) Generation: produce an initial response; (2) Atomization: decompose the response into atomic claims, each representing a single fact; (3) Abstraction: for each atom, elicit a verbal confidence score; if below a threshold θ, generate a sequence of progressively less specific abstractions and assign confidences, then select the most specific abstraction whose confidence meets θ (or abstain if none); (4) Reconstruction: recombine selected abstractions into a final coherent text. Risk is defined as the fraction of atomic claims unsupported by Wikipedia (using a fact-checking agent). Coverage measures retained information as the sum of information content of selected atoms divided by that of the original, where information of an atom is derived from the entropy reduction of the entity set satisfying the claim, estimated via Wikidata counts. By sweeping θ, one obtains a risk-coverage curve; the area under this curve (AURC) summarizes the trade-off. A calibration algorithm uses a held-out set to choose θ that statistically guarantees test risk near a user-specified target.

## Key Results
- SA reduces AURC by up to 27.73% compared to the redaction baseline.
- At matched coverage levels, SA achieves significantly lower risk than redaction, inline generation, and self-revision across all models and datasets.
- The threshold-selection algorithm reliably meets target risk on test data, with empirical risk close to the desired level within theoretical bounds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Granular Decomposition and Progressive Abstraction
The core claim is that replacing low-confidence atomic claims with higher-confidence, less specific alternatives yields a better risk-coverage trade-off than discarding entire statements. The mechanism: decompose the response into atomic claims (single-fact units), elicit a confidence score for each, generate an abstraction sequence (progressively less specific versions) for uncertain atoms, select the most specific abstraction whose confidence exceeds a threshold, and reconstruct a coherent output. This principled reduction preserves information while reducing factual error risk. The approach assumes (1) complete atomization, (2) confidence scores correctly rank atoms by correctness likelihood, (3) abstraction sequences maintain logical entailment (more specific implies less specific), and (4) reconstruction yields fluent text. Evidence anchors are provided in the paper’s Sections 3.1 and abstract.

### Mechanism 2: Quantifying the Reliability–Information Trade-off
Selective abstraction is evaluated via a risk-coverage framework that jointly measures factual accuracy and retained information. Risk is the fraction of unsupported atomic claims, verified by a Wikipedia fact-checking agent. Coverage is the ratio of information content between abstracted and original outputs, with atomic claim information computed as 1 - log(|Ec|)/log(|E|), where |Ec| and |E| are entity set sizes from Wikidata. Varying the confidence threshold yields an RC curve; AURC summarizes the trade-off. Underlying assumptions include stable fact-checking, information additivity, and that coverage meaningfully captures retained meaning. Anchors are found in Sections 4.1–4.3.

### Mechanism 3: Calibrated Threshold Selection for Target Risk
A calibration-driven algorithm selects a confidence threshold that guarantees, with high probability, test risk does not exceed a user-specified target. Given a calibration set, for each atom the system estimates the minimal threshold at which the chosen abstraction is correct. These thresholds are collected, and the algorithm picks the quantile corresponding to the desired risk level. Under exchangeability, this yields a statistically valid guarantee with margin ε and confidence 1–δ. This hinges on assumptions of exchangeability, accurate minimal thresholds, and deterministic atom risk. The method is detailed in Section 7 and Appendix H, with empirical validation showing empirical risk close to target.

## Foundational Learning
- **Selective Prediction**: Extends classical selective classification (“abstain when uncertain”) to long-form text, moving from binary abstention to graded specificity reduction. *Why needed*: Provides a framework for trading off information vs. reliability. *Quick check*: How does the risk-coverage trade-off in classification relate to the one for long-form generation?
- **Atomic Claim Decomposition**: Breaking text into self-contained statements each expressing a single fact. *Why needed*: Enables independent evaluation and abstraction of each fact. *Quick check*: What are the criteria for an atomic claim, and why must it capture exactly one entity–fact pair?
- **Confidence Elicitation for LLMs**: Obtaining numeric confidence scores from LLMs for each atomic claim, typically via verbal prompts. *Why needed*: Confidence drives which atoms get abstracted. *Quick check*: Why might token-level log-probabilities be a poor proxy for factual reliability, and what advantage does verbalized confidence offer?
- **Information-Theoretic Specificity**: Quantifying the information content of a claim via entropy reduction relative to a uniform prior over entities. *Why needed*: Defines coverage as the proportion of information retained. *Quick check*: How is the information of a claim computed, and why is Wikidata querying used instead of a uniform prior over all real-world entities?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Generator LLM -> Atomizer -> Confidence Scorer -> (if κ<θ) Abstraction Generator -> Selector -> Reconstructor -> abstracted output. Additionally, off-pipeline: Risk Evaluator (Wikipedia fact-checking agent) and Coverage Estimator (Wikidata via SPINACH) for metrics; Threshold Calibrator for deployment.

**Critical path**  
Prompt -> Generator -> Atomizer -> Confidence Scorer -> (for atoms below θ) Abstraction Generator -> Selector -> Reconstructor -> final response. Evaluator and calibrator are used during setup but not in forward pass.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Granularity*: Atomic decomposition enables fine control but risks incomplete sets or reconstruction incoherence; coarser granules (sentence-level) simplify but lose nuance.
- *Confidence function*: Verbal confidence offers better ranking but may be overconfident; token log-probabilities are cheap but correlate weakly with factual correctness.
- *Abstraction prompting*: Few-shot design impacts quality; generating too few levels limits coverage range, too many adds cost.
- *Reconstruction*: Varying ordering and merging can improve fluency but may drop or distort claims; strict claim adherence may produce disjointed text.

**Failure signatures**
- *Risk does not decrease with lower coverage*: Confidence scores fail to rank correctness; abstracted atoms are not the erroneous ones.
- *Coverage drops sharply with minor risk improvement*: Over-abstraction or overly aggressive threshold.
- *Reconstruction quality issues*: Repeated/missing claims, incoherent syntax, logical contradictions.
- *Threshold misprediction*: On new data, risk substantially exceeds target, indicating poor calibration or distribution shift.

**First 3 experiments**
1. *Atomization sanity check*: Run atomizer on diverse sentences; verify each atom expresses a single entity–fact pair and that union covers all information. Metric: human review of 20 random examples.
2. *Confidence scoring calibration*: Collect atoms with known correctness labels; compute AUROC of verbalized confidence vs. correctness. Target AUROC > 0.7.
3. *Full pipeline sanity check*: For a few prompts, run SA across thresholds; plot risk vs. coverage and compute AURC. Compare against redaction baseline; expect SA curve to dominate and AURC improvement > 0.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can confidence scoring for atomic claims be improved beyond verbal confidence to better predict factual correctness and enable finer-grained selective abstraction? The paper notes verbalized scores are imperfect and expects stronger confidence scoring to further enhance performance (Future Work). Resolution requires systematic comparison of various confidence functions (logprob, ensemble, self-consistency) across models and datasets, measuring AURC and atom-level AUROC.
- **Open Question 2**: How can dependencies between atomic claims be effectively modeled to improve abstraction coherence and overall reliability? Current SA treats atoms independently, which may cause incoherent reconstructions when related claims are abstracted inconsistently. Resolution involves implementing graph-based or attention-driven models that encode claim dependencies (coreference, discourse parsing) and evaluating RC curves and human coherence judgments.
- **Open Question 3**: How does the uniform prior assumption in the information-theoretic coverage measure bias selectivity across entity types, and can a non-uniform prior based on real-world frequencies yield a more accurate information estimate? The paper acknowledges real-world frequencies are not uniform but uses uniform for simplicity. Resolution requires replacing the uniform prior with frequency estimates from comprehensive sources (census, corpora) and checking whether abstraction decisions or RC curves change.
- **Open Question 4**: How robust is the Wikipedia-based fact-checking agent to domain shifts, and to what extent do its Wikipedia-centric limitations affect measured risk? The agent uses only Wikipedia, which has coverage biases. Resolution involves cross-evaluating against multiple knowledge sources/annotators on diverse claims, measuring agreement and correlating with AURC changes.
- **Open Question 5**: Can Selective Abstraction be generalized beyond factual correctness to other long-form tasks (e.g., creative writing, persuasion, moral reasoning), and how should risk and coverage be redefined? For non-factual tasks, correctness is subjective; the current information measure may not capture user-valued attributes. Resolution requires designing task-specific risk metrics (readability, persuasion effectiveness) and coverage measures (discourse diversity, lexical richness) and piloting SA with human evaluations.

## Limitations
- **Major uncertainties**: Exact LLM decoding parameters and few-shot examples are unreported, potentially affecting atomization and abstraction quality. The SPINACH agent's query generation logic is opaque, risking unreliable coverage estimates. Fact-checking agent errors could bias risk measurements.
