# Why LoRA Fails to Forget: Regularized Low-Rank Adaptation Against Backdoors in Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06305
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06305
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** RoRA reduces attack success rates dramatically (e.g., from 99.7% to 7.5% on BERT-SST-2) while maintaining clean accuracy within 0.5-2% of full fine-tuning.

## Executive Summary
Fine-tuning large language models with LoRA on poisoned data often fails to remove backdoor triggers, as low-rank updates lack both the spectral strength and proper alignment to overwrite malicious behaviors. This paper identifies that forgetting hinges on two factors: the effective spectral magnitude of the update and its alignment with clean-task directions. The authors propose Regularized Low-Rank Adaptation (RoRA), which integrates three mechanisms: clean-strengthened regularization (dropout on pretrained weights) to boost clean alignment, trigger-insensitive regularization (an orthogonality penalty) to avoid trigger-sensitive subspaces, and post-training spectral rescaling to match the pretrained model’s spectral magnitude. Experiments across BERT, RoBERTa, and LLaMA under BadNet and InSent attacks show RoRA reduces attack success rates by over 90% with negligible clean accuracy loss and minimal overhead.

## Method Summary
RoRA enhances LoRA through three coordinated components. First, dropout is applied to the pretrained weight matrix during training, weakening its contribution and forcing the LoRA update ΔW to encode stronger clean-task representations. Second, an orthogonality penalty Ω(A,B) = ||UᵀB||_F² + ||AV||_F² (based on the SVD of W_pre) is added to the loss, pushing ΔW to be orthogonal to the leading spectral subspaces of W_pre, where trigger-sensitive directions are assumed to reside. Third, after convergence, each layer’s scaling factor s is adaptively rescaled as s = σ_max(W_pre) / σ_max(ΔW), thereby matching the spectral magnitude of the pretrained weights and satisfying a theoretical forgetting threshold. These steps ensure that the effective update both aligns with clean tasks and possesses sufficient magnitude to suppress backdoor activations.

## Key Results
- RoRA reduces attack success rate from ~99.7% to 7.5% on BERT fine-tuned on SST-2 under BadNet attack, with similar gains on RoBERTa and LLaMA across BadNet and InSent.
- Clean accuracy degrades by only 0.5–2% relative to full fine-tuning, preserving task performance.
- Overhead is minimal (+6–15 ms per iteration), and the approach enhances other LoRA variants (PiSSA, DoRA, OLoRA).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Clean‑strengthened regularization**  
*Claim:* Increases the clean‑alignment coefficient ρ_cl by forcing LoRA to learn stronger clean‑task representations.  
*Mechanism:* Dropout is applied to W_pre during the forward pass, reducing its effective contribution. The LoRA update ΔW must then compensate to achieve accurate predictions, driving it to align with clean directions.  
*Core assumption:* Randomly masking pretrained activations sufficiently degrades reliance on W_pre.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract mentions clean‑strengthened regularization (dropout on pretrained weights); Section 5.1 details Dropout_p(W_pre) and its effect on enlarging ρ_cl.  
*Break condition:* Dropout rate p too high removes essential pretrained knowledge; p too low fails to weaken W_pre enough.

**Mechanism 2: Trigger‑insensitive regularization**  
*Claim:* Decreases the trigger‑alignment coefficient ρ_tr by pushing ΔW orthogonal to W_pre’s spectral subspaces.  
*Mechanism:* An orthogonality penalty Ω(A,B) = ||UᵀB||_F² + ||AV||_F² is added to the loss, where UΣVᵀ = SVD(W_pre). This suppresses projections of ΔW onto the row and column spaces of W_pre.  
*Core assumption:* Trigger‑sensitive representations are encoded in the top singular subspaces of W_pre.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract cites trigger‑insensitive regularization (orthogonality penalty); Section 5.2 derives the penalty and explains its role in lowering ρ_tr.  
*Break condition:* Regularization weight λ too small fails to enforce orthogonality; λ too large distorts ΔW and may harm clean accuracy. Also ineffective if triggers lie outside the top subspaces.

**Mechanism 3: Post‑training spectral rescaling**  
*Claim:* Increases the spectral strength of LoRA updates to exceed the forgetting threshold s^⋆.  
*Mechanism:* After training, for each layer compute σ_max(W_pre) and σ_max(ΔW) and set s = σ_max(W_pre)/σ_max(ΔW). This boosts the effective magnitude of sΔW to compete with W_pre’s dominant modes.  
*Core assumption:* When ρ_eff ≈ 1, s^⋆ ≈ σ_pre/σ_Δ; matching singular values ensures s > s^⋆.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract mentions post‑training spectral rescaling; Section 5.3 presents the adaptive layerwise rescaling rule motivated by Proposition 4.2.  
*Break condition:* If ΔW has very small σ_max, rescaling amplifies noise and degrades clean accuracy; layerwise uniform scaling may be suboptimal if scaling needs vary across layers.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** LoRA core: trainable matrices A, B with scaling s, update ΔW = sBA. RoRA additions: (1) Dropout applied to W_pre during forward pass; (2) Orthogonality penalty Ω(A,B) added to loss; (3) Post‑training layerwise rescaling of s.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize A, B (e.g., Gaussian/zero).  
  2. For each training iteration: compute loss = L_sup(y, Dropout_p(W_pre)x + sBAx) + λ·Ω(A,B).  
  3. After convergence, for each target layer: compute σ_max(W_pre) and σ_max(BA), set new s = σ_max(W_pre)/σ_max(BA).  
  4. Save final model with W' = W_pre + s·BA.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Dropout rate p: ↑p forces more reliance on LoRA but risks losing pretrained knowledge; ↓p reduces effect. Optimal around p=0.1.  
  - Regularization weight λ: ↑λ improves orthogonality but may harm clean accuracy; ↓λ weakens backdoor suppression. Typical λ=10.  
  - Rescaling: Layerwise adaptation is lightweight but assumes uniform threshold; using a single global s may be simpler but less effective.  
  - Computational overhead: +6–15 ms/iteration, negligible for most workflows.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High ASR (>50%) after RoRA: likely insufficient spectral strength (σ_max(ΔW) still too small) or poor alignment (ρ_eff ≤ 0).  
  - Clean accuracy drop >2%: indicates over‑regularization (p or λ too high) or rescaling amplifying noise.  
  - Orthogonality penalty not decreasing ||UᵀB||_F or ||AV||_F: λ too small or optimization stuck.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Main reproducibility:** Train RoRA on BERT‑large for SST‑2 under BadNet attack (r=8, α=16, p=0.1, λ=10). Verify ASR drops from ~100% (LoRA) to <10% while CA remains within 1% of full fine‑tuning.  
  2. **Ablation study:** Remove each component (dropout, orthogonality, rescaling) one at a time on RoBERTa‑SST‑2‑BadNet. Expect each removal to increase ASR, with orthogonality and dropout most critical.  
  3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Vary dropout p ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3} and λ ∈ {1,5,10,15,20}. Plot ASR and CA; identify stable ranges (e.g., p≈0.1, λ≈10).

## Limitations
- Theoretical scaling threshold s^⋆ assumes approximate alignment (ρ_eff≈1) and may not hold universally across layers or attacks.
- Orthogonality penalty assumes trigger‑sensitive directions lie in top singular subspaces of W_pre; backdoors could exploit rarer subspaces.
- Evaluation restricted to BERT/RoBERTa/LLaMA and BadNet/InSent; unknown for encoder‑decoder (T5, BART), multimodal models, or adaptive attacks.

## Confidence
- *LoRA forgetting failure due to spectral misalignment*: Medium. Theory is plausible but lacks cross‑architecture validation.
- *Each RoRA component (dropout, orthogonality, rescaling)*: High. Ablations consistently show necessity with large effect sizes.
- *Overall RoRA generalizability*: Medium‑High. Strong results on 3 models/2 attacks, but broader testing required.
