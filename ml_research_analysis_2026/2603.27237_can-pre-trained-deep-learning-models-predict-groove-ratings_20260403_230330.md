# Can pre-trained Deep Learning models predict groove ratings?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.27237
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27237
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** MuQ embeddings significantly outperformed others, achieving R² scores of 0.54 for groove, 0.59 for dance, 0.35 for listen, and 0.54 for party, while handcrafted MIR features performed poorly.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates whether deep learning models can predict human groove ratings from audio signals. Using embeddings from seven state-of-the-art audio models and traditional MIR features, the authors applied linear Ridge regression to a dataset of 148 songs with human ratings. MuQ embeddings achieved the highest R² scores (0.54–0.59), while MIR features yielded low or negative scores. Source separation analysis revealed that embeddings from the "other" stem (harmonic/melodic instruments) were most predictive, and PCA visualizations showed these embeddings clustered by musical style (funk, pop, rock). The findings suggest that groove prediction relies more on style-specific cues in non-rhythmic elements than on traditional rhythmic features.

## Method Summary
The experiment used 148 commercially available songs with human ratings on groove, dance, listen, and party dimensions. For each track, embeddings were extracted from seven pre-trained models (AudioMAE, CLAP, M2D, MATPAC++, MERT, MuQ, MusicFM) following model-specific pipelines (e.g., resampling to 16 kHz, pooling to a track-level vector). A baseline of 16 handcrafted MIR features (e.g., mirflux bands, pulse clarity) was also computed. Predictive performance was evaluated via Ridge regression (regularization α=0.2, solver "cholesky") using 5 repeats of 4‑fold cross‑validation. Additionally, source separation with Demucs produced stems (vocals, drums, bass, other) to ablate which instrument groups carry groove information.

## Key Results
- MuQ embeddings achieved mean R² of 0.54 for groove and 0.59 for dance, outperforming all other models.
- Handcrafted MIR features yielded low or negative R² scores, highlighting the superiority of deep representations.
- For MuQ, the "other" stem gave the highest groove R² = 0.42 among stems; PCA of its embeddings revealed clear clustering by genre (funk, pop, rock).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multimodal semantic alignment
**Claim:** MuQ's superior performance stems from its multimodal training, which forces the audio encoder to align music with textual descriptions, capturing semantic concepts (like "groove" or "funk") that correlate with human ratings.  
**Mechanism:** MuQ first uses self-supervised learning (masked acoustic modeling), then a contrastive alignment stage between audio and text embeddings similar to CLAP. This creates a shared space where audio and semantically related text (e.g., "funky bassline") are proximate, potentially encoding high-level musical attributes that are linearly separable for groove prediction.  
**Core assumption:** The textual descriptions used in training align with the perceptual dimensions (dance, party) rated by humans.  
**Evidence anchors:** The paper notes MuQ embeddings significantly outperformed others, and MuQ's architecture includes a text‑audio alignment phase.  
**Break condition:** If the training captions do not capture groove‑related semantics, or if the linear probe extracts style cues unrelated to textual semantics.

### Mechanism 2: Style-dependent cues in melodic/harmonic instruments
**Claim:** The model captures groove primarily through style-dependent timbral and harmonic signatures present in melodic/harmonic instruments ("other" stems), not through traditional rhythmic features.  
**Mechanism:** SSL models like MuQ learn holistic style representations; for funk, pop, and rock, the "other" stem (guitars, keyboards, horns) contains distinct spectral patterns that serve as a genre proxy, which strongly correlates with groove ratings in this dataset.  
**Core assumption:** The dataset's groove ratings are confounded with or strongly driven by musical genre (funk → high groove, rock → variable).  
**Evidence anchors:** Source separation showed the "other" stem achieved R²=0.42 for groove; PCA of its embeddings clustered by style; funk tracks (high groove) often include prominent horn sections.  
**Break condition:** If genre and groove are experimentally disentangled, the "other" stem's predictive power would collapse.

### Mechanism 3: Encoding of rhythmic microtiming in deep representations
**Claim:** Pre-trained audio embeddings contain a compressed, invariant representation of rhythmic "pocket" and microtiming feel that traditional MIR features cannot capture due to their handcrafted, dimensionally limited nature.  
**Mechanism:** SSL models trained on massive corpora develop sensitivity to subtle temporal phenomena (swing, shuffle, humanized timing) across contexts; these latent signals survive dimensionality reduction and are linearly separable regarding groove.  
**Core assumption:** Groove in this dataset is driven by rhythmic microtiming and syncopation, and these patterns are present in the embeddings without explicit rhythmic feature extraction.  
**Evidence anchors:** The paper states deep representations encode complex, style‑dependent groove components that traditional features miss; MIR features gave low or negative R².  
**Break condition:** If removing low‑frequency temporal modulations from embeddings destroys predictive power, while adding explicit microtiming features to MIR improves it, the mechanism would shift toward explicit rhythmic cues.

## Foundational Learning

- **Self‑Supervised Learning (SSL) Pre‑training**  
  *Why needed:* All top‑performing models are SSL‑trained. Understanding masked acoustic modeling (reconstructing corrupted inputs) explains why these embeddings capture structure without label supervision.  
  *Quick check:* What is the core objective function of Masked Acoustic Modeling, and how might learning to reconstruct spectrogram patches force a network to learn temporal and timbral regularities?

- **Linear Probing (Frozen Feature Evaluation)**  
  *Why needed:* The paper uses ridge regression on frozen embeddings to test representational quality, isolating the embedding's information content from the small dataset's noise.  
  *Quick check:* Why is a simple linear model (Ridge) preferred over a fine‑tuned neural network for this evaluation given ~150 songs?

- **Source Separation as an Analysis Tool**  
  *Why needed:* Separating audio into stems is for ablation—identifying which instrument groups carry groove‑relevant information in the learned representation.  
  *Quick check:* If the "other" stem predicts groove best, what does that imply about the locus of stylistic information within the full‑track embedding?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Data Loader -> Optional Stem Separator -> Embedding Extractor -> Linear Probe -> PCA Visualization

**Critical path (baseline replication):**  
1. Load a song, resample to 16 kHz.  
2. Pass the entire clip to the MuQ pipeline (may require chunking).  
3. Pool embeddings (mean over time).  
4. Run Ridge regression (α=0.2, solver="cholesky") on all 148 tracks with 4‑fold CV.  
5. Output R² for groove and generate scatter plot.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Embedding pooling:* Mean vs. max vs. [CLS] token; unspecified in paper, mean pooling is typical but affects R².  
- *Stem separation:* Demucs adds latency and may alter spectral content; using separated stems changes the embedding distribution.  
- *Alpha selection:* Paper used fixed α=0.2 without grid search; may be suboptimal.  
- *PCA visualization:* 2D projection may miss higher‑dimensional separations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- All R² < 0.0 → pipeline broken (wrong sample rate, pooling, corrupted model).  
- MuQ R² ≈ 0.0 but MIR R² ≈ 0.0 → dataset split or regression error.  
- "Other" stem R² > full audio R² → separation artifacts or groove signal in harmony/melody that full‑track embedding dilutes.  
- All models best on "dance" but worst on "listen" → rating normalization issue.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate MuQ full‑track results: target R²_groove ≈ 0.54 ± 0.03.  
2. Source separation ablation: extract MuQ embeddings from each stem separately; verify "other" stem R² > 0.4.  
3. MIR feature baseline: extract 16 handcrafted features; confirm R² near 0.0 or negative.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors identify several unresolved questions:

1. **Specific acoustic features:** Which acoustic features (e.g., microtiming, spectral cues) are most responsible for MuQ's high groove prediction accuracy? Future interpretability work is needed.
2. **Correlation with physical motion:** How do MuQ's predicted groove ratings relate to actual physical motion responses (e.g., dance‑like movements)? They propose using motion‑capture data.
3. **Genre proxy vs. groove encoding:** Is MuQ's prediction primarily driven by genre‑specific spectral cues (e.g., funk horn sections) rather than core rhythmic timing features? Source‑separation results suggest a possible confounding.
4. **Generalizability:** Do these findings generalize to larger, culturally diverse music beyond the 148 Western popular songs (funk, pop, rock) used? The dataset's genre concentration may limit universality.

## Limitations
- **Unknown preprocessing:** Exact audio resampling, chunking, and normalization before embedding extraction are unspecified, risking pipeline mismatches.
- **MIR feature extraction:** Parameters (window/hop sizes) for the 16 handcrafted features are unreported, hindering exact baseline replication.
- **Confounding of genre and groove:** The dataset's genre distribution (funk, pop, rock) is confounded with groove ratings, making it unclear whether embeddings capture groove per se or genre proxies.

## Confidence
- **MuQ superiority (R²≈0.54):** High (replication target is precise and embedding extraction is well‑defined).
- **Style cues in "other" stems:** Medium (supported by PCA clusters and ablation, but causal disentanglement from genre is lacking).
- **Rhythmic microtiming encoding:** Low (no direct evidence; MIR failure could reflect feature limitation rather than embedding strength).

## Next Checks
1. **Exact replication of MuQ full‑track results:** Implement the described pipeline (16 kHz resampling, MuQ‑large, mean pooling, Ridge α=0.2, 4‑fold CV ×5) and confirm R²_groove ≈ 0.54 ± 0.03.
2. **Stem separation sanity check:** Compare "other" stem embeddings against full‑track embeddings to ensure separation artifacts are not driving the observed advantage.
3. **Genre‑controlled regression:** Re‑run ridge regression within each genre (funk/pop/rock). If "other" stem predictive power drops sharply, the mechanism may be primarily a genre proxy.
