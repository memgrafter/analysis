# CoPE-VideoLM: Codec Primitives For Efficient Video Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13191  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13191  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CoPE-VideoLM reduces token usage by up to 93% and time-to-first-token by up to 86% compared to dense RGB models, matches/exceeds SOTA on 14 benchmarks (e.g., +2.4% on PerceptionTest with 80% fewer tokens), and scales to 8-hour videos within a 1M token context.

## Executive Summary
Video language models (VideoLMs) typically encode every video frame as RGB tokens, leading to high computational cost and coarse temporal coverage due to keyframe sampling. CoPE-VideoLM introduces a novel approach that extracts primitives (motion vectors and residuals) from standard video codecs. Only I-frames are processed by a frozen vision encoder, while lightweight delta-encoders convert P-frame primitives into compact, temporally ordered tokens that are pre-aligned with the visual features. This design drastically reduces the number of tokens and latency, enabling efficient processing of very long videos without sacrificing accuracy. Evaluated on 14 benchmarks, CoPE-VideoLM matches or surpasses open-source SOTA, demonstrating that codec-aware representations can make VideoLMs both accurate and practical.

## Method Summary
CoPE-VideoLM operates on videos encoded with a standard codec (e.g., MPEG-4). The system first extracts I-frames and P-frame metadata (motion vectors and residuals). The I-frames are fed into a frozen vision encoder (e.g., ViT) to produce frame-level tokens. For each P-frame, a lightweight delta-encoder processes its primitives to generate delta tokens that capture temporal changes. These tokens are concatenated in decode order to form a compact, temporally coherent sequence. The entire token stream is then supplied to a large language model (LLM) for video understanding. Pre-training aligns the delta-encoder output with the vision encoder's feature space, ensuring compatibility. By avoiding dense RGB processing and leveraging codec compression, the approach achieves substantial efficiency gains.

## Key Results
- Token usage reduced by up to 93% compared to dense frame models.  
- Time-to-first-token improved by up to 86%.  
- Matches or exceeds state-of-the-art open-source VideoLMs on 14 benchmarks; notably +2.4% accuracy on PerceptionTest with 80% fewer tokens.  
- Successfully scales to 8-hour videos within a 1M token context.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that standard video codecs already encode the essential changes between frames in a highly compressed form via motion vectors and residuals. By tapping into these primitives, CoPE-VideoLM avoids redundant processing of full RGB frames. The use of a frozen vision encoder for I-frames preserves high-quality spatial features, while the delta-encoders transform sparse codec data into dense tokens that maintain temporal ordering without needing every frame's pixel data. This representation naturally aligns with the autoregressive processing of LLMs, as tokens arrive sequentially. The pre-training step ensures that the delta tokens are semantically consistent with the I-frame features, allowing the LLM to reason over a much more compact yet informative sequence. Furthermore, the method inherits the compression ratios of modern codecs, enabling handling of extremely long videos.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
Video (encoded) -> Split into I-frames and P-frames -> I-frames : Frozen Vision Encoder -> I-tokens  
P-frames (motion vectors, residuals) : Δ-Encoder -> Δ-tokens  
I-tokens + Δ-tokens (temporally interleaved) -> Token Sequence -> Large Language Model (LLM)

**Critical path:** The video is parsed by a codec parser. I-frames are processed by the vision encoder to produce base tokens. For each P-frame in decode order, the delta encoder processes its primitives and emits delta tokens. The two token streams are merged in decode order, forming the final input to the LLM.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Efficiency vs. detail: Using codec primitives trades off some fine-grained pixel information for massive token reduction and speed.  
- Fixed fusion window: The Δ-encoder applies a fixed temporal window (e.g., 30 P-frames) to aggregate primitives; this may be suboptimal for varying motion.  
- Codec dependency: Reliance on standard codecs limits applicability to videos without accessible motion vectors/residuals (e.g., raw streams) and introduces sensitivity to encoding parameters.  
- Frozen encoder: While reducing trainable parameters, it may limit adaptation to domains with different visual styles.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Degraded accuracy on high‑motion sequences where motion vectors become less reliable (e.g., fast camera motion, occlusions).  
- Temporal misalignment if codec decode order is not correctly respected.  
- Performance drop when codec type or bitrate differs from training (e.g., HEVC vs. MPEG‑4).  
- Increased latency if Δ-encoder processing becomes a bottleneck with very long P‑frame runs.

**First three experiments to onboard:**  
1. **Baseline comparison:** Process a standard short‑video dataset (e.g., MSR-VTT) with CoPE‑VideoLM and a dense‑frame VideoLM, measure token count, end‑to‑end latency, and accuracy on a captioning task.  
2. **Ablation of fusion window:** Vary the fixed P‑frame fusion window size (e.g., 10, 30, 60) on a temporally sensitive dataset (e.g., Something‑Something V2) to observe impact on action classification accuracy and token usage.  
3. **Cross‑codec robustness:** Re‑encode a validation set with different codecs (H.264, HEVC) and bitrates, then evaluate CoPE‑VideoLM’s accuracy and token count stability; compare to the original MPEG‑4 setting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **B-frame support**  
   - **Question:** How can CoPE‑VideoLM be extended to support B-frames, which rely on non‑causal dependencies?  
   - **Basis in paper:** “Our current approach focuses on I- and P-frames, lacking support for B-frames and their complex non‑causal dependencies” (Section 5).  
   - **Why unresolved:** B‑frames require both past and future frames for decoding, conflicting with LLMs’ causal processing. A decode‑order solution is proposed but unevaluated.  
   - **Resolution evidence:** Implement a B‑frame‑capable variant (e.g., using decode order or a non‑causal model) and measure accuracy/efficiency against the P‑frame‑only method.

2. **Raw codec primitives vs. tensors**  
   - **Question:** Does operating directly on raw codec primitives (block‑wise motion vectors and quantized DCT coefficients) outperform the tensorized representation in token efficiency and accuracy?  
   - **Basis in paper:** “For future work, it would be interesting to stay closer to the raw codec primitives, by operating directly on sets of block‑wise motion vectors and quantized DCT coefficients, which could offer even better computational and token efficiency.”  
   - **Why unresolved:** The current implementation uses a pre‑tensorized form; raw primitives might preserve finer details or sparsity but remain untested.  
   - **Resolution evidence:** Build a model that consumes raw sparse motion vectors and DCT coefficients (e.g., via sparse attention), and compare token counts, speed, and benchmark performance.

3. **Adaptive P‑frame fusion**  
   - **Question:** Can an adaptive P‑frame fusion window that varies with motion content improve performance over the fixed fusion window?  
   - **Basis in paper:** “We use a fixed P‑frame fusion window, which is suboptimal for tasks with varying motion” (Section 5).  
   - **Why unresolved:** A fixed window (e.g., s=30) ignores motion dynamics; too many frames in high‑motion scenes may cause blurring, too few in static scenes may waste tokens.  
   - **Resolution evidence:** Design a motion‑adaptive fusion scheme (e.g., split P‑frames when motion magnitude exceeds a threshold) and evaluate on temporal reasoning tasks for gains.

4. **Codec parameter sensitivity**  
   - **Question:** How sensitive is CoPE‑VideoLM to codec parameters such as codec type (H.264, HEVC), bitrate, and encoding quality?  
   - **Basis in paper:** “Exploring sensitivity to codec type, bitrate, and encoding quality remains an open question.”  
   - **Why unresolved:** All experiments used MPEG‑4 at a single GOP size and quality; different codecs alter information stored in primitives, affecting performance and token usage.  
   - **Resolution evidence:** Re‑encode evaluation videos with various codecs/bitrates, extract primitives accordingly, and analyze trends in accuracy and token usage.

## Limitations
- **External validation gap:** The paper has zero citations and moderate neighbor FMR (0.407), indicating limited independent replication; claims rely on self‑reported benchmarks.  
- **Codec dependency:** Performance may vary across codecs (H.264 vs. HEVC) and settings; motion vectors/residuals are not universally accessible in real‑world video streams.  
- **Encoder constraints:** The frozen vision encoder and lightweight Δ‑encoders assume pre‑aligned feature spaces, potentially limiting adaptation to novel visual domains or extreme motion.
