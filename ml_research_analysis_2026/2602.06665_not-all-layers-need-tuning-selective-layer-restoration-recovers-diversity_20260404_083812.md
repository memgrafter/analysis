# Not All Layers Need Tuning: Selective Layer Restoration Recovers Diversity
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06665
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06665
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** Selective Layer Restoration (SLR) is a training-free, zero-inference-cost method that restores diversity in post-trained LLMs by selectively reverting a contiguous interval of layers to pre-trained weights, achieving large diversity gains (e.g., +112.7% entropy on QA) with minimal quality loss (~ -2%).

## Executive Summary
Post-training LLMs via instruction tuning or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) often suffers from mode collapse, where outputs become repetitive and diversity is severely reduced. Existing solutions—such as decoding adjustments, prompt engineering, or costly retraining—offer incomplete fixes. This paper introduces Selective Layer Restoration (SLR), a simple yet effective training-free intervention: it identifies a specific contiguous interval of transformer layers that, when restored to their pre-trained weights, recovers suppressed diverse modes while retaining instruction-following capabilities. The restoration interval is selected using a cheap proxy task, Constrained Random Character (CRC), which measures diversity under explicit validity constraints. Across three model families (Llama, Qwen, Gemma) and three tasks (creative writing, open-ended QA, reasoning), SLR substantially increases diversity metrics (e.g., entropy, coverage, Pass@k) with negligible quality degradation and combines additively with sampling- and prompt-based diversification methods.

## Method Summary
SLR operates on a post-trained model \(M_{\text{post}}\) and its corresponding pre-trained checkpoint \(M_{\text{pre}}\). The core idea is that mode collapse is localized to a specific contiguous range of layers \([i, j]\). To find this interval without expensive downstream evaluation, the authors design the Constrained Random Character (CRC) proxy task: models generate sequences starting with a randomly chosen valid first token (e.g., an English letter), and diversity is measured as entropy over the set of valid completions. A grid search over all contiguous layer intervals is performed on CRC to identify the interval that maximizes diversity subject to a quality constraint (validity \(\geq 0.9 \times\) validity of \(M_{\text{post}}\)). The selected layers in \(M_{\text{post}}\) are then replaced with the corresponding layers from \(M_{\text{pre}}\), producing the final \(M_{\text{SLR}}\) model. This process requires no additional training and incurs zero inference overhead.

## Key Results
- On open-ended QA, SLR boosts entropy by 112.7% and coverage-n (fraction of unique correct answers) by 169.5% over the post-trained baseline, while preserving answer accuracy.
- On creative writing, average diversity (measured by embedding dissimilarity) improves by 42.5% with only a ~2% quality drop as judged by a proprietary model.
- On reasoning tasks, Pass@k consistently increases, indicating better recovery of multiple correct reasoning paths.
- SLR’s gains are additive with decoding methods (e.g., higher temperature) and prompt-based techniques (e.g., verbalized sampling), and it outperforms a proxy-tuned model-soup baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Localization of Mode Collapse**  
Post-training (e.g., SFT, RLHF) primarily modifies a functionally distinct contiguous interval of layers responsible for suppressing alternative, diverse modes present in the pre-trained model. Restoring only that interval to pre-trained weights recovers access to those suppressed distributions while retaining instruction-following calibrations from the untouched layers. Evidence: The paper formalizes the problem as selecting an interval \([i, j]\) to maximize diversity subject to a quality constraint, implying localization. The resulting diversity–quality Pareto front (Figure 2) shows that many intervals offer improvements over the post-trained model, supporting the hypothesis that mode collapse is not uniformly distributed across layers.

**Mechanism 2: CRC Proxy Generalization**  
The Constrained Random Character (CRC) task provides a valid, low-cost signal for selecting restoration intervals that transfer to downstream tasks. CRC creates a controlled setting where validity is a binary token constraint and diversity is entropy over valid completions. This eliminates semantic ambiguity, enabling efficient grid search over intervals. The optimal interval on CRC generalizes because the same layers that suppress low-level stochastic diversity under token constraints also suppress semantic diversity in open-ended generation. Evidence: Ablation in Section 4.5 shows that proxy-guided intervals significantly outperform arbitrary (early/late) restorations on downstream QA metrics, confirming transferability.

**Mechanism 3: Orthogonality to Sampling and Prompting**  
SLR reshapes the underlying probability distribution of the model by restoring pre-trained weights in specific layers, whereas decoding (e.g., temperature) and prompt-based methods (e.g., verbalized sampling) operate on sampling from a fixed distribution or input framing. Therefore, their effects are orthogonal and additive. Evidence: Figure 4 and Tables 2, 4, 6 show that SLR’s diversity gains persist under higher temperature and verbalized sampling, with consistent additive improvements.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transformer Layer Functional Specialization**: Different layers serve distinct roles (e.g., early layers for syntax, mid-layers for knowledge, late layers for instruction following). This modularity is essential to SLR’s premise that restoring a specific interval can target mode collapse without harming instruction adherence. Quick check: If specialization holds, restoring only the first few layers should minimally recover diversity, as they handle surface features, not high-level distribution steering.
- **Mode Collapse in Post-Training**: Post-training concentrates probability mass on a narrow set of “safe” responses, reducing output diversity. Simply increasing sampling temperature fails because it adds noise to the already collapsed distribution without restoring forgotten modes. Quick check: Why might temperature scaling not recover diversity lost in RLHF? Because it only perturbs sampling from the same collapsed distribution; it does not reintroduce suppressed modes into the model’s weights.
- **Diversity Metrics (Entropy, Coverage, Embedding Dissimilarity)**: Different tasks require different metrics—e.g., entropy and coverage-n for QA, embedding-based dissimilarity for creative writing. Misunderstanding these can mislead interpretation. Quick check: In QA, why is coverage-n a useful complement to entropy? Entropy can be high if many *incorrect* answers are generated; coverage-n specifically measures recovery of *valid* distinct answers.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** M_pre (pre-trained checkpoint), M_post (post-trained checkpoint), CRC Proxy (generates constrained prompts and computes validity Q_CRC and diversity D_CRC for any interval [i, j]), Interval Searcher (grid search over contiguous [i, j] on CRC to find interval maximizing D_CRC subject to Q_CRC ≥ 0.9 * Q_CRC(M_post)), SLR Constructor (merges M_post and M_pre by copying weights of layers i through j from M_pre into M_post), Evaluation Harness (task-specific samplers and metric calculators, e.g., Gemini judge for creative writing, LLM canonicalization for QA).

**Critical path:** Formulate hypothesis that mode collapse is localized → design CRC proxy task → grid search on CRC to select optimal [i, j] → construct SLR model by weight restoration → evaluate on downstream tasks (QA, creative writing, reasoning) → compare against M_post and model-soup baseline.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Contiguous vs. non-contiguous intervals: Restricting to contiguous layers keeps search space polynomial but may miss optimal non-contiguous patterns.  
- Proxy task simplicity: CRC is cheap but low-level; a more semantically complex proxy might generalize better but be costlier to evaluate.  
- Quality threshold (q_min): Set at 90% of post-trained CRC validity; higher thresholds preserve more quality but may sacrifice diversity, lower thresholds do the opposite.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Quality collapse: Q_T(SLR) drops significantly (>5%) below Q_T(M_post), indicating restored layers were critical for instruction following.  
2. No diversity gain: D_T(SLR) ≈ D_T(M_post), suggesting wrong interval selection or that mode collapse isn’t localized to those layers.  
3. Proxy-downstream mismatch: CRC shows a good interval, but downstream diversity degrades, indicating the proxy does not capture the functional role relevant to the target task.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate CRC landscape:** For a single model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B), run full grid search over all contiguous intervals. Plot the Pareto front (diversity vs. quality) and verify that the post-trained model sits at high-quality/low-diversity with visible trade-offs.  
2. **Single-task validation:** Apply the CRC-selected interval to open-ended QA. Compare SLR vs. M_post on precision and coverage-n to confirm predicted diversity gain with minimal quality loss.  
3. **Ablation - interval choice:** For the selected [i, j], create SLR-Early (restore first l layers) and SLR-Late (restore last l layers) with same l = j-i+1. Run on QA; expect SLR to outperform both, proving specificity of the chosen interval.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** Does Selective Layer Restoration (SLR) maintain its effectiveness when applied to large language models with significantly more parameters (e.g., >10B or >100B), and does the CRC-guided interval selection transfer to such scales?  
- **Basis in paper:** In the paper's conclusion (Section 5, "Limitations and Future Work"), the authors state: "First, we evaluated SLR only on ∼7–9B parameter models; it remains to be seen whether the CRC landscape and the transferability of proxy-guided restoration intervals persist at larger scales, where post-training dynamics and layer specialization may differ."  
- **Why unresolved:** Experiments were conducted exclusively on 7-9B parameter models. It is unknown if the layer-localization of mode collapse and the diversity-quality trade-offs identified via CRC hold for larger models, which may exhibit different functional specialization or post-training behaviors.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic studies applying SLR to models of increasing scale (e.g., 13B, 34B, 70B, 100B+) using the same CRC-guided intervals derived from smaller models or re-derived for each scale. Evaluation should measure diversity and quality across the same tasks to determine if benefits persist or diminish with scale, and whether the optimal restoration intervals change.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** To what extent is SLR complementary to other decoding and prompt-based diversity enhancement methods beyond min-p sampling and verbalized sampling, and under which conditions does it provide the greatest marginal improvement?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note in Section 5: "Second, while we demonstrate composability with representative decoding and prompt-based interventions, a more comprehensive evaluation across temperature sweeps, alternative sampling and truncation schemes, and broader prompt-based methods would clarify where SLR provides the largest marginal gains."  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only tested SLR combined with a limited set: min-p sampling (with temperature 1 and 1.5) and verbalized sampling (K=4). Other popular decoding strategies (e.g., top-p, top-k, typical-p, contrastive search) and prompt-based methods (e.g., chain-of-thought, self-consistency, diverse beam search) were not evaluated. It remains unclear whether SLR always adds value, interacts synergistically, or sometimes conflicts with other methods.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive empirical study that systematically varies decoding parameters (temperature, top-p, top-k, etc.) and prompt templates/methods across multiple tasks and models, comparing baseline (post-trained) vs. SLR models. Analyzing interaction effects would reveal which combinations yield the largest diversity gains with minimal quality loss, and in what scenarios SLR is most beneficial.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Can more fine-grained weight-space interventions, such as restoring only specific sub-layers (e.g., attention heads or feed-forward networks) within transformer blocks, achieve better diversity-quality trade-offs than restoring entire contiguous layers?  
- **Basis in paper:** In the limitations, the authors propose: "Finally, while our method restores entire layers, future work could explore more fine-grained weight-space interventions, such as layer-wise interpolation or sub-layer restoration (e.g., attention heads or FFNs), to enable finer control over the diversity–quality trade-off while preserving the training-free and inference-cost-neutral advantages of SLR."  
- **Why unresolved:** SLR currently operates at the layer level, replacing all parameters of contiguous layers. However, different sub-components within a layer (multi-head attention vs. feed-forward network) might contribute differently to mode collapse and diversity. Coarse layer restoration might inadvertently degrade some functions; sub-layer selectivity could allow more precise recovery of diverse behaviors while preserving quality-critical functions.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments that vary which sub-layers are restored (e.g., only attention heads in certain layers, only FFNs, or combinations) using the same proxy task (CRC) to guide selection, then evaluate downstream tasks. Comparing diversity and quality metrics across sub-layer strategies vs. whole-layer SLR would identify whether finer granularity improves the Pareto frontier, and whether the optimal intervention granularity is task-dependent.

## Limitations
- **Contiguous interval assumption:** Restricting to contiguous layers may miss optimal non-contiguous patterns; no theoretical justification for this design choice.
- **Proxy task simplicity:** CRC uses character-level constraints, which may not fully capture semantic diversity mechanisms in complex tasks; transferability is assumed but not theoretically explained.
- **Scalability & generality:** Results are shown for 8B parameter models; performance on larger models or varied post-training (e.g., DPO vs. SFT) is unvalidated.

## Confidence
- **Localization hypothesis (Mechanism 1):** Medium. Empirical evidence (Pareto fronts) supports it for tested models, but break condition (universal Pareto improvement) isn't proven across architectures.
- **CRC proxy validity (Mechanism 2):** Medium-Low. Ablation shows proxy intervals beat arbitrary ones, but corpus lacks direct parallels; no guarantee of cross-task generalization.
- **Additivity with decoding/prompting (Mechanism 3):** Medium. Tested additively in experiments, but orthogonality claim rests on limited task combinations (QA, writing, reasoning).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-family replication:** Run full CRC grid search on Qwen2.5 or Gemma families; verify interval selection patterns and diversity gains hold outside Llama.
2. **Direct vs. proxy optimization:** For a held-out downstream task (e.g., creative writing), directly optimize contiguous intervals via small-sample evaluation; compare selected interval to CRC’s recommendation to quantify proxy mismatch.
3. **Threshold sensitivity sweep:** Vary q_min (80%, 95%, 99%) on CRC; measure downstream diversity/quality trade-offs to identify robustness bounds and potential failure regions.
