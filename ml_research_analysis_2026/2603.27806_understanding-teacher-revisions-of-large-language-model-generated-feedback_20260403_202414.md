# Understanding Teacher Revisions of Large Language Model-Generated Feedback
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.27806
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27806
*   **Reference Count:** 31
*   **Primary Result:** Teachers accept 77.8% of LLM-generated feedback unedited; when they edit, they primarily shorten longer drafts and shift feedback from explanatory toward corrective forms.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how K-12 teachers revise formative feedback generated by large language models (LLMs) before it is delivered to students. Analyzing 1,349 feedback instances from 117 teachers, the authors find that the majority of AI-generated feedback is accepted as-is. When revisions occur, they are characterized by a net shortening of text and a systematic shift away from the LLM's default "high-information" explanatory style toward more concise corrective or reinforcement messages. Predictive models using only the AI's text achieve modest performance (AUC=0.75) in identifying which drafts will be edited, suggesting that revision decisions are heavily influenced by teacher-specific and contextual factors not present in the feedback text itself.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes a dataset of 1,349 pairs of initial LLM-generated feedback and corresponding teacher-edited final messages collected from a live educational platform. Teachers first tag a student's short answer as correct/partial/incorrect, after which an LLM generates a draft feedback message that teachers can accept, edit, or rewrite. The analysis employs three main approaches: (1) quantitative textual analysis comparing word counts and semantic similarity (via Sentence Transformers embeddings) between AI drafts and final messages; (2) predictive modeling using logistic regression, a shallow MLP, and gradient-boosted trees, trained on AI feedback embeddings and evaluated via group cross-validation to predict revision likelihood; and (3) qualitative coding of a subset (n=834) of messages into four pedagogical types (Corrective, High-information, Reinforcement, Punishment) to assess pre/post-revision shifts.

## Key Results
*   **High Acceptance Rate:** Teachers leave 77.8% of AI-generated feedback unedited.
*   **Editing Pattern:** For edited feedback, teachers shorten longer drafts (mean reduction of 6.4 words) and preserve semantic content (high cosine similarity).
*   **Pedagogical Shift:** Revisions systematically move feedback away from the LLM's "High-information" default (59.9% pre-edit) toward a more balanced distribution including more "Corrective" and "Reinforcement/Punishment" types.
*   **Predictive Modesty:** Models using only AI feedback text achieve fair performance (AUC=0.75) in identifying revisions, indicating significant influence from unobserved factors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Verbosity Reduction
*   **Claim:** Teachers revise primarily to reduce perceived verbosity and cognitive load.
*   **Mechanism:** When LLMs produce longer drafts, teachers compress them, as evidenced by a net shortening (mean -6.4 words) while maintaining high semantic similarity (cosine ~0.97). Edits focus on conciseness, not content overhaul.
*   **Core Assumption:** Teachers prioritize efficiency and manageability in student-facing materials.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract & Section 4.1 document length reduction; high cosine similarity indicates preservation of meaning. Corpus neighbors echo teacher concerns about AI output volume but lack quantitative anchors.

### Mechanism 2: Pedagogical Realignment
*   **Claim:** Teachers revise to align feedback with their pedagogical preferences, often simplifying the LLM's explanatory tendencies.
*   **Mechanism:** The LLM defaults to "High-information" explanations. Teachers revise these toward more directive "Corrective" or evaluative "Reinforcement/Punishment" forms, signaling a preference for brevity or different evaluative framing.
*   **Core Assumption:** Teacher-held pedagogical beliefs (e.g., against overscaffolding) conflict with common LLM output patterns.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract & Section 4.3 show a significant pre/post shift in coded types from High-information to more balanced distribution. Corpus hints at teacher agency in AI adoption.

### Mechanism 3: Influence of Unobserved Factors
*   **Claim:** Modest model performance (AUC=0.75) indicates revision decisions depend on non-textual factors beyond the AI feedback.
*   **Mechanism:** While AI text features provide signal, the performance ceiling suggests teacher dispositions, student-specific context, or situational constraints (e.g., time) are primary mediators.
*   **Core Assumption:** Revision is a situated decision influenced by variables not captured in the feedback string.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract & Section 5.3 explicitly state the modest ceiling and that decisions "are not determined solely by the properties of AI-generated feedback." Corpus discusses intervention factors like student history.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Human-in-the-loop AI Hybrid Adaptivity**  
  *Why needed here:* The study treats teachers as active editors of AI output, not passive consumers. This hybrid system shapes the observed acceptance and edit patterns.  
  *Quick check:* If 50% of teachers never edit, what does that imply about the "human" component's role in the loop?
*   **Concept: Pedagogical Feedback Typologies**  
  *Why needed here:* The analysis hinges on coding feedback into types like "Corrective" and "High-information" (based on frameworks from Hattie & Timperley). Understanding these categories is essential to interpret the observed pedagogical shifts.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a teacher intentionally convert a detailed "High-information" message into a brief "Corrective" one?
*   **Concept: Representational Similarity & Embeddings**  
  *Why needed here:* The study uses sentence embeddings (cosine similarity) to quantify how much teachers preserve the AI's semantic content versus rewrite it. This metric underpins the conclusion that edits focus on conciseness.  
  *Quick check:* What does a high cosine similarity (e.g., 0.97) between AI draft and final edited text tell us about the nature of teacher edits?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Student Response Input -> Teacher Correctness Tagging -> LLM Feedback Generator -> Teacher Interface (Edit/Accept) -> Final Feedback Delivered to Student. Offline: Embedding Engine & Predictive Model analyze AI/Teacher text pairs; Coding Schema classifies pedagogical type.

**Critical path:** Student Answer -> Teacher Tag -> LLM Draft -> **Teacher Edit Decision** -> Final Feedback. The edit step is the key mediation point.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
*   Verbosity vs. Utility: More detailed AI drafts provide more material to edit but increase editing burden.
*   System Uniformity vs. Teacher Agency: A single LLM prompt may not align with all teachers' pedagogical styles, evidenced by the bimodal edit-rate distribution.
*   Real-time vs. Retrospective Analysis: The platform supports live editing; the study analyzes logs post-hoc, missing in-situ reasoning.

**Failure signatures:**  
*   High unedited rate + low revision prediction accuracy: AI output strongly dictates student experience, reducing teacher agency.
*   Low semantic similarity in edits + shift toward Reinforcement/Punishment: Teachers fundamentally disagree with AI's assessment/pedagogy.
*   Extreme bimodal editing: System may be ill-fitted for half the teachers (no edits) or overly burdensome for the other half (constant edits).

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Replication of Edit Rate Distribution:** Extract edit rates per teacher from a new cohort; test if the bimodal pattern (e.g., 50% zero-edit) holds using Gaussian mixture modeling.
2.  **Length Compression Analysis:** For edited items, regress word reduction on initial AI word count; confirm a significant negative slope (longer drafts are shortened more).
3.  **Pedagogical Shift Validation:** Recode a sample using the 4-type schema; replicate the pre/post shift from High-information to Corrective/Reinforcement using a different coder set or an automated classifier trained on original codes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** Which specific teacher-level attributes (e.g., AI self-efficacy, pedagogical beliefs, experience) explain the observed bimodal distribution in editing behavior?
*   **Open Question 2:** Does teacher revision of LLM-generated feedback improve, worsen, or have no effect on student learning outcomes compared to unedited AI feedback?
*   **Open Question 3:** Beyond the coarse "high-information" vs. "corrective" dichotomy, what are the precise pedagogical intents and micro-decisions (e.g., tone adjustment, error prioritization, cultural localization) driving substantive revisions?
*   **Open Question 4:** Can predictive models of revision be significantly improved by incorporating context beyond the AI text—specifically, the student's original response, the teacher's correctness tag, and situational constraints?
*   **Open Question 5:** To what extent is the high rate of unedited AI feedback (77.8%) a signal of alignment versus a symptom of teacher workload, automation bias, or lack of scrutiny?

## Limitations
*   **Data Scope:** Single-platform sample limits generalizability to other educational contexts or grade levels.
*   **Coding Subjectivity:** Pedagogical typology relies on human judgment; inter-rater reliability is not reported, risking coding drift.
*   **Ecological Validity:** Edits occurred in a live system but without real student consequence stakes; behavior may differ in actual classroom use.

## Confidence
*   **Verbosity Reduction Mechanism:** High. Directly supported by word count changes and high semantic similarity metrics.
*   **Pedagogical Realignment Mechanism:** Medium. Supported by coded type shifts, but category boundaries may be blurry.
*   **Non-Textual Factors Mechanism:** High. Authors explicitly state this; the AUC=0.75 ceiling is a strong empirical signal.
*   **Data Representativeness:** Medium. Single-platform data is a common constraint but noted by authors.
*   **Ecological Validity:** Low. The "in-the-wild" setup has real teachers but artificial stakes.

## Next Checks
1.  **Bimodal Distribution Replication:** Test the edit-rate distribution (50% zero-edit) in a new, independent teacher cohort using Gaussian mixture modeling.
2.  **Edit-Type Disentanglement:** Classify a sample of edits (deletion, rewording, addition) to verify that length compression occurs without significant semantic loss (e.g., measure lexical diversity changes).
3.  **Contextual Feature Ablation:** Incrementally add features like teacher ID, student prior performance, or time-of-day to the prediction model; quantify the AUC improvement over the text-only baseline.
