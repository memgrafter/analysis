# Exploring SAIG Methods for an Objective Evaluation of XAI
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.08715
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08715
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The paper presents the first review of 16 Synthetic Artificial Intelligence Ground truth (SAIG) methods for image XAI evaluation, proposes a five‑dimensional taxonomy, uncovers strong design interdependencies, and highlights a lack of consensus on faithful XAI methods, calling for standardization.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the objective evaluation of Explainable AI (XAI) by investigating Synthetic Artificial Intelligence Ground truth (SAIG) methods. It reviews 16 SAIG approaches for images and introduces a taxonomy spanning five dimensions: image composition (type, position, background), ground truth definition/value, generability, and evaluation metrics. Key findings include strong design interdependencies—e.g., generability correlates perfectly with real‑world images and no background (Cramér’s V=1.0), and binary ground truth aligns closely with spatial alignment metrics (V=0.91)—and a notable absence of agreement across SAIG studies on which XAI methods are most faithful, despite evaluating 32 mostly post‑hoc techniques. This emphasizes the urgent need for standardization and integrated frameworks to render SAIG evaluations reliable and comparable.

## Method Summary
The reproduction entails extracting Table 1 (SAIG method properties) and Table 2 (XAI method performance claims) from the paper, encoding categorical variables, and performing quantitative analyses. Specifically, pairwise Cramér’s V is computed to assess interdependencies among the seven taxonomy dimensions, and a co‑occurrence matrix is built to visualize which subcategories appear together across methods. The objective is to verify the reported design interdependencies and the lack of consensus, thereby confirming the paper’s core arguments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Synthetic ground truth as an objective benchmark**  
SAIG methods generate artificial images with embedded discriminative features (shapes, colors, positions) whose relevance for the classification task is predefined. After training a model on such data, its decision rule aligns with these known features. XAI explanations can then be compared against the ground‑truth masks using metrics like spatial alignment or distribution distance. This assumes synthetic features capture model behavior transferable to real‑world scenarios. *Evidence*: The abstract notes that SAIG generates artificial ground truths directly; Section 3 describes concrete datasets such as Toy Color, Decoy MNIST, and an8Flower. *Break condition*: if the model learns shortcuts not aligned with the intended features, the evaluation becomes misleading.

**Mechanism 2: Design interdependencies as a decision guide**  
Statistical analysis (Cramér’s V) of the 16 SAIG methods reveals strong correlations among design choices (e.g., between position and background, between generability and image type). These interdependencies help researchers select compatible configuration combinations (e.g., mosaic‑based, real‑world images) for more generalizable evaluations. *Evidence*: Section 6 includes the dependency graph (Figure 9) and co‑occurrence heatmap (Figure 10) showing V=1.0 for several pairs. *Break condition*: if future SAIG methods deliberately diverge from observed patterns, the predictive power of these interdependencies may weaken.

**Mechanism 3: Lack of consensus drives standardization**  
Comparing the best‑performing XAI methods reported in each SAIG study shows no agreement, indicating that current evaluations are sensitive to design choices and not yet reliable. Recognizing this fragmentation motivates the community to develop unified benchmarks and protocols. *Evidence*: The abstract mentions the notable lack of consensus; Section 6.1 discusses the disagreement explicitly. Neighboring work (e.g., arxiv:2412.05592) also warns about evaluation inconsistencies. *Break condition*: without widespread adoption of standards, the lack of consensus may persist.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Generator -> GT Encoder -> Model Wrapper -> Metric Engine -> Result Analyzer

**Critical path**:
1. Define evaluation objective (fidelity, robustness, etc.).
2. Choose SAIG configuration: image type, positioning, background, GT definition/value, generability.
3. Generate dataset and corresponding GT.
4. Train or select a model with sufficient accuracy on the synthetic task.
5. Apply targeted XAI methods to the model.
6. Run Metrics Engine for each explanation vs. GT.
7. Interpret results and draw conclusions about XAI method quality.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Simplicity vs. Realism*: Simple geometric shapes offer clean GT but may not reflect complex model behavior; real‑world images increase ecological validity but introduce noise and require careful GT definition.
- *Binary vs. Multivalue GT*: Binary masks are straightforward and work with many metrics but ignore attribution strength; multivalue GT enables richer assessment but demands more careful design.
- *Generalizability vs. Control*: Generalizable mosaic methods let users plug in their own images, enhancing applicability, but rely on user choice; predefined non‑generalizable datasets provide controlled experiments but cannot be easily adapted.

**Failure signatures**:
- Low model accuracy on synthetic task: Model may not align with intended GT, invalidating evaluation.
- Metric saturation: Spatial alignment metrics can yield near‑perfect scores even for unfaithful explanations.
- Distribution shift between synthetic training and test: Models may overfit to spurious cues, causing explanations to reflect artifacts.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication of Toy Color**: Build the 5×5 RGB dataset, train a simple CNN, compute explanations with Gradient, Integrated Gradients, and LRP. Evaluate using Focus to see whether methods correctly identify corner and top‑middle pixels.
2. **Mosaic vs. Random position comparison**: Generate two SAIG variants from the same real image set: one using a 2×2 mosaic (predefined positions, no background) and another with random placement of class‑specific objects on uniform background. Compare XAI methods using spatial alignment metrics to observe how position strategy affects relative performance.
3. **Multivalue GT sensitivity test**: Use Miró‑Nicolau et al.’s geometric shape generator with pixel‑wise importance values. Apply several post‑hoc methods and compute Earth Mover’s Distance. Gradually add noise to test robustness of the metric and stability of rankings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Unified SAIG evaluation framework**: How can a framework harmonize diverse approaches to ensure reliable, comparable assessments? *Basis*: Authors explicitly call for integration. *Why unresolved*: Current methods vary widely; no shared standards, leading to inconsistent XAI rankings. *Evidence needed*: Systematic mapping, standardized formats and protocols, cross‑validation, demonstration of stable rankings.
- **Impact of design dimensions**: What is the effect of each dimension (GT definition, image type, background, position) on SAIG outcomes, and can interdependencies be disentangled? *Basis*: Dependency analysis shows strong associations. *Why unresolved*: Correlations identified but not causality; unclear if single‑dimension changes independently alter rankings. *Evidence needed*: Controlled factorial experiments, meta‑regression, sensitivity analysis.
- **Causes of divergent best XAI methods**: Why do SAIG studies disagree on which XAI methods are most faithful? *Basis*: Comparative study reveals lack of consensus. *Why unresolved*: Specific causes (metrics, GT encoding, dataset composition, training) not systematically investigated. *Evidence needed*: Replication across common SAIG datasets/metrics; analysis of correlations with design choices; user studies.
- **Metrics for multivalue ground truth**: How should metrics be designed for continuous relevance scores to capture both spatial correspondence and magnitude fidelity? *Basis*: Multivalue GT appears in few methods and lacks a dominant metric. *Why unresolved*: Existing metrics may not fully capture nuanced alignment; no consensus on best metric. *Evidence needed*: Propose candidate metrics combining spatial and continuous value matching; evaluate on multivalue GT datasets to assess correlation with ground truth or human judgments.

## Limitations
- The review covers only 16 SAIG methods; newer or overlooked methods may alter the taxonomy and interdependencies.
- Classification of methods into subcategories relies on the authors’ interpretation; inter‑rater reliability is unknown.
- Cramér’s V analysis uses a small sample (n=16), making correlations sensitive to variations in single methods.
- Identification of “best” XAI methods in Table 2 may be ambiguous due to differing evaluation protocols.
