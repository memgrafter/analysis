# LLM-WikiRace Benchmark: How Far Can LLMs Plan over Real-World Knowledge Graphs?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16902
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16902
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: Frontier LLMs achieve >95% success on easy Wikipedia navigation tasks but fall to ≤23% on hard tasks, with looping and replanning failures as dominant breakdowns.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces **LLM-WikiRace**, a benchmark that tests large language models’ ability to plan multi-step paths through Wikipedia by following hyperlinks. Models operate under partial observability—they see only the current page and a filtered set of outgoing links (nearest 50 to the target by oracle distance)—forcing them to leverage world knowledge and implicit look-ahead. Results show that while state-of-the-art models solve short, easy paths almost perfectly, performance degrades sharply as path length increases. A “planning gap” emerges: beyond a threshold of world knowledge, differences in planning and replanning capability drive success. Even top models frequently enter loops on hard tasks and fail to adapt, highlighting a fundamental limitation in long-horizon, adaptive reasoning.

## Method Summary
The benchmark uses a static Wikipedia graph (April 2024 snapshot). For each game instance, the engine selects a source and target page with shortest-path length between 3 and 8 steps. At each turn, the engine:
1. Retrieves the current page’s outgoing links.
2. Filters them to the 50 whose targets have smallest oracle distance to the goal (oracle distances are hidden from the model).
3. Constructs a prompt containing: the current page, the target, the history of visited pages, and the filtered list of links (each with a numeric index).
4. Queries the LLM agent, which must output the index of the next link.
5. Transitions to the chosen page and repeats.
The game ends if the target is reached (success) or after 30 steps (failure). The engine logs success/failure, path efficiency, step count, loop occurrences, and parsing errors. This design isolates planning under partial observability while bounding action space and cost.

## Key Results
- Frontier models (Gemini‑3, GPT‑5, Claude Opus 4.5) exceed 95% success on easy (3-step) tasks but plummet on hard (8-step) tasks; the best model (Gemini‑3) succeeds in only 23% of hard games.
- A “planning gap” is identified: models with similar world-knowledge scores (probed via a direct link‑prediction task) show widely divergent in-game success rates, indicating planning/replanning as the key differentiator.
- Looping is a major failure mode: on hard tasks, top models encounter loops in 86–92% of failures, with recovery rates below 10%; loop frequency correlates strongly with failure (regression coefficient −1.02).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Forced Look‑Ahead via Partial Observability
- **Claim**: LLM‑WikiRace creates a realistic planning scenario by presenting only local link information, forcing models to leverage world knowledge and look‑ahead reasoning.
- **Mechanism**: The game engine filters outgoing links to the 50 nearest the target (by oracle distance) but does not reveal distances, and supplies the current page, target, and history. The agent must infer connectivity and plan a multi‑step path under partial observability.
- **Core assumption**: Pretrained LLMs encode sufficient world knowledge about Wikipedia content and link semantics to evaluate link relevance and simulate forward steps implicitly.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] “models must efficiently navigate Wikipedia hyperlinks step by step … requiring look‑ahead planning and the ability to reason about how concepts are connected in the real world.”
  - [section] “The task poses a challenging planning problem over a large, real‑world knowledge graph, requiring agents to combine long‑horizon planning, semantic reasoning over real‑world concepts, and the operational use of world knowledge.”
  - [corpus] Corpus includes “Ask‑before‑Plan: Proactive Language Agents for Real‑World Planning”, a related benchmark probing planning in open‑domain settings.
- **Break condition**: If the agent were given the full graph or unlimited computation per step, the need for implicit planning would vanish, reducing the task to trivial graph search.

### Mechanism 2: Planning Gap Beyond World Knowledge
- **Claim**: Once basic world knowledge is saturated, planning and long‑horizon reasoning become the primary differentiators of performance—a “planning gap”.
- **Mechanism**: The authors decouple world knowledge (via a direct link prediction classification task) from path planning. Models with similar knowledge accuracy show diverging success rates on WikiRace, indicating that planning ability limits top performers.
- **Core assumption**: World knowledge can be measured independently of planning via a static classification probe.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “Our analysis shows that world knowledge is a necessary ingredient for success, but only up to a point, beyond this threshold, planning and long‑horizon reasoning capabilities become the dominant factors.”
  - [section] Figure 1 plots F1 Score on the knowledge probe vs. average success rate, showing reasoning models above the linear trend of instruct‑tuned models; the authors label this the ’planning gap’.
  - [corpus] “LLMs Still Can’t Plan; Can LRMs?” independently reports planning limitations in LLMs.
- **Break condition**: If knowledge and planning were perfectly correlated, the scatter would lie on a single line with no gap.

### Mechanism 3: Replanning Failure and Looping
- **Claim**: Even top‑performing models systematically fail to replan after setbacks, often entering persistent loops.
- **Mechanism**: Trajectory analysis quantifies loop frequency (pages visited >1 time) and recovery rate (success given a loop). A strong negative correlation (regression coefficient −1.02) ties looping to failure, and qualitative traces show model awareness without corrective action.
- **Core assumption**: Adaptive control—detecting and escaping loops—is a distinct capability separate from forward planning.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “Trajectory‑level analysis further reveals that even the strongest models struggle to replan after failure, frequently entering loops rather than recovering.”
  - [section] Section 5.2 reports loop frequencies: Gemini 3 encounters loops in 86% of hard tasks with 10% recovery; Claude Opus 4.5 loops in 92% with 1% recovery, and Figure 5 shows the correlation.
  - [corpus] “Plan‑on‑Graph: Self‑Correcting Adaptive Planning of Large Language Model on Knowledge Graphs” proposes self‑correcting planning to mitigate looping, suggesting the community recognizes this failure mode.
- **Break condition**: Models that reliably detect and break loops would show a much weaker or absent correlation between loop frequency and success.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Partial Observability in Sequential Decision Making**
  - Why needed here: The agent only sees the current page and outgoing links, not the full Wikipedia graph. This forces inference of global structure and planning under uncertainty, which is a central challenge the benchmark highlights.
  - Quick check question: How does limited information at each step affect the agent’s strategy compared to having full graph visibility?

- **Concept: Long‑Horizon Planning and Credit Assignment**
  - Why needed here: The optimal path lengths (3‑8 steps) mean early choices have delayed consequences; errors compound over time. Understanding horizon effects explains why the task is difficult even with correct world knowledge.
  - Quick check question: Why might a model that performs well on single‑step link prediction fail on this multi‑step task?

- **Concept: Replanning and Adaptive Control**
  - Why needed here: The paper identifies that even successful models often cannot revise a failing plan, leading to loops. Distinguishing planning a correct path from adaptively monitoring execution is essential to interpreting results.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between planning a correct path and successfully executing it in a dynamic environment?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Wikipedia Graph (static CSR) -> Game Engine (oracle, filtering, prompt builder, state tracker) -> LLM Agent (link selector) -> Logger (metrics, loops, suboptimal steps)

**Critical Path**:
1. Engine initializes with a source page.
2. For each step ≤ 30:
   - Retrieve current page’s outgoing links.
   - Filter to up to 50 links with smallest oracle distance to target (oracle distances hidden).
   - Build prompt (current, target, filtered links, history).
   - Query LLM agent; parse selected index.
   - Transition to chosen page.
   - If page == target: log success and stop; else continue.
3. If target not reached within 30 steps: log failure.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Link filtering (50 links) reduces prompt size and cost but may omit crucial longer paths; 50 chosen as balance between realism and manageability.
- Fixed step limit (30) ensures termination and matches observed plateau in completion rates, but may penalize models that recover later.
- Static Wikipedia snapshot (April 2024) guarantees reproducibility but ages, potentially affecting real‑world relevance.
- Oracle distances used only for filtering, never shown to model, to preserve planning challenge while bounding action space.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Looping: repeated visitation of same page (detected via history).
- Dead ends: selecting links that lead into subgraphs far from target, causing step exhaustion.
- High suboptimal step count: success but with inefficient path.
- Prompt parsing errors: invalid index output leads to automatic failure or retry.

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. **Main benchmark evaluation**: Run each model on easy/medium/hard splits; record success rate and average suboptimal steps (reproduce Table 1).
2. **World knowledge probe**: Evaluate models on the binary classification task of direct link existence (Section 5.1) to separate knowledge from planning.
3. **Loop analysis**: Sample trajectories, compute loop frequency and recovery rates, and reproduce the regression in Figure 5 to validate the replanning failure hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does multi-step RL fine‑tuning improve hard‑task success?**  
   Basis: Section 7 proposes extending DAPO to multi‑step RL; one-step DAPO failed on hard tasks; impact unknown.
2. **How does planning frequency affect performance and looping?**  
   Basis: Section 7 cites understanding planning frequency as key; models may not replan effectively.
3. **Can RAG with graph connectivity info improve navigation?**  
   Basis: Section 7 lists retrieval‑augmented generation; models lack graph awareness; adding a retriever could help.
4. **What causes the planning gap and how to close it?**  
   Basis: Fig 1 and Sec 5.1 show planning gap between similar‑knowledge models; unknown factors differentiate planning ability.

## Limitations
- **Static Benchmark**: Wikipedia snapshot (April 2024) may not generalize to evolving graphs or other knowledge sources.
- **Partial Observability Design**: Filtering to 50 “nearest” links via oracle introduces an artificial action space; results may not hold if link count or heuristic changes.
- **Loop Definition & Recovery**: Loop detection based on page revisits (not semantic repetition); causal attribution limited.

## Confidence
- **Planning Gap Exists**: High confidence (supported by controlled knowledge probe vs. in‑game divergence, consistent across top models).
- **Looping is a Major Failure Mode**: High confidence (strong negative correlation, high loop frequencies on hard tasks).
- **World Knowledge is Necessary but Not Sufficient**: Medium confidence (knowledge probe shows correlation, but probe’s simplicity may underestimate integrated use).
- **Generalization to Broader Planning**: Low confidence (benchmark narrow—Wikipedia links; transfer to other domains speculative).

## Next Checks
1. **Ablate Link Filtering**: Vary number of filtered links (10, 50, 100, unfiltered) to test if planning gap and loop frequency persist under different action space constraints.
2. **Probe Explicit Planning**: Run a controlled experiment where models must output a full planned path before execution, then compare success to closed‑loop setting to measure cost of implicit vs. explicit planning.
3. **Intervention on Loops**: During failure trajectories, insert a system prompt warning of repetition and asking for replanning; measure change in recovery rate to isolate recognition‑vs.‑adaptation failure.
