# From Out-of-Distribution Detection to Hallucination Detection: A Geometric View

## Quick Facts
- arXiv: 2602.07253
- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07253
- References: 20
- Result: Reframes hallucination detection as OOD; geometric detectors NCI and fDBD achieve 3-10 AUROC point gains on reasoning tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles hallucination detection in large language models (LLMs) on reasoning tasks, an area where existing methods falter. It re-conceptualizes the problem as out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and adapts two geometric OOD detectors – NCI (feature proximity to weight vectors) and fDBD (distance to decision boundaries) – to the LLM setting. Key innovations include an analytical, data-free proxy for training feature statistics and a selective computation scheme for fDBD that operates only on a top‑k subset of tokens for efficiency. Experiments on CSQA, GSM8K, and AQuA datasets using Llama and Qwen models show that both methods outperform single‑sample baselines (e.g., Perplexity) and multi‑sample baselines. Notably, fDBD with an appropriate k (e.g., 1000 on CSQA) yields AUROC improvements of 3–10 points, demonstrating strong, scalable, and training‑free hallucination detection under both greedy and stochastic decoding.

## Method Summary
The authors introduce two post‑hoc detectors that operate on the penultimate layer embeddings and the linear language head. NCI measures the cosine similarity between the centered embedding and the weight vector of the predicted token; misalignment indicates hallucination. fDBD approximates the distance from the embedding to the decision boundary of the correct class by considering a limited set of alternative tokens (top‑k) and normalizing by the distance to the global feature mean. Both detectors rely on an analytical estimate of the global training mean, derived from the head parameters using the concept of Decision‑Neutral Closest Point, which avoids storing large datasets. For sequence‑level decisions, per‑step scores are averaged, and a threshold determines the final binary verdict. The approach is training‑free, single‑sample, and compatible with any autoregressive decoder.

## Key Results
- Both NCI and fDBD consistently surpass single‑sample baselines like Perplexity and Max P on reasoning datasets.
- fDBD with selective k (e.g., k=1000 on CSQA) achieves AUROC gains of 3–10 points over the strongest baselines.
- The methods are efficient, scalable, and maintain high detection performance under both greedy and stochastic decoding regimes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: NCI – Feature Proximity to Weight Vectors
- **Claim:** A lower cosine similarity between the centered penultimate feature and the weight vector of the predicted token signals hallucination.
- **Mechanism:** Compute per‑step score `s_NCI(z_t) = cos(w_̂c, z_t – µ_G) / ||w_̂c||_2`, where `w_̂c` is the weight vector for the predicted token `c`, and `µ_G` is the analytical mean. The sequence‑level score is the average over steps.
- **Core assumption:** Correct generations produce embeddings aligned with their corresponding weight vectors, whereas hallucinations are mis‑aligned and resemble out‑of‑distribution features.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Section 3.2 define the proximity score and Figure 1a shows hallucinated embeddings have significantly lower proximity. The NECO paper (FMR 0.511) supports neural‑collapse‑based OOD detection.

### Mechanism 2: fDBD – Distance to Decision Boundaries
- **Claim:** A smaller normalized distance to the decision boundary indicates higher hallucination risk.
- **Mechanism:** For each step `t`, consider the top‑k alternative tokens `K_t` (excluding the top‑1). For each `c in K_t`, compute the approximate distance `˜D(z_t, c) = |(w_̂c – w_c)ᵀ z_t + (b_̂c – b_c)| / ||w_̂c – w_c||_2`, normalized by `||z_t – µ_G||_2`. The per‑step score is the average over `K_t`, and the sequence score averages across steps.
- **Core assumption:** Correct embeddings lie deep within the correct decision region, while hallucinated embeddings are near the boundary and would flip with small perturbations.
- **Evidence:** Abstract lists fDBD; Section 3.3 formalizes decision regions and Theorem 3.3 gives the lower‑bound approximation; Section 4.3 shows restricting to top‑k improves AUROC. The OOD detection survey (FMR 0.568) validates decision‑boundary‑based OOD methods.

### Mechanism 3: Analytical Mean Proxy
- **Claim:** The analytical proxy for the training feature mean (Decision‑Neutral Closest Point) eliminates the need for large‑scale data statistics while maintaining or improving detection performance.
- **Mechanism:** Solve `z* = argmin_z ||P(Wz + b)||²`, where `W` and `b` are the language head parameters and `P` centers the logits. The closed‑form `z* = -(Wᵀ P W)† Wᵀ P b` serves as `µ_G` in both detectors.
- **Core assumption:** The true mean of penultimate‑layer training features lies near the point where the classifier is maximally uncertain (equalized logits), a consequence of neural collapse.
- **Evidence:** Abstract highlights the data‑free proxy; Section 4.2 derives it and Table 1 shows it outperforms an empirical mean estimate on CSQA.

## Foundational Learning
### Out‑of‑distribution (OOD) detection
- **Why needed:** The paper re‑frames hallucination detection as an OOD problem; understanding basic OOD concepts clarifies the motivation and justifies the geometric approach.
- **Quick check:** What is the key difference between post‑hoc OOD detection for images and token‑level OOD detection in LLMs?

### Neural collapse
- **Why needed:** NCI directly builds on neural collapse theory, where features of the same class collapse to their mean and weight vectors become equiangular. This justifies using weight‑vector proximity as a detection score.
- **Quick check:** State the two properties of neural collapse observed in the terminal phase of training.

### Linear decision boundaries in multi‑class classifiers
- **Why needed:** fDBD computes distances to hyperplanes separating token classes; understanding how these boundaries arise from the linear head is essential.
- **Quick check:** For a linear model with weights `w_i` and biases `b_i`, derive the equation of the boundary between the class with highest logit and another class `c`.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Transformer backbone → penultimate embedding `z_t` → Language head (`W`, `b`) → Analytical mean module (computes `µ_G`) → NCI module / fDBD module → Aggregator (averages per‑step scores) → Threshold module (binary decision).

**Critical path:**  
1. Run LLM generation; collect `z_t` and logits at every step.  
2. Determine the predicted (top‑1) token and the top‑k alternative set per step.  
3. Compute per‑step NCI and fDBD scores using `µ_G`.  
4. Average scores across all steps to obtain sequence‑level `S_NCI` and `S_fDBD`.  
5. Compare `S` to a threshold; flag hallucination if `S ≤ τ`.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- NCI: `O(d_model)` per step, low latency; fDBD: `O(d_model·k)` plus `O(|V|)` for top‑k selection, higher cost but potentially better AUROC.  
- Choice of `k` in fDBD: small `k` reduces cost but may miss informative boundaries; large `k` adds overhead. The paper finds a sweet spot (e.g., `k=1000` on CSQA).  
- Analytical proxy removes data storage and preprocessing; may be slightly less accurate than an empirical mean if full training data are available.  
- Both methods are single‑sample and training‑free, unlike multi‑sample baselines that require additional forward passes.

**Failure signatures:**  
- fDBD’s AUROC drops dramatically when `k` is too small (e.g., `k < 10`), indicating reliance on a sufficient alternative set.  
- NCI near random (AUROC ~0.5) suggests weight vectors are not discriminative or centering is incorrect.  
- High variance under stochastic decoding could indicate geometric scores are not robust to sampling variability.  
- Performance collapse on highly diverse reasoning chains may signal that neither score captures uncertainty.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline comparison:** Evaluate NCI, fDBD (with selected `k`), and single‑sample baselines (Perplexity, Max P) on a held‑out reasoning dataset (e.g., CSQA validation) to confirm AUROC gains.  
2. **Alternative set size ablation:** Sweep `k` across `{1,10,100,1k,10k,All}` for fDBD, plot AUROC, and identify a robust plateau region.  
3. **Stochastic decoding robustness:** Run all detectors under temperatures `{0.2,0.5,0.8,1.0}` with multiple seeds; compute mean and standard deviation of AUROC to verify stability (as in Table 3).

## Limitations
- The assumption that hallucinations are always OOD may fail for plausible but incorrect generations that remain within the training distribution.
- The analytical mean proxy hinges on neural collapse properties that are not firmly established for large autoregressive LLMs; pseudo‑inverse instability could distort scores.
- Evaluation is limited to three reasoning datasets; it is unclear whether results transfer to open‑ended tasks or other modalities.
