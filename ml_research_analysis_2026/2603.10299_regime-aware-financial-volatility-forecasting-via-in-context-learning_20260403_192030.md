# Regime-aware financial volatility forecasting via in-context learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10299  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10299  
- **Reference count:** 11  
- **Primary result:** A regime-aware in-context learning framework reduces overall MAE/RMSE for volatility forecasting and achieves ~27% lower high‑volatility MAE than GJR‑GARCH on S&P500.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles nonstationary financial volatility forecasting by proposing a regime-aware in-context learning approach. It avoids fine‑tuning a pretrained LLM (GPT‑4o‑mini) and instead conditions its predictions on demonstrations sampled from a regime‑labeled pool. The pool is built via an oracle‑guided refinement loop on training data, with each sample labeled as high- or low‑volatility. At inference, the current regime is estimated from recent realized variance, and demonstrations are selected conditionally to guide the LLM. Experiments on S&P500, NASDAQ, and EUR/USD show improved overall MAE/RMSE and a substantial reduction in high‑volatility forecasting error compared to classical models (HAR, GJR‑GARCH) and naive LLM baselines.

## Method Summary
The pipeline consists of offline pool construction and online inference. Offline, historical training samples are processed: each input window of 7 days of log returns and realized variance is formatted into a textual prompt. An initial LLM prediction is iteratively refined (3 iterations) using oracle feedback (ground truth, error, heuristic) to produce a final prediction. This tuple (prompt, refined prediction, regime label based on next‑day realized variance exceeding the training 80th percentile) is stored. From these, a fixed pool of 500 refined demonstrations is split into high‑ and low‑volatility subsets. Online, for a new test sample, the current regime is estimated by averaging the last 3 realized variances and comparing to a fixed threshold (τ′). Five demonstrations are sampled from the corresponding subset (or via a fixed prior) and concatenated with the current input prompt. The frozen LLM generates the next‑day volatility forecast from this context.

## Key Results
- Overall MAE and RMSE are reduced compared to classical baselines (HAR, GJR‑GARCH) and naive LLM setups (one‑shot, random selection) across all three datasets.
- High‑volatility MAE is approximately 27% lower than GJR‑GARCH on S&P500, demonstrating superior adaptation during turbulent periods.
- Conditional sampling based on the estimated regime label outperforms random and fixed‑prior strategies, particularly for high‑volatility forecasts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Conditional Demonstration Sampling
- **Claim:** Demonstration selection conditioned on an estimated volatility regime adapts the LLM’s in‑context predictions to regime‑specific dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM receives historical examples explicitly drawn from the high‑ or low‑volatility subset matching the inferred current regime (via recent realized variance). This provides context reflecting the anticipated volatility scale and persistence.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can infer and apply regime‑dependent forecasting rules from relevant in‑context examples without parameter updates.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “demonstrations are sampled conditional to the estimated market label”; [section 3.3.1] “demonstration selection is conditioned on an estimated volatility regime at test time”.
- **Break condition:** Inaccurate regime estimation or an insufficient/ noisy demonstration pool (e.g., too few high‑volatility examples) leads to irrelevant context and degraded performance.

### Mechanism 2: Oracle‑Guided Refinement for Demonstration Quality
- **Claim:** The iterative feedback loop during training yields higher‑quality, regime‑labeled demonstrations than raw historical data points.
- **Mechanism:** For each training sample, the LLM’s initial prediction is refined over multiple iterations using oracle feedback (true next‑day volatility, error magnitude, heuristic such as recent average). The final refined prediction better exemplifies the mapping from history to accurate volatility.
- **Core assumption:** The refinement elicits more accurate and robust predictive behavior for that input context than a single‑shot prompt.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2] “This feedback‑driven refinement loop allows the LLM to explicitly reason about its prediction errors and perform structured test‑time numerical correction.”
- **Break condition:** Overfitting to the oracle’s heuristic or mismatched feedback format may produce demonstrations that are noisy or non‑generalizable.

### Mechanism 3: Pretrained LLM as a Sequence Reasoner
- **Claim:** A fixed pretrained LLM, prompted with structured numerical time‑series representations, possesses inherent capabilities for volatility forecasting.
- **Mechanism:** Historical log returns and realized variances are transformed into a textual sequence (p_input). The LLM’s next‑token prediction over a numerical output space, conditioned on this prompt, yields a volatility forecast. The model’s pretraining on diverse sequences is assumed to impart general temporal reasoning that transfers to this numerical task.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s tokenization and attention can process the structured prompt as meaningful patterns, and its latent knowledge contains relevant volatility dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “deploys pretrained LLMs to reason over historical volatility patterns”; [section 1.2] cites prior work (e.g., Gruver et al., 2023) showing LLMs can forecast numerical time series via prompting.
- **Break condition:** If prompt engineering (format, scaling, precision) fails to align volatility patterns with the LLM’s pretraining distribution, latent numerical reasoning may be ineffective.

## Foundational Learning

**In‑context Learning (ICL)**  
- **Why needed:** The entire framework relies on ICL to adapt a frozen LLM to the forecasting task without weight updates. Understanding how demonstrations bias next‑token prediction is fundamental to designing the pool and selection strategy.  
- **Quick check:** If you provide an LLM with a sequence of [historical returns, volatility] followed by a [target volatility] for several examples, what internal mechanism does the paper assume allows it to generalize this mapping to a new, unseen history?

**Volatility Regime Detection**  
- **Why needed:** The core innovation is conditioning on the volatility regime. This requires understanding how regimes are defined (threshold τ on realized variance), estimated from recent data (st), and how the label determines which demonstration subset (D_high or D_low) is used.  
- **Quick check:** How is the current volatility regime label (ˆR_t) estimated at inference time, and what is the critical constraint to prevent data leakage?

**Demonstration Engineering**  
- **Why needed:** Demonstration quality directly impacts performance. This concept covers the oracle‑guided refinement loop (training) and conditional sampling strategies (inference), which together form the system’s “knowledge base.”  
- **Quick check:** What are the two main differences between the “random selection” baseline and the proposed “conditional selection via estimated label” strategy for picking demonstrations from the pool?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Preprocessing -> Oracle‑Guided Refinement -> Demonstration Pool -> Regime Estimation -> Conditional Sampler -> LLM Inference

**Critical path:**  
Input x_t -> Regime Estimate ˆR_t -> Sampler -> K Demos -> LLM Prompt -> Forecast ˆν_{t+1}

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Simplicity vs. Performance: Random sampling is simple but suboptimal. Conditional sampling on estimated label improves high‑volatility performance but risks low‑volatility degradation if regime detection is noisy. The fixed‑prior approach (α) is a middle ground.  
- Pool Quality vs. Cost: Oracle‑guided refinement (3 iterations, 500 demos) increases offline computational cost but aims for higher‑quality, regime‑specific examples.  
- Leakage Prevention: The regime threshold τ for labeling training data is derived only from the training set. The inference threshold τ′ and window m for st must be chosen *a priori* and fixed before test evaluation to avoid lookahead bias.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Regime Misclassification: If recent volatility st poorly proxies the next‑step regime (e.g., during rapid transitions), conditional sampling pulls demonstrations from the wrong pool, likely causing large errors.  
- Demonstration Pool Skew: If the training set has very few high‑volatility periods (or refined predictions for them are poor), D_high is small/noisy, limiting conditional sampling benefits in turbulent markets.  
- LLM Numerical Drift: The LLM may output forecasts on an incorrect scale (e.g., orders of magnitude off) due to poor numerical grounding in text prompts, leading to systematic bias.  
- Refinement Overfitting: The oracle’s heuristic guidance H_t during refinement might train the LLM to make corrections specific to that heuristic, which may not generalize to unseen test‑time patterns.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Main Evaluation:** Compare overall MAE/RMSE and regime‑wise MAE of the proposed regime‑aware in‑context learner (with label estimate) against classical baselines (HAR, GJR‑GARCH) and naive LLM baselines (one‑shot, random selection) across all datasets (S&P500, NASDAQ, EUR/USD). Expected: Conditioned LLM shows improved high‑volatility MAE, confirming regime adaptation.  
2. **Ablation on Sampling Strategy:** Isolate the contribution of conditional sampling by comparing “random selection,” “fixed prior,” and “label estimate” within the same refined LLM pipeline. Expected: “label estimate” achieves lowest MAE_high, showing the value of test‑time regime adaptation.  
3. **Cross‑Dataset Generalization:** Train the demonstration pool on one dataset (e.g., S&P500) and test on another (e.g., NASDAQ) without modification. This tests the portability of the regime‑aware prompting strategy across different assets. Expected: Performance may degrade relative to in‑dataset testing, indicating some asset‑specific tailoring is beneficial, but the conditional approach should still outperform random/one‑shot baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can retrieval‑based demonstration selection improve regime‑aware in‑context learning for volatility forecasting compared to random or conditionally sampled demonstrations? The conclusion suggests this as a future direction, but the paper only evaluates random and conditional (fixed‑prior/label‑estimate) strategies.
2. To what extent does the proposed regime‑aware in‑context learning framework generalize to asset classes with different volatility structures (e.g., cryptocurrencies, commodities) and to multi‑regime scenarios beyond binary high/low labeling? Evaluation is limited to three datasets (equity/FX indices) and a binary 80th‑percentile threshold.
3. How does the choice of pretrained LLM (architecture, size, training data) impact the efficacy of regime‑aware in‑context learning for volatility forecasting? The paper uses only GPT‑4o‑mini, with no ablation across models.
4. Can the oracle‑guided refinement loop for demonstration construction be automated or replaced with unsupervised methods to remove dependency on ground‑truth volatility during training? The refinement relies on an oracle providing true realized variance and a heuristic; robustness to oracle accuracy or alternative signals is unexplored.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt template (format, precision, scaling) for converting numerical sequences into text is unspecified, risking LLM numerical drift and poor reproducibility.
- Regime estimation uses a short recent window (m=3) and a fixed threshold τ′; sensitivity to these hyperparameters and potential for misclassification during transitions are not analyzed.
- Experimental evaluation is confined to three assets (S&P500, NASDAQ, EUR/USD); cross‑asset and out‑of‑sample generalizability remain unclear.
- Possible data leakage if test‑derived statistics inadvertently influence τ′ or pool construction; the protocol for threshold selection is not fully explicit.
- Oracle‑guided refinement incurs non‑trivial offline computational cost; scalability to larger datasets is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Overall MAE/RMSE reduction** (vs. classical and naive LLM): *Medium* (empirical evidence on three datasets but reproducibility hindered by missing prompt details).
- **Conditional sampling advantage** (especially high‑volatility MAE): *Medium* (gains consistent but hinge on accurate regime estimation and pool quality).
- **Oracle‑guided refinement improving demonstration quality**: *Low* (no ablation isolating its contribution; benefit confounded with overall pipeline).
- **LLM ICL for volatility forecasting**: *Medium* (extends prior work; however, prompt design and numerical grounding are critical unknowns).

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt reconstruction:** Obtain or infer exact prompt structures (including any normalization/scaling) and re‑run predictions to verify numerical stability and distribution match reported metrics.
2. **Regime estimation sensitivity:** Systematically vary the recent window size (m) and threshold selection method (training‑only quantile vs. rolling quantile) to measure performance robustness and detect potential leakage.
3. **Cross‑asset generalization:** Train the demonstration pool on one asset (e.g., S&P500) and evaluate on other markets (NASDAQ, EUR/USD, plus additional indices/commodities) without adaptation; test if conditional ICL retains advantage.
