# RetailBench: Evaluating Long-Horizon Autonomous Decision-Making and Strategy Stability of LLM Agents in Realistic Retail Environments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.16453
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16453
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The Evolving Strategy & Execution framework improves operational stability and efficiency of LLM agents in a retail simulation, but performance degrades significantly as environment complexity increases.

## Executive Summary
RetailBench introduces a benchmark for evaluating long-horizon autonomous decision-making of large language model (LLM) agents in dynamic, realistic retail environments. The core contribution is the Evolving Strategy & Execution framework, which separates high-level strategic reasoning from low-level action execution, enabling adaptive and interpretable strategy evolution over extended durations. The framework is tested across eight state-of-the-art LLMs and three difficulty levels (Easy, Middle, Hard) in a supermarket simulation. Results show that the proposed framework outperforms Reflection and Plan-and-Act baselines on key operational metrics: higher average days survived, increased daily sales and income, and lower product expiry and return ratios. However, performance drops markedly as the environment becomes more complex (e.g., inclusion of daily news items and larger product catalogs), revealing fundamental limitations of current LLMs in handling scalable, multi-factor, long-horizon decision-making.

## Method Summary
The task is to evaluate LLM agents on long-horizon autonomous decision-making within a supermarket simulation. The environment state comprises product details, inventory levels, supply conditions, demand signals, external factors, and financial status. Agents can perform actions such as adjusting prices, placing replenishment orders, querying information, updating memory, or ending the day. Evaluation metrics include the number of days survived (survival), average daily sales and income (higher is better), and expiry and return ratios (lower is better). The proposed Evolving Strategy & Execution framework operates in two phases per day: a strategy phase where the agent analyzes the current state and formulates a high-level plan, and an execution phase where the agent selects concrete actions based on that plan. This approach is compared against two baselines: Reflection (which reasons after each day or step) and Plan-and-Act (which generates a plan and immediately acts without separation). Experiments are conducted with eight LLMs (including both open- and closed-source models) across three difficulty configurations: Easy (5 categories, limited funds, no news), Middle (20 categories, increased funds and rent, no news), and Hard (20 categories plus 20 daily news items affecting demand).

## Key Results
- The Evolving Strategy & Execution framework yields higher average days survived, daily sales, and income, alongside lower expiry and return ratios compared to Reflection and Plan-and-Act baselines.
- Agent performance declines substantially as environment complexity increases from Easy to Middle to Hard, indicating scalability challenges.
- The results highlight fundamental limitations of current LLMs in managing multi-factor, long-horizon decision-making under dynamic conditions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The separation of strategy and execution reduces the cognitive burden on the LLM, allowing it to focus on high-level planning without being distracted by immediate action selection. The strategy phase incorporates reflection on historical data and forecasting, producing a coherent plan that guides the execution phase. This division leads to more consistent strategies over time, better adaptation to changes, and improved interpretability of the agent's reasoning process.

## Foundational Learning
- **Long-horizon sequential decision-making**  
  *Why needed*: Retail operations require planning over many days, with actions having delayed effects (e.g., ordering, pricing).  
  *Quick check*: Simulate 30 days with a simple rule-based agent; verify that cumulative rewards increase or stabilize.

- **Strategy-execution separation**  
  *Why needed*: Reduces context window pressure and improves stability by decoupling high-level planning from low-level action selection.  
  *Quick check*: Measure variance of high-level strategic statements across adjacent days; lower variance indicates stable strategy.

- **Multinomial logit (MNL) demand model**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a realistic, differentiable model of customer choice among products based on price, reviews, and attributes.  
  *Quick check*: For a fixed set of SKUs, compute choice probabilities and confirm they sum to 1 and respond logically to price changes.

- **External news events**  
  *Why needed*: Introduce stochastic demand shocks that test the agent’s adaptability.  
  *Quick check*: Inject a news item (e.g., price hike on a key ingredient) and observe expected shift in demand for affected categories.

- **Replenishment and pricing actions**  
  *Why needed*: Primary levers for controlling inventory and revenue; must respect business constraints.  
  *Quick check*: Ensure generated actions adhere to valid ranges (prices > 0 and ≤ 50, order quantities within storage capacity).

- **Evaluation metrics (survival, sales, expiry/return)**  
  *Why needed*: Capture both financial performance and operational health (e.g., waste, customer dissatisfaction).  
  *Quick check*: Correlate metrics with simulated cash flow; days survived should monotonically increase with net profit.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
State -> Strategy Module -> Plan -> Execution Module -> Actions -> Environment -> New State

**Critical path**:  
1. Agent receives current state (inventory, financials, demand, news).  
2. Strategy module analyzes state and produces a high-level plan (e.g., pricing strategy, replenishment targets).  
3. Execution module translates the plan into specific actions (e.g., set price of SKU X, order quantity of SKU Y).  
4. Actions are applied to the environment, updating internal state and producing feedback (sales, revenue, expiries).  
5. Agent proceeds to the next day.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Separation of strategy and execution adds latency but improves interpretability and stability; without it, agents may produce incoherent actions.  
- Using a single LLM for both phases balances simplicity but may limit specialization; a dedicated LLM per phase could improve performance at higher cost.  
- The framework relies on the LLM’s ability to maintain a coherent plan across days; long contexts may cause plan drift.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High day-to-day variation in the “execution_strategy” field (low Jaccard similarity) indicating unstable strategic direction.  
- Incomplete coverage of relevant information sources in the final strategy (e.g., ignoring supplier prices or reviews) correlating with suboptimal sales.  
- Generation of hallucinated SKU identifiers or invalid dates/assumptions.  
- Execution of actions outside permissible ranges, such as negative prices or excessive order quantities.

**First experiments**:  
1. Implement the MNL demand model from the description and validate that purchase probabilities are logically consistent (e.g., increase with lower price, higher reviews) and sum to 1 over the choice set.  
2. Run the Evolving Strategy & Execution agent with GPT‑4 on the Easy configuration, using the exact prompts from Appendix C; verify that average days survived and average income are within ±5% of the values reported in Table 1.  
3. Recreate the heuristic upper bound policy (as described in Section 4.1); if the performance gap between this policy and the best LLM agent exceeds 10%, investigate possible missing environmental factors (e.g., supplier lead times, demand elasticity parameters).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Key open questions emerging from the work include:
- How can LLM agents be made more robust to plan drift over long horizons, especially as context length increases? (The design tradeoffs note that long contexts may cause plan drift.)
- Would separating the strategy and execution phases onto dedicated LLMs improve performance and stability compared to using a single LLM for both? (The tradeoffs mention specialization as a promising but untested direction.)
- What architectural modifications are needed to scale the framework to significantly larger product catalogs and more frequent news events without performance loss? (The Hard condition already shows substantial degradation.)
- How can hallucinations and invalid actions be minimized in the execution phase? (Failure signatures include these issues.)
- Can the missing environmental details (e.g., supplier lead times, exact demand elasticity) be reliably inferred to close the performance gap with the heuristic upper bound? (The limitations highlight missing factors.)

## Limitations
- The environment implementation is not released; reconstruction from textual description risks subtle discrepancies affecting reproducibility.  
- Critical details such as tool schemas, inference parameters for closed-source models, and the heuristic upper bound policy are omitted, hindering full verification.  
- The evaluation covers only eight LLMs and three difficulty levels; broader generalization to other models or more complex retail settings remains untested.

## Confidence
- Framework improves stability/efficiency: **Medium**. The improvements are consistent across Easy and Middle settings but become less pronounced in Hard, suggesting moderate confidence.
- Performance degrades with increased complexity: **High**. The experiments demonstrate a clear monotonic decline in survival, sales, income, and increased waste across all models as difficulty increases.
- Strategy evolution is interpretable: **Low**. While the strategy phase produces textual plans, the paper does not provide systematic human evaluation; interpretability is assumed rather than measured.
- Fundamental LLM limitations for scalable decision-making: **Medium**. Results indicate significant challenges, but the precise nature of the limitations (e.g., reasoning depth, context management) remains speculative.
